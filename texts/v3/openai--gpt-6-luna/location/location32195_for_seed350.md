The water is warm enough to feel breathed upon. It fills the low country to the crowns of its trees, then climbs higher without making a shore. Beyond the nearest thicket, the whole region is submerged beneath a dim, green-brown ceiling: not quite the underside of a sea, not quite a sky. Rain falls upward through it in thick ropes, gathering into bright beads that hang for a moment before sinking through leaves and mud.

Nothing here is old enough to have become gentle. The land has only recently surfaced—or is still trying to. Fresh black shelves of basalt break through the water at steep, unfinished angles. Pale clay banks slough away in slabs. Ridges of new silt buckle and split, their edges as sharp as broken pottery. Whole islands rearrange themselves in the space between one thunderclap and the next: a spit of mud becomes a channel; a channel swells into a basin; a stand of trees tilts, roots exposed, and remains upright after the earth beneath it has vanished.

Vegetation occupies nearly every gap. It is lush, but not soft. Broad translucent fronds crowd the surface, their undersides veined with dull orange light. Reed forests clatter together in the current. Rope-thick roots hang from drowned branches and run down through the water, sometimes meeting the bottom, sometimes passing through it. There are mats of floating greenery dense enough to bear the weight of silt, branches, and scattered machine parts. Their surfaces are slick with mineral bloom. When the mats buckle, they fold like wet fabric and reveal layers underneath: more leaves, more roots, submerged trunks, pale fungal fans, and the black water below.

There are no animals. No fins disturb the shallows; nothing nests in the reeds or calls from the trees. The leaves still open and close. Seed pods swell, rupture, and vanish into the current. Some stems turn toward sounds that have no source. A few fronds resemble ears, but they do not hear anything that a living creature could hear. The vegetation is not a substitute for animal life so much as a different kind of population: crowded, restless, and strangely attentive.

The water carries the whole noise of the place. It booms against new stone, hammers the reeds, and bellows through gaps in the drowned forest. Beneath that, machinery runs continuously. Somewhere in the haze, a pump or turbine strikes a slow, enormous rhythm; closer by, a chain rattles under the water with the frantic insistence of something being dragged. A factory horn sounds from no visible building, its note stretched and bent by the current until it seems to come from every direction at once. The noise is deafening, but it has no single loudest point. One can stand in the middle of it and feel that the roar is not around the body but inside the body, vibrating the ribs and teeth.

Industrial structures are scattered through the flooded growth. A loading crane rises from a half-sunk platform, its long arm ending in a cluster of rusted hooks. The hooks swing although the air is still. A line of concrete pylons disappears into the water, reappears far off among the reeds, then seems to continue above the surface as a row of dark marks in the dim ceiling. A small pumping station clings to a low basalt island. Its windows are clouded from within, its pipes swollen with vines. The station’s generators are active, though no cables lead to them and no fuel is stored there. Their steady electric hum interferes with the thunder, making the water’s surface shiver in geometric patterns.

There are no roads, only interrupted indications of roads: a strip of asphalt under the shallows, a white painted line climbing out of the water and ending on a tree trunk; a rusted signpost whose arrow points down. Occasionally a stairway emerges from the mud, descending into water that is no deeper than a palm’s width. Looking down those steps, however, one sees a vastness of dark water and suspended lamps, as if the stairway had opened into an immense interior.

Light is scarce and unreliable. The day does not brighten so much as thin out. A few patches of milky glow drift beneath the clouded surface, illuminating leaves from below. There is no dependable direction to them. Shadows collect where there ought to be light and loosen where objects ought to cast them. A person might see their reflection on the surface, then watch the reflected face turn toward a sound before they have done so. Far-off structures appear briefly crisp, with every rivet visible, though they are miles away—or perhaps directly behind the viewer, hidden by a stand of reeds.

Distance does not behave consistently here. The landscape is open enough to extend beyond the immediate thicket, but crowded enough that every route is tangled with growth, cables, floating timber, and water-buried machinery. Across a channel, a low green shore may seem reachable in minutes. The crossing then takes an hour, while the shore itself recedes at the same pace. Sometimes a place draws near without being approached. A black tower is visible through the rain; a blink later, its reflection fills the water below, though the tower has disappeared from the horizon.

The current is forever beginning. Water rises in sudden domes, pours sideways through the reeds, and falls in sheets from the open air. In some channels it flows uphill, carrying leaves, rust, and whole mats of vegetation toward the high ground. The force of it shifts the young banks constantly. Fresh earth crumbles into the flood with a muffled collapse, while new ridges thrust upward behind it. Silt clouds the shallows, forming curtains that move against the current. In those curtains, the outlines of buildings sometimes appear: smokestacks, roofs, a row of windows lit in a warmer darkness. The shapes dissolve when the water clears, leaving no debris.

The place seems to be assembling itself from whatever it can reach. Plant matter knots around iron. Iron grows smooth with mineral deposits. Stone comes apart in clean, recent faces; the pieces drift together and fit, imperfectly, into structures that resemble walls or piers. A gear turns slowly beneath a mat of leaves, though it has no shaft and drives nothing. Water passes through it and emerges in a hard-edged column that remains upright for several seconds after the surrounding flood has changed direction.

There are rules, though none can be trusted for long. The water is always moving, but not always from one place to another. The reeds bend to the noise, yet the noise often follows them. No living animal leaves a track, but impressions appear in mud: enormous prints filling with water, or tiny ones climbing vertical tree trunks. The machines continue to work without operators. The vegetation spreads, but some patches shrink when watched. Nothing is entirely submerged or entirely dry. Each surface is also a passage into something else.

At the center of the flooded region, the industrial hum grows low enough to feel like weather. The reeds thin, and the water opens around a cluster of fresh stone slabs, each dark and slick, newly lifted from below. Their edges are angular and clean. A thin forest grows through them, already dense with leaves. Between the slabs, the water boils upward in silent fountains. Above them hangs a pallid brightness, not a sun but a broad absence in the dimness, illuminating nothing directly.

Here the roar briefly resolves into separate sounds: the crack of splitting stone, the shudder of a turbine, the hiss of rain passing upward through the canopy. Then they braid together again, louder than before. The water rises over the slabs. The new forest bows, straightens, and turns its leaves toward the unseen light. Somewhere beneath it, a machine starts with a deep metallic cough, and the whole landscape shifts a few inches to the east.
