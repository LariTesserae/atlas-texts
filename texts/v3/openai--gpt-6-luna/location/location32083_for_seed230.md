The town occupies a single stone shelf high above the lower valleys, small enough that a person can cross it in an hour, but built so tightly that from the ridge it looks like a city compressed into a block. Its outer wall follows the shelf’s natural edge. Beyond it, the mountain falls away in pale, weathered faces, and the next ridge stands across a gap of cool air.

Nothing here looks newly made. The ground is worn smooth where feet have passed for generations, but the stone has not been ground down to dust. Steps dip at their centers; corners are rounded; lintels bear shallow grooves from tools whose shapes are still familiar. Houses are built from the same gray rock as the shelf, fitted together without much mortar. They rise two or three storeys, with narrow lanes between them and bridges crossing overhead. From certain alleys the roofs nearly meet, leaving only strips of sky.

There is little wood. What beams and shutters the town has are dark, close-grained, and carefully reused. Most doors are heavy slabs. Water runs through open channels cut beside the lanes, guided toward covered cisterns beneath small squares. The channels are dry more often than not. At intervals, moisture gathers on the uphill walls and trickles down in thin threads. Rain comes briefly, with no drama: a veil of drops, a darkening of the stone, then the slow return of the usual dry chill.

The town’s builders have arranged themselves around this scant water. Houses nearest the seepage have deeper cellars and small courts open to the sky, where jars collect condensation. Farther out, the walls are thicker and the courtyards smaller. There are no fountains, only basins with worn rims and a few public taps that release water in measured turns. A person can hear a cup being filled across a quiet square.

Vegetation survives in cracks and protected corners: low gray-green herbs, a few thorned bushes on the outer slope, moss where water lingers beneath stairways. No tree rises above the roofs. The town keeps a few planters against sun-facing walls, but they hold more soil than growth. Small birds appear occasionally, often alone, picking at the seams between stones. They do not nest in great numbers. Most of the time the only living movement is a shutter lifting, a figure crossing a bridge, or a thread of water finding its way along a channel.

The air is cool and thin, clear except when a band of cloud rests against the mountain. Such cloud does not roll through so much as settle along the town’s outer edge, softening the far view while leaving the lanes visible. The light is ordinary daylight, pale on stone and modest in the shadows. At evening, the buildings hold the last brightness on their upper faces; below, the streets darken early. Smoke from cooking fires rises straight when the air is still, then thins away above the roofs.

Sound is contained by the walls. Footsteps travel a long way along the stone lanes. A handcart creaks over a bridge. Somewhere a mallet taps steadily as a craftsperson fits a patch into an old roof. Voices are low, not from fear, but because there is little need to call. Wind can be heard along the outer wall, though within the town it arrives as a cool breath through a passage and is gone.

The place has settled into its height. Its lanes follow the same routes as the ledges beneath them; its drains empty where the slope has always allowed them to empty. Repairs are made with matching stone, and new rooms are usually fitted into existing walls rather than built outward. The town changes by substitutions: a roof tile replaced, a threshold smoothed further, a cistern cleaned and sealed. No one expects the ridge to move, the channels to change course, or the weather to become generous. Even the clouds seem to know where the shelf ends.

From the outer parapet, the roofs gather close around the town’s central square, then stop. Beyond them lie bare slopes, shallow gullies, and the wide, quiet distance of higher land. The settlement feels both dense and small: a handful of streets holding to one ledge, surrounded on every side by the patient emptiness of the mountain.
