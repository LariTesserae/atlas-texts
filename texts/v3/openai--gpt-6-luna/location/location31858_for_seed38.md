The high country is so old that it has forgotten how to be a mountain range.

From a distance it looks almost level: a green-black tableland bowed beneath a low ceiling of cloud. Only at its margins does the land reveal its height, where it breaks into immense shelves and falls away through mist. The cliffs are not bare stone. They are furred with hanging gardens, ribbed with roots, and veiled in long curtains of vegetation that descend so far they vanish before the lower valleys can be seen. Water threads down those curtains in countless fine lines. It never quite becomes a waterfall. It simply keeps arriving.

The plateau’s surface is soft underfoot, though there is no soil in any ordinary sense. Generations of leaves have grown, died, and grown through one another until they have made a resilient mat, deep as a house in some places and as yielding as a sponge. Beneath it, the oldest plants have become terrain. Their knotted trunks lie buried in green layers; their roots form ridges, basins, and archways. One may walk for days across what appears to be a plain, then discover that it is the crown of a single ancient growth, broad enough to have its own streams and weather.

Rain is frequent, but rarely falls in drops. The air gathers water on every surface: along the fine hairs of leaves, in the shallow cups of flowers, on the pale skin of exposed roots. Now and then the whole country exhales. Mist lifts from the plant-mat and travels in slow sheets across the upland, dimming the nearest shapes and erasing the far ones. The warmth remains inside it. Even in the grayest hours the air is mild and close, fragrant with wet bark, crushed green, and the mineral smell of stone that has not seen sunlight for an age.

Light reaches the plateau in a diluted form. There is no deep night, only a gradual passage from gray to darker gray, and then a faint silvering of the clouds. The vegetation makes its own dimness. Overhead, broad leaves overlap like roof tiles, and below them hang smaller leaves, mosses, tassels, and soft threadlike growths. The few open spaces feel less like clearings than shallow bowls scooped in a living ceiling. Here a person can see the sky, but it seems very far above, pale and without a distinct sun.

Water has no fixed course for long. It travels through the plant-mat as much as over it. A narrow stream may disappear under a bank of moss and reappear several miles away, warm and clear, flowing from the hollow stem of a tree. Pools form in the folds between roots. Some are no wider than a handspan and deep enough to reflect the cloud ceiling; others occupy entire valleys, their borders blurred by reeds that grow outward year after year. The water is not still. It moves with the slow patience of sap, pressing through the landscape, filling old hollows, softening edges already worn nearly flat.

The country is crowded, though it does not feel busy. Plants occupy nearly every surface and most of the space between surfaces. There are towering groves of pale columns whose crowns dissolve into cloud, mats of dark, tiny leaves that creep over stone, and thick fans of straplike growth that bend beneath the wet air. Nothing seems to compete sharply. A plant will grow across another plant, then be grown across in turn. The oldest trunks are porous and cool, their interiors hollowed into chambers. Some have stood so long that smaller trees have rooted in the humus collected on their shoulders. Their bark bears no clear rings. It is layered in broad, folded plates, as if the tree has been slowly wrapping itself around the centuries.

Flowers are uncommon in the open air, but when they appear, they are large and pale, almost transparent. They open without scent and hold beads of water along their edges. After several days they close again, not wilting but simply withdrawing into the leaves. Their petals leave behind a faint silver dust that clings to nearby growth. In the dimness, these occasional blooms are less like color than like brief patches of remembered daylight.

Animals are plentiful, but most have adapted to be quiet. Small, broad-backed creatures move through the living ground beneath its surface, making slow bulges that pass underfoot and flatten behind them. There are long-legged waders in the shallow pools, dark-bodied and still until they lift their heads to drink. Their eyes catch what little light there is and return it softly. In the high branches, something like a flock of bats unfolds and folds again, though the wings are fringed with leaves and the creatures do not call. At dawn—if it is dawn—one may hear a deep, far-off clicking from within the groves, a sound like stones settling in a flooded cave.

Most of the time, the loudest noises are made by water. A drip strikes a leaf. A stream slips beneath a root. Somewhere a pool overflows its low green lip. These sounds do not carry far. The thick growth seems to absorb them, and even the wind arrives softened, moving the upper canopy in a slow and nearly inaudible sway. When a larger gust crosses the plateau, the whole forest bends as one body. For a moment the cloud ceiling opens into shifting lanes of gray. Then the leaves settle, and the land resumes its long stillness.

Scattered across this immense living surface are signs that something once made a more deliberate use of the place. A narrow stone stair descends into a green hollow and stops at the level of the moss. A low wall, rounded by centuries of rain, runs for a few dozen paces before it disappears beneath roots. In one grove, several standing stones lean together like old teeth. They are not carved with writing. Their faces are smooth, though one bears a shallow spiral worn so faintly it may have been made by water alone. The plants have not broken these remains so much as incorporated them. Moss fills the joints. Thin roots pass through cracks. A tree has grown around one pillar and held it upright in the curve of its trunk.

There are no roads, only places where the ground has grown firmer through repeated passage. These paths wander between pools and under low arches, sometimes ending at a drop in the plateau where mist pours upward from below. A traveler could follow one for many days and never encounter another person, yet find fresh signs of life: a cup of folded leaves left open to catch rain, a patch of plants carefully pressed flat, a small cairn half swallowed by groundcover. Whether these are dwellings, markers, or simply things the land has taught its inhabitants to do is not easy to tell.

The plateau changes on a scale too slow to watch. A pool fills in over generations. A cliff edge retreats by the width of a root. One grove leans imperceptibly toward another until their crowns meet, and the space between them becomes a tunnel of living green. From high above, if there were a way to see the whole country at once, its plains would resemble the surface of a sleeping creature: rounded, furred, and breathing mist through countless pores.

Nothing here seems newly made. Even the young growth carries a sense of inheritance, rising from beds of older growth that are themselves not quite dead. The land is not still; it is simply moving with such patience that movement has become indistinguishable from permanence. Water works its way through the roots. Plants settle onto stone and soften it. Animals pass without leaving much behind. The plateau continues to rise, not upward, but deeper into itself.
