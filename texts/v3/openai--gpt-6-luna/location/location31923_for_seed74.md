The settlement occupies a shallow basin, low enough that rainwater gathers but not low enough to become a marsh. Its boundary is easy to miss: a gradual change from open, stony ground to walls and roofs almost entirely taken over by plants. The whole place could be crossed in a few minutes. It has the quiet, self-contained scale of a hamlet, though its buildings and machinery suggest that it once worked at a much larger pace.

There is very little bare ground. Thick mats of small leaves bridge cracks in the paving; pale roots stitch together the edges of steps and culverts. Reeds fill the old loading yards, and soft green growth hangs from gantries in long, even curtains. The vegetation is not merely reclaiming the town. It has become its surface. Roofs are padded with moss and low, branching shrubs. Drainpipes wear collars of living root. In places, leaves grow in the narrow spaces between machine parts, flexing patiently around gears that turn too slowly to harm them.

Yet the growth is not a tangled wilderness. It is dense, but orderly in a way that feels old rather than tended. Plants follow the lines of foundations and water channels, filling hollows, leaving the broad stone walkways mostly clear. Here and there, open courtyards interrupt the green—bare, cool spaces where the paving has been worn smooth by weather and use. A few dark windows look onto them. Nothing moves behind the glass.

The buildings are squat and practical: brick workshops, riveted sheds, a little boiler house with a narrow chimney, all softened by years of damp. Their corners have slumped, but not dramatically. Erosion has made its arrangements here. Brick faces are rounded at the base where water has repeatedly run across them; metal has darkened and thinned, but the larger frames remain. The place is neither newly ruined nor actively collapsing. It has settled into a durable, weathered shape.

Rain comes intermittently, fine and cold, more often as a change in the air than a proper shower. Moisture beads under the eaves and on the broad leaves. It gathers in shallow basins cut into the paving, then slips away through grates into a network of channels beneath the streets. In dry spells, the stone remains dark for a long time, and the air holds the smell of wet iron, crushed greenery, and soil hidden under masonry.

Somewhere below the workshops, a pump runs. It does not roar; it gives a low, steady pulse, pauses, and begins again. A belt turns behind a wall. A valve ticks as it cools. These are the main sounds, separated by long stretches of quiet. Leaves rub softly against one another when the wind enters the basin. There are few birds, and no obvious larger animals. Occasionally a small shape slips through the growth, but the vegetation seems to contain its own activity: seeds releasing, tendrils tightening around rails, drops falling from leaf to leaf, water moving where it cannot be seen.

The surviving machinery is connected to the living district by an old system of shallow pipes. It once heated growing rooms and carried water to the planting beds. Now it serves no single clear purpose, but parts of it still operate. Warmth passes through the lowest walls in narrow bands, and the roots have learned those routes. They gather along the pipes, then divide at the joints, tracing the town’s buried engineering with uncanny accuracy. Where the system has gone cold, the roots pass on.

Light is ordinary, though softened by foliage and cloud. The basin never seems fully dark, even under low weather: pale sky reflects from wet paving, and the green catches whatever light there is. In the evening, a few industrial lamps still come on, not all at once. Their yellow glow reveals the open lanes and makes the crowded leaves look almost black. Then they switch off in no discernible order.

Everything here is active, but nothing is hurried. Water cycles through the old channels. The pump lifts it. The plants draw it down again. Metal shifts with cooling and warmth; roots widen seams; rain smooths the stone. The town’s machinery and its vegetation have reached a quiet, imperfect agreement. Neither has taken the whole place. Instead, each continues according to a rhythm established long ago, in a small district that has become very good at staying where it is.
