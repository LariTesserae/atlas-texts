The first thing to understand is that the ground is alive, and that “ground” is not quite the right word.

Beneath the surface lies a broad, warm hollow—more a region than a cavern, with distances that disappear behind curtains of growth and return as echoes from somewhere else. Its ceiling is low in places, lost in others. The stone has not finished deciding what shape to take. Ridges bulge through the living cover like bones under skin; pillars are blunt, freshly swollen, and sometimes split while you watch. Dust sifts continually from the half-formed roof. Where it lands, it is caught by a mat of pale fibers and fed downward into the dark.

There is almost no free water here. No streams, pools, or damp stone. The air is dry enough to rasp in the lungs and carries the taste of warm iron, bitter sap, and something like struck flint. Yet vegetation occupies nearly every surface. It grows by taking what the rock gives: salts, gases, heat, and the fine mineral powder always falling from above. Its fluids are thick and sparing, more resin than sap. Leaves are rare. The plants make broad membranes, folded fans, fibrous shelves, and cords that pass from wall to wall, turning the hollow into a continuous woven mass.

The largest growths resemble trees only at a distance. Up close they are bundled columns of tough, translucent strands, swollen at intervals with seed-knots. Some lean across the open spaces and meet overhead, making tunnels beneath their own ribs. Others spread into flat, many-layered fans that tremble without wind. Their surfaces are stippled with pores that open and close in waves. From these pores comes a faint, steady exhalation: warm air, dry and aromatic, carrying spores that cling to fur, shell, skin, and stone.

Light enters through scattered cracks in the crust, but it does not reach the floor as shafts. The living canopy breaks it into a broad, ordinary-looking brightness, the kind of light in which there are shadows but no clear sun. Pale mineral seams also shine from within the walls—not brightly, but enough to make the whole place seem lit from several directions at once. The colors are subdued until something moves: rust-red fibers, cream-colored fans, black-green scales, the sudden electric blue of a creature’s throat.

Everything moves.

The fauna is so abundant that there is no stillness to mistake for emptiness. Small jointed animals pour along the plant cords in streams. Flat, many-legged grazers rasp the mineral crust from the walls. Furred things with blunt faces live inside the root-mats, pushing their way out in bursts and vanishing again. Higher up, pale gliders sail between the growths, their wings snapping open like sheets being shaken. Some creatures are little more than mouths and gripping feet; others have grown elaborate crests, vibrating feelers, or sacs that pulse with sound.

The sound is enormous. It is not one roar but an accumulation: the scrape of grazing, the knock of shells, the soft thunder of falling stone, the continual clatter of feet on hollow stalks. Seed-knots crack open with reports like handclaps. The growths themselves creak and grind as they extend, sometimes with the deep groan of a door being forced apart. Calls travel poorly through the crowded air, so the animals have become louder and stranger. A chorus of whistles, rattles, coughs, and booming notes rolls from one district to another. Even the quietest corners have a tremor in them.

The place is still being made. Mineral crusts are forming along the oldest surfaces; elsewhere, raw stone is exposed and pale, as if the hollow has been opened only recently. Growth can bridge a gap overnight, then split the rock beneath it the next day. Whole sections of the floor rise by slow degrees as mats of roots and mineral fibers thicken underneath. In some areas, the ground is merely a skin over a soft, hot interior. It dents beneath a heavy step and returns, leaving a brief impression threaded with glowing dust.

There are scattered signs of inhabitants who are not simply part of the swarm. Here and there, a platform has been lashed between the stems with braided plant fiber. A ladder of knotted roots climbs to a dry shelf above the busiest animal paths. Small shelters—little more than roofs of woven membranes—are wedged against the stone. Their makers use bone, shell, sharpened mineral flakes, and the hard, cured stalks of local plants. They do not clear the growth around them. They prune it, coax it, and build where the living architecture already offers a ledge.

A settlement is recognizable only by what it has trained to behave differently. A cluster of seed-pods has been bound shut to keep out pests. A corridor of thick stems has been bent into an arch. A mound of pale fungi—if fungi is the word—has been fenced with thorny fibers. The inhabitants mark paths with pungent resin, though the marks never last long. The creatures eat them, or the plants grow over them, or a shifting wall of new stems makes the old path irrelevant.

The rules here are simple, but not gentle. Nothing can remain empty for long. Any exposed surface is claimed by fibers; any still object becomes a perch, a nest, or a feeding place. Heat rises from below and gathers beneath the living ceiling, driving currents that carry spores and animals through the region. The growths respond to vibration. A stamp, a falling rock, or a heavy footfall can send a shiver through miles of connected stalk, waking distant beds of seed-knots. The ground answers with new growth, and the new growth brings more animals.

There is no horizon to look toward, only successive chambers and thickets, each one opening briefly before being swallowed by the next. The landscape has the feeling of a body in the middle of a feverish dream: warm, crowded, loud with its own construction. It does not seem old. It seems to have begun recently and to be trying, with immense energy and little patience, to become a world.
