The town stands on a high shelf of young stone, above a wide basin where the land has not yet softened into familiar shapes. Ridges run long and blunt toward the horizon, but their edges are still fresh: pale faces of split rock, raw gullies, shelves of gravel newly exposed by water. Here and there, dark seams show where the stone has cooled in layers. The slopes are steep without being mountainous, and the open distance makes the settlement feel small even where its walls and terraces spread across the shelf.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in a single grand flow. It beads on the rock, gathers in shallow pools, and slips through cracks beneath the paths. Springs emerge from the hillside in clear threads. After rain, the whole plateau seems to sweat: walls darken, grasses bow under droplets, and a fine haze hangs low over the basin. Narrow channels, cut and lined by hand, lead this water past houses and gardens toward cisterns at the town’s lower edge. Their routes are practical and old enough to be worn smooth, but the channels need constant clearing where fresh grit settles.

The air is warm for this height, cool only in the shade of stone buildings and beneath the broad leaves of the gardens. Light falls plainly across the plateau. On clear days, the far ridges are easy to see; after rain, they fade by degrees into a soft gray distance. There is no dramatic change in the weather, only the steady alternation of damp and drying, cloud and open sky.

Vegetation grows in scattered, generous patches. Tough grasses occupy the open ground between paths, while damp hollows hold thick mats of low green plants and stands of straight-stemmed reeds. Around the springs, broad-leaved shrubs and small trees gather close enough to cast a continuous shade. The gardens are lush where water is directed to them: beds of fibrous roots, climbing vines, and pale seed heads that release on the wind. Elsewhere the ground remains mostly bare, with plants rooted in cracks or in thin, dark soil caught against the stone.

There are no animals. No calls rise from the basin, no insects tremble in the grass, no tracks cross the wet paths. The absence is not announced by anything strange; it is simply a fact of the place, as ordinary to its inhabitants as the presence of water. The plants seed by wind, split, or spread through the channels. In the gardens, people gather fallen fruit and loosen soil by hand. Nothing rustles unless wind or water moves it.

The settlement is built from what the shelf offers: pale blocks pried from the nearest slopes, dark slabs laid as lintels, and timber hauled from groves along the springs. Houses sit low against the wind, with flat roofs and deep doorways. Some walls are plastered in clay that turns ochre when dry and nearly brown when wet. Paths follow the easiest ground, rising and dipping between courtyards, storage sheds, covered cisterns, and small squares open to the sky. From above, the town seems loosely arranged, but its channels and footways form a careful network, following the natural fall of the plateau.

The tools are simple. Stone wedges, wooden levers, baskets, ropes, hand-cut channels. A few objects are made from soft metal, hammered thin and repaired often. There are no tall towers or heavy roads. The town has grown by adding one terrace, one wall, one watercourse at a time, leaving broad intervals of grass and exposed rock between its clustered buildings.

Sound travels clearly here, but there is little to carry. Water makes the most persistent noise: a small, bright trickle at the springs, a duller murmur where it passes beneath stone covers. Wind moves across the grass with a dry brushing sound. A roof creaks now and then as it warms. Beyond these things, the town is quiet. Even the people seem to speak softly, as though the open land can hear them from far away.

The plateau does not appear to be changing quickly. Its sharp cuts and fresh slopes suggest a young landscape, but the springs follow settled courses and the town’s older walls remain where they were laid. Rain continues to rinse loose grit from the heights. Water continues to find its way through the same cracks. The land is still taking shape, yet it does so so gradually that each day resembles the one before it.
