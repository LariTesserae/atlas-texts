The place is called the Bell Pocket, though there is no bell in it. It lies beneath a hillside of ordinary stone, sealed from the weather and from the sky. You could pass over it a thousand times without knowing: the whole of it fits inside a single buried chamber, perhaps thirty paces long, with a ceiling low enough at the edges to stoop beneath.

The chamber is crowded with a city.

Its houses rise in close ranks from the damp floor, some no higher than a person’s waist, others stacked four or five tiers up against the walls. They are built of dark stone, clay brick, and pale blocks cut from the chamber itself. Narrow stairs twist between them. Little bridges cross the channels in the floor. Doorways, blind windows, niches, and plugged-up arches make the buildings look inhabited even when they are empty. There are no streets broad enough for a cart. A person would have to turn sideways to pass in many of the lanes, brushing both walls at once.

Everything is close, and everything is old. Edges have softened. Steps are worn into shallow bowls. The lowest courses of the buildings have taken on a slick, black sheen from centuries of water touching them and retreating. Here and there, mineral deposits have grown over an arch or filled the seam between two stones, not dramatically, but with the patient assurance of something that has been happening for a very long time. Nothing is crumbling. The city has settled into its damage.

Water lies just beneath the floor. It seeps through hairline cracks and collects in gutters too narrow to step over without noticing. A shallow, dark pool fills the chamber’s center, bounded by a low stone curb. Its surface seems almost still until a drop strikes it, sending rings outward that touch the walls before they fade. The walls sweat. The air is cool, heavy, and faintly metallic, with the smell of wet slate and old clay.

There is no green here. No roots have found a way through the stone, and no moss softens the masonry. The few pale films in the dampest cracks are mineral, not living. A rare, translucent cave-creature may be found under a loose paving stone, folded into itself like a scrap of wet paper; it withdraws at the touch and is seldom seen twice. The city’s narrow basins and channels seem made for more inhabitants than it has now.

The sound is immense for such a small place. Water murmurs in the hidden seams, taps steadily from the ceiling, and rushes somewhere behind the western wall. The close-packed buildings catch and throw every noise back. A single footstep becomes a scatter of footsteps, arriving from lanes that are empty. A whisper can come back as a low, rough murmur. At the chamber’s heart, the drip from the ceiling strikes a hollow stone bowl with a deep, regular note. It is that note, perhaps, that gave the pocket its name.

The rhythm never quite varies. The pool remains at the same level. The damp lines on the walls do not climb or recede. Dust gathers on the upper ledges but never thickly; the water below takes it, and the chamber returns to its settled hush—or would, if the hush were not so full of sound. Even the echoes seem fixed in their paths. Stand in the same doorway on different days and the room answers in the same order: drip, murmur, rush, return.

No one knows whether the city was built by people who lived in the chamber or by people who only came down to tend the water. Its tools were simple: hand-cut blocks, fired clay, wooden pegs long since darkened and swollen in their sockets. There are no lamps left, only shallow cups set into walls where a flame might once have been. The darkness is not complete. A thin, sourceless dimness gathers around the pale stone and finds the wet edges of things. It does not brighten the lanes so much as make their turns visible.

The Bell Pocket does not feel abandoned so much as held in place. Its crowded roofs, its narrow stairs, its black gutters and steady water all belong to one arrangement, worn smooth by time but never displaced. Above it, the hillside may erode grain by grain. Below it, the water may travel through the rock for ages. Here, in the hidden city, every drop returns to its basin, every sound finds its old wall, and the little streets wait in the cool dark exactly as they were made.
