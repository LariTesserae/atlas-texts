The basin lies below the old coastal shelf, broad and shallow, its far rim often lost behind curtains of rain. Nothing here has settled into a finished shape. The ground is newly raised in places, a jumbled spread of black gravel, pale clay, and slabs of stone with edges still sharp enough to catch a boot. Between them run countless narrow channels. Water gathers, divides, and gathers again, shining faintly in the near-dark.

It is cold without being cleanly frozen. Rain falls often, though sometimes it seems to rise instead: mist lifting from pools, beads forming along the undersides of leaves, thin streams weeping from cracks in the rock. The air tastes of wet iron and crushed green things. Low clouds drag over the basin, and daylight reaches the ground only as a dull gray presence. At night, darkness collects beneath the vegetation and in the cuts between ridges; the few lights scattered across the landscape look less like lamps than small, stubborn acts of refusal.

The plants are abundant and crowded. Low willow thickets lean over the channels; dark reeds knit the marshes together; moss and liverwort pad the fresh stone. In sheltered hollows, broad-leaved plants grow nearly to the knee, their surfaces waxy with cold water. Everything seems to be taking advantage of every inch that can hold a root. The vegetation is lush, but not soft: stems are tough, leaves thick, and branches bow under rain without breaking. Here and there, a young stand of trees rises above the lower growth, its trunks pale and flexible, its crown shivering in the wind.

There is no quiet. Water hammers the basin from above and rushes through it below: over stone lips, beneath mats of roots, into unseen culverts. Wind sweeps across the open ground and rattles the reeds in long, rolling surges. The combined roar is so constant that lesser sounds disappear inside it. Only near the water’s edge can you distinguish individual calls: the sharp bark of marsh birds, the croak and chatter of animals hidden in the brush, the clicking of many small lives moving through wet leaves. At times the whole basin seems to be making one enormous, unbroken sound, as if the land were still draining itself.

The weather changes quickly. A channel that was ankle-deep at dawn may spread across the path by noon. Fresh banks slump into the water; stones roll and knock together beneath the current. Wind flattens whole patches of reeds, then releases them, and the next gust arrives from a different direction. Water appears in places that were dry yesterday—not by miracle, but through the ceaseless rearrangement of saturated soil, pressure, and runoff. The basin is young enough that its routes have not become reliable.

Animals are everywhere, though rarely in the open. Small bodies rustle among the roots and reeds. Dark waterfowl cross the pools in abrupt, low flights. Larger grazers move in loose groups along the firmer ridges, leaving deep prints that fill almost immediately with rain. In the distance, something splashes hard enough to be heard through the storm. The animals know the ground better than any map: they use raised strips of gravel, avoid certain soft margins, and gather beneath the same wind-sheltered faces of rock.

Human construction is scattered across this unsettled country. A few squat shelters stand on the higher ground, joined by narrow paths of dark, porous stone. There are mooring posts where no permanent shore exists, and half-submerged towers that rise from the marsh like stems too regular to be natural. Their surfaces are seamless, without visible joints or fasteners. In the dark, thin lines along their sides glow and fade—not brightly, but with the measured pulse of instruments at work.

The technology is advanced enough that its workings are difficult to read, yet it does not make the basin feel alien. The towers monitor the shifting water table and adjust their buried anchors against the moving ground. Flat, almost invisible arrays above the shelters gather energy from the storm and disperse it into the soil. Where the paths cross a channel, they do not rest on bridges; the water passes through openings that seem to change shape under load, holding steady without quite appearing fixed. These devices do not tame the landscape. They make it possible to remain in it.

Their presence is easy to overlook. The loudest engineering is the weather, the water, and the living things packed into the vegetation. A person crossing the basin walks by instinct, watching where the reeds bend, where the stones have been newly uncovered, where the current has begun to pull at the banks. The place offers no lasting stillness—only brief, workable intervals between one rearrangement and the next.
