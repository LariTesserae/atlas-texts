The hollow is no larger than a small room, though the mountain around it rises out of sight. Its rim is a broken ring of dark stone, slick with water and old enough that every edge has been softened. Beyond the rim: high air, cloud, and the suggestion of a much larger world. Inside, the rock folds inward and holds its own weather.

Light reaches the hollow only as a dim grayness. It gathers briefly on wet ledges, then disappears into the green-black beneath them. The floor is a shallow basin of peat and fine grit, packed so closely with moss, creeping leaves, and low, jointed stems that little bare earth can be seen. Ferns grow in the cracks between stones. Their fronds overlap one another, and the smallest leaves have rooted in the moss on the larger ones. Nothing stands very tall. There is no room.

Water is everywhere without seeming to go anywhere. It lies in the basin under a skin of fallen leaves, beads on the rock, and darkens the roots that cross the walls. A narrow seam in the stone supplies a clear thread of water. It enters a cup-shaped pool, passes beneath a shelf of moss, and comes out lower down, though the pool never appears to rise or empty. The stream is too slight to be heard. A hand laid against the rock would feel it cold and steadily damp.

The hollow is crowded with animals. Pale snails stipple the walls; small, translucent insects cling beneath the leaves; beetles occupy every sheltered notch. Flat-bodied salamanders lie in the runnels, their dark backs almost indistinguishable from wet stone. A knot of tiny frogs fills one pocket between roots. Higher up, a few furred creatures huddle in a cleft, their eyes catching no light but seeming to know when they are watched. There is no sign of hunting or flight. Each creature occupies its place with the ease of something that has been there for a very long time.

The silence is not empty. It has a close, padded quality: the faint settling of water, the soft rasp of a leaf against a leaf, a sound like breathing that may come from the crowded growth—or from the hollow itself. Even when a creature moves, it makes little noise. Movement is rare and does not disturb the whole. A salamander may shift one forefoot; a fern tip may incline under a bead of water. Then the arrangement is as it was.

Nothing here seems to decay, exactly. Leaves darken but do not collapse. The moss thickens around a stone without hiding it. Water wears the rock smooth, but no new scar appears. The hollow has reached a balance so old that change has become difficult to distinguish from its opposite: every droplet seems to arrive at the same instant it leaves, every new green shoot already accounted for among the crowded stems.

There are no paths, marks, tools, or made things. The only boundaries are the stone rim and the darkness under the shelves. To enter is to step into a place with no obvious way through it, yet no resistance either. The moss yields softly underfoot, and the animals make room without scattering. After a little while, the hollow feels less like a patch of mountain one has found than a small, complete world that has quietly included one.
