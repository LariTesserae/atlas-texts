The city of Vey grows on ground that has not quite decided to be land.

It lies low beneath a broad, dim sky, across a plain of black silt and pale new stone. The stone has risen in sharp plates, as if the earth were still pushing its way through a crust; edges are clean enough to cut a boot sole, and every few years a whole shelf tilts or splits. Rain comes in brief, warm bursts. It slicks the rock, beads on the leaves, gathers in hollows, then vanishes into cracks too deep to see. Between storms, moisture hangs in the air. It films windows, metal, skin.

Vey is called a city, though most of it is distance.

Its towers are far apart, standing singly on the plain or in loose, deliberate groups. Some are old concrete forms softened by vines; others are thin, bright structures that seem to have been drawn upright from the ground. Long bridges cross the empty intervals between them, spanning marsh, fractured stone, and shallow green water. A transit line passes overhead without rails or visible support. Now and then its silent carriages slide through the dark, each one carrying a few lit windows across kilometers of open air.

The people who built the city seem to have planned for emptiness rather than against it. They left broad margins around every habitation: clearings for floodwater, migration, heat, or reasons no surviving plan explains. Roads run straight for a long time and then end at a wall of reeds. Streets have grown into paths among the vegetation. In the abandoned districts, doors open onto fig trees, and whole rooms are full of roots, feathers, and rainwater.

The vegetation is lush, but it does not make the land feel crowded. Tall grasses stand in wide fields, each stalk distinct in the open light. Trees rise in scattered groves, with smooth pale trunks and canopies like dark green islands. Ferns crowd the mouths of the new fissures. Flowers bloom in the mineral cracks, translucent at their edges, and close whenever the ground trembles. Along the watercourses, broad-leafed plants spread over the mud, where their roots hold fresh banks in place. Here and there, a trunk grows through a roof or a bridge deck; the city’s machines bend around it instead of cutting it down.

The water is intermittent, but never entirely absent. A shallow river moves through Vey in several directions at once, dividing around knuckles of stone and sometimes rejoining beneath the streets. Some channels are natural; some were cut by machines that no longer operate. At night, condensation gathers on the underside of bridges and falls in steady drops. Pools collect in the hollows and reflect a sky that is usually too dark to make out. Their surfaces quiver with the passage of things beneath them.

There is abundant life here. Small, bright animals thread the grasses in quick, almost soundless waves. Long-legged grazers pass in scattered herds, pausing beneath the towers to strip leaves from their lower walls. In the pools, silver shapes turn just below the surface. Birds travel in great loose flocks, though they do not fly together so much as negotiate the air in shifting layers. Their calls carry across the plain.

Everything carries.

The city is deafening—not with the close press of traffic and crowds, but with sounds that have miles to unfold. Wind moves through the bridge cables and makes them sing in overlapping pitches. Reeds hiss along the channels. The transit lines groan though no carriage is near. Faraway birds seem to call from just above the rooftops. When rain begins, it strikes the enormous leaf canopies, the metal spans, the exposed stone, and the shallow water all at once. For a while there is no other sound in the world.

Then, from deep underground, comes a slow cracking noise.

The ground is still young. Its formations have not settled into habits. Fresh ridges appear where yesterday there was flat silt. Whole patches of land rise a hand’s breadth during the night, bringing buried cables and old foundations to the surface. In some places, the erosion seems to run backward: gravel lifts from a channel and lays itself along the banks in neat new lines. The city’s instruments register these changes in colors that no longer correspond to any known measurement. Its structures adjust with tiny shifts of light, flexing joints, suspending their weight from unseen points. The technology is so advanced that its visible parts resemble architecture only by courtesy.

At the far edge of Vey, one can see the beginning of another district—or what might become one. A wide, bare shelf of stone extends toward the horizon, scored with narrow channels and dotted by stakes of dark glass. Nothing has been built there. Still, lights sometimes move beneath the rock, tracing patient curves. After a storm, the shelf is green with seedlings. By morning, some of them have vanished, leaving behind small circular hollows that breathe warm air.

People are present, but rarely in numbers. A figure crosses a bridge, a lamp gliding beside them without touching the ground. A few windows brighten in an isolated tower. On certain evenings, distant terraces fill with silhouettes watching the marsh. Nobody waves. Nobody seems surprised when an enormous animal steps out of the reeds and walks through the empty avenues, or when the city briefly dims all its lights to listen to the earth.

Vey does not feel abandoned. It feels spacious enough for every presence to have its own weather.

The towers stand apart. The animals pass between them. Water gathers, dries, and returns. New stone pushes up through old construction, and the vegetation covers whatever it can reach. Above it all, in the persistent dimness, the city’s unseen systems keep making small corrections—holding bridges level, redirecting rain, listening through the foundations.

Sometimes, after the loudest storms, all of Vey falls quiet for a few seconds.

In that pause, one can hear the plain growing.
