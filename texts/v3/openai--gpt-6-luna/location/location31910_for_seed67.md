The plain has no horizon you can trust. It is low, level, and so broad that its weather seems to have settled there permanently: a warm grey brightness, the air full of fine suspended water, the far distance softened not by mist exactly but by innumerable sheets of condensation. Rain falls often, though without drama. It beads on every surface, gathers in seams, and moves through shallow channels at a pace almost too slow to see.

Nothing beneath it is natural. The ground is a continuous construction: pale ceramic plates, dark poured composites, metal grates, vast rounded conduits. Their joints run straight for miles, then meet at angles too precise to have been worn into place. Here and there the surface rises into low ramps or broad humps, all with the clean, sharp edges of recent manufacture. There is no soil, no exposed stone, no green. Water occupies the spaces between structures, and even those spaces appear designed: basins, troughs, channels, reservoirs whose banks are smooth and unbroken.

The water is warm. It lies thinly over the flats and deeper in the long, windowless cuts between platforms. Its surface carries a faint oily shimmer, not pollution so much as the reflection of the world’s machinery: overhead spans, ranks of vent mouths, repeating arches that disappear into the wet distance. Somewhere below, pumps turn with such regularity that their vibration has become part of the ground. Their sound is nearly inaudible. The place is quiet enough that a single drop striking a metal lip can seem to have traveled a great distance.

The structures are industrial, but not ruined. Towers of pipe and pressure chambers rise from the plain at measured intervals; broad housings crouch over concealed mechanisms. Some are striped with mineral deposits where water has been allowed to weep down their sides. Others remain clean, their surfaces slick and unmarked. The machinery runs, yet nothing appears to be happening. Gauges hold steady. Fans rotate too slowly to stir the air. At each outlet, water emerges at the same rate, into the same basin, under the same pale light. The system has the uncanny composure of a machine that has completed its work but cannot be switched off.

Then the fauna becomes visible.

It is everywhere, though at first it can be mistaken for another feature of the construction. Thick, pale bodies lie folded along the channel walls. Translucent discs cling to the undersides of bridges. Long jointed animals occupy the gutters in dense overlapping layers, their backs patterned like the panels above them. In the shallow water, blunt-headed creatures rest shoulder to shoulder, packed so closely that the water must pass over them rather than around them. They are not decorative, and they are not all the same: some have shells like glazed bowls, some soft mantles that pulse faintly, some many small limbs held tucked against their bodies. There are creatures in the pipes, in the cisterns, in the recesses behind every grille.

They do not scatter when approached. Most do not move at all. A surface tightens and relaxes; a fringe of feelers draws in; a broad flank makes one slow contraction, sending a shallow ripple along the channel. The mass of life is overwhelming, but its behavior is restrained, as if the whole population has learned the same unhurried rhythm. Nothing hunts in the open. Nothing calls. Food, if it is food, arrives through fine slots in the engineered floors, or is carried in the water itself. There are no plants to shade the pools, and no sign of decay. The animals’ pale skins and mineral-patterned shells make them seem assembled for this place, though they are unmistakably alive.

The abundance changes the feeling of the landscape without changing its stillness. A basin may look empty from above, then reveal itself as a tightly packed bed of sleeping backs. A pipe-mouth that seems black with depth is ringed with small eyes, each reflecting the ordinary light. Beneath the wet grates, bodies lie in layers, scarcely distinguishable from one another. The world has not been abandoned. It is occupied past any comfortable measure.

Its strangeness is quiet, almost practical. The channels fit the bodies. The bodies fit the channels. Rounded creatures settle into hollows in the manufactured banks; narrow ones thread between the grilles; broad, plated animals rest beside housings that give off a soft warmth. Perhaps the structures were made for them, or perhaps generations of life have found uses in every surface. It is hard to tell where one arrangement ends and the other begins.

There is no visible tide, no strong current, no wind bending anything. The warm dampness never quite lifts. Water gathers, runs, evaporates, gathers again. The machinery maintains a balance so exact that the clouds above seem fixed in their slow formation. The animals breathe, feed, and occasionally turn in their crowded resting places, but the landscape as a whole barely alters. It is not frozen. It is held.

Across the immense plain, faint rows of lights remain steadily lit beneath the cloudy sky. Their reflections tremble in the water, though the water itself is almost still. Far away, a pressure valve releases one soft breath of steam. A thousand nearby bodies answer with barely perceptible pulses. Then the sound is gone, and the low constructed world settles back into its unbroken, living quiet.
