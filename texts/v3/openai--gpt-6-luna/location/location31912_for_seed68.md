The place is called the Sluice Garden, though there is only one sluice and the gardens grow wherever the town has failed to pave.

It occupies a shallow bowl no wider than a few streets, tucked between low ridges of dark, recently exposed earth. The ground is still raw in places: sharp shelves of stone, gravel fans, cuts where rainwater has not yet softened the edges. Thin streams appear after showers and vanish into cracks or runnels, leaving a smell of wet iron and crushed leaves. Most days are merely cool and damp. Moisture beads on railings and factory windows; water gathers in the shade beneath loading ramps, in the seams between paving slabs, and in old wheel ruts that have become narrow ponds.

The town is compact but organized. A short line of brick workshops follows the basin’s easiest grade, linked by a belt conveyor, a drainage channel, and two narrow roads that loop back on themselves. The buildings are sturdy rather than grand: corrugated roofs, soot-dark chimneys, patched glass, pipes carried over doorways. A small engine house runs through most of the day, its steady clatter traveling clearly across the open spaces between buildings. When the wind is still, the whole basin seems to be humming. Metal knocks, belts slap, water ticks through grates. Nothing is enormous; the machinery sounds loud because the landscape is small.

Beyond the workshops, vegetation gathers in thick, irregular bands. Ferns crowd the drainage ditches. Willow and alder lean over the channels, their roots holding the loose soil in place. Moss fills the shaded joints of retaining walls; grasses and broad-leafed weeds rise from abandoned foundations. A few kitchen gardens occupy the warmer ground behind the houses, where cabbages and climbing beans grow among salvaged pipes and timber. Greenery does not cover the town so much as press in from its margins, finding every damp surface and unclaimed corner.

There are animals everywhere, though few are large. Swallows wheel under the factory eaves, bats fold themselves into roof gaps, and small birds rummage through the weeds beside the rail line. Frogs call from the water pockets after rain. Rats move with confidence along the warm pipes, and foxes thread the edges of the settlement at dusk. Their activity is most obvious in the sounds: chirring, splashing, wingbeats, quick scuffles in the brush, all layered over the machinery. The place is loud without being crowded.

Its changes are slow. A ditch shifts a little each wet season. Roots push mortar apart. A new stain spreads down a wall; a pond fills with leaves; a track becomes a path. The town’s workers replace worn belts and clear the channels, but they do not fight the small encroachments of the greenery. They have learned which plants bind the banks and which roots lift the paving. The industrial and the living have reached a workable arrangement, not peaceful exactly, but familiar.

In ordinary daylight, the basin looks almost plain: grey roofs, green margins, pale sky reflected in narrow water. Then something catches the light—a slick stone newly uncovered by runoff, a row of windows clouded with condensation, a bright bird crossing the dark mouth of a pipe—and the place seems newly made, still deciding what shape it will take.
