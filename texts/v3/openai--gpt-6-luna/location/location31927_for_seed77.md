The land is low, and it goes on so far that its farthest reaches are usually hidden by weather. There are no mountains to break the horizon—only long, shallow rises, broad basins, and terraces too large to recognize as terraces until the light catches their edges. Everything has been made. The ground is laid in courses of fired earth and pale mineral blocks, packed so tightly that no native soil shows between them. Here and there, whole stretches have been poured smooth, the color of ash or old bone; elsewhere, the surface is a mosaic of clay bricks, each brick the size of a house.

Nothing looks mechanical. There are no engines, cables, or metal frameworks, no glittering devices half-buried in the plain. The construction is patient and plain: walls, channels, raised roads, shallow basins, platforms. Some of it may have been shaped by hands long ago, but the builders are absent, and their work has become the only ground there is. Even the smallest fragments seem made rather than broken from a mountain. The land has no quarry scars, no exposed natural rock—only seams where one finished surface meets another.

It is still young. Edges remain crisp; long walls have not yet slumped into rubble. Some courses have settled unevenly, leaving narrow steps and hairline cracks. In places, unfinished embankments stand above the plain, their faces still rough with packed clay. The landscape feels less ruined than newly assembled, as if its immense construction is proceeding so slowly that no one can tell where it is happening. Heat firms the exposed surfaces by day. At night, moisture gathers in the seams, and the next morning those seams darken and soften again.

Rain comes irregularly. It may fall for an hour across a region so wide that its edges cannot be seen, or pass in a few dark bands that leave most of the plain untouched. Water travels along the built channels, not always toward any visible destination. It collects in broad, shallow reservoirs and low courts, where the hot air soon draws it back into the sky. After rain, the whole landscape gives off a faint mineral smell: wet clay, warm stone, a trace of salt. Mist lies close to the ground in the basins, then thins and lifts before the sun has climbed far.

The sky is ordinary, though the scale of the land makes its weather feel private. Clouds can spend half a day crossing the horizon. Heat trembles above the long level surfaces, blurring distant walls until they seem to float. The light is clear and unremarkable; it falls across the plain without drama, exposing every slight change of height. At dusk, the pale ground holds the last warmth and the channels become thin bands of shadow.

Vegetation is scarce. A few tough, low plants root in broken joints where dust and moisture have gathered—small gray-green leaves, a little salt grass in the wetter basins. Their presence feels incidental, not like the beginning of a forest. There are no animals. No wings cross the open sky, no tracks interrupt the dust. The rare plant patches stand alone in their courts, separated by miles of clean, empty paving.

The quiet is not absolute. Water ticks and clicks through narrow channels after a shower. Hot surfaces creak as they cool. Somewhere beyond sight, a section of wall settles with a soft, single thump; much later, the sound reaches across the plain, diminished almost to nothing. Most of the time, there is only the small dry hiss of heat and the movement of distant weather.

The place is active, but not hurried. Water comes and withdraws; clay shrinks, cracks, and swells; dust gathers in the joints and is rinsed away. Every cycle leaves the construction slightly altered, though nothing has yet had time to become a ruin. The land is neither raw wilderness nor finished city. It is a made world in its first long season of settling, vast and sparsely occupied, still learning how to hold water, warmth, and silence.
