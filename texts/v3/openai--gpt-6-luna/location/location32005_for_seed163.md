The uplands begin where the road runs out of trees.

A broad, high shelf of pale stone opens beneath a hard, bright sky. The air is cool enough to keep the wetness sharp: water beads on every surface, gathers in shallow pans, and shines in the cracks. Far off, the land rises again in low, unfinished ridges. Nothing has had much time to become a mountain. The rock lies in broad slabs and blunt shoulders, newly exposed, its edges barely rounded. Meltwater and rain have started to find their routes, but most of the gullies are still tentative—thin dark lines wandering over stone, pooling, vanishing, and reappearing.

Plants have taken nearly everything they can hold, though they do not crowd the view. A close, continuous felt of moss and wiry groundcover lies over the rock, green-black where saturated and silver where the wind has combed it flat. From it rise scattered clumps of tall sedge, pale grasses, and stiff, narrow-leaved plants, each separated by a surprising amount of open ground. Their roots make a shallow net beneath the surface. In places, that net has lifted into low pads and terraces, softening the shelves and making the young terrain seem briefly upholstered. Water stands in the hollows between them. Some pools are clear enough to show the stone beneath; others are dark with floating leaves and fine green threads.

The vegetation is the landscape’s building material. It rims the pools, stitches loose stones together, and grows in long strips across the windward slopes. At the stream crossings it forms dense, spongy banks, but the high ground remains open: patches of living green separated by wet mineral flats, enough space between each growth for the sky to show through. There are no forests here. A few low, bent shrubs cling to the lee of boulders, their branches all brushed in one direction. The plants seem less to occupy the plateau than to be slowly persuading it into shape.

Water is everywhere, but rarely deep. It threads over the slabs in bright films, pours from one shallow basin to the next, and drops from small ledges in curtains that break apart before they reach the ground. After rain, the whole shelf gleams. Condensation gathers along the undersides of leaves and the lips of rock shelves, falling in separate, steady ticks. In the morning the pools breathe a faint mist; by midday the light burns it away, leaving a sharpness to every reflection.

The sound is enormous. Water roars across the uplands in many small voices that join into one continuous rush, loud enough to fill the spaces between distant features. Wind drives through the sedges with a dry, metallic shivering, then strikes the wet stone and comes back as a hollow whistle. A few isolated machines add another register: the low, even thunder of pumps, the clank of a sluice gate, the grinding pulse of a generator housed in a squat metal shed. Even at a distance, the machinery sounds less like a settlement than a small disturbance in the weather.

The structures are far apart. A service track crosses the shelf in a pale, straight line, disappearing behind a rise and returning near a water station. The station stands on concrete feet above the saturated ground, its pipes vanishing into the plant mat. Nearby, a rusted gantry spans a stream that has not yet decided where its channel will be. There are marker posts, cable runs, and a few low buildings with bright roofs, all practical and exposed. No town gathers around them. They look placed here to measure the land, or to borrow its falling water, not to claim it.

Animals are scarce enough that their absence becomes part of the openness. Now and then a small bird crosses the shelf, low against the wind; its call is swallowed almost at once. Insects hover above the warmer pools on calm days. The water gives up bubbles from the green beneath it, and the plants flex subtly under the pressure of the wind, but little else moves.

Nothing here feels ancient, though the stone is older than the life covering it. The plateau is still arriving. Its pools have not settled into permanent basins; its streams are still testing slopes; its plant mats thicken by imperceptible degrees, holding soil where there was only grit and making new edges for the water to follow. The industrial equipment imposes a measure of order, but only locally: a channel diverted here, a level kept constant there. Beyond the pipes, the land continues its slow, unfinished work.

On a clear day, the brightness makes the distance difficult to judge. The wet flats flash white, the green mats flatten into a dark weave, and the far ridges hover with no haze between them. The wind, the water, and the machines all roar without urgency. Underneath that deafening sound, the place changes almost too slowly to see.
