The place is a summit under the sea.

It stands high above the surrounding abyss, a narrow shoulder of a young volcanic ridge, though the water over it is deep enough to erase any sense of shore. The whole landscape could be crossed in a few minutes. Its boundaries are abrupt: on one side, a steep blue drop; on the other, a rib of fresh black stone curving away into greenish light. Nothing here has had time to soften. Basalt lies in swollen, glass-skinned folds, split by sharp seams. Pale mineral deposits crust the fissures, and here and there the rock has cooled into rounded pillows, as if the ridge were still exhaling its birth.

Daylight reaches the summit clearly. It comes down in wavering columns, ordinary sunlight made strange only by the water between it and the sky. The upper stones shine with a slick pewter brightness. Below them, shadows gather quickly, not quite black but deep and saturated, crowded with movement. The water is cool and heavy. It presses close on every side, yet carries such a quantity of life that the place feels less like an empty sea than a packed room.

Animals fill the gaps between stones. Schools of small, translucent fish flicker in and out of the basalt’s folds, turning together with the precision of gears. Broad, flat creatures cling to the rock, their fringed edges stirring like paper in a draft. Pale eel-like animals thread through the mineral cracks, disappearing into vents that are too narrow to admit them—or seem so. A cloud of bead-sized swimmers hovers over one basin, each body giving off a brief blue pulse. The pulses pass through the group in a slow sequence, like a signal relayed down a line.

Nothing grows here in the familiar sense. No fronds, grasses, or rooted green. The stone is bare except for mineral films and the animals that coat it, overlap it, and move through it. Some surfaces have a dull, metallic sheen; others are furred with fine crystals that tremble in the current. At the mouths of the vents, black flakes gather in drifts like industrial soot. They are not refuse. They are the continual fallout of the ridge’s chemistry, sorted and deposited by pressure, heat, and water.

The summit makes a tremendous noise. It comes not from any one source but from everything pressed into the small area at once: the low concussion of water passing over the ridge, the click and rasp of shells against stone, the crackle of mineral seams cooling, the thud of bubbles bursting against the rock. Deeper down, vents draw water through the new crust and return it in dense, shimmering plumes. These rise only a short distance before being combed sideways by the current. Their steady roar has the quality of machinery heard through a wall. There are no machines here, no platforms or pipes, no worked objects. Yet the rock behaves like a vast, unfinished plant: drawing in, sorting, heating, precipitating, releasing.

The strongest currents pass over the highest ribs. They carry schools of animals from one hollow to another, and make the whole crowded summit pulse with coordinated motion. But the landscape itself changes slowly. A fracture widens by a grain at a time. A ledge grows a pale lip of mineral. The fresh black surfaces take on their first dulling skin. The animals return to the same sheltering seams, season after season, while the ridge inches upward beneath them.

There is an oddness to the place that does not announce itself as magic. Distances seem unreliable under the clear water: a crack at arm’s length may continue farther than the summit is wide. Some fish cast shadows that lag behind their bodies. The blue flashes in the hovering swarm occasionally appear beneath the stone rather than above it. Still, the rules are consistent enough to learn. Light comes from above. The ridge rises toward it. Water streams over, through, and around everything. Life packs itself into every available space.

At the edge of the summit, the last black ledge ends cleanly. Beyond it, the bottom drops away and the sunlight thins. The animals do not cross that boundary all at once. They gather there in dense, shifting layers, facing the open water as though listening to something far below. Behind them, the vents continue their patient industrial rumble, and the young stone keeps cooling.
