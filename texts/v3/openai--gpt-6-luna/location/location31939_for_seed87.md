The city is hidden inside a split in the seabed: a water-filled chamber no wider than a cupped hand, sealed on three sides by raw black stone. There is no view of the ocean from within it. The world ends at every wall, then begins again in the narrow blue seam where the chamber breathes.

Its buildings have only just begun to be. Pale tubes crowd the floor, stacked and leaning against one another; shell-rims make balconies around their mouths. Between them, delicate bridges of mineral thread span gaps barely wider than a grain of sand. Nothing is straight. Walls curl as they grow, narrow towers bud from older towers, and whole streets terminate in soft, unfinished knots. Here the city seems planned; a moment later it looks like a deposit of frost or a thought taking shape in stone.

The materials are the chamber’s own small offerings: flakes of basalt, translucent shell, grains of pale silt, the fine white grit shed by living bodies. There are no tools, no lights, no signs of deliberate excavation. The inhabitants build by sticking, secreting, nesting, and making room for one another—though not always kindly. A tube closes over its neighbor. A shell settles where a passage used to be. The city is organized only in the way a thicket is organized: by proximity, appetite, and the repeated discovery of a place to hold fast.

Cold water presses through everything. It moves in a slow pulse, drawn in and out through the seam, carrying a faint drift of fresh silt. The chamber’s dim light comes from far above, strained through layers of seawater and stone; it lies in the uppermost hollows like diluted milk. Most passages remain dark. There is no true day here, only the slight brightening and dimming of the water overhead.

Life is crowded into every ledge. Small pale animals graze the mineral films. Many-legged things thread between the tubes, vanishing into openings that seem too small to admit them. Filaments wave from cracks. A few soft, dark fronds cling to the highest stones, sparse as stray hairs. Something with a translucent body rests beneath a shell arch, its many tiny limbs stirring the water without seeming to move. The city is so dense with presences that empty space feels temporary.

Yet it is quiet. No voice, no machinery, no hard clatter of construction. There are only almost-sounds: a grain of grit ticking once against stone; a shell rim brushing another; the muffled whisper of water narrowing through the seam. These noises do not travel far. The chamber keeps them close, as if it were listening to itself.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing is old enough to be ruined. The floor is still lifting in little deposits; new rims appear along the tubes; a passage narrows, then opens again as its builders shift. The chamber may one day become a larger cavern, or the seam may close and preserve the city in darkness. For now it holds: a small submerged neighborhood of cold water, tender stone, and innumerable lives, growing inward where no outside eye would think to look.
