The district occupies a shallow bowl no wider than a few streets. From above, it looks almost empty: pale roofs, long open lanes, and courtyards with more floor than anything standing on them. Then the green resolves. It is not growing over the city so much as being the city. Streets are edged with rootwork; walls are layers of bark, reed, and old leaf-mat, grown into planes and corners. Doorways open into rooms whose ceilings are woven from living branches. Broad leaves roof some alleys, while others lie uncovered beneath the ordinary, warm light of the sky.

Everything is low. There are no towers, only squat terraces and occasional blunt mounds where one building has folded into another and been held there by roots. Their surfaces are smooth from age, polished in places by rain, claws, and the passage of many small bodies. In dry spells the stonework shows through, cracked into tidy paving patterns. When rain comes, water gathers briefly in those cracks and in the cupped leaves above. It trickles through walls, beads on stems, and vanishes into the ground before the streets can flood. The air stays damp anyway, full of green smell and the faint mineral odor of old masonry.

The vegetation fills every boundary and leaves the middle strangely bare. Vines crowd the eaves; mats of small white flowers seal the gutters; trees have grown in courtyards without taking up much room, their crowns braided into arches over empty space. Roots descend through stairwells and reappear in kitchens. Ferns grow in the seams of benches. Yet whole rooms remain clear, swept-looking, and vacant, as if the plants have agreed to leave them open. The district is dense at its edges and hollow at its heart.

There are animals everywhere, though rarely in the open all at once. Small, many-legged things travel beneath the paving. Bright birds occupy the rooflines, and pale, long-bodied mammals slip through wall gaps too narrow to admit them. Insects cluster around every damp place. At certain hours the empty courtyards fill with wingbeats, then clear so quickly that it seems the sound, rather than the animals, was what gathered there.

Sound is the district’s weather. The leaves rasp and clap against one another; seed husks snap in their pods; birds call over the grinding chorus of insects. A low, continuous drumming comes from underfoot, where roots knock against buried stone. When the wind enters the narrow lanes it is multiplied into a great rushing roar. Even when the air is still, the sound remains—made by tiny things, each one close and distinct, until their combined noise is too large to place. Speaking in the open courtyards is pointless. The walls carry a voice around corners, return it from above, and sometimes deliver it from a doorway the speaker has not yet reached.

The district has been settled into this condition for a long time. Its old arrangements are still legible: lanes meet at squares; channels run beneath the paving; thresholds mark where one room ends and another begins. But the geometry does not always hold. A street may be shorter on the return journey. A doorway may open onto the same courtyard from two different buildings. Shadows occasionally fall toward the light. These are not dramatic events here. No one can say when they began, and the living structures have grown around them as if they were ordinary features of the ground.

At dusk, the greenery darkens without closing the place in. Small pale blossoms open along the walls, and the shallow pools catch the last light. The open rooms remain open; the lanes remain empty between one passing flock and the next. The whole settlement trembles with life, but its life leaves a remarkable amount of space untouched—an old, green city whose loudest inhabitants are mostly too small to see.
