The plain is green all the way to the horizon, though almost none of it is soft.

It is a level country of interlocking stems, pressed leaves, braided roots, and broad, rigid fronds fitted edge to edge like roof tiles. Beneath them is no soil. The ground is a manufactured mat of old vegetation: generations of stalks flattened, resin-bonded, and grooved into a dark, springy pavement. Here and there, pale mineral seams show through, but they are narrow as cracks in a floor. There is no mud, no standing water, no glint of wetness. Even the plants seem to have forgotten what liquid is.

The canopy is low. It rises in long, shallow swells, no taller than a city’s outer wall, then sinks again into avenues and basins. From a distance the country looks almost smooth, a vast green plate under a hard white sky. Up close it is crowded with construction. Ribs hold the leaves apart. Cables of woody fiber run between towers of hollow stalks. Thick, petaled fans are bolted into the larger frameworks, their edges trimmed to a uniform curve. Some structures resemble factories; others resemble trees that have been given blueprints and told to grow according to them. The distinction is not always useful.

The factories are made of plants, and the plants are made in factories. Long sheds of overlapping fronds house slow presses, combing machines, and great insulated drums that turn without noise. Their surfaces are pale, waxed, and almost luminous. Narrow belts carry cuttings from one chamber to another, where they are sorted by color, stiffness, and the pitch they make when tapped. The machines run on stored heat and the tension of dried fibers. They do not roar. Their work is heard only as a faint, intermittent ticking—like a seed settling in a shell.

There is no true breeze, but the landscape moves. Across the plain, fronds tilt in sequence, a mile-long adjustment passing from one district to the next. Leaves fold their pale undersides toward the light, then lock into place. Root-cables tighten under the ground and shift the mats by fractions. Once or twice in a day, the whole green surface seems to inhale: the canopy lowers, the stems draw in their joints, and a dry breath travels through the galleries beneath. The breath carries dust made from flower scales, ground leaf, and the fine bright powder of mineral growth.

The light is abundant and strangely even. It casts hardly any shadows. Instead, the surfaces divide it into cool colors: green, silver, and the faint violet sheen of old resin. Far overhead, the sun appears as a white aperture in a sky without depth. At certain hours, every leaf turns toward it at once. Then the country brightens until the horizon disappears, and the distant factories float like darker patches on a sheet of light.

The air is cool, but not refreshing. It has the dry, clean smell of cut stems and warmed stone. No water gathers in it. Moisture is taken apart as soon as it arrives, drawn into living tissue or bound into powders too fine to see. The plants drink from the air’s trace elements and from one another’s remains. Their roots do not search downward for nourishment; they travel sideways, seeking old structural matter, exchanging minerals through contact. A dead plant is not a thing that has stopped. It is a building material awaiting its next use.

Life here has a strict economy. Most of the plants are not individuals. They are districts, production lines, or load-bearing walls, joined by a common system of root and signal. A bright change in one leaf may travel for days beneath the plain before appearing elsewhere. Some groves bloom only once in a century, releasing clouds of papery seed that tumble low across the ground like scraps from a great ledger. The seeds do not sprout where they fall. They are gathered by the sorting belts and carried to designated beds, where machines press them into prepared slots.

Fauna is scarce. There are pale, narrow creatures that live in the ventilation shafts, but they are seldom seen in the open. They have no mouths suited to eating living plants and no need to hunt. Sometimes a cluster of them will move across a wall, each creature laying its body along the next, forming a temporary seal over a split in the structure. When the split closes, they disperse into the hollow stems. Nothing sings. Nothing calls. The few animals here seem to have learned that sound is a kind of waste.

At the far end of a maintenance avenue, the land becomes difficult to read. A line of pylons recedes toward the horizon, each one the same height and distance from the last. Walk toward them, and the interval never changes. Turn back, and the avenue is shorter than it was before. The plant-mat gives no sign of a slope, but distant structures appear overhead, inverted beneath the plain’s surface, their roots hanging upward in a second country that may be a reflection or a neighboring layer of the world. Workers’ marks sometimes appear on both sides of a wall at once, though no one has passed through it.

The world does not break dramatically. It makes small administrative errors. A row of leaves repeats for several miles, down to the same scar and the same curled edge. A factory’s windows show the interior from a day that has not yet happened. The shadow of a tower arrives before the tower does, moving over the green plain with calm, deliberate speed. The plants accept these discrepancies as ordinary conditions. Their stems bend to accommodate a passage that is not there. Their roots fasten around the foundations of structures that, by every visible measure, have not been built.

Over centuries, the place has worn itself into balance. The edges of old platforms have softened under continual growth. Resin has filled the seams between manufactured panels. The original lines of the first settlement—or first garden—are nearly lost under layers of maintenance, but the whole landscape still follows their geometry. Its straight avenues, its measured basins, its immense repeating beds: all of these persist under the organic disorder, like a grid remembered by a dream.

At night, if it is night, the green withdraws its color. The canopy becomes a broad field of pale shapes, bright enough to see by but not to navigate. In the darkness underneath, small lamps move through the service galleries. Their light passes from one plant-built chamber to another, and leaves open to receive it. The factories keep turning. The roots keep tightening. Across the plain, a million green surfaces make their quiet adjustments, maintaining the country one dry, precise movement at a time.
