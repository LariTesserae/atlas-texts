The upland begins above the clouds and continues so far that the clouds sometimes seem to have been caught beneath it.

It is a high, sun-struck tableland, not level so much as gently shouldered: long backs of stone rise and fall beneath a covering of plants, while narrow ravines divide the country at intervals too broad to seem like valleys. The rock is old and dark, worn to rounded shelves and shallow basins. Here and there, a cliff exposes the plateau’s layered interior, but most of it is hidden under a deep mat of roots, fallen stalks, and pale, fibrous soil. The ground gives slightly underfoot, as if the whole upland were a living roof stretched over stone.

Almost everything that grows here belongs to a few immense kinds of plant. Their forms vary with the terrain. Across the open backs of the land lie low, interwoven sheets of tough leaves, silver-green and hard as straps. In hollows, where moisture lingers, rise thickets of jointed stalks, some taller than towers, hollow inside and banded with dark rings. From their crowns hang sprays of narrow leaves that turn edge-on to the sun. On the slopes, root-fans grip the rock and spread into woody ribs, making natural terraces. The plants do not so much occupy the land as make its surface. A person can walk for days on a woven skin of stems without seeing bare soil.

The vegetation is abundant, but the country is not crowded. Growth is arranged in enormous patches, each with its own shape and weather. Between the high stalk-fields are clearings miles across: pale, low, and almost empty except for mats of ground-leaves and the occasional solitary tree-form, whose trunk is a bundle of fused roots broad enough to shelter a village. From a ridge, these openings look like lakes without water, lying among dark green forests. Their edges are uneven, continually advancing or retreating by the width of a hillside over generations.

Moisture arrives in brief, private ways. There is little rain, and when it comes it often falls only on one part of the plateau. Clouds snag on the tallest stalks; their undersides bead with condensation, then release slow showers that patter for an hour through the foliage. At night, cool air settles into ravines, and dew gathers on the leaves in such quantity that the morning ground shines. The plants collect it in folded cups and grooved stems. Small pools form in root-hollows, then vanish by noon. In the broad clearings, water is rare enough that its traces are easily read: darkened ground, a crowd of footprints, a ring of plants growing taller than their neighbors.

The heat is strongest in the middle of the day. Sunlight is ordinary, neither dim nor strange, but it falls through a restless haze rising from the green. Above the plateau, thermals build and drift; farther off, they lift dust and seed fluff into slow, towering columns. The air smells of warm sap, dry leaf-fiber, and stone. After rain, it smells briefly of crushed herbs and damp earth, and the stalks release a sharp, resinous odor as their outer skins swell.

The animal life is immense, though it does not fill every space at once. Much of it moves in circuits too wide to follow. Herds of heavy, long-legged grazers cross the clearings in migrating bands, leaving the low growth cropped to a smooth nap. Their feet make a distant, continuous thunder that may be mistaken for weather. They browse without haste, stripping leaves from the lower stalks and carrying pollen and seed on their hides. Behind them come smaller animals in restless waves: burrowing runners, climbing things with pale jointed limbs, and flocks of broad-winged scavengers that rise together when a herd reaches a ridge.

The stalk-forests have their own inhabitants. Bright, many-eyed creatures cling to the hollow stems and pulse their throats to call across the gaps. Large, soft-bodied pollinators pass through the upper leaves like drifting stones. Predators wait where the root-mats make tunnels, or lie flat in the pale clearings, their backs patterned to resemble sun-bleached litter. Nothing here is especially hurried, but almost everything is occupied: a stem is a road, a leaf-fold a nest, a root-cavity a hunting blind. Even the quiet ground is alive beneath its skin.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms a traveler. The plateau is deafening, not with one great roar but with innumerable layers. Insects rasp and click inside the stalks. Leaves knock against one another high overhead. Flocks cry out in rough, synchronized bursts. Herds grind across the plain. Hollow stems carry calls for miles, turning the thickets into resonant instruments; a sound made in one basin may return from another as a lower, altered note. When the wind passes through a stand of mature stalks, the whole forest booms like a vast wooden drum. There are moments when the noise rises into a single pressure, so complete that a person can feel it in the chest and teeth. Then, without warning, one tract falls quiet, and the silence is as large as a lake.

This is not a landscape of constant upheaval. It has been making itself for a very long time. The stalk-fields bend, break, and regrow along familiar lines. Old root-banks hold their shapes through droughts; ravines deepen slowly, gathering runoff from the occasional storms. A fallen giant becomes a bridge, then a ridge, then soil. The plateau’s order is visible in its repetitions: grazing paths worn into the same shallow arcs, clearings maintained by generations of feeding animals, and thickets that close over old ground with patient certainty.

There are scattered signs of people. On ridges, simple shelters stand above the dampest hollows, built from split stalks, lashings, and slabs of light stone. Some are no more than platforms roofed with overlapping leaves; others have narrow towers for watching the herds. A few paths have been cleared through the vegetation, though most disappear after a season’s growth. Travelers collect water from stem-joints, cut tools from the hard outer rind, and leave little behind that lasts. From a distance, a shelter resembles a broken seed-case. It is easy to walk past one without knowing it is built.

The upland follows several rules that become apparent only with time. The tallest plants do not always grow where the soil is deepest, but where the wind is most regular. The driest clearings are often the safest places to sleep, because predators prefer the cover of root and stalk. Water does not collect at the lowest point so much as wherever the plants have learned to hold it. And sound travels farther than sight: a traveler may hear an animal approach from beyond the horizon, while the animal itself remains hidden among stems only a few paces away.

By evening, the heat loosens from the stone. Dew begins forming in the upper leaves, and the plateau turns from green to black by degrees, retaining a faint shimmer where moisture catches the last light. Then the calls begin again—not louder than before, but more clearly separated, each one coming from a distance that cannot be judged. The great upland breathes around its sparse clearings, its thin water, its scattered shelters, and its moving multitudes. Nothing about it feels newly made. It feels instead like a world that has settled into a form so complete that even its strangeness has become ordinary.
