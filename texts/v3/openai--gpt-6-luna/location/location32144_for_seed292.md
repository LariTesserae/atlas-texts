The high country is underwater.

From above, the sea gives no hint of what lies beneath it: no island chain, no broken line of cliffs, only a broad, gray-blue reach under a low ceiling of cloud. Then the seafloor rises abruptly. Mountain walls lift from the dark in immense terraces, their summits still far below the surface, their shoulders worn smooth by an age of water. Between them lie valleys wide enough to have held rivers, forests, whole weather systems. Now they are filled with cold current.

The oldest stone has the calm, softened look of something that has already endured everything. Ridges have been planed down; cliffs are scalloped into ledges and alcoves; the pale rock bears long grooves where glaciers once passed, though no ice remains here. In places the mountains break into sheer drops, descending through blue haze until their bases vanish. Elsewhere, ridged plateaus stretch away like the roofs of drowned kingdoms. The place is ancient in its shapes, but never still.

Water moves through the high country in great, restless rivers. Currents spill over saddles and ledges, pouring down slopes in sheets that bend kelp and carry whole clouds of plankton before them. In the narrow passes, the flow is loud enough to feel in the bones. Storm tides arrive from the open sea and strike the mountainsides; deep surges answer from below. Sometimes the water runs uphill for a while, drawn over the crest by a passing pressure front, then breaks apart in curtains of bubbles and foam. Nothing here is a true calm. Even in the sheltered valleys, the water stirs the sand, shakes loose stones, and combs the forests back and forth.

Those forests are thickest on the upper slopes, where the light reaches in long, wavering shafts. Kelp stands in crowded groves, its fronds streaming like banners, some broad as roofs, some fine as grass. Dark seagrass fills the basins. Sponges, soft corals, and branching colonies gather on every shelf and buttress, layering the old stone with green, gold, rust, and ivory. In the deeper hollows, where the light thins, the growth becomes spare but no less crowded: pale fans, beadlike tendrils, mats of living color that cling close to the rock.

Life is everywhere, and it travels with the water. Shoals flash along the cliff faces, turning together so sharply they seem to fold into themselves. Larger fish patrol the channels. Crabs and small scavengers move through the forests; eels thread the cracks; translucent creatures drift above the valley floors. When a current changes, the whole ecology responds at once. Fronds bow. Clouds of animals lift from the stone. The valley darkens with moving bodies, then clears again as the tide carries them onward.

The sound is immense and near. Water booms against the mountain faces, rumbles through tunnels, rasps over gravel, and hisses through kelp. Fish make brief clicks and knocks; shell beds chatter when the surge passes. In the deep basins, these noises gather into a continuous, low thunder. A person could hear a storm coming from far away—not as wind, but as a change in the pitch of the seafloor.

Human marks are scattered and small beside the terrain. A few old metal towers stand on broad shelves, their frames furred with growth. Cables lie slack across the rock, disappearing beneath gardens of kelp. There are the remains of pumping stations and pressure doors, blunt industrial shapes built for work rather than beauty. Some still turn when the currents catch them: slow wheels, vanes, a great rusted drum rolling back and forth by fractions, as if the drowned machinery were trying to remember its purpose. Their vibration mingles with the roar of the water. No lights burn in them now, except the faint glimmer of organisms living inside the housings.

The rules here are simple, and severe. The mountain makes the weather. A ridge can divide a tranquil basin from a violent passage only a few hundred meters away. Every channel has its own tide, and every shelf its own season of bloom and loss. The old stone does not change quickly, but the living surface changes by the hour. Forests are bent flat and rise again. Silt lifts, travels, and settles in new drifts. A reef is stripped bare in one storm and crowded with life by the next.

Seen from a great distance, the drowned range looks almost gentle: a vast, folded country, its highest ridges faintly silvered by daylight and its valleys fading into blue. Nearer, its scale becomes difficult to hold in the mind. The slopes go on past sight; the forests contain weather; the sound of the currents fills the space between one mountain and the next. It is a landscape that has reached its final shape and is still being remade—not by age, but by the ceaseless passage of the sea.
