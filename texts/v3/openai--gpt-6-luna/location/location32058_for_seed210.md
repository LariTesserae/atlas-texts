At the crown of the ridge lies a basin small enough to cross in an hour, though the path never seems to bring you closer to its far edge. It is bounded by fresh, dark stone: sharp shelves and torn-looking fins, as if the mountain rose only yesterday and cooled in a hurry. Water beads on every surface. It gathers in shallow pools among the rocks, hangs in clear curtains from ledges, and seeps upward through the moss.

The air is cold and dim. A low cloud sits inside the basin rather than above it, softening the slopes and turning the nearest trees into green silhouettes. Nothing here is deadened by the damp. Ferns crowd the cracks; pale grasses lean out over the pools; thick mats of cushion moss cover the stone wherever a foot might land. Between them grow slender, glassy stalks with tiny leaves that ring softly when the fog touches them.

There are animals everywhere, though rarely all in view at once. Small black birds flit between the rocks. Bright-eyed, long-legged things pick their way through the grass. In the water, translucent shapes turn beneath the surface, and now and then the pools answer with a brisk, metallic chattering. The calls carry strangely: a bird’s note may sound from the opposite slope before the bird has opened its beak, or arrive as a faint echo from somewhere underfoot.

The basin has the sound of an immense machine working out of sight. A deep thrum rises through the stone; above it, water hisses, clicks, and pulses in narrow channels. There are occasional clanks, too—measured, heavy sounds with no visible source. The vegetation barely sways. The water never quite spills over. Each droplet pauses at the lip of a leaf, then falls, and is somehow still there when you look again.

No buildings or tools occupy the basin. Its industriousness belongs to the land. The rocks warm and cool in narrow bands; mineral seams hum like running belts. A black fissure breathes mist at steady intervals, while a nearby pool draws water down without lowering its surface. These processes feel precise and tireless, as if the mountain were manufacturing weather, or sorting the rain into forms too small for a human eye to recognize.

The place changes very little. Clouds keep their station. A bird may fly across the basin for what seems like an afternoon, its wings beating against the same patch of air. Moss grows, but never thickens; water moves, but never leaves. Even the fresh edges of the stone remain sharp, unsoftened by time. The basin is not frozen. It is running perfectly, and has been running this way for longer than its young cliffs have existed.
