The high country begins where the clouds ought to end.

It rises in long, weathered shelves above a world no one below can see: a plateau of black stone, peat-dark soil, and shallow water spread so widely that its far edge has its own weather. Warm rain is almost constant, though not always falling. Sometimes it hangs in the air as a close, wet pressure; sometimes it ticks against leaves for days; sometimes the rocks themselves sweat, and water gathers in their seams before slipping away in slow, hidden streams.

There is no light here. Not dimness, not the last shade of evening: no visible glow by which to measure distance or shape. The sky is an immense absence. The land is learned by sound, touch, smell, and the small shocks of air made by nearby things. A person standing still can hear rain arriving across the plateau as a softening of the dark, then feel it stipple the face.

The terrain is old and settled into its own immense arrangements. Basalt ridges rise out of the water like the backs of buried beasts. Between them, broad bogs lie almost level, interrupted by islands of stone, hummocks of root, and deep pools whose bottoms are far below the reach of a pole. Erosion has rounded the high places and filled the low ones. The plateau has not been made gentle; it has simply had a very long time to become itself.

Vegetation crowds every available surface. Trees stand close enough for their trunks to knock together in the wind, their crowns lost somewhere overhead. Thick mats of moss and low, tough-leaved growth blanket the rock. Reeds grow in the flooded hollows, tall and hollow-stemmed, making a clattering choir whenever the weather moves through. Vines bind whole stands together. Their roots travel over the ground in ridges and under it in nets, so that the earth is never quite solid underfoot. Leaves collect rain and release it in sudden showers long after the clouds have passed.

The plants make little use of sight. Their leaves are broad and soft, arranged to gather water from the air; their flowers announce themselves with scent, warmth, or a faint tremor through the roots. Some open only when a certain animal passes, sensing its weight. Others shed their seeds in response to distant thunder—or to the bass throb of a running engine.

There is plenty of animal life. Small things live in the peat and water, busy enough to make the ground rustle continuously. Larger creatures move through the reeds with a sound like dragged timber. Nothing needs to call softly. Long-throated marsh animals send their voices over miles of black water; flocks answer from the canopy, their wings beating the damp air into a rough, papery roar. Some calls are notes, some are pulses too low to hear clearly but strong enough to feel in the ribs. Predators follow these vibrations. So do scavengers. The night is not quiet; it is a densely inhabited place making itself known.

The most reliable landmarks are industrial.

Every so often, one encounters a pump house on a stony rise, or the skeletal legs of a drilling tower sunk into the marsh. They are scattered, not numerous, but each is large enough to establish a territory of sound. Old generators hammer behind insulated walls. Conveyors carry mineral nodules from pits whose edges have slumped and softened into bog. Pipes run over the ground, beneath it, and along the ridgelines; their joints click as they cool, and their interiors groan when the deep water shifts. The machines are maintained by small crews who navigate by cable, handrail, and the changing character of each structure’s noise.

The industry does not conquer the plateau. It has been absorbed into its rhythms. Moss thickens over the warm sides of engines. Roots seek the condensation along pipes. Birds nest in the cross-bracing, and animals learn which machines frighten prey into motion. In places, a stopped pump has become a pond; the pump’s broad casing is now an island, its interior full of rainwater and life. Elsewhere, the machines still work, though no one remembers who first set them running. Their steady rhythms are so old that the animals have incorporated them into their calls.

The darkness has rules. Sound travels strangely across the wet ground: sometimes a whisper arrives from far away with painful clarity, while a shout seems to vanish at arm’s length. Water carries vibration better than air. A footstep on a dry ridge may pass unnoticed; a toe dipped into a pool may tell every listening thing in it exactly where the disturbance began. The safest paths are therefore not necessarily the firmest ones. They are the routes where the reeds, insects, and distant machinery produce enough noise to conceal a traveler.

Nor is the dark empty. It has depth, pressure, and temperature. Warm air collects beneath the vegetation and pours along the low ground. Cold drafts descend from high stone shelves, carrying the smell of mineral water and wet iron. Fog presses against the face like fine cloth. When the wind stops, the land seems to draw breath inward: drip after drip finding its way down into the old, hidden channels.

The plateau is active on a scale difficult to hold in the mind. Rain moves across it in broad, slow fronts. Water rises through the soil, disappears into fissures, and returns several valleys away. Reeds flower and collapse. Animals migrate from one flooded basin to another, following changes in the deep current. Machines shift their load as the ground settles beneath them. A person can spend a lifetime beside one pump and never hear the same combination of rain, calls, and distant engines twice.

Here, distance is not seen. It is counted in echoes, changes of smell, the thinning or thickening of animal noise. A ridge is known by the way thunder breaks against it. A lake is known by the absence of footfalls, and by the hollow reply when a stone is thrown. The far side of the plateau may be a hundred miles away, but it announces itself now and then with a storm, a migration, or the answering beat of a factory that has been running in the dark for generations.
