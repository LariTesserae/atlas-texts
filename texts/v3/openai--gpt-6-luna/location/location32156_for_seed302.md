The plateau begins above the cloud line, where the air is cold enough to sting the inside of the nose and the light seems to have nowhere to soften. It falls across a broad, crowded country of pale stone: ridges pressed close together, knobs, fins, shelves, and blunt towers, all weathered into the same settled shapes. There is little bare ground between them. The stone fills the view, and beyond the first ranks of formations the land continues in further ranks, fading to silver beneath the glare.

Water lies everywhere, though rarely in deep pools. It threads the narrow seams between rocks, gathers in shallow basins, and spills from ledges in uninterrupted sheets. The rock itself weeps. Beads collect along its undersides and run together into rivulets that vanish into cracks, only to emerge again a little farther downhill. The water is clear, cold, and faintly mineral-tasting; its surfaces catch the light so sharply that the plateau appears scattered with shards of sky.

The sound is immense. Thousands upon thousands of little falls combine into a single, steady roar, dense enough to press against the ears and crowd out thought. Close by, individual trickles can be heard only as brief, higher notes within it. The sound does not swell or recede. It is simply there, as constant as the brightness.

There are no plants. No moss softens the stones, no dark stain of lichen marks the sheltered faces. Even the wettest hollows are bare, their floors made of smooth mineral crust and fine pale grit. Here and there, a pool holds a few minute, translucent animals, almost invisible until they move; they keep to the water’s dimmer seams and are seldom seen twice. Their presence feels less like a beginning than a small exception in a place otherwise given over entirely to stone and water.

The formations are old, but not ruined. Edges have been rounded, channels worn into the rock, basins patiently deepened, yet nothing looks actively in the process of becoming something else. The falls keep their courses. The pools remain at their rims. The crowded stone holds its shape. Even the light, reflected upward from wet ledges and pale surfaces, seems fixed in place.

From a rise, the plateau has the scale of a whole region: a high, close-packed expanse under a brilliant sky. Its many surfaces make distance difficult to judge. Near and far are both sharp with glare, while the unbroken roar keeps the place sealed inside itself. It is not silent, not empty, and not exactly still. But its movement has settled so completely into the world that it feels like another kind of stillness.
