The district is small enough to walk across in a few minutes, though it is difficult to walk in a straight line. It occupies a shallow basin between old retaining walls, a tangle of workshops, sluices, and narrow elevated passages packed so closely together that their roofs almost touch. There is no clear boundary between street and machinery. A lane may pass under a boiler house, emerge beside a reed-choked channel, and end at a staircase descending into warm water.

Everything is wet. The stones sweat. Water threads down brickwork in permanent dark seams, and gathers in the joints of paving blocks worn smooth by generations of overflow. The channels are shallow and slow, their surfaces filmed with green. Here and there, water rises through grates with a quiet, insistent burbling, then slips away into pipes whose destinations are not apparent. The air is warm and heavy; even in the dimmest passages, the walls seem to breathe out a little heat.

Plants have found every opening the district can offer. Thick mats of moss cover the shaded sides of buildings. Ferns grow from cracked mortar and from the shelves of rusted machinery. Broad-leaved vines climb over railings, cross conduits, and disappear beneath floor plates, where their roots have worked into the old brick. Reeds crowd the narrow pools. Their leaves brush the undersides of walkways, making a soft papery rasp beneath the harsher sounds of the city. The growth is lush but not wild: it has occupied this place for a long time, settling into its allotted gaps without ever quite overcoming the structures around it.

The buildings are made of dark brick, riveted iron, glazed tile, and thick glass clouded by condensation. Rust has rounded the edges of ladders and bolts. The oldest walls are bowed slightly inward, not from collapse but from a long accommodation with water. New pipes have been fitted alongside old pipes; new walkways laid over older ones. Some machinery is so deeply built into the district that it seems to be part of the bedrock. A large wheel turns behind a screen of ferns, its spokes passing at the same unhurried interval, year after year.

The sound is immense for such a small place. Pumps thrum through the walls. Belts slap and catch. Water knocks in the pipes, then rushes away with a hollow, resonant roar. These noises overlap until the whole basin seems to possess a single voice, low and crowded, with sharper notes flickering through it: a valve’s metallic chatter, a steady hammering from somewhere beneath the pavement, the echo of a drip falling into a hidden cistern. There are no birds over it, no insects in the reeds, no animal cries from the water. The district’s sounds are all made by water, metal, and the structures that contain them.

The light rarely becomes bright. It enters through high, soot-dark windows and arrives already filtered, a dull olive or tarnished gold. Lamps burn behind protective cages, their glow smeared by damp air. In the covered lanes, daylight is only a pale suggestion at either end. The pools hold a little light, but not reflections exactly: their surfaces show dim, wavering versions of pipes and roof beams, sometimes from angles that the eye cannot find above them.

Nothing here moves quickly. The pumps cycle with great patience. Water levels lift and settle by inches over long periods. Vines extend across a railing almost imperceptibly; rust blooms along a seam from one season to the next. Yet the place is never still. Every surface is being altered, very slowly, by condensation, seepage, roots, heat, and the persistent labor of machines.

The strangest rule of the basin is that its water seems to remain within it. It enters through the walls, rises from the ground, and gathers in the channels, but the district never floods and never dries. The pumps remove it, though the channels are always full again. Their mechanisms run without anyone attending them. No doors stand open to a larger city, and no voices come through the walls. The district does not seem abandoned so much as complete: an old, self-contained arrangement of wet stone, patient machinery, and green growth, continuing its quiet work in the dim.
