The country begins where the ordinary mountains should end.

It is a high, old upland, lifted long ago and worn almost level again. From a distance it looks like a single immense roof, tilted toward the horizon: a sweep of pale stone interrupted by solitary towers, blunt ridges, and the occasional peak left standing after the rest of the range was planed away. Up close, the roof is not level at all. It is scored by gullies, sunk into broad basins, and broken open by ravines so deep that their bottoms seem to belong to another weather.

The stone is warm-colored—ash grey, old ochre, the faint green of mineral stains. Its edges are rounded, not sharp, though now and then a fresh collapse leaves a raw white scar. This is a landscape that has had time to become gentle and has not finished being violent. Whole slopes give way without warning. Blocks tumble for minutes, knocking against one another down the cliffs, and the sound travels so far that it can be mistaken for thunder.

There is little water, but it is never quite absent. Clouds catch on the high rim and comb across the plateau in low veils. For hours the air may be dry and bright; then moisture beads along the undersides of ledges, gathers in stone bowls, and slips down narrow channels. Rain arrives in brief, forceful bursts. The gullies darken, small streams appear, and water runs over the old rock as if it has just remembered the way. By evening, most of it is gone into cracks or mist. A few hidden pools remain, deep in shaded hollows, reflecting the sky with startling clarity.

Where water lingers, the vegetation is lush. Ferns crowd the clefts. Broad-leaved shrubs root in pockets no wider than a hand, and trailing vines descend from overhangs into empty air. There are stands of low, wind-shaped trees in the sheltered basins, their trunks bent permanently toward whatever protection they could find. From afar, these green patches look like islands scattered over stone. Between them lie long reaches of bare upland, open enough that a traveler can see the weather coming from an hour away.

The air is warm, but never still. Wind pours across the roof, then plunges into the ravines and comes back upward in strange, forceful gusts. It whistles through cracks, booms in hollow cliffs, and sets the grasses shivering all at once. The land is deafening—not with continuous noise, exactly, but with vast, irregular sound: wind battering the stone, distant collapses, sudden torrents, the deep rush of cloud passing over the high ground. A shout can vanish in one hollow and return from another, stretched thin and unrecognizable. In certain clefts, even a small sound seems to continue after it should have ended, as if the rock were reluctant to give it back.

For all this noise, the country feels nearly empty. Animals are scarce. One may see a dark-winged shape gliding far below the rim, or find small tracks around a pool after rain, but rarely an animal itself. The few creatures that live here are quiet, elusive, and built for waiting. The plants seem more numerous than they are because their green is so vivid against the stone.

Human presence is similarly sparse: a cairn at a pass, a low shelter assembled from slabs, a line of shallow cuts on a flat face of rock. Here and there, a crude channel guides rainwater into a cistern. The work is careful but modest, made with hand tools and patience. No roads cross the upland. People who come here travel by ridgeline, keeping the ravines in sight but out of reach. Their shelters are placed where the wind briefly divides around a rock, and their fires are small enough not to challenge the dark.

The world’s disturbances are large, but not constant. A calm morning may hold for half a day; then a front breaks over the plateau, wind driving mist sideways, rain hammering the bare stone, and loose scree hissing down the slopes in countless little avalanches. The old channels fill and empty. A cliff loses a ledge. A pool is covered in grit; somewhere else, a new one forms. Nothing happens quickly by the measure of a life, yet the surface is always being rearranged.

At sunset, the height becomes most apparent. The lowlands are hidden by haze, and the plateau seems to hang above them, a vast, worn island in the sky. Warm light touches the scattered trees and the rims of the ravines. Far away, a ridge catches the last brightness and holds it a little longer than the rest. Then the wind rises again, moving across the ancient stone with the sound of a distant sea.
