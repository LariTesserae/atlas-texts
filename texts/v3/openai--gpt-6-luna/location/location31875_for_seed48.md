The city lies low in a broad, shallow basin where the tide reaches farther inland than the maps suggest. There are no trees, no reeds along the channels, no green on the rooflines. The ground is stone, mud, salt crust, poured concrete: surfaces polished by years of water moving over them. Rain comes often, though not always as a proper shower. Sometimes it hangs in the air as warm condensation, gathering on pipes and windowless walls until the whole city seems to sweat.

Beyond the last seawall, the basin opens into flats of dark silt and pale mineral deposits. The horizon is near, but the land feels wide: long sluiceways, settling ponds, flooded quarries, and stretches of bare ground interrupted by low industrial blocks. Old water has rounded the edges of everything. Corners slump; iron stains the concrete in rust-colored fans; channels have worn their own paths beneath walkways and service roads. This is not a place being made. It has settled into its shape and keeps wearing it down.

The settlement was built around the water rather than against it. Factories stand on raised pads, linked by pipe bridges and narrow causeways. Tanks, pumping stations, filter beds, and shuttered warehouses form loose neighborhoods, with broad expanses between them. At this scale the city is substantial, but it never becomes crowded. One can walk a long way beneath power lines and see no one, passing instead through the enormous empty intervals between active works. Some buildings run day and night; others have been vacant for decades, their lower floors repeatedly flooded and drained.

The light is dim, flattened by a low ceiling of cloud and the haze of warm moisture. Even at midday it seems late. Lamps along the canals make blurred yellow columns on the water. The air smells of wet stone, salt, hot metal, and the faint sourness of standing silt. In places, steam slips from vents and drifts ankle-high across the roads, disappearing into the damp before it can rise.

The quiet is deceptive. Machinery gives a steady, distant thrum, but the sound is absorbed by wet walls and broad water. A pump starts somewhere out of sight; a chain knocks softly against a lock gate; rain ticks on sheet metal. Most of the time the city seems to listen to itself.

Then the water moves.

At certain tides, fish arrive in such numbers that whole channels darken and travel as one slow, rippling surface. Shoals turn beneath the docks, flashing silver all at once, then vanishing into the murk. Crabs mass along the margins and over the lower steps, clattering through drainage grates and across the mud flats. Shrimp and small fish pack the warmer outflows, drawing larger predators into the canals. On the exposed flats, seabirds gather in dense, shifting lines—quiet until they rise, when their wings briefly blot out the dim sky.

Between these gatherings, the open water looks almost empty. A single fish may hold in the shadow of a pier. A crab crosses a concrete apron. The city’s animals are not spread evenly through it; they come in tides, migrations, and feeding seasons, overwhelming one place while leaving the next unnervingly bare. The pumps and sluices alter their routes, and the animals have learned the schedule of the works as surely as the operators have. They follow warm discharge, shelter in flooded basements, and wait at gates that open only when the basin rises.

Nothing here needs to be strange to seem uncanny. The water is merely warm, the weather merely persistent, the animals merely numerous. Yet after rain, when the streets shine black and the channels fill to their rims, the city can appear less like a place built beside an ecosystem than a set of dry ledges temporarily granted to it. Then the tide turns, the animals withdraw or gather elsewhere, and the pipes begin their low, patient pumping again.
