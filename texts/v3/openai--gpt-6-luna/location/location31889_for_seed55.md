The water is cold enough to keep its own silence.

It covers a continent of low ground: not a trench, not a steep descent into the deep, but an immense drowned plain whose farthest ridges rise only a little beneath the surface. In some places the ceiling of water is thin and gray, and a weak daylight filters down through it. Elsewhere, where the plain folds beneath old shelves, there is no light at all. The water moves slowly across these distances, carrying a fine suspended silt that settles almost as soon as it is stirred. Nothing here is quite buried, and nothing is quite clean.

The ground is made.

Beneath the silt lie fitted courses of stone, immense and regular, softened by age until their joins are difficult to see. Long avenues run straight under the water, though their paving has buckled in places under the weight of centuries. Walls divide the plain into broad basins. Pillars stand at intervals too even to be natural, their tops furred with growth. Here and there, entire slopes are faced with brick or packed clay, the work of hands repeated until it seems less like construction than geology. There is no untouched earth beneath the structures. Even the deepest foundation stones rest on older laid stones.

The builders are gone. They left no towers reaching for the sky and no bright engines, only a patient order of steps, channels, retaining walls, reservoirs, and platforms. Their tools must have been simple: stone mauls, wooden levers, baskets, woven ropes. Yet the work extends beyond sight, basin after basin, each fitted to the next. The whole country may have been shaped by a civilization that understood its own smallness and built accordingly. It did not conquer the water. It gave the water places to go.

Now the channels are full. Their water is nearly still, except where the broad current passes between the old walls. The current takes several days to cross some districts. It carries pale threads of vegetation, flakes of softened mortar, and the occasional leaf that has traveled far from its growing place. On the old roads, water has gathered in shallow troughs. Under arches, it lies black and undisturbed.

Vegetation is everywhere, but it grows as if it remembers the walls that first held it. Long, ribbonlike fronds hang from ledges and drift in the mild current. Dense beds of dark moss cushion stairways and fill the seams between stones. Pale stalks rise from courtyards where the water is a little brighter, their tips marked with faint, cold-green light. In the deepest basins, plants grow slowly and close together: soft forests of narrow leaves, each one trailing against the next, all leaning in the same direction. They do not wave much. The water is too quiet for that.

There are gardens still arranged in beds. Their borders have slumped, but the old geometry remains: concentric rings, narrow irrigation trenches, rectangular plots separated by low walls. The cultivated growth has gone feral without becoming wild. It spreads along the channels, fills the reservoirs, and climbs the buildings in ordered mats. Some old varieties seem unable to grow outside the places prepared for them. They cling to their terraces and walls, rooted in packed earth brought here long ago. Others have crossed the boundaries and knitted neighboring districts together in a continuous green darkness.

No animals move through it. No fins cut the water. No shells open in the silt. There are no calls, no clicking, no hidden rushes of life in the beds. The plants grow, decay, and grow again without being eaten. Their fallen leaves gather in the courtyards and dissolve into a brown, fine sediment that lies over the paving. The only motion besides water is the gradual yielding of old materials: a wall face shedding grains; a frond turning, almost imperceptibly; the slow sinking of a roof into a room already full of silt.

The scale is hard to judge from within. A traveler could follow one avenue for a season and never reach its end. The structures are dense enough that no part of the plain is truly open: platforms overlap with embankments, gardens crowd the remains of houses, archways lead into waterlogged courts, and beneath the courts are older levels with their own passages. Yet each district has a broadness to it, a sense of room beneath the low water. You can look through several arches at once, down a long corridor of stone and plant, until both disappear into darkness. The place is crowded with construction and growth, but no single form dominates it.

Above, the surface is sometimes visible as a pale trembling lid. In calm weather it lies far overhead, beyond the reach of the tallest structures, and the world below holds only a diluted grayness. When clouds gather, that gray thins to nearly nothing. In the deepest parts, darkness is not a sudden fall but a long withdrawal of detail. First the color goes, then the edges. The old walls remain as differences in black: a blacker arch, a slightly less black terrace, a green-black grove pressed against a pillar. The vegetation’s faint luminescence does not illuminate the place so much as make its darkness legible.

Cold has settled into the masonry. It passes through stone and clay without seasons anyone can easily perceive. The water changes temperature only gradually, and the plants follow a slow calendar marked by currents and the faint alteration of light above. Nothing hurries. Growth that might take weeks elsewhere takes years here. A fallen lintel can remain balanced across its supports for centuries. A channel clogged by silt is not cleared; the water finds a narrower route beside it, then narrows that route, then makes it the new way.

Sounds are rare and travel far. A loose slab may shift with a low, muffled groan. Water passing through a breached wall makes a quiet, steady murmur that can be heard for a great distance, though it is never loud. Sometimes a long vibration comes through the stone—not a voice, not machinery, but the plain settling against itself. It fades before it can be traced. Most often there is only the small noise of fronds touching one another, soft as cloth in another world.

Nothing here announces that it is ruined. There are no heaps of wreckage, no violent scars. The built world has passed into its final arrangement. Its builders’ work has become the bed of the water; the water has become the weather of its rooms. The gardens have inherited the courtyards, and the silt has inherited the roads. This is not a drowned city surrounded by nature. It is a constructed landscape so old, and so completely given over to water and growth, that the distinction has worn away.

Somewhere beneath the green beds, under the old paved basins, the first stones remain: level, aligned, still holding. They are hidden by everything that came after them, and they have not moved.
