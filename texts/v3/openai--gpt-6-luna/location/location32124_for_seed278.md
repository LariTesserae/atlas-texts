The highland begins without a threshold. For days, the ground rises by such small degrees that the change is felt first in the thinning air, then in the widening distance between one ridge and the next. At last there is no higher ground to look toward: only a vast table of stone, tilted beneath a low ceiling of cloud.

The plateau is cold even when the clouds break. Light comes through them as a dim, pewter wash, without a clear direction. Basalt shelves and pale, wind-polished rock show through a thin skin of frost. Here and there, old glaciers have left broad hollows and smooth-backed ridges, their work so long finished that the land seems settled into its scars. In the hollows, water gathers briefly after rain or thaw, black and still in shallow basins. Elsewhere it hides underground, making damp seams in the rock and beads of condensation beneath overhangs. The streams are few, narrow, and hard to follow; they vanish into cracks and reappear far away.

Vegetation keeps close to the ground. Lichen stains the stone in dull greens and rusts. Cushion plants occupy sheltered pockets no larger than a handspan. There are no woods to interrupt the view, no soft margins between land and sky. The emptiness is immense, but it is not vacant.

The animals are everywhere, though rarely all at once. From a ridge, the plain may seem bare until the stones begin to move. Herds of broad, low-bodied grazers travel across it in widely spaced fronts, their backs plated with frost and mineral dust. They feed not on grasses, but on lichen, salt crusts, and the fine living film that forms around damp rock. Smaller animals follow their paths: pale burrowers slipping between stones, dark-winged scavengers circling far overhead, and countless tiny things tucked into seams in the ground. In certain basins, the whole surface appears to ripple—not with water, but with bodies turning beneath the snow-thin crust.

Their abundance has the patience and organization of a natural industry. The great herds scrape, grind, and sift the highland as they pass. Their blunt mouths comb mineral layers from the rock; their heavy feet crush it to powder; their migrations carry nutrients from one basin to another over distances too great to be held in a single view. Some have hollow chambers in their chests that warm incoming air and condense moisture. Others bear branching, metallic ridges along the skull, which collect the faint charge of storm clouds and pass it through the body in slow, controlled pulses. Nothing is made here, yet the land is worked. The animals extract, refine, transport, and return material in cycles older than any human measurement of machinery.

The industrial quality is not noise or smoke. It lies in scale, repetition, and the precise fit between living bodies and mineral ground. A passing herd can sort a slope more evenly than a machine, though it may take a century. Its routes recur with such regularity that shallow grooves have been worn into the stone. In the largest valleys, these grooves meet and separate like channels in a vast, weathered diagram. There are no roads, fences, towers, or abandoned works. The only marks are those made by weather, water, and the weight of life.

Sound is scarce. The thin air seems to take it away. Wind is often present as pressure rather than voice; cloud-shadow glides over the plateau without a stir of leaves. A herd moving nearby gives off almost nothing—perhaps a muted click as mineral plates touch, perhaps the soft rasp of mouths against stone. From far away, these motions are silent. One sometimes senses their passage through the soles of the feet: a mild vibration travelling through the old rock, fading long before its source appears.

The weather changes slowly. Moisture arrives in veils, leaving the stone darkened for a day or two; then frost gathers again in its pits and seams. Storms form beyond the rim of the plateau and may take hours to approach. The animals know their timing. They turn across the open ground in immense, unhurried flows, leaving wide intervals of bare rock between one body and the next. When the storm passes, the herds spread out once more, and the highland resumes its nearly motionless look.

This place has not been preserved from change. It has simply found a scale at which change becomes hard to see. The hollows deepen by inches; the herds alter their routes by miles across generations; the damp seams shift beneath the stone. Frost, mineral, animal, and cloud continue their quiet work. The plateau remains cold, dim, and enormous, with life concentrated in its hidden machinery and scattered, unmistakable movement across the open ground.
