The basin is small enough to know by its edges. A low rim of dark stone encloses it on three sides; the fourth disappears beneath a wall of factories and their extensions, added, braced, and added to again until the buildings seem to have grown out of the rock. Nothing here is tall. The chimneys are squat, the roofs nearly level, the streets shallow-cut. Even the skyline sits low beneath a cool, colorless sky.

Water comes irregularly: a fine rain that beads on iron, condensation that gathers under eaves, thin runnels finding their old courses through the paving. The ground is mostly mineral—worn slate, black grit, pale deposits crusted around drains. No grass grows in the cracks. No weeds have claimed the ledges. There is scarcely a soft edge anywhere, except where the weather has polished stone smooth or laid down a slick film that shines like oil.

The settlement is industrial, but quiet in its arrangement. Here, a row of stamping sheds; there, a pressure house with thick pipes threading over the street at head height. Narrow rails vanish beneath loading doors. Ventilation fans turn behind iron grilles. Most machines run at the same pitch, with the same patient vibration, as if the whole district were holding one note. Their surfaces are old and matte with soot. Repairs are visible everywhere, but nothing looks recently broken. The place has settled into its workings.

And it is full of animals.

They cover the roofs, pack the gutters, and crowd the broad stone shelves along the basin wall. They cling to pipes in layered bands, each creature tucked close against the next. From a distance the roofs seem furred with dark gray; closer, the fur resolves into thousands upon thousands of small, hard-backed bodies, each with a pale rim around its shell and a pair of narrow sensory frills folded against its sides. They are not quite mollusks, not quite insects. They neither flee nor gather in a rush. They simply occupy every surface that catches a little moisture.

The people—if people still live here—have made room for them without making a spectacle of it. Walkways have raised lips to keep the creatures from spilling into the lanes. Drain covers are patterned with fine slots. At the doors of the workshops, shallow stone trays collect rainwater, and the animals cluster there in dense, motionless crescents. Pipes are left exposed, not because they are unfinished, but because their cool undersides are among the creatures’ preferred resting places. The town’s architecture seems less built against the fauna than built around its settled presence.

There is no obvious food for such a multitude. No plants, no flowering growth, no visible herds of smaller things. The animals feed on something too slight to see: the mineral slick left by condensation, perhaps, or the faint residue in the factory runoff. Along the walls, where warm exhaust meets the cold air, a fine gray film appears. It is gone by morning, though nothing seems to have scraped it away. The creatures keep their mouths pressed to the stone and iron, and the stone keeps acquiring its film. This is the whole ecology: moisture, residue, shell, and the slow work of surfaces.

The sound is enormous for so little motion. Fans murmur. Presses strike at intervals so regular they seem less like events than parts of the air. Rain ticks on sheet metal. Beneath all that is the continuous clatter of the animals: minute clicks as shell touches shell, a dry rasp of feeding, thousands of tiny joints adjusting themselves by a fraction. No single sound is loud, but together they fill the basin. It can feel as though the place is speaking in a language made entirely of small hard syllables.

The creatures do move, but only by degrees. A line of them will have shifted along a pipe since the previous day, though no one can say when the shift occurred. One ledge empties as another thickens, not in a wave, but through an accumulation of imperceptible decisions. Even the weather seems caught in a narrow habit: dampness comes and goes, the air stays cool, the same drains carry the same thin water toward the same dark grates. The basin does not change so much as maintain itself.

In places, the animals have grown into the built world. Their shells make rough collars around bolts and pipe joints. Pale mineral bands run beneath them where their bodies have rested for years. Old clusters have been cemented into the masonry by successive films of damp; when one dies, it remains in place, its shell indistinguishable from the mineral lumps around it. The oldest walls are stippled with these fused colonies, until the distinction between creature and building becomes difficult to hold.

There is a strange comfort in the consistency. Nothing blooms, nothing decays quickly. Nothing sweeps through the basin or departs for another climate. The factories do not thunder into life; they are already running. The fauna does not swarm; it is already there. Rain falls and dries. Moisture gathers and thins. Metal, stone, and shell continue their quiet negotiations, year after year, beneath the same low sky.

The place is loud, crowded with life, and almost perfectly still.
