The city occupies a hollow no larger than a town square, though its ceiling is lost in darkness and its walls bend away as if the ground has only just remembered how to open.

It is below the surface, but not deep enough to be dry. Water threads down the walls in clear, warm sheets, gathers in shallow channels, then vanishes through grates that have no visible pipes beneath them. The stone is fresh-faced: pale, sharp-edged, veined with mineral colors that have not yet dulled. Here and there, the rock has the smooth, swollen look of something still cooling. Condensation beads on every railing and trembles in the vibration of the place.

Buildings rise directly from the stone. They are compact, narrow, and layered: walkways folded above service streets, little towers nested into recesses, bridges crossing the open center at different heights. Their surfaces are dark ceramic and glassy metal, with seams of soft light running through them. Nothing looks old, but much of it seems to have been repaired, altered, or grown around something else. Cables travel inside transparent conduits. Doors slide into walls that are not quite flat. A stairway may descend for several flights and arrive only a few steps below where it began.

The machinery is too advanced to appear mechanical in any familiar sense. Some structures hang without supports, held in place by faint distortions in the damp air. Others bend light around their edges. A low, pulsing field seems to keep the hollow stable; when it passes through the stone, the walls briefly shine from within, revealing that the city extends farther than its visible streets suggest. The system does not feel perfectly controlled. It breathes, falters, corrects itself.

Plants occupy the places the builders left wet and unlit. They are lush, but sparsely distributed: broad dark leaves on rooftops, soft moss in the joints between paving slabs, pale root-fans spreading over the rock like slow frost. Their green is subdued by the gloom, though the undersides of some leaves glimmer with a blue, watery sheen. They drink from the walls and channel runoff into cisterns. No birds call from their branches. No insects drift above the water. The city’s gardens are entirely still except when drops strike their leaves.

For all its emptiness, the place is loud. Water drums on metal roofs and rattles through enclosed gutters. Somewhere beneath the streets, enormous pumps turn with a hollow, patient thrum. The city’s field makes a thin note that shifts as one walks, becoming a rough chord at the edges of the square. Sounds travel strangely: a drip nearby may ring as if it fell in a distant cavern, while a heavy impact can be swallowed almost at once. There are no voices to interrupt this weather of machinery and water.

The light comes from narrow strips set into floors and walls, and from the occasional luminous mineral seam in the rock. It is enough to navigate by, but not enough to see the far edges of things. The upper levels dissolve into a charcoal haze. Beneath the walkways, shallow water reflects a brighter version of the city, trembling whenever the pumps change rhythm.

Nothing living moves here, yet the landscape is not inactive. Water changes routes from hour to hour. Condensation gathers, falls, and is drawn away. New mineral teeth push from cracks in the stone; fresh channels cut through the softest ground. The buildings answer these changes by shifting a fraction at a time, their foundations adjusting with quiet precision. The place is being built and eroded simultaneously, though neither process seems to know which one is winning.

It feels less like a city abandoned than one designed for a population that has not arrived—or has become so small, so distant, that the city no longer registers its absence. For now, the hollow belongs to its systems, its young stone, and the steady water finding a way through.
