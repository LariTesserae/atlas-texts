The place is called the Sill: a small, high shelf of new black stone caught between two shoulders of a mountain. You can cross it in twenty minutes. At its widest, it is no more than a few hundred paces across, though the drop beyond its rim is hidden by cloud and seems to continue much farther than the mountain ought to allow.

The stone is young. Its edges are sharp enough to catch cloth, and its surfaces still show the violence of their making: folded sheets of basalt, pale seams of glass, knobs of mineral that have not yet weathered into recognizable shapes. Warm water wells through the cracks. It runs in thin, clear threads over the rock, gathers briefly in shallow bowls, then vanishes into fissures or lifts away as vapor. In a few places it appears to climb—beading upward along a slanting wall, drawn toward a narrow seam in the air before spilling over the lip. The effect is not dramatic. It happens slowly, with the indifference of a natural law.

The heat is steady, more like the breath of a kiln than direct sun. The air is wet enough to shine. Condensation pearls on the undersides of ledges and falls from them at long intervals. There is ordinary daylight above the shelf, pale and unobstructed, but the low cloud around the mountain softens distance. A person standing at one end can see every stone at the other and still feel that the place extends behind them, folded into a much larger weather.

Vegetation has found only a few footholds, but where it grows, it grows lavishly. Thick mats of green cling to the warmest channels. Long leaves, waxy and nearly translucent at their edges, fan out from cracks in the stone. Small red flowers open directly into the mist; they have no visible stems. Here and there, a tuft of silver grass bends in a wind too slight to feel. Between these islands of growth, the rock remains bare and dark, leaving wide spaces of wet mineral and empty air.

There is little animal life. Once, a small white thing may dart under a shelf of stone—perhaps an insect, perhaps a fragment of the cloud with a purpose. Tiny snails leave bright tracks across the moss, then disappear into the warm seams. No birds call overhead. Nothing hunts openly. The few living things seem to have learned that the Sill rewards patience more than movement.

The scattered constructions are easy to mistake for natural formations. A low black arch stands over one spring, its surface unmarked except for a row of pinprick holes. A waist-high post rests in a pool without touching the water; a thin gap remains between its base and the surface, though the pool bends around it as if around a submerged stone. Farther on, a narrow frame of dull metal is set into the cliff. It carries no wires, no lights, no visible controls. When the mist thickens, the frame sometimes holds a perfectly dry rectangle of air.

These devices are old, or else they have been designed to look older than weather. Their work is difficult to distinguish from the mountain’s own habits. Near the post, water gathers into a bead and hangs there, unmoving, through several gusts. At the arch, steam falls back to the ground in a fine warm rain. The metal frame seems to make a small alteration in distance: the cliff beyond it may be a step away or half a mile, and the eye cannot decide. Nothing flashes or hums. The Sill’s technology has no interest in announcing itself.

Most of the time the place is quiet. Water makes the main sound: a trickle in the cracks, a soft patter from the ledges, the occasional plink of a drop landing in a hollow. Under that is a low vibration, felt more in the teeth than heard, which comes and goes with the warmth of the stone. When it stops, the silence is so complete that the mist seems to have weight.

The Sill changes by small measures. Springs open, close, and open somewhere else. A green mat will spread across bare rock over several days, then withdraw overnight, leaving the stone clean and wet. One of the cliffs sheds a handful of fresh shards each morning. Nothing here has settled into a final shape; even the oldest-looking surfaces hold the tension of something still cooling, still choosing where to flow.

At the outer rim, the cloud parts now and then. Below is not a view of the country, but a blank expanse of pale vapor, with occasional darker folds moving far beneath the shelf. The wind does not rise from it. The Sill simply stays above the hidden world, hot and damp, green in scattered places, and newly made enough that its rules can still be overheard if you stand very still.
