The basin has no edge visible from the ground. It runs beneath a broad, pale sky, a low country of warm water and built stone extending farther than weather can cross in a day. From above, it might look like a single city; from within it, the city seems less like a settlement than a geological condition.

There are no trees, no grass, no soft green anywhere. The surfaces are mineral: dark ceramic, salt-glazed masonry, old metal rubbed smooth by water, and the dull pearled stone of the basin itself. Buildings crowd together until streets become narrow seams between their walls. Some are old enough to have settled into the ground, their corners blunted and their foundations furred with mineral deposits. Others have been folded over them, lifted on struts, threaded through their interiors. The city has been built and rebuilt so many times that its age is visible not in ruins but in layers: a buried canal glimpsed through a floor grate; a row of arched doorways now below the waterline; an obsolete bridge serving as the ceiling of a newer market.

Water is everywhere, though nowhere in great lakes. It wells up through joints in the paving, drips from the undersides of roofs, runs in shoulder-wide channels between blocks, and beads on the warm walls even when the air is clear. The lowest passages are ankle-deep or waist-deep, depending on the hour. There is no single tide to explain it. The basin’s water moves through a web of gates, pressure chambers, old natural faults, and machines whose operation is too continuous to distinguish from the weather. It rises in one quarter while draining from another. A street may be dry at dawn and pouring by noon.

The city keeps a permanent thunder. Pumps throb beneath the paving. Vents exhale in long, rough breaths. Water rushes through enclosed conduits with the sound of a river trapped inside a wall. Above that, construction cranes groan, warning horns call across the roofs, and the close-packed neighborhoods make every noise return from several directions at once. Even indoors, there is no true quiet. People speak by signs, lights, and touch. Small bells are used only for emergencies; their notes vanish almost at once into the roar.

The light is ordinary—sunlight, largely unfiltered—but it has little room to fall. It comes down in shafts between roofs and glances from wet stone, brightening the upper levels while the passages below remain blue-grey. After rain, the whole place shines without becoming beautiful in any gentle sense. Reflections break apart in moving water: windows, signs, cables, the occasional pale face at a hatch. Warm mist gathers over the channels and folds through the alleys, but never thickly enough to hide the city for long.

The inhabitants of the water are numerous. Blind, pale fish hold in the current beneath bridges, turning together when a pump changes pitch. Armored scavengers patrol the margins of flooded floors, climbing stairwells when their usual channels run dry. At night, broad-bodied creatures rise from the deep cisterns and press their mouths to the warm undersides of streets, filtering whatever the city sheds. Smaller animals live in wall cavities and drainworks, quick and glossy as spilled oil. Some are descended from ordinary river fauna; others have been shaped by generations of life among filters, cables, and chemical runoff. They are not decorations or pets. The city makes room for them the way it makes room for water: grudgingly, constantly, and not always where it intends.

There is little that resembles a natural landscape, but the place is not lifeless. In cracks and tanks, almost invisible films of mineral-feeding organisms cloud the water. They are not plants, and no one calls them gardens. The animals graze on them, or on one another, or on the endless meal of residue carried from the higher, busier districts. The food chain is narrow and crowded, a system that has learned to use nearly everything. When a drainage gate fails, the resulting bloom of small life can draw a procession of larger creatures through the lower streets. People know which doors to seal.

From district to district, the ground barely changes elevation. There are no commanding hills, only ramps, piled foundations, terraces, and the shallow rise of old deposits beneath the newer construction. Yet the scale is immense. Storm fronts can travel across the basin for days, gathering heat over one sector and unloading rain on another. The rain is warm. It drums on roofs and hammers along the spillways, filling the city with so much sound that the streets seem to shake. Then it stops, and the water continues moving through the machinery as if the storm had merely added another voice to an old conversation.

Everything is in motion, but little is new. The channels have worn their routes deep into the city. Certain walls bow by a few fingers’ width each decade, and the structures around them adjust without ceremony. Buildings flex on buried supports. Walkways slide aside when the pressure beneath them changes. Whole blocks can be shifted by carefully timed sequences of locks and counterweights, not quickly, but with an inevitability that makes the residents plan their lives around moving architecture. There are official maps, though none are entirely current. A route is reliable only if you know what has happened to it this week.

At intervals, the city opens into broad, low plazas: old basins emptied for maintenance, exchange yards, gathering floors surrounded by tier upon tier of buildings. Their surfaces are marked with concentric lines showing the maximum depth of water in different seasons. On ordinary days, these plazas are dense with stalls, carts, and people. On storm days they empty in minutes, and the floodgates close with a series of impacts felt through the soles.

Far below the busiest streets, buried behind several generations of construction, lie the original contours of the basin: soft mineral shelves, drowned hollows, long fractures where warm water still enters from under the plain. The city has not conquered this ground. It has learned to distribute its pressure, divert its seepage, and listen for changes in the old stone. Every so often, the stone answers. A tremor passes through the walls. The channels run backward for a few seconds. Somewhere, a whole neighborhood’s worth of pipes begins to sing.

Then the city answers in turn—gates opening, platforms lifting, pumps changing cadence—and the water finds its way again.
