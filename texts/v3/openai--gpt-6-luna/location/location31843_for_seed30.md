The place occupies a shallow hollow on a high ridge, small enough that you can see its edges from the middle. Beyond them, the mountain falls away into cloud. The hollow is not quite a basin yet. Its floor rises in blunt, unfinished shelves of black stone, with pale seams showing where the rock has split but not settled. Nothing here looks old. Edges are sharp; surfaces seem newly exposed, as if the ground has only just remembered how to be ground.

Warm water beads on everything. It gathers in cracks, runs a few inches, then pauses in clear trembling ribbons that sometimes climb uphill before spilling back down. The air is dim and close, full of fine mist. Light comes through the cloud as a soft, colorless glow, though every so often a pool flashes with a blue brightness of its own. There is little soil. A few low, waxy plants cling to the wet stone, spaced far apart, their roots silver and exposed. Between them grow thin rust-colored fans that fold shut when touched by shadow.

The life is mostly small, and it is everywhere. Pale crabs with jointed glassy legs move in crowds through the water channels. Winged things hover above the warm cracks, making a noise like a hundred tiny tuning forks. Under the shelves, something larger shifts from one dark opening to another; you hear its claws click, but rarely see more than a shoulder of striped hide. The animals do not seem to mind the scattered structures. They nest in their shadows and drink from their seams.

Those structures are too spare to make a settlement: three narrow towers, a low arch, and a cluster of black rods planted at uneven intervals in the stone. Their surfaces are smooth, without doors or visible joints. Rain passes through the arch without wetting it. Near the rods, sound bends strangely. A shout returns from the wrong direction; a falling droplet may ring like a struck bell. The towers appear to be measuring the hollow, though their instruments point not outward but down, into the stone.

The ground is still being made. At intervals, the rods give a faint pulse. Then the shelves shiver, and new ridges rise a finger’s breadth from the floor, clean and sharp as folded metal. Water pours from them. The whole hollow answers with a deep, crowded roar: stone settling, water snapping through fresh channels, wings and claws scattering. Afterward, the mist thickens, and the new ridges begin to shine.

Nothing erodes here in the ordinary way. The water does not wear the stone down so much as persuade it into another shape. A channel may vanish overnight, while a smooth knob of rock grows from a flat place. The small animals learn these changes quickly. They gather where the next warm fissure will open, though no one can see how they know.

At the rim, the ridge is dry and wind-scoured. Within the hollow, the air remains warm, wet, and loud. It feels less like a finished landscape than a sheltered thought taking form: a few hundred steps of raw mountain, busy with life, watched over by silent machines that may be keeping it from becoming something else.
