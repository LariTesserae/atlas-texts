The district is small enough that, from its northern wall, you can see nearly all of it: a shallow basin of buildings, bridges, ducts, and narrow squares, enclosed by a low rim of dark stone. Yet it is difficult to see the whole place at once. Rooflines overlap at unlikely angles. A street may be visible through three windows in three different buildings, each window looking down on a different stretch of it. The district occupies little ground, but seems to have been built with no agreement about where the ground is.

The stone beneath it is new, or newly exposed. It has the unfinished look of something still deciding what kind of rock it is: black plates with pale seams, soft gray bulges cooling into harder shapes, hollows lined with a mineral that resembles frost. Here and there the paving gives way to raw substrate. Nothing has yet worn the edges smooth. Even the oldest stairways appear newly cut, their corners sharp enough to catch the scant light.

Buildings crowd the basin. They are tall, though none is tall enough to dominate; their upper stories press shoulder to shoulder, joined by narrow catwalks and covered conduits. Facades are made of blue-black ceramic, dull metal, glass that clouds from within, and a few ancient-looking blocks that seem to have been quarried from the ground directly beneath them. The construction is exact, but not always intelligible. A tower’s foundation may begin on one side of a street and emerge, several floors up, on the other. Some walls continue underground farther than the buildings are wide. In the tightest courtyards, a dozen surfaces occupy what ought to be one corner.

The machinery is everywhere and almost silent. Fine filaments run under the paving, visible when the stone is wet. Small emitters hang beneath cornices like beads of rain. They hold buildings upright, perhaps, or keep the district’s dimensions from loosening. At intervals, a circular frame stands across a lane, open in the middle, without a door or any apparent purpose. Passing through one can bring a person out a few paces farther along than the lane allows. The extra distance is never consistent. Locals—if there are locals—do not appear to notice.

Cold settles low here. It lies in the lanes and under the bridges, gathering around the bases of walls. The upper windows sometimes catch a faint, ordinary daylight, but the streets remain in a soft gray half-light, as if the sun were shining on a nearby place. There is no obvious sky from within the basin. Above the rooftops is a milky blankness, interrupted by a rare, thin glimmer that might be cloud, glass, or the underside of something immense.

Moisture arrives irregularly. It beads on rails, gathers in the seams between paving stones, and runs down walls that have no gutters. Sometimes a brief rain falls only in one courtyard. Sometimes an entire block sweats. Water gathers in shallow black pools, too still to reflect accurately: one may show the pavement above it, or a ceiling that does not exist, or a room in which the viewer is standing. The pools do not deepen. They evaporate slowly, leaving pale salt fans and delicate crusts that resemble maps of streets not yet built.

Vegetation is scarce. A few strands of dark moss grow in the warmest pipe-joints. In a recessed square, a cluster of thin white stalks has pushed up through a crack, each ending in a clear bead that trembles without falling. They have no leaves. Elsewhere, green appears as a discoloration on the stone—until it shifts position between visits. There are no trees large enough to soften the architecture, and no birds cross the open parts of the basin. Once, perhaps, a small animal has left a line of prints in the frost. The marks stop at a blank wall, then resume on its far side.

Sound is sparse and strangely selective. Footsteps carry too clearly through the covered passages and vanish in the open squares. A pipe may click once after an hour of silence. Somewhere behind the walls, a mechanism makes a long, low tone, so quiet it is felt in the teeth more than heard. Rain on the roofs is nearly inaudible; rain inside a sealed stairwell can sound like a crowd whispering behind a curtain. There are no engines, no market voices, no ordinary hum of a city. The district is densely occupied by structures and almost empty of noise.

Its changes happen slowly. A stair acquires one extra step over several days. A seam in a retaining wall widens by the thickness of a fingernail, then closes again. The stone rim of the basin is still rising in places, not enough to see moving, but enough that old survey marks disappear beneath it. New foundations form without crews. They begin as a faint geometry of light in the air, then acquire weight: first a corner, then a floor, then a doorway opening onto a room whose interior is not yet furnished—or not yet decided.

The place has rules, though they are less like laws than habits. Straight lines remain straight only while someone is looking directly along them. A doorway left open for too long may begin to lead to a different room. Measurements agree when made on the same day and disagree when compared later. Water remembers its container; if poured from a cup into a basin, it may retain the cup’s roundness and lie on the surface as a shallow lens. Objects left unattended do not disappear so much as become uncertain: their outlines soften, and their names become harder to recall.

Nothing here seems to be collapsing. The district is not a ruin, and its distortions do not feel violent. It is under construction, but not by hands. It is being assembled from matter, measurement, and expectation, each influencing the others. The buildings press close because the small basin is trying to contain more city than it has room for. The cold preserves things while they are being determined. The dampness gathers in unfinished joints. Beneath the paving, the young ground continues to take shape.

At the district’s lowest point is a narrow square with no monument, only a circle of unmarked stone. Moisture collects there even in dry weather. If you stand in the center, the streets around you seem to settle into their correct positions. The walls stop leaning. The far corners become briefly reachable. Then, somewhere overhead, a single drop of water falls upward and disappears into the pale blankness above the roofs. The square shifts by a few inches, and the city resumes becoming.
