The plateau is called **the Hush**, though nothing there is quiet.

It lies high above the world’s lower weather, a broad upland of old stone and pale mineral dust. The horizon is not mountainous so much as patiently worn down: long shoulders of rock, shallow basins, shelves that run for miles before dipping out of sight. Once, immense forces lifted this country. Since then it has had more time than violence. Wind has rounded the cliffs to blunt edges and scoured the valleys almost level. The oldest rock shows through everywhere, blue-black and faintly metallic, as if the plateau were the exposed floor of something buried before the world learned to change.

There is no standing water. No streambeds, no wet mud, no gleam of a lake beneath the dim sky. In the deepest folds, pale salts gather in thin, delicate crusts. Frost may silver the stone, but it disappears without a melt: it is drawn into the living ground or taken apart by the air. Water exists here mostly as a rumor in the chemistry of things.

The ground is alive.

A low, continuous vegetation covers the plateau from edge to edge, though not as grass covers a meadow. It is a skin of dark, branching growth—flat, jointed, and faintly translucent at the edges, like a vast colony of mineral lace. It follows the stone’s contours, filling cracks, bridging small gaps, and forming shelves over hollows. In open places it lies close to the ground, dull green-black under the weak light. Here and there it rises into stands of stiff, narrow fronds, each one taller than a person, spaced widely apart. Their surfaces are ribbed like instrument reeds.

The plants do not need rain in any familiar sense. They draw trace vapors and charged particles from the high air, and take their bulk from the stone, slowly loosening its minerals into fibrous tissue. A living patch can take centuries to cross a basin. It does so without haste, but almost nothing in the Hush is truly barren. Even the barest rock carries pale threads in its fissures, and the threads go on growing long after the cliff face has stopped falling.

The air is cool, thin, and full of a fine grit that never quite settles. Light comes through it as if strained by layers of smoke. At midday the sky is a muted pewter; shadows are shallow and blurred. The fronds catch what little brightness there is and return it in narrow, cold glints. At night the plateau does not become black. Instead, small points of light appear underfoot, spaced far apart: the slow pulse of colonies exchanging energy through their shared root-lattices.

The first sound is a deep, irregular beating. Wind passes through the taller growth, and the fronds answer—not with a rustle, but with a resonant metallic moan. It travels across the flats in waves. Some pitches linger for several seconds after the gust has passed, held in the ground itself. In the old basins the sound gathers and grows immense, a rolling chord that seems to come from beneath the stone. The plateau is loud in the way a great machine is loud: not with a single roar, but with many simultaneous operations.

There are animals everywhere, though few are visible at once. Small, pale runners slip between the ground-growth, their many feet ticking over the stone. They feed on the mineral nodules that the plants produce, then vanish into openings too narrow to admit a hand. Larger things travel by sound. Long-backed grazers emerge from behind the low ridges in groups of three or four, their hides patterned with plates of hardened plant-fiber. They move slowly, wearing polished channels through the living ground. Above them, almost lost against the grey air, wide-winged hunters ride the plateau’s steady updrafts. Their wings make no sound until they fold; then the air cracks like a struck sheet of metal.

At certain times, the animals all turn in the same direction. The fronds turn too. No one in the settlements agrees on what they are listening for. Some say it is a signal from the deep-rooted colonies. Others say the plateau is remembering an earthquake that happened before there were animals to hear it.

Human construction is scattered across the Hush, arranged in small settlements rather than cities. A town may occupy a whole ridge but contain only a few hundred buildings: low black dwellings set between outcrops, elevated walkways, wind towers, and narrow bridges that cross the living mats without crushing them. From a distance the settlements resemble instruments laid out on a table. Their roofs are curved to catch the wind’s pressure, and their walls are made of folded ceramic and thin metal that flexes rather than resists.

The oldest structures are nearly indistinguishable from the plateau. They were built around the growth, not over it. In some places the plants have grown through the walls, woven themselves into stairwells, and sealed doors without damaging the surrounding architecture. Newer engineering is stranger. Between settlements, a traveler may come upon a line of slender markers standing in empty ground. They seem to do nothing. Yet the air around them holds a faint distortion, and when the wind moves through, the deep beating of the plateau changes pitch. The markers are tuning the landscape—taking vibrations from the rock and returning them elsewhere, across distances too great for a cable.

The roads are not roads, exactly. They are clearings maintained in the living mat, each marked by a discontinuity in its growth. Some guide travelers from one settlement to another. Others lead to places no one remembers building. The oldest paths sometimes curve away from the present terrain, crossing shallow hollows where no bridge stands and climbing slopes that no longer exist. People follow them at their own risk. The paths are accurate according to a map made thousands of years ago, though the plateau has changed since then.

Nothing here moves quickly, but change is constant. Wind loosens mineral grains. The plants feed on them. Animals crop the growth and carry its seeds to new ground. Settlements adjust to the pressure of passing herds, and their machines adjust to the changing notes of the plants. Once every few years a deep vibration travels under the plateau. Fronds flatten in sequence, as if an invisible hand had passed over them. At that time the sound towers fall silent, the animals shelter in the shallow basins, and every settlement dims its lights.

Afterward, the ground is never quite the same. A new ridge may have risen by the width of a finger. A valley may have become a little shallower. Somewhere, a forgotten path may reappear.

The Hush is not empty, and it is not crowded. Its life is spread thin over an enormous patience. Its cities are small, its distances are long, and its oldest inhabitants are neither the people nor the animals, but the dark, quiet vegetation beneath them—living almost everywhere, growing by increments, turning stone into habitat, and listening to the world through its roots.
