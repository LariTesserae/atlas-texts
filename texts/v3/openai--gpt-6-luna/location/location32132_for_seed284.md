The place is called the **Upper Pocket**, though there is nothing pocket-sized about its drop.

From outside, it is only a seam in the shoulder of a mountain: a narrow, dark slit between two plates of newly lifted stone. Wind passes over it without a whistle. No stream reaches it, and no green thing marks its entrance. But the seam opens inward into a steep, enclosed shaft, a hidden vertical district folded into the mountain’s height. Its walls are close enough to touch in places, crowded with ledges, bridges, ducts, stairs and bolted-on rooms. Look down from the highest platform and the lowest lights disappear into mineral haze. Look up and the roof is a dim, jagged suggestion. The whole world is contained here, but its verticality makes it feel immeasurable.

The rock is young and sharp-featured. Fresh fractures show pale, metallic interiors beneath dark outer crusts. There are no softened slopes, only shelves that end abruptly, blades of stone, and rubble caught in corners where it has not yet found a way farther down. The surfaces are dry, cold, and faintly oily with mineral dust. A person’s glove comes away silvered. Water exists only as a rumor: a trace of frost behind a warm exhaust pipe, a bead of condensation in some inaccessible seam, quickly taken up by the stone or drawn into the machines. The air tastes of iron and old electricity.

The settlement is built into every available face. Rooms hang from the walls in tiers, joined by narrow catwalks and ladders. The oldest structures are squat blocks of dark stone, fitted into natural recesses; newer ones are riveted metal boxes, pipework, gantries and maintenance cages. Here a stair vanishes behind a bank of spinning wheels. There a cable-carriage crawls along a track, carrying a crate no larger than a boot. Smoke-colored lamps glow behind grates. Their light is weak and uneven, and the crowded architecture divides it into bars and slits. Most of the district remains in half-darkness.

Industry is everywhere, though never quite loud enough to dominate the place. A pump knocks patiently somewhere below. Belts turn with a dry whisper. Gears shift, pause, and take hold again. A press gives one muffled thump every few minutes. Sound travels strangely in the shaft: a small metallic click can be heard far above, while a shouted word is swallowed by the layers of machinery and stone. The general quiet is not peace so much as the discipline of a place that has learned to work without wasting noise.

The district is turbulent in its small, constant ways. Pressure changes send gusts plunging through the vertical passages. Loose dust rises in narrow columns, hangs, and settles on a different floor. A suspended bridge may sway gently for hours, then jerk hard enough to strike the wall. Mechanical lifts stop at the wrong landings; counterweights move without warning. The inhabitants have marked safe routes in paint, but the marks are continually being scraped away, covered, or carried off by the wind. Nothing here changes all at once. Instead, every ledge is slowly rearranged by little accidents.

There is abundant animal life, though none of it resembles the animals of the outer world. Small, pale climbers crowd the stone seams: long-bodied, many-footed things with reflective eyes and hooked toes. They travel along cables in flowing knots, parting around machinery and closing behind it. Flat-winged fliers nest in the warm throats of exhaust stacks, pouring out at dusk in dense, nearly silent streams. Some are no bigger than a thumbnail; others, when their wings open, are almost the width of a person’s hand. They feed on mineral crusts, machine-fuzz, and one another. Their tracks stipple the dusty platforms. Their shed skins collect in corners like thin scraps of paper.

The people leave offerings for them—not food, exactly, but warm grit from the furnaces and little bowls of powdered metal. No one admits that this keeps the creatures close. No one wants them too close either. The fliers will nest inside a sleeve if it is left hanging overnight; the crawlers can pass through a gap too narrow to show light. In a district with so little water and so little living cover, every warm seam becomes habitat.

The Upper Pocket has one rule that everyone understands: **nothing that falls is considered gone until it has stopped making noise**. A bolt dropped from a high catwalk may strike platforms for minutes before vanishing. A broken machine can shed parts through several levels, each impact setting off another chain of motion. Even the largest structures seem provisional. The settlement is not built on stable ground so much as caught, for now, among the mountain’s ribs.

Beyond the highest habitation, the shaft narrows into darkness and cold. Beyond the lowest, the walls pinch around an old industrial throat whose purpose no one remembers. Between them, the Pocket keeps working: packed tight against the stone, its lamps dim, its machines turning, its creatures climbing in restless tides. It is hidden from the open air, but it is never still.
