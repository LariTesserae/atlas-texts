The city occupies a shallow bowl where the land has been worn almost level. From a little distance it looks like a patch of dark green pressed into the wet earth, small enough to walk across in an afternoon. Up close, it has depth: root-cellars beneath the streets, towers grown into the canopy, and old machinery suspended in the mist above the roofs.

Nothing here is quite dry. Water beads on every surface, gathers in the seams of stone, and runs in clear threads down the flanks of buildings. The ground gives underfoot—not mud, exactly, but a springy mat of interlocked roots and moss over something dense and ancient. Here and there the mat parts to show black rock, polished smooth by ages of water. The rock is cut with shallow grooves that might be natural channels or the remnants of some first attempt at plumbing. The water follows them either way.

Plants form nearly everything. Thick trunks rise through courtyards and stairwells; their bark has been trained into ledges, lintels, and balconies. Broad leaves overlap above the lanes, making a high green ceiling that catches the rain and releases it in patient drips. The buildings are not hidden inside the vegetation so much as negotiated with it. A window has grown around a branch. A wall gives way to a curtain of pale roots. A footbridge is a living stem, hollowed just enough for people to cross, and it flexes gently when the wind pushes through.

The settlement is urban in its habits, if not in its appearance. Its few lanes connect stations, water courts, sleeping niches, market platforms. There are signs, though some are projected into droplets of light that cling to leaves and vanish when the rain shakes them loose. There are transit lines, too: almost invisible filaments strung between the higher trunks, along which small capsules slide without sound. They stop at platforms grown from the trees, take on a passenger, and continue. No engine is visible. The line seems to anticipate its own turns, bowing aside from a branch before the branch has finished growing.

The city is sparse. Long stretches of lane are empty, and whole courtyards are left to ferns and shallow pools. A few people move through them, carrying baskets or devices that resemble folded seeds. They do not hurry. The place supplies no straight path for long: roots lift the paving, water cuts across a stair, a new shoot closes yesterday’s passage. Instead, routes are marked by faint changes in the air—warmth, pressure, a slight brightening in the leaves. The inhabitants read these signs easily. Visitors mostly follow them into dead ends.

Above the canopy, the light is ordinary: soft daylight under a clouded sky. Within the city, it breaks into shifting pieces. Water lenses sunlight into moving flecks, and leaves turn their undersides silver whenever the wind passes. Now and then a surface catches a color that is not in the sky—an oil-blue flare, a brief red seam running through a stone—and then settles back into green and brown. The technology here is too quiet to announce itself. It alters the way things meet: rain divides around certain doorways, roots avoid buried cables, and a pool can hold the reflection of a room that is not currently built.

The ground is old beyond any local measure. Erosion has rounded every exposed edge, flattened the surrounding country, and left the city resting in a shallow, persistent damp. Even its newest structures have the look of things that have been rubbed smooth. The first stones are no longer distinguishable from the bedrock. The oldest roots have grown through the ruins of older roots, and under those, perhaps, the outlines of a settlement whose name has been absorbed into the soil.

There is plenty of animal life, though it seldom gathers in conspicuous herds. Small, glassy insects hover above the water and vanish into the leaves when approached. Long-legged birds step along the rootways, dipping their beaks into openings that seal behind them. Something larger moves in the flooded margins: a slow disturbance beneath a raft of flowers, followed by a row of ripples travelling in the wrong direction. At night—when the canopy darkens, but never becomes fully black—the branches click and mutter with the settling of wings, shells, and tiny feet.

The loudness comes in layers. Water patters on the leaves, pours from the eaves, and drains through hidden channels with the steady rush of a distant crowd. Insects make a thin, electric whine that rises and falls in patches. The transit filaments give off a low harmonic note when a capsule passes, felt first in the ribs, then heard in the teeth. Beneath all of it is a slow clatter from somewhere underground, like an enormous mechanism turning over in its sleep.

The weather is always nearly changing. Warm air collects between the buildings; then a gust slips through the canopy and shakes loose a sudden rain. Pools overflow, drain, and refill. Leaves fold against one another, then open again. A walkway may be passable in the morning and submerged by noon, though the water never seems to threaten the settlement. The whole place adjusts as it moves: architecture flexing, routes shifting, channels rebalancing themselves.

At the center of the bowl is a quiet square where the vegetation thins. Here, water wells up through a ring of dark stone and spreads into a shallow basin. No roof covers it. The surrounding trunks lean away, making a space wide enough to see the sky. People gather there in ones and twos, never for long. Sometimes the basin shows the clouds overhead. Sometimes it shows a pale, immense pattern turning beneath the city, as if the visible town were only a small surface on a machine that has been running since before the land was flat.

Then a gust passes through. The image breaks. A bird calls from the roof of a root-grown hall. Water resumes its measured falling, and the city goes on changing by increments too small to notice until the path home is somewhere else.
