The town occupies a shallow hollow in a plain of pale mineral crust. It is small enough that, from its highest roof, you can see its boundary: a broken ring of low ridges, sharp-edged and newly exposed, as if the ground had only recently decided to become land. Nothing in the hollow is high. The roofs and the ridges share nearly the same horizon.

The buildings are close together, but the town is strangely empty. Its narrow streets thread between blocks of chalk-colored stone; then, without warning, they widen into a court large enough to hold a crowd that never comes. There are covered ways, blank walls, little doorways set too low for comfort, and tall communal halls whose interiors seem larger than their exteriors allow. All are made without machines. The blocks were cut, lifted, and fitted by hand—or by hands unlike ours—and sealed with a pale cement that has hardened into a single skin. No metal glints anywhere. There are no pipes, wires, lamps, wheels, or tools left behind. A few dry channels run along the streets, built to carry water, but they have never held any.

The stone is not quite stone. It powders under pressure, though the walls remain sound. At the corners, the newest surfaces are keen as broken glass. Older faces have gone soft and matte, but erosion is slow here; there is no rain to round an edge, and little wind to worry it. The ground is powdered with fine salt and mineral dust, which lies in the streets like pale ash. Footprints remain for a long time, except in places where the dust lifts by itself and drifts—not away, but upward, in thin, wavering veils.

There is little color. The light is dim and cool, a gray illumination with no clear source. At some hours the roofs cast shadows that fall toward the light rather than away from it. No one seems to notice. The town’s long lanes are filled with a soft, even gloom, while the open courts are only slightly brighter. Looking upward, one sees a low, blank sky—or perhaps a ceiling of cloud so seamless it has become difficult to call it sky. Nothing falls from it. Nothing gathers.

The silence is the first thing a visitor learns to distrust. There is no wind-noise, no birdcall, no creak from the buildings. Even a shout would not travel far. Sound seems to stop at the edge of the body that made it. Touch a wall, and the contact is felt clearly through the hand, but the knock cannot be heard. In the deepest halls, the silence has a pressure to it, like a room waiting for a word it has forgotten.

Yet the town is active. Its changes are slow but constant. A thin ridge of salt appears overnight across a threshold; by the next dim interval it has been drawn into a line along the opposite wall. Streets subtly alter their slope—not enough to stumble on, but enough that a dropped pebble may roll uphill. Doorways migrate by inches from one day to the next. The buildings do not move in any obvious way; rather, their arrangement seems to be reconsidered whenever no one is looking directly at it.

Sparse life persists in the cracks. Low, dark plants grow in sheltered corners, each a tuft of stiff, threadlike leaves pressed close to the ground. They have no blossoms, and no green that can be distinguished from charcoal. Their roots run along the surface instead of into it, tracing the seams between stones. Where several tufts meet, the mineral dust has a faint warmth. Once in a while, a small pale creature crosses an empty court. It moves in short, exact steps, stopping at each shadow as though testing it. The creatures leave no tracks.

At the center of the town stands a round public space enclosed by walls, with no roof and no visible entrance. Its floor is perfectly level. In the middle is a shallow basin carved from the same pale material as the buildings. It is dry, of course, but never dusty. Fine grains collect around its rim, then disappear before they can settle inside. The basin gives the impression of having been made for something that is not water.

The place is not abandoned in the ordinary sense. Nothing has fallen down. Nothing has been reclaimed by vines or sand. The walls are maintained by their own patient adjustments; cracks close, edges sharpen, and doorways keep their proportions. But there are no inhabitants to be seen, and no sign that anyone is expected to return. The town holds its shape with the disciplined stillness of a gathering after everyone has gone home.

Remain long enough, and the empty courts begin to feel occupied. Not by ghosts, exactly, but by arrangements: a route someone once took, a queue that formed beneath a certain lintel, a room whose proportions seem to insist on a particular number of bodies. The town remembers use without remembering users. Around it, on the young mineral plain, nothing moves but the upward-drifting dust. Inside it, the streets continue their silent revisions.
