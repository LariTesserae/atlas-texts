The high country begins without a proper edge. It rises out of the lower world in a long, uneven shoulder, then keeps rising by increments too small to notice and too large to ignore. From above, its pale plateaus and dark basins seem to go on past the curve of the weather. Clouds form over one end of it, travel for days, and spend themselves over the other. The air is cold even in the bright part of the year. Light falls plainly across the land: white on the mineral flats, blue in the hollows, briefly gold where the sun reaches water.

Much of the ground is new. It has the rough, undecided look of something still arriving. Broad sheets of basalt lie beside powdery deposits of glassy stone; in places the rock has cooled into smooth bulges, and in others it is split into open seams with their interiors still warm. There are few ravines and no old river valleys. Water has not yet had time to make its signatures permanent. It spreads over shallow ground, gathers in black pools, and slips away beneath thin crusts, reappearing a few kilometres on as a line of weeping stone. After rain, the flats are briefly reflective enough to confuse the horizon.

The land is damp more often than it is wet. Mist clings low in the morning, caught in the hollows and around the legs of the towers. Rain arrives in narrow moving fronts, often with a clear sky visible behind it. Meltwater and condensation feed a lacework of marshes at the margins of the uplands. There, reeds grow in scattered, low bands—not fields so much as pauses in the stone. Their tough leaves are dark green, almost black, and bend under beads of water. Lichen takes hold on exposed mineral faces. Elsewhere, vegetation is sparse: small mats of tough grass, pale cushion-plants, and creeping root systems that find warmth in the ground’s shallow fissures.

The settlements are not crowded, but they are unmistakably cities. They stand far apart across the plateau, their districts joined by elevated causeways and buried transit lines. From a distance each one seems modest: a low cluster of roofs, a few narrow towers, a bright ring of infrastructure. Up close, a city unfolds in layers. Habitable terraces hang from load-bearing frames set deep into the young rock. Buildings can settle by fractions of a millimetre without cracking; their foundations measure the ground’s slow movement and adjust in response. Roads are heated only where ice would be dangerous. Water is caught from the air, drawn from shallow aquifers, and returned to the marshes in carefully measured channels.

The engineering is advanced enough that its workings are hard to separate from the place. A span across a basin has no visible supports; it holds itself by distributing its weight through fields of tension anchored on either side. Freight moves beneath the causeways in silent capsules. Survey instruments drift above the unfinished land, mapping new fractures and detecting where the crust has begun to knit. Nothing shines extravagantly. The city’s machinery is designed to be quiet, and most of it is quiet: the low vibration of a power core under a service floor, the soft click of a valve opening inside a wall, the whisper of a transit car passing far below.

There is little human noise in the open country. The distances swallow it. A voice may carry across a frozen pool and then vanish; a door closing in the city becomes, a moment later, only a small disturbance in the wind. Most days the loudest sound is water—dripping from a ledge, ticking against a roof, or moving under the thin skin of a marsh. During certain weather, though, the land makes a sound of its own: a deep, gradual groan as one wide shelf of cooling stone presses against another. The sound travels too slowly to locate. It can be mistaken for distant thunder.

Animals are everywhere, though not all at once. The marsh margins teem with small life: translucent insects, burrowing crustaceans, narrow-backed fish in the warmer pools. Herds of long-legged grazers move across the plateaus in scattered lines, keeping their bodies to the lee of low ridges. They are followed by broad-winged hunters that use the rising weather to cross great distances without flapping. At night, pale scavengers come down from the stony uplands to feed along the water’s edge. Their tracks appear in the morning frost and disappear again under rain.

The abundant life does not make the country feel lush. It makes it feel occupied. There are eyes in the reeds and movement in the shallows, but between one gathering of animals and the next lie kilometres of bare stone. Even the cities leave generous intervals of empty ground around themselves. The residents preserve these distances deliberately. They have learned that the land is not finished, and that open space is both a safety margin and a kind of respect.

The plateau is still rising. The change is slow—less a heave than a steady correction—yet the instruments record it everywhere. New ridges appear where the crust buckles. Pools migrate as the ground tilts by almost imperceptible degrees. Some years a stretch of road must be unmade and rebuilt because the stone beneath it has shifted; some years the city adds a district on the newly settled edge, where the rock has become stable enough to bear weight. The people do not speak of conquering the high country. They speak of keeping pace with it.

In clear weather, the whole place can look almost gentle: a pale sky, low green seams of vegetation, quiet water, buildings set cleanly into the mineral expanse. Then a weather front crosses the plateau and turns that calm into scale. The cloud bank darkens an entire region at once. Rain moves over the empty miles. The towers become small marks on a vast, wet surface, and the new land continues, beneath them, to form.
