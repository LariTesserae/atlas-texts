The country rises in long, deliberate steps.

From a distance it resembles a mountain range softened by green: ridges, saddles, high shelves of land, and deep valleys between them. But there is no exposed stone. Every face is faced—brick, poured concrete, ceramic tile, old iron darkened by weather. The slopes are held in place by walls thick enough to have streets on top of them. Tunnels pass through the ridges; bridges cross the valleys in several layers, some so high above the lower roads that their undersides disappear into haze. The original ground, if there was one, lies far beneath the work. Here, the landscape is all construction: a continent of terraces, towers, culverts, stairs, rail beds, retaining walls, and platforms, joined so continuously that it has become difficult to say where the city ends and the mountain begins.

The heights are immense. At the upper levels, warm air moves cleanly over roofs and gardens, and a person can look across miles of green terraces to where the next ridge stands against the sky. Lower down, the same view is broken into layers: balconies beneath viaducts, planted ledges above loading yards, narrow bridges crossing narrow bridges. The whole region is open, but never empty. Wherever there is room for a handspan of soil, there is a trough, a pot, or a built bed. Figs and climbing beans spill over parapets. Tall grasses grow in the seams between paving slabs. Orchards cover entire shelves of the hillside, their roots contained in deep masonry boxes. Vines soften the towers and curtains of leaves hang from the undersides of walkways. The greenery makes the construction seem almost gentle, though nothing here is small.

Water comes irregularly. Rain may pass in a brief warm sheet, silvering the roofs and pouring down stairs in shining channels. Much of it is caught: directed through gutters, filters, pipes, and cisterns, then lifted again by pumps to gardens higher on the slopes. In the dry intervals, moisture gathers as condensation on shaded walls and in the cool throats of tunnels. Drips persist in places the sun cannot reach. There are pools in old reservoirs, canals tucked between districts, and narrow rills running beside paths, but no broad natural river. Water is a managed visitor, welcomed, divided, stored, and sent on.

The vegetation is fed by that arrangement. It is abundant enough to make shade at midday, yet it grows in a world of measured boundaries. A garden may be a whole mountainside, but its soil rests on a roof. Tree trunks rise from tiled courtyards; reed beds filter water in concrete basins; moss gathers along the damp joints of a stairwell. Nothing has quite escaped the design, though plants have occupied it so thoroughly that the design now includes their unruliness.

Animals are everywhere. Swifts stitch through the open levels between towers, gathering in dark clusters beneath bridges. Pigeons and bright, long-tailed birds crowd the orchard ledges. Insects thrum around flowering vines and collect over the shallow cisterns. Small lizards sun themselves on warm tile. Rats travel the service passages with the confidence of old residents. Goats browse in enclosed terraces, their bells audible across the stairs; fish flash in the cleaner reservoirs. At dusk, bats pour out of the ventilation arches and wheel above the lamps. These are not intruders in the built country. Their routes, nesting places, and feeding grounds are part of its settled life.

The place is loud, but not with a single noise. Engines hum in their housings. Freight lifts groan up the steep faces. Somewhere, a factory presses sheet metal in patient strokes; somewhere else, a saw whines, then stops. Water clatters through an open sluice. Birds argue from the parapets. The sound travels strangely through all the cavities: a cart rattling on a faraway bridge may seem to pass overhead, while a nearby conversation is swallowed by a stairwell. In the great ventilation courts, voices return as a soft, delayed murmur. At night the machinery lowers its pitch but never entirely ceases. The whole landscape has a working sound, like a vast assembly that has been running so long no one thinks of it as starting.

Its age shows not as ruin, but as adjustment. New conduits are fitted beside old ones. A wall has been reinforced, then faced over; a disused rail line has become a garden walk; a factory roof has accumulated enough planters to be called a park. Old surfaces have faded to the colors of bread crust, smoke, and rainwater. Moss marks the dependable leaks. The structures have settled into their loads. Nothing moves quickly here except birds, lifts, and water after a storm. The slopes, districts, and habits change by degrees: a few new levels added over a generation, a staircase diverted around an orchard, a whole terrace slowly taken over by shade trees.

On a clear day, the sun makes the upper roofs almost white. Warm light falls through the stacked bridges and reaches the lower courts in bright, narrow bands. From the highest walkways, the built ridges continue in every direction, green and geometric, fading toward a pale horizon. The immense elevation gives the air room to move, while the close-packed structures keep it full of voices, leaves, birds, and machinery.

It feels less like a city placed upon a landscape than a landscape that has been built, inhabited, and patiently taught how to live.
