The basin has no true horizon. It is so broad and so low that distance loses its shape: the farthest ridges look less like mountains than a discoloration in the air, a bruise that never quite clears. Underfoot, the land is nearly level, but nothing about it is still. A skin of shallow water covers most of the plain, warm and dark, trembling constantly beneath a sky the color of old pewter. Here and there, long shelves of stone rise by inches. Their edges have been worn soft by ages of water, until the whole world seems rubbed down to its last, smooth form.

The ground is packed with matter. Silt lies over clay, clay over crushed shells, over stone ground to flour, over older stone that may once have been something else. The layers are so compressed that they make a solid, yielding floor, like a vast mattress beneath the water. When the surface gives way, it does not open onto a clean hollow. It folds inward through more layers, each one dense with the same black grit and pale mineral threads. The basin has been filling for longer than anything living could remember, and yet it never seems to fill.

Water stands everywhere, sometimes ankle-deep, sometimes only a shining film. It seeps from hairline cracks in the stone, beads on the sides of exposed ridges, and gathers in long channels that appear to run uphill. Rain comes often, but it is difficult to tell when it begins. The whole atmosphere is already wet. Mist hangs close to the ground and beads on the skin; in places it thickens into drifting walls, veiling outcrops a few paces away. Drops fall upward from some of these walls, rising in slow, uneven streams into the low clouds. The clouds accept them without disturbance.

There is little green. Sparse growth clings to the higher stones: mats of rust-red thread, pale fans of lichen, little black cups that hold their own beads of water. Once in a long while, a cluster of reedlike stalks appears at the edge of a pool. They are almost transparent and make no shadow. They bend when the water passes over them, but the water passes both ways at once, so their movement is always uncertain. Nothing tall grows here. Nothing has room. Beneath the thin surface, perhaps, there is a forest of roots or a dense tangle of ancient life; the plain gives no sign of it except for occasional patches where the water swells and pulses as if breathing through a closed mouth.

Animals are rare, and the ones that appear are difficult to distinguish from the land. A small, jointed shape may cross a shoal, leaving no track; a dark curve in the water may be a back, a floating stone, or a strip of shadow detached from anything. Sometimes the surface dimples in a line that travels for miles, though nothing breaks through it. There are no calls, no wings, no visible herds. Whatever lives here has learned to be almost entirely still, or almost entirely elsewhere.

The sound is enormous.

A deep, continuous roar rises from the basin—not the roar of surf, exactly, but the combined noise of countless small impacts: water against stone, droplets against water, grit grinding beneath the crust. It has a weight to it. It fills the chest and blurs the edges of thought. Every so often, a sharper sound cuts through: a long metallic groan from some distant shelf of rock, or a sudden cracking report from beneath the plain. These sounds roll across the basin and return from every direction, so that the listener cannot tell whether they are approaching or receding. In the thickest mist, a person’s own footsteps seem to arrive before the foot has lifted.

Scattered across this immense flatness stand a few remnants of construction. They may be old pillars, or natural spires worn into a regular shape; low walls half-submerged in water; a ring of stones whose gaps do not match the curve of the circle. Their surfaces are smooth, but not in the way water smooths rock. They are smooth as if the details have been erased from the world around them. No tool marks remain, if there were any. No road connects them. They simply rise from the saturated plain at intervals so great that their presence feels more like punctuation than shelter.

The rules of direction are unreliable. A ridge glimpsed in the mist can remain at the same apparent distance through a day of walking. A pool may reflect the sky above it, or a different sky: one with brighter clouds, or a sun low on the horizon, though the real sky is uniformly dim. Shadows gather under things that cast none. On some days the basin tilts—not physically, but in the way a room seems to tilt just before sleep—and water begins streaming toward the highest-looking point. The flow can continue for hours. Then, without an event to mark it, the water lies flat again.

Time behaves similarly. The stones are immeasurably old, their surfaces worn to a final softness, while the weather seems to be happening for the first time. Storms do not cross the basin so much as assemble within it. A bank of cloud forms at ground level; rain lashes sideways through the fog; shallow water rises in broad, heaving sheets. The plain can alter its own arrangement by inches, then miles: channels cut, ridges sink, islands appear where there was only open water. Yet afterward the changes look ancient. Their edges are already rounded, their surfaces already settled. It is as if the land can make a new thing only by remembering it as something very old.

At the height of a storm, the whole basin becomes a single moving surface. Water, fog, stone and sky lose their separate boundaries. The roar swells until no individual sound can be heard, and the ground shivers beneath it. Flecks of pale light flicker in the mist, sometimes far away, sometimes just beneath the water. They do not illuminate anything. For a few breaths the world seems crowded with openings—places where the plain might continue into a different plain, or where the sky has been pressed down and folded under the surface.

Then the storm passes, though nothing visibly travels away. The water settles. The mist thins. The sparse growth resumes its minute, patient bending. The ancient basin remains, low and warm and packed to its limits, full of water, full of noise, full of motion—and so worn down by time that it has nowhere left to go except through itself.
