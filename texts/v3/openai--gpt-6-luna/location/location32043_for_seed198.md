The highlands begin where the rain seems to have forgotten how to fall straight. It drifts sideways through a dim, pewter-colored air, gathering on railings and roof edges, slipping down the faces of walls in narrow, patient threads. Beyond the first rise, the land opens into a long succession of ridges and basins, all visible at once in pieces: a spillway shining between two industrial roofs, a green slope stitched with stairways, the dark ribs of a distant factory vanishing into cloud.

Nothing here is quite ground. Every surface has been poured, laid, riveted, or fitted into place. The hills are held in terraces of old concrete, their angles softened by many seasons of rain. Roads cross the slopes on bridges and heavy embankments; drainage channels run beside them, under them, and sometimes through the buildings they serve. There are no bare banks, no raw cliffs. Where rock would once have shown, there are retaining walls faced with damp stone or molded panels, stained brown and black around their seams. The whole region looks less built over than built through—an enormous, settled apparatus whose shapes have weathered until they seem almost geological.

Water is always close, though seldom in great sheets. It beads on cables, clouds the panes of enclosed walkways, and pools in the shallow crowns of old roofs. It gathers in the seams between paving blocks, where small leaves have rooted and spread into dense green mats. On the lower terraces, it travels in open runnels, swift and dark, disappearing beneath grated crossings with a hollow gulp. Higher up, it seeps from joints in the retaining walls. The walls do not leak dramatically; they weep steadily, as if the whole country were sweating from within.

Vegetation has taken every allowance. It fills the narrow spaces between rails, climbs mesh screens, and thickens in the troughs built to catch runoff. Ferns and broad-leaved shrubs crowd the sheltered stairwells. Moss pads the undersides of bridges where the light never quite reaches. There are trees, but they have been given particular places: sunk into concrete wells on platforms, planted in orderly rows along reservoirs, trained against the wind behind factory walls. Their crowns make a low, continuous canopy over some of the older districts. Roots are contained in grates and masonry boxes, though here and there they have lifted a paving slab or pressed a hairline crack wider. The greenery is lush without being wild; it has found its way through a world that mostly anticipated it, and occasionally it has outgrown the plans.

The buildings crowd one another across the slopes. Workshops, pumping stations, housing blocks, covered markets, and old power halls join at awkward angles, connected by pipes and enclosed passages. Their roofs overlap until the towns appear to have grown a second, lower terrain: slanted planes of metal, glass, tar, and planted beds. Beyond them, other towns climb the next ridge, linked by freight lines and service roads. The settlement does not end so much as thin into reservoirs, processing yards, and broad shelves of machinery, then thicken again on the far side of the basin.

It is cool even at midday. The cloud ceiling lies low enough to hide the highest works, and light arrives as a diluted gray, brightening the wet surfaces but casting few shadows. Lamps are already on in many places—yellow squares behind fogged windows, blue indicators on control cabinets, red points traveling slowly along cranes. At night the region does not become dark so much as withdraw into layers: a nearby walkway, a lit window, the faint luminous smear of another district beyond the rain.

The machinery is active everywhere, but the sound is strangely restrained. Turbines turn behind insulated walls. Conveyors carry their loads under covered roofs. Water passes through sluices and pipes with a subdued, constant murmur. Now and then a metal gate strikes its stop, or a distant engine climbs in pitch and falls away. Most of the time there is only rain ticking on different materials, the hush of runoff, and the close rustle of leaves stirred by drafts between buildings. The quiet feels maintained, like a carefully balanced setting on a large machine.

Yet the landscape is never still. It changes by the hour—not in great upheavals, but through overlapping corrections. A floodgate shifts, redirecting a channel. A freight bridge rolls slowly across a gap. Pumps start in one district, and somewhere lower down a basin empties enough to expose steps and weed-darkened concrete. Mist moves through the streets and gathers under the elevated tracks. On a wet slope, a bank of planted trays is taken apart and reassembled on the neighboring terrace while crews in reflective coats work beneath hoods and dripping cables. The arrangement of things is constantly being revised, though the old walls, worn steps, and long-established channels give each revision a sense of continuity.

The region has settled into this agitation. Its systems are old enough to be scarred and trusted. Concrete has rounded at the corners where water has passed for decades. Handrails are polished where thousands of hands have used them. Spare parts are stored in the same recessed alcoves as they were before the newest ducts went in. Every surface seems to have acquired a customary use: a ledge for seed trays, a warm pipe for drying gloves, a flat roof for rain barrels, a strip of ground between loading bays for waist-high reeds.

Animals are scarce. A few small birds shelter under the loading platforms, appearing only as brief movements in the dimness. Insects gather around the warmer lights where wet vegetation meets metal. Occasionally, a dark shape slips through a drainage cut—something small that has learned the routes between water and cover. The great noise of industry has not emptied the place, but it has left little room for anything that cannot adapt to its schedules and narrow margins.

From a high overlook, the built world seems to continue beyond its own limits. The terraces descend and rise, crowded with channels, roads, houses, gardens, pipes, and towers, until rain and distance merge them into one textured gray-green expanse. Water shines wherever it can be seen. Plants fill the seams. Machinery turns behind walls. Nothing is pristine, nothing is untouched, and nothing appears abandoned. The place is damp, orderly, overfull, and in motion—an old highland landscape that has learned to behave like a living industrial system, and a living system that has learned to endure its own industry.
