The place is called the Upper Hollow, though it is not hollow in any useful sense. It is a small basin near the crest of a mountain, cupped between three rounded shoulders of stone. From the rim, the basin seems almost empty: a dark, shallow bowl, perhaps a few hundred paces across, with long stretches of bare ground between its scattered growths. The mountain rises above the weather, but the hollow has its own damp air. Warm mist gathers there and stays.

There is no visible sky. Darkness fills the bowl so completely that distance has no reliable measure. A person can see a wet stone at their feet and, beyond it, only the suggestion of ground. Yet the dark is not uniform. Water holds a little more of it than rock does; leaves seem to deepen it. The few old structures in the basin are black shapes against blackness, except where rain has polished their edges.

The stone is ancient, worn down to broad, soft forms. It has the look of something that has finished changing. Ridges have slumped into shelves; shelves have dissolved into shallow basins. Here and there, thin seams of mineral cross the rock in curves too regular to be cracks. Rain follows these seams upward as often as down. It beads beneath overhangs, gathers into clear hanging threads, and draws itself along the stone toward pools at the basin’s lowest point. The water is never deep. It lies in thin skins across the ground, or slips unseen beneath moss and root.

Vegetation is lush, but it does not crowd the place. There are mats of broad, rubbery leaves, low ferns with dark, feathery fronds, and pale grasses that bend under the weight of condensation. One tree grows in the center of the basin: thick-trunked, many-branched, and strangely low, as though centuries of wind taught it to keep close to the earth. Its leaves are soft and warm to the touch. Nothing shines. Flowers open without color, as faint, velvety shapes among the foliage. They release a sweet, cellar-damp scent that comes and goes with the mist.

Animals are everywhere, though seldom all at once. Small, warm bodies pass through the undergrowth; clawed feet tick over stone, wings brush leaves, and something large moves patiently along the basin’s rim. The animals make the hollow loud. Their calls rise from different distances: a hollow bark, a chorus of clicking notes, a deep, resonant bellow that seems to come from under the ground. Sometimes the sounds overlap until the whole basin hums. Then, abruptly, they stop, and the silence feels less like peace than a held breath.

Nothing here hurries. A pool may take a season to overflow its shallow lip. A fallen branch can remain where it lands for decades, slowly softened by moss. Even the animals move with a measured certainty, as if every path has been walked before. The changes are real, but they are too patient to watch: leaves uncurl, stones settle, a new runnel appears where water has been worrying the slope for a hundred years.

Among the plants stand several structures. None is large. One is a narrow arch of dark material set into the hillside, with no door and no visible seam. Another is a squat tower no taller than a person, half sunk into moss. Its surface is smooth, but rain beads on it in tiny, independent rings, each moving toward a different point. There are also scattered metal pins in the stone, spaced too far apart to mark a fence and too deliberately placed to be debris.

The structures do not glow or make a sound. They seem to belong to the same old quiet as the rock. But the water bends around them without touching them, and the mist thins into clear, still-edged passages near their walls. Once in a while, one of the pins turns a fraction of a degree. No one sees it happen. The animals simply alter their routes, and after a few years the paths have shifted.

The rules of the Upper Hollow are not written anywhere. Water may climb a slope if it has a seam to follow. Sound travels farther than sight, but it does not always return from the direction it went. A creature that leaves the basin is not necessarily gone; it may appear again on the opposite rim after a while, unhurried and dry. The old structures seem to keep these arrangements in balance, though they give no sign of noticing the world around them.

In daylight, if there is daylight above the cloud, the basin does not brighten. The darkness is a condition of the place, not a shadow cast upon it. Still, a visitor can learn its geography: the central tree, the low stone shelves, the shallow water, the scattered constructions, the rim where the animals gather to call. By the time that geography feels familiar, it may have altered by less than a step.

And somewhere in the noise of wings, claws, water, and distant voices, the hollow continues to hold its small, damp silence.
