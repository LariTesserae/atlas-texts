The lowland begins where the ground stops pretending to be dry. It is not a swamp so much as a country with a great many damp places: shallow basins under the trees, black seepage between roots, rainwater held in the cupped leaves of plants, and narrow channels that appear after a storm and vanish again into the soil. The earth is warm and dark, packed with old leaf-matter. Here and there it breaks into pale shelves of clay, smooth from generations of brief floods. Nothing looks freshly made. The hollows are settled into their shapes; the roots have learned where the water lingers.

Vegetation fills nearly every opening. Broad leaves overlap low branches; vines pass from one trunk to the next without touching the ground. Ferns and reeds crowd the wetter pockets, while dense shrubs take over the slightly higher ground. There is no clean line between forest and clearing. Even the open places are busy with grass, thorny shoots, creepers, and young trees advancing slowly toward one another. A person standing there would have to look down to find a place to put a foot, and up to see any sky.

The heat is close and persistent. Moisture comes and goes: a rain that drums briefly on the canopy, a mist that gathers before dawn, a long dry interval in which the mud skins over but never quite loses its chill beneath the surface. The air smells of warm bark, mineral water, crushed greenery, and the faint sourness of things returning to the ground. Darkness is the land’s usual condition. Under the thick roof of leaves, even midday arrives as a weak greenish dusk; at night, the trees merge into a single depthless mass. The few open pools catch a little sky, but their reflections are quickly broken by insects touching down.

The place is full of animals. Most are not large, but they are present in numbers that make solitude difficult to believe in. Insects stipple the air. Small creatures move continuously through litter and roots; birds call from so close together that their notes overlap into a rough, intermittent murmur. Larger animals pass unseen through the brush, bending stems and leaving broad, wet impressions in the soil. Now and then something heavy exhales beyond sight, and the surrounding calls stop—not all at once, but in widening circles. Then they begin again. Nothing about this abundance feels uncanny. It is simply what happens when warmth, water, and shelter have gone on being available for a very long time.

For all that life, the landscape is mostly quiet. It has no constant roar, only layers of small sounds: droplets slipping from leaf to leaf long after the rain has ended; a bird making one low note at a time; the soft rasp of movement through grass. The silence between them is substantial. A distant mechanical drone sometimes enters it, so low it might at first be mistaken for weather. It comes from far beyond the dense green, where the flat horizon is lost in haze. On a clouded night there may be a dim, fixed glow there, pale as a coal under ash. No road, fence, or structure reaches this country. The industrial world is only a remote vibration, a reminder that somewhere beyond the wet darkness, people have made a different kind of landscape.

Here, change is slow. Channels shift by inches; trunks thicken; old fallen trees become soft ground for new growth. A storm can rearrange a patch of the forest, but the larger pattern scarcely alters. The land seems crowded because it is, yet it does not feel hurried. Every surface has been occupied for a long time, and every living thing has found a way to persist in the dark.
