The place is high enough that weather arrives already tired.

It lies on a broad shoulder of mountain, enclosed by low, rounded ridges worn smooth over an age too long for sharpness. Nothing here looks newly broken. The cliffs have softened into shelves; the boulders have slumped into the ground; even the ravines seem less cut than remembered. A thin skin of dark stone shows through the soil in long, level plates. In the hollows, cold mist gathers at dawn and stays until the sun has climbed well above the ridges.

The whole inhabited area could be crossed in an hour. It is a small settlement, though no one is likely to call it that now. Its buildings stand in loose rows along a set of ancient terraces, with great quiet spaces between them. Some are houses with doorways low enough to make entering feel like a bow. Others are long, roofless halls whose walls have settled into the earth. Steps descend to courtyards, then go on descending after the courtyard is gone. The oldest lanes are shallow channels in the stone, polished by feet, rain, and perhaps by something heavier moving through them long ago.

There is no metalwork, no glass, no visible machinery. The walls are built from slabs gathered nearby, stacked without mortar or fitted so closely that moss has had to search for a seam. Roofs are made of thin stone flags, or have caved in and become gardens. A few lintels bear shallow carvings—curves repeated until they resemble rings in wood, or the lines water leaves on silt. The markings are too worn to read, but the people who made them expected them to last. They have.

What grows here is low and surprisingly abundant. Pale grasses tuft between paving stones. Moss thickens in the shade, turning steps into soft green ramps. Ferns unfold under walls where condensation beads each morning. Here and there, dwarf shrubs cling to the warmer faces of the ruins, their branches bent flat by wind. The plants seem to have arranged themselves around the old construction rather than overrun it: a garden that has forgotten its gardener but retained the boundaries.

Water is scarce in the open and everywhere in small amounts. Rain comes intermittently, often as a brief rattling sweep across the roofs, then vanishes into cracks and shallow basins. Dew collects on stone faces that never see the sun. A few cisterns remain, open to the sky and nearly dry; one or two hold black water clear enough to reflect the clouds. At the settlement’s lowest edge, a narrow seep runs between two walls. It does not make a stream so much as a dark line of wetness, interrupted by pools. In winter it freezes into thin translucent skins, which crack loudly when the temperature turns.

The light is ordinary, even gentle. Clouds pass overhead at a sensible pace. The sun warms south-facing stones and leaves the rest cold. There are no perpetual dusk effects here, no glow from the soil; the strangeness is subtler. Sound behaves oddly among the ruins. A gust entering one empty hall can produce a tremendous hollow note, as if the whole settlement were a jar being blown across. Other winds make the doorways clap and the loose roof stones knock together. Sometimes a sound begins in one courtyard and seems to come back from another, a moment later and lower in pitch. The place is loud in bursts, then almost perfectly still.

There are few animals. Small birds cross the roofs singly, never flocking. Insects gather around the wet stones during warmer spells, then disappear. On rare mornings, tracks appear in the frost: narrow prints from something that moves between the terraces without seeming to use the lanes. Nothing has been seen clearly enough to give it a name. The plants show no sign of being eaten, though a few patches of grass are pressed down as if something has slept there.

The settlement is not empty in the absolute sense. Its emptiness has structure. One courtyard is kept clear of growth, though no path leads to it. In another, all the ferns lean toward a blank wall. A low door at the far end of a roofless passage is sealed by a stone too large to have been carried there, yet it sits without a crack around it. No one tends these things. No one has disturbed them in generations.

Weather and life continue to alter the place, but slowly, and without much ambition. Moss advances by the width of a finger. Rain loosens a slab; frost lifts it, then leaves it tilted. Each spring, a little more water finds the old channels, and each summer, the channels dry again. The terraces are not being reclaimed so much as incorporated. Stone, soil, and construction have reached a truce.

From the highest ridge, the settlement looks small enough to cover with one hand. Beyond it, the land folds away into pale mountain distances, old and nearly featureless beneath the sky. There is no road visible, no smoke, no movement except cloud and the occasional wing. The town remains arranged for arrivals: doors facing the lanes, steps leading down, empty courtyards open to the weather. But nothing in the surrounding country suggests that anyone is coming.
