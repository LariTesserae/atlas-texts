The hollow lies beneath a low hill, no larger than a small grove aboveground. Its roof presses close in places, then lifts into a single rounded chamber where warm air gathers. The way in is a split in the limestone, half hidden by hanging roots. Daylight reaches only a short distance through it, thinning to a greenish dusk. Farther inside, the dark is not complete: pale mineral walls hold a faint sheen, and patches of fungus show the color of old ivory. They do not glow so much as make the darkness easier to distinguish.

The floor has been shaped by water, though water is rarely here in quantity. It runs in fine threads after rain, follows grooves worn smooth over a long time, and collects in shallow basins before soaking away through cracks. Some basins stay damp all year; others dry to a skin of white mineral crust. The stone is warm underfoot. In the deeper pockets, heat rises from the earth and condenses on the roof, where droplets swell slowly on the tips of roots and fall with an occasional soft tick.

Vegetation fills nearly every place where it can take hold. Thick roots descend from the roof and spread across ledges. Small ferns, waxy-leaved creepers, and pale, jointed shoots grow in the half-light, their leaves turned toward the entrance or toward the damp shine of the walls. Moss softens the edges of stone steps and old cracks. Near the pools, the growth is crowded and dark green; farther back, it becomes sparse, colorless, and strangely delicate. There is no broad expanse of open ground. Every patch of earth is already occupied by roots, fronds, or the slow, velvety spread of something fungal.

Animals are abundant, though the place gives little warning of them. Small crabs work among the stones at the water’s edge. Blind, pale beetles move through leaf litter in dense, patient lines. Cave swallows nest near the entrance and dart through the chamber in brief, muffled passes; their wings make only a brush of air. A few larger, long-bodied lizards keep to the warm ledges, where they wait almost motionless for insects. The tiny sounds of this traffic—a scratch, a click, a wet shift in leaves—are easily mistaken for the settling of the cave.

The quiet is part of the hollow’s character. Nothing echoes far. Soft soil, hanging roots, and thick growth absorb most sounds. Rain above becomes a distant pressure rather than a patter. In still periods, one hears a drop falling into a pool, the slow flex of a root, or the faint rasp of an animal moving just beyond sight. The life here is busy, but it does not announce itself.

There are traces of human presence, old enough to have become part of the stone. A low wall divides one damp pocket from another, built from irregular blocks without mortar. Two or three broad steps descend to a basin, worn shallow by feet that no longer come. Near the entrance stands a squat pillar, its top softened by moisture and furred with moss. Nothing is fitted, sealed, or worked with precision. The structures seem less like ruins than small attempts to arrange the hollow—to guide water, keep a path clear, or make a place to sit out of the rain.

The landscape changes by degrees. After storms, the upper roots drip for days, the pools widen, and the animals draw nearer to the entrance where fresh food washes in. During hotter spells, water retreats into hidden seams. The leaves curl slightly; the cave’s warm breath dries the exposed stone, and the crabs crowd into the remaining wet hollows. Then the roof begins to sweat again. Water gathers, falls, and the whole sheltered ecology resumes its steady exchange.

Nothing here is vast or newly broken. The chambers have settled into their shape; the walls are rounded, the channels familiar, the living things arranged around reliable warmth and unreliable moisture. Yet the place is never still. It keeps moving at the scale of roots lengthening, pools shrinking, insects hatching, and rain finding its old way underground.
