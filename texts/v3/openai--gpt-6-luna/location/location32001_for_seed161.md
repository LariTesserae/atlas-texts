The place is called the Crown, though it is less a peak than the last broad thought of a mountain.

Everything around it falls away. From its rim, the land is not visible: only a dim, warm-colored haze, and beneath that the sheer, many-layered sides of the massif, dropping until they disappear. The Crown itself is small enough to cross in an afternoon. Its stone has been worn smooth over ages, not polished, but settled: ridges rounded down to long backs, hollows softened into shallow bowls, old fractures filled with grit and the pale roots of plants. The highest ground is almost level. It feels less like a summit than a remnant of one, a fragment left behind after time had done nearly all it could.

There is no stream, no pool, no glint of water anywhere. The stone is dry to the touch, and the warm air carries dust rather than mist. Yet the Crown is thick with life. Low trees crowd the hollows, their trunks squat and twisted, their leaves small, waxy, and layered so densely that from above they resemble a dark green canopy stitched with bronze. Between them grow ropes of tough grass, thorny shrubs, and cushions of pale flowers that open for only a few hours in the dimmest part of the day. Their roots run deep into seams in the rock. Some plants hold a little moisture in fleshy stems; others take what they need from the air at night, condensing it on fine hairs and drawing it in before dawn. Nothing here wastes a drop.

The light is always subdued. A veil of high dust dulls the sky, and the Crown’s own uneven ridges throw broad shadows across its interior. The brightest places are not sunlit slopes but patches of pale stone that seem to gather the light and give it back weakly. In shaded courtyards and beneath the trees, green turns almost black. The warmth remains, held close to the ground and in the rock; even at night the stones give off the day’s heat.

A settlement occupies the center of the plateau, built so closely into the terrain that it takes a while to see where town ends and hillside begins. Houses are made from flat slabs of weathered stone, fitted without mortar and roofed with branches, reed-like stalks, and pressed layers of bark. They stand in tiers around old outcrops, joined by narrow stairs and covered passages. Small terraces divide the steeper ground into patches for hardy crops. A few walls are much older than the houses they support, their blocks rounded at the edges and sunk into the earth. No one knows who laid them. The living have simply built on what was already there.

The people have few metal tools. They cut, scrape, grind, and lever, working stone and wood by hand. Their cisterns are mostly dry; the useful ones catch brief condensations from the night air, or store the faint moisture pressed from roots and harvested plants. Water is measured in careful tasks: enough for seed, for a sick animal, for a new child. The town does not grow outward. There is nowhere to go. Its paths fold back on themselves among gardens, sheds, sleeping ledges, and tiny squares where people gather in the shade.

The sound is astonishingly full. Insects rasp from every crack in the rock. Birds call from the trees and answer one another across the clustered roofs. Small burrowing animals scratch beneath the paving stones; larger, shy shapes move through the scrub beyond the outer houses, leaving the leaves trembling long after they have passed. At dusk, the colony roosts fill the air with wingbeats and chatter. Human voices, grinding stones, wooden hammers, and the sharp cries of animals mingle into a constant clamor, amplified by the enclosing ridges. Silence here is not the absence of sound but a brief pause in the chorus, when even the insects seem to draw breath.

The Crown changes slowly. A branch may take decades to thicken. A wall may stand for generations before one stone slips free. The wind carries grit over the plateau, but there is so little loose earth left to move that it mostly polishes what is already exposed. Each year resembles the one before it: warm, dry, dim, crowded with small lives. The old mountain continues to wear away, but on any human timescale it seems finished—an ancient height reduced to a quiet, inhabited table above the world.
