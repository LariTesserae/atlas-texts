The hollow begins as a cleft in the earth and widens until it is difficult to believe there is ground above it at all. It runs for miles beneath a low country of stone, a shallow, buried basin with long galleries opening into chambers the size of valleys. The ceiling is usually out of sight. Here and there it lowers into a dark vault of fractured rock; elsewhere it lifts away, leaving a dimness so broad it feels almost like weather.

The stone is pale and newly broken: gray limestone veined with black, ochre stains where water has passed, and abrupt faces still sharp enough to catch at cloth or skin. Slabs have slipped from the walls and lie in tilted rafts across the floor. Between them, gravel gathers in fans. The place has the look of something recently uncovered, though nothing here seems to be happening. The fractures are fresh in outline, the rubble not yet rounded, but the water has already found its accustomed routes and the stone sits quietly in them.

Moisture comes in fits. Most of the floor is dry, cold dust over hard rock. Then, without much warning, there is a stretch of slick mud, a runnel clear enough to show the grains beneath it, or a low basin holding a skin of water. Drips fall from the ceiling at widely separated points. They make small, emphatic sounds, each one followed by a long wait. Near the pools the air smells faintly mineral and clean; farther away it is dry, with a trace of animal musk.

There is little green. A few pale growths cling to wet seams: thin mats of moss, translucent threads, low fans of fungus pressed against the stone. They are not enough to soften the place. The largest expanses remain bare, silver-gray under the weak, groundless light. That light arrives through cracks high above—narrow leaks from the world’s surface, blurred by dust and distance. It brightens no more than a shallow patch of floor. Beyond those patches the hollow keeps its dimness, though the eye gradually learns the shapes of the walls and the faint sheen of water.

The animals are everywhere.

They occupy the hollows in immense colonies, but not as a single moving mass. Small cave crickets stipple the ledges and floor, their long antennae lifted like fine wires. Pale beetles gather under fallen slabs. Bats roost in the roofs of the larger chambers, crowded so thickly that certain ceilings seem furred; between the colonies, broad stretches of bare stone remain. Blind fish hold to the deepest pools. In the dampest galleries, salamanders lie among the gravel, still as fragments of the floor until water stirs around them. There are also larger animals: broad-backed, soft-eyed grazers that browse the sparse fungal growths, and long-limbed scavengers that move along the rubble slopes. They do not seem rare, but neither do they hurry. All are adapted to the same cool dark, and none appears surprised by the others.

Their presence makes the hollow loud. The sound is not a roar but a continuous accumulation: the dry ticking of insects, claws skittering over stone, the flutter and scrape of wings, the thin calls of bats returning from unseen passages. In some chambers, the noise fills the space so completely that a footfall disappears inside it. In others, sound carries for a very long way. A drop of water can be heard striking a pool across a gallery; a cry can travel down several bends and come back altered by the stone. There are pockets of near-silence, but even there, close at hand, something clicks or breathes.

Despite all this life, the place has little sense of change. The colonies shift within their familiar limits; the water follows the same shallow channels; the larger animals pass and return along established routes. There is no season here in the ordinary sense, only the faint difference between wetter weeks and drier ones. The air stays cool. Nothing grows quickly enough to transform a wall, and nothing erodes quickly enough to change the passage from one visit to the next. The hollow is not dead still, but settled—an equilibrium of countless small motions.

Its rule is simple: what can live without sun must live close to something else. Animals gather near water, shelter, or food, and leave the bare reaches between those necessities almost empty. The open galleries give them room to spread, but the stone decides where the crowds can thrive. From a distance, the life seems overwhelming; from within it, there are long solitary crossings over cold rock, with the sound of the colonies always somewhere ahead.
