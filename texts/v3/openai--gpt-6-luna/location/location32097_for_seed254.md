The Underbeds occupy a shallow hollow beneath an old district: not a cavern so much as a room that has been enlarged, repaired, and partitioned for generations. Its boundaries are close enough to feel—brick retaining walls, dark bedrock, the low vault of a ceiling—though the place is broad and crowded within them. There is no open ground. A person could cross the whole settlement in a few minutes, if the narrow ways between its workings were clear.

They are not clear. Green has taken the floors, walls, pipes, and roofs, and made them all part of one continuous growth. Thick mats of water-loving plants cover the ground like resilient flooring. Stems rise from cracks in the masonry and knit together into partitions; broad leaves overlap above passageways, making a second, soft ceiling beneath the stone. Roots bind old brickwork, descend into drains, and climb the legs of machinery. Here and there, whole buildings seem to have been absorbed into the growth, their windows plugged with packed foliage, their corners rounded by years of leaf and root.

The plants are ordinary in their needs, if extraordinary in their abundance. They grow toward the low lamps and toward the dampest seams, filling any gap left unclaimed. Their surfaces shine with condensation. Water is always near: seeping from the rock, sweating from the pipes, gathering in shallow channels that run beside the footways. The channels are narrow, but they never seem to empty. Their surfaces carry a film of fallen leaves and fine green debris toward covered drains, where the water disappears to be pumped back through the beds.

Warmth lingers under the roof. It comes partly from the earth and partly from the machinery: motors, boilers, and old heat exchangers set into service bays between cultivation blocks. The air is thick and mild, with the clean, heavy smell of wet stems and soil. Near the engines it tastes faintly metallic. Mist forms around cold pipes and hangs beneath the lamps before settling as another bead of water on the leaves.

The lamps are few, shaded, and practical. Their amber light barely reaches beyond the nearest surfaces. Most of the Underbeds are legible only by degrees: the pale undersides of leaves, a glimmer on standing water, the slow red blink of a gauge behind a curtain of vines. Darkness collects in the narrow spaces above the pipework. Nothing moves there. No birds nest in the rafters, no insects tick through the vegetation, no small bodies stir in the drains. The ecology has no animal life to it. The plants, the water, and the carefully maintained cycles of heat and nutrients are enough to fill the place.

It is also unmistakably built. Beneath the greenery, the settlement’s plan remains orderly. Service corridors run straight where the ground permits; iron walkways bridge the wetter beds; old trolley rails thread between squat processing sheds. Numbered valves are fixed to brick pillars. Pipes carry warm water into the beds and return it through filters. Heavy presses compact cut foliage into dense blocks, while conveyors move them toward storage rooms. The machinery is industrial, but not hurried. Pumps turn with a steady, burdened rhythm. A press closes once, releases, and waits while its load settles. Ventilation fans draw air through the chambers in long, regular breaths.

The sound is immense for so small a place. It gathers in the low ceiling and becomes a constant roar of motors and moving water, broken by the hollow clank of a turning wheel or the deep knock of a press completing its cycle. Beneath that is the soft, nearly inaudible work of the plants: leaves shifting under their own weight, water traveling through stems and channels, growth pushing slowly against its restraints. The Underbeds are loud, but nothing here is sudden. Even the machinery seems to keep time on a scale of hours and seasons.

Change comes by accumulation. A walkway narrows over years as roots thicken beneath it. A pipe is replaced, then gradually covered again. One cultivation wall is cut back and harvested; its cleared stones remain visible for a few weeks before new growth reaches across them. The pumps keep the water level almost exactly where it has always been. The lamps burn low. The old buildings hold their shape beneath their living coats.

Nothing in the Underbeds feels wild in the sense of being uncontrolled. Nor does it feel fully domestic. It is a settled, damp infrastructure in which plant and construction have reached an uneasy but durable agreement: walls support stems, stems protect pipes, pipes feed beds, and the beds slowly return themselves to the machines. The whole district seems to be waiting—not for an event, but for the next ordinary cycle of water, light, cutting, and regrowth.
