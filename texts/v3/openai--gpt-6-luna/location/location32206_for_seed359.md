The place is called the Close, though no one agrees what it is closed against.

It occupies about the space of a small courtyard. A wall surrounds it on every side, shoulder-high in some places, towering in others—not because the wall rises, but because the ground beneath it has remembered different heights. The entrance is a low arch of hand-shaped clay blocks. Beyond it, the Close is packed so densely with paths, beds, pillars, trellises, and little roofed alcoves that there is nowhere to stand without being among something.

Nothing here is natural in the sense of being unmade. The ground is a fitted skin of fired tiles, broken pots, pressed ash, and pale brick. The walls have been built up in courses, each irregular brick bearing the thumbprint or palm-mark of whoever formed it. There is no soil. Instead, narrow troughs of porous clay run between the paving, and the plants root into them: roots fine as hair, roots braided like cord, roots that lie on the surface and hold fast to the warm ceramic. The edges of the tiles are fresh and sharp. The Close feels recently assembled, though its makers are absent and its bricks have no dust on them.

It is lush. Leaves overlap leaves until the alleys beneath them turn green and dim. Some plants resemble familiar things—broad, waxy fronds; delicate stems with clustered blossoms—but the resemblances never hold for long. A vine will climb a trellis by looping around the empty spaces between its poles. A low shrub has leaves on both sides, and whichever side faces you is always the underside. White trumpet flowers open toward the walls. Their interiors are patterned with tiny brick arches. Near the center stands a tree no higher than a person, its trunk formed of pale, jointed lengths like a bundle of pipes. Its canopy is crowded with dark leaves that shine softly, though they are dry to the touch.

There is no standing water, no wet ground, no fountain, no sound of dripping. The warm air carries the smell of sun-heated clay, green bitterness, and something like crushed seed. Moisture seems to have been replaced here by a slow exchange of salts and light. The plants draw nourishment from the mineral seams in the paving; their sap is clear and faintly gritty, and dries almost as soon as it leaves a cut. The gardeners—if there are gardeners—have left shallow bowls of powder beneath the thickest growth. They may be food. They may be ashes. Sometimes, by morning, the powder has been combed into neat little furrows.

The inhabitants are everywhere but seldom announce themselves. Beetles, pale moths, many-legged things the size of fingernails move in the joints of the walls. A bright-backed lizard waits upside down beneath a lintel, its toes gripping the air just short of the brick. Tiny birds—or creatures that have borrowed the shape of birds—thread through the foliage without stirring it. Their wings make no sound. In the stillest corners, small round animals with soft faces sleep in the mouths of unused jars. None flee from a visitor. None quite appear to look at one, either.

Sound is scarce. A footstep makes a dry click, followed by a second click from somewhere in the wall. The leaves do not rustle, though they move continually by fractions: a tilt, a settling, a slight turn toward a warmth that may not be the sun. From time to time comes a delicate ticking, like a seed tapping against a pot. The ticking might come from the roof, or from under the paving, or from the listener’s own teeth. It stops if noticed directly.

The light is ordinary and warm, entering from above, but the sky cannot be seen. Looking up reveals a close-woven ceiling of green and clay lattice, through which sunlight falls in soft squares. The squares do not always agree with the openings. A patch of light can lie across a solid wall; a shadow can fall uphill. At midday, a person’s shadow may detach by a finger’s width and continue along the path after they have stopped. It never gets far. By evening it returns to the feet, though the Close has no clear evening and no visible horizon.

The paths are narrow, paved, and mostly level. They turn at angles too tight for carts, though there are no carts here. One leads to a doorway with no room behind it. Another descends three steps and arrives at the same spot from below. The walls are covered in small niches, each holding a different piece of the Close: a folded leaf, a stone shaped like a tooth, a warm brick, an empty cup. Some objects recur. If an object is removed, its niche is not empty for long; it holds the object’s shadow, which has the same weight when lifted.

The rules of the place are gentle until tested. A person can walk from one end to the other in a few minutes, but cannot say which end was which. Counting the arches produces a different number each time. The tree has a far side only when no one is looking at it. The plants do not grow quickly, yet the garden is never quite the same from one visit to the next: a new shoot in an old crack, an arch where yesterday there was a shelf, a bed of blossoms occupying the exact footprint of a visitor who stayed too long.

Nothing appears to be decaying. Nothing appears finished, either. The Close is a handmade enclosure still being assembled by patient, invisible hands—or perhaps by the roots, by the light, by the small creatures that sleep in the jars. In the warmth, among dry leaves and carefully laid brick, the whole compact world continues its quiet work: fitting, rooting, repeating, making room where there was none.
