The basin is so low that the horizon seems to have been rubbed down. It runs outward in a shallow, dim sweep of black mineral flats and thin water, with no trees, no grass, no stalks—nothing reaching up. The land is old beyond the feeling of ruins. It has been worn nearly smooth by ages of cold water, though here and there the last hard ridges remain: blunt ribs of dark stone, glassy seams, shelves scored with grooves too regular to be called cracks and too ancient to look made.

Water lies everywhere without quite gathering into lakes. It films the flats, pools in shallow basins, beads on stone, seeps from the ground in clear curtains. The air is full of fine suspended wetness. Droplets gather on surfaces that seem to have no surface at all, hanging briefly in the dark before falling upward, or sideways, or vanishing into the water below. The cold does not sparkle. It dulls. It turns the shallow floods to a skin of cloudy ice, then breaks that skin from underneath with slow, heavy pulses.

The place is crowded, despite its openness. From a distance the basin looks empty; close in, every patch of water is occupied. Dense schools of pale, jointed animals slide beneath the film. Some are no larger than a hand, some long as barges, their bodies armored in mineral plates worn smooth by the current. They have no visible eyes, but they turn together when the air changes. Smaller things cluster on the ridges like wet beads, clicking as they feed on films of metal and salt. Larger animals drag themselves across the flats, leaving furrows that fill almost at once. Their outlines are difficult to keep in mind: a row of legs becomes a wheel of limbs, a folded shell opens into a dark, pulsing throat, a low animal passes under the water and seems to leave a second, fainter version of itself behind.

There is no vegetation, and nothing here appears to be waiting to become vegetation. Instead, life has found the seams between water and stone. It clings, crawls, rings, mills, and burrows. Certain colonies spread in dense metallic crusts, but they are not rooted; they turn slowly against the prevailing flow, rasping the rock with fine, repeated movements. Their surfaces are slick and ribbed, their colors the subdued shades of old machinery: lead, tarnished silver, the green-black of deep water. When disturbed, they release swarms of tiny swimmers that immediately organize themselves into whirling bands.

The basin’s industrial character is unmistakable, though nothing has been built. There are no towers, roads, or manufactured objects. The machinery is alive, or the landscape has become so accustomed to life that it performs machinery without it. Broad animal migrations pass through the shallows in synchronized ranks, pumping water and slurry through channels worn into the plain. Along certain ridges, colonies of shell-backed creatures grind stone into fine sediment with a steady, rasping rhythm. Farther out, enormous submerged bodies move beneath the flats, turning the flooded ground into a slow procession of pistons and bellows. Their motion drives the tides. Their exhalations send low clouds of vapor rolling across the basin.

The sounds are loud and layered. Water knocks against stone. Plates chatter and scrape. The smaller animals make a constant bright clicking, like rain striking a sheet of metal. Underneath it all is a deeper thrum—irregular, immense, felt through the soles before it is heard. At intervals, a whole region of the basin begins to vibrate. The sound rises into a grinding roar, then passes away toward the horizon, though nothing visible seems to have moved. In its wake, the water flows in a new direction.

The rules here are local and unreliable. A ripple may arrive before whatever caused it. Footprints fill with water from above. Sounds can cross the plain faster than the wind, then return from behind you in a different voice. The far shore sometimes appears close enough to touch; at other times, a ridge that should be nearby recedes until it is almost indistinguishable from cloud. The animals seem untroubled by these failures of distance and sequence. They navigate by pressures, temperatures, or some sense the landscape has not given to human bodies. When the basin’s immense cycles change, they alter course together, leaving whole stretches of water briefly vacant—then crowded again with different creatures, as if the old population had passed through a door.

The light is always insufficient. It comes through a ceiling of low cloud and wet haze, spreading without direction. Dark ridges shine faintly. The flats hold a weak reflection of the sky, but sometimes the reflection shows a different weather: a brighter cloud bank, a black vertical line, the pale underside of something vast moving overhead. Looking up reveals only the same unbroken dimness.

Nothing is young here. Even the animals carry the worn, settled look of things that have endured too many cycles to distinguish change from habit. Yet the basin is never still. Water moves. Bodies feed, migrate, molt, grind, and sink. The ancient plain continues to process itself—stone into silt, silt into water, water through the multitude—making a sound like a factory heard from beneath the earth.
