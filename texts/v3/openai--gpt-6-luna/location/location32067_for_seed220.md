The district lies beneath the oldest part of the city, though “beneath” is not quite right. It occupies a pocket in the deep stone where the ground has been hollowed, settled, and hollowed again over many centuries. The surface world is absent here except for the occasional tremor of a passing train, felt through the ceiling as a slow pressure moving from one side of the dark to the other.

It is not a large place. A person could walk from its outer retaining wall to the farthest inhabited chamber in less than an hour. Yet the streets have the peculiar emptiness of something built to hold many more people than it ever does. Long avenues run between blunt, windowless buildings; bridges cross shafts whose bottoms cannot be seen; stairways descend to platforms where no trains arrive. The spaces between structures are broad and bare. Even the busiest square might contain only a maintenance crew, a few lights, and the steady noise of the city’s machinery.

The stone here is dark ochre, almost brown, with bands of mineral that catch no light until water passes over them. The earliest walls are rough and swollen where the rock pushed back against the cutting tools. Later construction has been fitted into them with impossible neatness: ribbed pylons, suspended conduits, doors that seem to have been poured into their frames. Nothing is shiny. Metals have gone matte with age, their surfaces crossed by fine marks like handwriting. Where the buildings meet the cavern, their corners have been rounded by slow abrasion. The place has had time to become itself.

Warm air sits low in the passages. It smells of wet dust, hot stone, and a faint mineral sweetness, like rain coming through a clay roof. Water is never quite present in quantity. It gathers in shallow basins under the pipes, beads on the ceiling, and sometimes falls in irregular showers from a seam that was dry the day before. In the larger streets, the paving is dry and powdery; in narrow service corridors, the walls sweat. A channel runs through the district, no wider than a person’s shoulders, carrying a dark trickle between low curbs. It vanishes beneath one building and emerges, warm and singing, on the other side.

There is almost no plant life. A few pale threads of moss grow around a leaking valve. In one courtyard, a shallow trough holds a cluster of small, translucent leaves that open only when the pumps change rhythm. Their roots grip the concrete without soil. Elsewhere, a fine green film appears and disappears on damp walls, too thin to make the stone look alive, though it lends it a bruised color. The rare animals are small and difficult to distinguish from the infrastructure: blind, soft-bodied things that shelter in drains; a scatter of pale legs retreating beneath a grate; once, perhaps, the click of something larger moving inside the wall.

The light is scarce and mostly indirect. A few red indicator lamps burn in recessed panels. Blue-white lines move slowly beneath certain floors, not illuminating so much as outlining the edges of things. In the main concourse, a ceiling high above the street occasionally brightens with a diffuse amber glow, as if a sun has passed behind thick cloud. It lasts only a few minutes. During those intervals the empty buildings cast long shadows, and every surface seems old enough to remember being uncovered.

The loudest sound is the infrastructure. Deep pumps beat through the foundations. Somewhere overhead, a series of gates opens and closes with the slow, immense clang of a harbor. Ventilation ducts carry distant voices, though no one can tell whether they are current or merely trapped in the system. Water ticks into metal trays. The city is full of small impacts: relays shifting, pressure chambers knocking, cables tightening. When all of it falls briefly quiet, the silence is not peaceful. It feels like a held breath—and the next change in the machinery arrives with startling force.

The district is still active. Its systems adapt to conditions no resident can see. Corridors warm when the rock cools; doors unlatch before anyone reaches them; the trickle in the central channel thickens and thins according to pressures far beyond the district’s boundaries. Some of the buried devices do not appear to operate by turning, pumping, or carrying energy. They seem to persuade the surrounding stone into different arrangements. A wall may be dry for decades, then open along a perfect seam and release a burst of warm mist. An empty lift may rise through its shaft, stop at a floor that no longer exists, and return bearing a skin of condensation.

People say the place was once a neighborhood. Others say it was built to be a neighborhood and never quite got around to needing one. There are doors with brass handles worn smooth by hands, though the rooms behind them are sealed. There are civic markings on the pillars—numbered blocks, distance symbols, faded street names—but the routes they describe do not always agree with one another. Some passages loop back through the same square after several kilometers of measured walking. The district does not shift dramatically. It has no appetite for spectacle. It simply maintains a private geometry, adjusted by weather, pressure, and time.

At the far edge, the paved streets stop. The old stone continues into a low, irregular chamber where the ceiling leans close enough to touch. Here the machinery is quieter, and the air is damp. Water drips into a basin worn smooth as a thumbprint. In its surface, the few lights of the underground district appear as a tiny, trembling skyline. Then a pump engages somewhere behind the walls, the whole basin shivers, and the reflected city breaks into dark pieces.
