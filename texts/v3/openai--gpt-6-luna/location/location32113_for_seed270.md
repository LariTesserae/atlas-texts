At first it seems like a seam in the ground: a dark line between two shelves of stone, low enough that a hand could cover it. The shelves are not quite level. They have been worn toward level for so long that their irregularities have become shallow, polished swells, like the bottom of an old bowl. A thread of ordinary gray daylight slips into the seam and loses strength after a few feet. Beyond it, the landscape keeps going—not far, but farther than the opening suggests—into a pocket of close, cool air.

The place is dry in the absolute sense. No trickle, no damp shine, no hidden pool. The stone is pale mineral gray, banded with old rust-red and the dull white of salt. Fine dust lies in the hollows, though the broad surfaces have been swept bare by ages of movement. Nothing here grows upward. There are no stems or fronds, no soft green thing to break the colors of stone.

Instead, the ground moves.

At first the movement looks like a vibration in the dust. Then the dust separates into bodies: small, many-jointed animals, pale-backed and dark underneath, packed shoulder to shoulder in the cracks. Some are scarcely larger than grains of seed; others are long as a thumb, low and armored, their feelers combing the stone. They flow over one another without quite climbing, parting around the deeper grooves and closing behind themselves. Their shells carry the same chalk, iron, and salt as the terrain. From a distance, whole patches seem merely to change texture.

The pocket is dense with them. They fill fissures, line the roof, crowd the undersides of stone ledges. A few larger forms push through the traffic: broad, blind grazers with rasping mouths, followed at a measured distance by narrow predators whose bodies fold and unfold like metal tape. When one predator strikes, a brief whirl opens in the crowd. The sound rises sharply—hard little taps, scrapes, dry rattles—then is swallowed by the constant clatter of everything else. There are also small, high clicks, repeated in pulses. The calls ricochet around the chamber until it seems that the rock itself is ticking.

A thin mineral skin coats some of the stone, almost invisible except where the light catches it. It is not vegetation, and has no leaf or root; it is a chemical crust, fed by the ancient contact of different minerals. The smallest animals scrape it away. Larger ones eat them. The hunters take the larger ones, and the remains are gathered by tiny scavengers that can pass through cracks scarcely wider than a hair. Whatever sustains the whole crowded system is slow and meager. Nothing is wasted, and nothing appears to have room to die alone.

The chamber’s old age is written everywhere. Its edges have been rounded nearly out of existence. The roof has settled into a low arch, and the floor slopes by no more than the height of a knuckle toward a shallow basin of powdered stone. Here, in the widest part of the pocket, a few human-made objects have been trapped and softened by time: a blackened strip of iron, a gear with several teeth worn blunt, the broken mouth of a pipe. They are scattered, not arranged. No workshop survives around them, only these remnants, too embedded in the stone to be carried away. The animals have taken possession of their hollows. They stream through the gear’s central opening and nest in the pipe’s dry throat.

Now and then the iron gives a faint tick as the cool air shifts. The click draws a ripple through the crowd. Millions of small feet answer it, not all at once but in successive waves, as if the whole pocket is passing a signal from wall to wall. The animals move between feeding grounds, hideaways, and the narrow daylight entrance in patterns too intricate to follow. Near the opening, they become fewer and larger; deeper in, they are nearly continuous, a living texture over the rock.

Nothing here is grand enough to be called a valley or a plain. Yet there is a ridge where the stone rises, a basin, cliffs only a few inches high, and long corridors cut by time and traffic. The sky is a pale slit. The weather is a faint breathing of cool air through the entrance, enough to shift dust and stir the animals’ antennae. Outside, the world may be broad. Inside this hidden fold, landscape and population have become almost the same thing: ancient stone, scattered iron, and a dense, restless multitude making a loud life in the last small hollows.
