The high basin is made all the way down.

There is no soil beneath it, no buried bedrock waiting to be uncovered. The ridges, valley floor, and steep shoulders are packed from pale fired earth and mineral mortar, shaped into a continuous, crowded construction. From a distance, the place might be mistaken for a range of bare hills. Closer, every slope resolves into courses: curved retaining walls, stair-steps, plugged apertures, broad roofs laid one upon another. The land has been built so densely that no space remains between its parts. A roof becomes a terrace; a terrace becomes the wall of a cistern; the cistern rests on a slope that is also a roof.

The forms are young-looking, though their age is hard to guess. Their edges remain clean and sharp. Corners have not yet softened, and the pale surfaces show little scouring. Here and there, a fresh break exposes the interior: tightly tamped layers, darker seams of grit, a few rounded stones set into the mixture like ballast. The oldest-looking surfaces are only faintly dulled. Nothing has had much time to wear away.

Water is everywhere, but never in a natural pool. It beads on the sloping faces and gathers in channels too narrow to be called streams. It threads through gutters, vanishes into square-mouthed drains, then reappears lower down as a line of seepage from a wall. After rain, the entire basin glistens. Even in dry spells, moisture hangs in the joints and makes dark hairlines across the pale construction. The roofs sweat. Steps weep. At the bottom of every hollow, unseen water continues its slow passage through the built mass.

The channels are arranged with an exactness that can be felt before it can be understood. They carry water from the high outer ridges toward the interior, then divide it again and again, until each terrace receives a little and gives most of it onward. No channel runs straight for long. They bend around cisterns, descend under bridges, split at stone lips, and enter slots no wider than a hand. The system seems designed not to drain the basin, but to keep it damp everywhere without allowing any one place to become a lake.

The air is cold and clean, with a mineral taste. Brightness lies across the whole landscape: a white, high light, strengthened by reflection from wet ceramic and pale mortar. Shadows are crisp beneath projecting eaves, but the open slopes remain washed in silver. Farther ridges show through the clear air, stacked in descending bands of terraces and parapets, until their details become indistinguishable from the clouds. There may be a horizon beyond them, but the basin offers no view of it.

Nothing grows. There are no roots in the seams, no green film on the damp walls, no scattered moss where water lingers. No animal calls from the roofs or hides in the drains. The few dark marks on the surfaces are mineral stains, soot, or old water. The place has the silence of an object too large to be held in one’s hands: not empty, exactly, but occupied everywhere by its own structure.

The sounds are small. Water ticking into a channel. A thin, steady trickle behind a wall. The hollow note of a drop falling into a deep cistern. Wind passes over the upper parapets with a soft brushing noise, then slips away. There is no roar of open water, no rustle of leaves, no wingbeat. Now and then a long, low creak travels through the construction as a wet wall shifts against another. It is quiet enough that these sounds seem to belong to the whole basin rather than to any single place.

The workmanship is primitive in its means, not in its ambition. The surfaces carry the marks of hands, paddles, and simple molds: shallow thumb hollows, uneven joins, repeated little ridges where wet material was pressed into place. There are no great metal spans or delicate mechanisms. Instead, the makers relied on weight, slope, and repetition. Every wall supports another wall. Every opening has a lip to catch water or a sill to slow it. The architecture does not stand apart from the terrain; it is the terrain.

At this height, cloud sometimes settles into the lower channels and drifts among the terraces. The mist makes the basin seem to breathe, though nothing living stirs. It gathers first in the deep cuts, fills them to their edges, then thins away in the bright air. Water advances and withdraws by the smallest degrees. A blocked gutter may darken a whole slope over the course of a season. A new seep may slowly mark a pale wall with a vertical thread. Beyond such changes, the landscape keeps its shape.

The place seems governed by one rule: nothing is allowed to fall freely. Water must be caught, divided, guided, and caught again. Even the slopes are not slopes for long; they become steps, basins, channels, ledges. The whole highland is a vast, close-packed apparatus for holding dampness in the open air. It does not appear to be waiting for life to return. It appears complete without life, still performing its patient work beneath a bright, cool sky.
