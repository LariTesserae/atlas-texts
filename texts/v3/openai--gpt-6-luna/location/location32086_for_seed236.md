The plateau is small enough to know by its edges. It rises abruptly from a plain of pale mineral dust, a dark, freshly lifted shelf of basalt and glass no wider than a village’s walk. Its rim is still sharp. Here and there the stone has split into thin black blades, and the youngest cracks show bright interiors—copper-colored, greenish, or the white of old bone. Nothing has had time to soften them.

There is no water on the plateau. No pool hides in the hollows, no stream threads the settlement, no dark soil gives way underfoot. The air is warm and thin, with a faint mineral taste; the light is ordinary and steady. In the shallow places between rocks, life has made a different kind of abundance. Plants crowd the ground in tight cushions and climbing mats, their leaves waxy or translucent, their colors ranging from deep bottle green to bruise-purple and rust. Some grow in rigid fans, like folded metal. Others make soft, fernlike fronds that tremble only when a visitor brushes them. Their stems hold thick amber resins instead of anything that sloshes. The plants take what they need from the air and the stone, slowly, with the patience of deposits forming.

The settlement is built among them rather than cleared around them. Low houses of fitted volcanic slabs lean into the slope, their roofs layered with broad leaves and pale mineral tiles. Narrow passageways run between walls and root-masses, widening unexpectedly into courtyards no larger than rooms. Steps have been cut into the young rock, but the cuts are still crisp; mosslike growths, brittle and dry to the touch, have begun to trace their edges. The dwellings seem old only because they are close together. Their stones are clean-faced and unweathered, their corners still sharp.

Nothing here is mechanical. The inhabitants have shaped the plateau with hands, patient heat, and repeated use: stones stacked into windbreaks, thorny branches trained into gates, shelves coaxed from living trunks. Paths are marked by smoothness rather than signs. Where many feet pass, the black ground shines faintly; where no one goes, the plants reclaim the narrow space without hurry. There are no wheels, lamps, or metal fittings. At dusk, if it can be called dusk, the walls and leaves simply turn a deeper color.

The creatures are everywhere, though few announce themselves. Small six-legged animals press flat beneath the broad leaves. Pale, folded things cling to the undersides of ledges. Long-beaked grazers stand motionless in the lanes, picking resinous seeds from the plants one at a time. The largest animals are no taller than a person’s waist and move as though each step must be considered. Their eyes catch the light, but they make almost no sound. Even the winged creatures pass without a rush of air, gliding from roof to roof on membranes thin as leaf skin.

The silence is not empty. It has fine grains: the dry tick of a seedcase opening, a soft scrape beneath a stone, the faint brush of fronds against one another. Voices, when they occur, are low and brief. Sound seems to be absorbed by the thick vegetation and the close-built walls before it can travel far. Across the whole plateau, a call might reach only the next courtyard.

The place changes, but on a scale difficult to watch. A house wall settles by a finger’s width over many seasons. New green spreads along a crack; a crack opens farther beneath it. The settlement’s paths shift around the slow growth of its plants, and the plants grow into the shapes left by the paths. At the rim, where the plateau meets open air, the young rock keeps shedding small sharp fragments. They gather below in a narrow apron of dark scree, while above them the little city stays crowded, warm, and almost still.
