The place fits inside a low ring of embankments: a few streets, a basin, the backs of several factories, and the wet ground between them. Beyond the levees the land lies almost level with the water. Beyond that, little can be made out through the dim haze.

It is hot here, even under the roofs. Heat gathers in the brickwork and steel, and the air holds the smell of wet iron, tar, warm mud, and algae. Water is everywhere without quite becoming a flood: it beads on pipes, seeps through joints in the embankment, and stands in shallow channels along the streets. The basin is the color of tea. Its surface is usually still, except where a drain pours into it or a fish breaks through.

The ground looks newly made. Fresh-cut banks have crisp edges, and the mud between paving stones has not yet weathered into a uniform gray. There are raw faces of concrete where repairs have been added, pale against older, darkened walls. Here and there, ballast, brick fragments, and broken shells show through the thin soil. Nothing rises very far above the roofs. The whole district seems settled onto the lowland rather than grown from it.

Buildings are scattered rather than crowded, but their arrangement is deliberate. A pump house stands beside the basin; warehouses face a short service road; narrow pipes and covered conduits connect them in straight, practical runs. The factories are modest by the scale of cities—low sheds, stacks, tanks, a few gantries—but their machinery gives the place a feeling of constant occupation. Motors thrum behind louvers. A belt ticks over in a loading bay. Somewhere, a pump keeps up its blunt, patient knock.

The light is weak and brownish. It reaches the streets indirectly, filtered through vapor, awnings, and the high sides of buildings. Under the sheds it is almost evening at every hour. Lamps shine on damp floors and in the windows of control rooms, but their light seems to stop at the walls. On the open water, a thin brightness gathers where the sky shows through.

Vegetation is scarce. A few tough reeds grow along the basin’s rim; moss darkens the shaded concrete, and small weeds occupy the seams of the oldest paving. There are no generous gardens, only patches where life has found a neglected corner. The absence of green makes the animals more noticeable. Gulls crowd the roofline and quarrel over the water. Swallows stitch low arcs through the humid air. Frogs call from drainage culverts; small fish flick beneath the outfalls; rats move confidently along the retaining walls. Insects cloud the lamps at dusk and vanish into the warm dampness before morning. Their abundance feels ordinary here, as much a part of the infrastructure as the pipes.

The sound is loud but not dramatic. It is made of layers: machinery, water entering grates, gulls, metal doors rolling shut, voices briefly raised to be heard and then swallowed by the surrounding hum. At the far side of the basin, a chain knocks against a mooring post at regular intervals. The noise does not build toward anything. It simply occupies the space.

For all the pumps and passing workers, the place seems remarkably fixed. Water comes in, water is lifted out, and the same narrow paths remain wet. The machinery is maintained, the embankments hold, and the basin keeps its measure. Even the animals seem to know their routes. There is change in the smallest motions—the ripple from a drain, the turn of a bird, a drop gathering on the underside of a pipe—but the landscape itself does not appear to be going anywhere. It has found a balance between land and water, work and neglect, and holds it in the heat.
