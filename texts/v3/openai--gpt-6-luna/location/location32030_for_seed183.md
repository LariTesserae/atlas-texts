The high country is called the Choir of Steps, though nothing here sings on purpose.

It occupies a broad shelf near the top of a mountain range, where the air is thin and cold enough to make every breath feel freshly made. The shelf is not level. It rises in immense pale terraces, one above another, cut from a stone so light it seems almost luminous: chalk-gray in shadow, white as salt in the sun. Between the terraces, cliffs drop into blue distances. The farthest ridges stand so close together that they resemble a wall, but the wall shifts when you look away and becomes a valley again.

Water is everywhere, but rarely where it ought to be. It beads on the undersides of overhangs. It gathers in shallow basins whose floors are dry and whose rims spill steadily. Narrow streams cross the stone, then climb a few inches up a slope before remembering to descend. After rain, which arrives often and without clouds, the whole landscape shines. Water runs down the faces of cliffs in fine bright threads, vanishes into the rock, and emerges from the stone above, as if the mountain were passing it through itself.

The terraces are crowded with mineral forms. Ribs of flint lean against one another; shelves of translucent calcite overlap like plates; knobs and hollow chimneys press close around the paths. There is little bare ground. Yet there is no vegetation—not a stem, not a lichen, not even the stain of a root. The stone’s colors come from mineral seams: rust-red lines, smoky violet patches, greenish bands that are only green in reflection. No animal crosses the open ledges. No insect turns in the light. The only movement is water, weather, and the occasional settling of stone.

The sun is almost too bright. It casts hard shadows beneath every ledge, but the shadows do not always fall in the same direction as their objects. Sometimes they point uphill. Sometimes they arrive a little after the thing that made them, sliding into place with the patience of a door closing. Looking directly across the terraces is easy; looking at them obliquely is not. Distances shorten and stretch. A tower of stone that seemed an hour away may stand just beyond the next pool, while a nearby stair of natural shelves retreats toward the horizon each time you approach it.

The noise is constant. Water chatters, hisses, and claps against the stone. Wind pushes through the mineral chimneys and produces a low, crowded resonance, as if the plateau were full of enormous empty vessels. At intervals, a deep note passes through the ground. It is felt in the teeth before it is heard, and afterward every small sound—drips, footsteps, the click of a loose pebble—seems briefly to belong to it. There are no voices here, but the place can be difficult to hear yourself think in.

Human works appear only now and then. A little bridge of fitted slabs crosses a cleft where the water runs upward. A shelter stands on a high shelf, its walls made from hand-set stone and its roof weighted with rounded blocks. Neither structure is ruined, though both look very old. Their makers used simple tools and left no decoration except shallow grooves beside the doorways. In those grooves, water collects without spilling. The bridges have no rails. The shelters have no windows facing the sun.

The paths are marked by waist-high cairns, each assembled from stones that should not balance but do. Travelers say the cairns are useful because they remain visible in fog. This is not quite true: they remain visible in clear weather too, but in fog they appear closer than they are, and sometimes there are more of them. The cairns are not dangerous. They are simply not reliable witnesses.

There are rules to the Choir, though they are rules of habit rather than law. Water will run uphill only on bare stone. A shadow can be crossed, but it is better to cross it at its narrowest point. A sound repeated twice will usually return; a sound repeated three times may return from a place that has not yet been visited. The terraces hold their shape over long spans of time. Their cliffs are rounded and settled, worn by weather into a stable complexity. Still, a person may look back and find a ledge has acquired an extra step, or a basin has moved a few paces without disturbing the water inside it.

Nothing grows here, and nothing hunts. The landscape has no ecology in the familiar sense, only exchanges: stone gives water passage; water polishes stone; air fills the hollows; sunlight exposes the seams. The bright, wet terraces remain in motion without appearing alive. Their changes are slow enough to be ancient and quick enough to notice.

At dusk, the light does not dim so much as thin. The high ridges keep their white blaze, while the lower clefts turn a clear, depthless blue. Water continues to cross the terraces, softly booming where it falls and softly booming where it rises. From far away, the whole shelf seems to shimmer—not with heat, but with a slight disagreement about where it is. Then the first stars appear below the horizon, reflected in the cliff faces, and for a little while the mountain seems to stand suspended between two skies.
