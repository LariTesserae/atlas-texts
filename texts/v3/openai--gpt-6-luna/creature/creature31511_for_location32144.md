Nothing here lives in one place for long.

The smallest lives are first to answer the current. Plankton thickens in the lee of a ridge; minute animals rise from the sand; the water clouds with feeding bodies. Shoals arrive after them, silvering the cliff faces, turning as one whenever the flow shifts. In their wake come the hunters: long-jawed fish that hold station in the passes, and broad, dark shapes that rise from the deeper valleys without disturbing the shoals until the last moment. When the feast has moved on, crabs pick through what remains. Eels take the cracks. The forest closes around them, fronds streaming back into place.

There are larger travelers, too—pale, wide-bodied animals that cross the mountain range on the deep currents. From a distance they resemble pieces of the seafloor set adrift: ridged backs, long fins flexing slowly, small growths clinging to their skin. They do not fight the water. They let it carry them through the passes, steering with a faint tilt of the body. At certain saddles they gather, dozens at a time, their low calls sounding through the rock. The mountains return those calls altered. A note begun in one valley may arrive in another as a tremor through the stone.

The smaller dwellers know the old machinery better than they know the open slopes. In the rusted towers, translucent shrimp hang in the sheltered interiors, and pale crabs stack shells in the corners. Eels sleep in cable conduits. Anemones open and close on the slow-turning wheels, whose motion stirs the water just enough to feed them. When a drum gives a deep, uneven knock, the animals pause. Some vanish into their hiding places. Others answer with clicks, as if the structure were a reef with its own strange voice.

Among the cracks and ledges live the most watchful inhabitants: soft-bodied, many-armed creatures that can flatten themselves against stone or pour through an opening scarcely wider than an eye. Their skin takes on the colors of the place—green-brown in kelp shadow, pale as old limestone in the deep hollows, stippled with sparks when startled. They keep separate dens but share the richer hunting grounds. One may leave a crab shell beside a neighbor’s crevice; another may return a useful length of cable, stripped of its bright growths. Whether these exchanges are courtesy, warning, or simply habit is hard to tell.

They learn the mountain by touch and pressure. A young one follows an elder through a narrow pass, keeping one arm on the rock as the current tries to turn them. It learns where water accelerates, where sand will lift, which ledges remain quiet when the storm tide comes. It learns that a sudden stillness is not safety but the pause before a surge. Later, it makes the passage alone, changing color as it goes: dark in the kelp, stone-pale beneath an overhang, almost invisible when it drifts above the valley floor.

There are seasons in their lives, though the season may last only a few hours. A bloom draws them upward. A storm sends them deep. A landslide opens fresh feeding ground, and for days the valley floor is busy with scavengers. Then the current turns, carrying the cloud of life elsewhere, and the mountains seem empty again. The residents remain, tucked into stone, rooted to ledges, or traveling the hidden rivers between peaks.

They do not rule this country. They read it. They know the difference between a current that will bear them and one that will grind them against a cliff; they hear the storm’s approach in a change of pitch through the seafloor. Their homes are temporary, their paths inherited only by practice, their safety dependent on the exact shape of a passing tide. When the water changes its mind, they change with it.

At night—or what passes for night beneath the clouded surface—the forests dim to a scattering of living lights. The travelers sound across the basins. In the towers, small creatures shift among the rusted ribs. In a cleft high on the slope, a watchful animal rests with its arms folded close, feeling the mountain tremble beneath it. All around, the sea moves through the drowned high country, and everything that lives there listens.
