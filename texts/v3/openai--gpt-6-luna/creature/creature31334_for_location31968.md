The first inhabitants are easy to mistake for pale slips of clay caught in the seams.

They come out where the walls are wettest: small, narrow-bodied things, no taller than a thumb joint, with arms folded close and legs made for gripping. Their skin has the same soft, chalky look as the terraces, but it warms beneath a touch. They have faces only in passing. A crease opens where a mouth might be; two dark pinholes appear, then vanish when the head turns. They do not speak. When they need one another, they tap the walls.

The tapping moves through the whole crown. A reply may come from a stairway three valleys away, or from the mountain outside. Then one of the little figures sets off, taking routes that seem impossible for its size: across a damp ceiling, through the narrow gap behind a waterfall, down a staircase that returns it to the place it began. It arrives carrying nothing anyone can see. At the destination, a new channel opens in the clay, or a pool gathers in a hollow that was dry a moment before.

They are not quite builders. They don’t lay bricks or raise walls. They press their small palms to unfinished surfaces and wait. The clay softens beneath their hands. A ridge swells up; a door-shaped hollow deepens; a ledge grows outward like a tongue. Sometimes the little figures retreat as the structure forms, watching with the patient attention of midwives. Sometimes they are caught by it—a wall growing around their ankles, a roof sealing over them—and disappear without struggle. Later, a pale shape will pass behind the wall, moving with the slow assurance of something that has found its proper corridor.

Perhaps they are being made by the place as it makes itself. Perhaps they are the last remembered gesture of builders who no longer exist. The inhabitants do not seem to distinguish between these possibilities. They tend the damp leaves, clear the drains with their fingers, and crouch beside the basins to watch the steam collect. If a plant pushes through a crack, they guide its stem along the wall. If a new wall rises through a patch of greenery, they lift the leaves aside until the clay has set.

There is another presence, less easy to see. It lives in the intervals: the air pocket behind a tile, the dark beneath a stair, the space between one drop of water and the next. The little figures pause when it passes. Leaves incline along its unseen route. The thin-winged insects grow still. Once, a pale thing darts between the terraces, and every tap in the walls stops until it is gone.

Whatever this larger presence is, it has no single body. It may be the landscape’s attention, spread through its channels and joints. It may be the mountain listening from within itself. The little inhabitants seem to regard it as one regards a sleeping elder: familiar, powerful, and not to be woken without cause.

They never climb to the highest terrace. They tend the stairs that lead there, repairing a step, wetting a cracked surface, replacing a fallen leaf. When the stairs bring them back to their beginning, they simply turn and try another flight. They do not appear disappointed. A place that is still becoming has no need to arrive.

At times, one of them stands alone on a parapet, looking up into the clouded heights. Its small, blunt face catches the light. Then the walls give their single hollow knock, and the figure presses one hand to the clay in answer.

Somewhere above it, water begins to run uphill.
