At first, the basin seems empty of anything that might call itself an inhabitant.

Then one of the dark, swollen leaves unfolds.

It does so so slowly that the movement is easier to feel than to see: a change in the closeness of the green, a new gap in the layered fronds. Beneath it is a pale body, narrow and jointed, its limbs folded along the stem as if it had grown there. A veil of fine filaments lies over its back. Where the gold light touches it, faint amber lines show beneath its skin, branching and gathering around a small, smooth hollow that might be a face. It has no mouth that can be seen. It does not look toward the observer. It turns instead toward the light, though the light has not shifted.

There are others. Once noticed, they appear throughout the groves: one stretched along a shelf of warm stone, another tucked upright inside a pillar of bundled stems, a third nearly indistinguishable from the pale fronds hanging above a pool. They are few, or else very good at being mistaken for what surrounds them. They keep no paths open and leave no lasting tracks. A body may pass through the mats without flattening them, or may remain in one place so long that leaves grow over its shoulders and make it part of the grove.

They are neither wholly animal nor wholly plant. Their hands are delicate fans of pale fingers, suited to holding a stem or cupping water; their feet end in soft pads that take the grit without disturbing it. They drink from the air as much as from the pools, drawing the beads of condensation down through those fine filaments along their backs. In the deepest hollows, some have become nearly transparent. In the brighter places, their skin takes on the warm color of honeyed stone.

They do not speak. Their company is made of small exchanges: a hand resting briefly on another’s shoulder, a slow opening of the fingers, a faint deepening of color along the throat. Sometimes one lifts its head and another answers by turning a leaf. These gestures might be greetings, or questions, or only the sharing of a sensation—a patch of heat, a drop of water, the nearness of another body. Nothing in the basin is hurried enough to require a distinction.

The stone rings and little shelters belong to them, though the structures seem almost too modest to have been made. The bare patch inside a ring is kept clear because it is warm beneath the feet. The leaning shelter is a place to gather when the mineral roof sweats heavily, its slabs arranged to catch the rare drops that fall. The lonely arch marks no road so much as a meeting: a place where two groves grow close and two inhabitants can reach one another without parting the leaves. Their building does not oppose the landscape. It gives a few intentions a shape.

Perhaps they are old. Perhaps they have not yet begun. Their bodies show no scars, their shelters no repairs. They know no storm, no season of hunger, no darkness that would make them gather close. Their lives seem less like a succession of days than like a gradual settling into the world: a filament finding a crack, a hand learning the curve of a stone, one presence discovering another in the green.

And yet they are not entirely still.

In one of the wet hollows, the delicate line in the mud is fresh. At the far edge of a light shaft, a pale figure has lifted itself from a bed of leaves. It stands with one hand against the rock and its head tilted toward the open basin. The leaves around it have not bent. The dust beneath its feet remains undisturbed.

For a long while, it does nothing.

Then, with the grave care of something making a choice for the first time, it takes one step into the light.
