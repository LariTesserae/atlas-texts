The first sign of the inhabitant is not a body, but a pause.

The water drives itself around the chamber, carrying grit and little bright flakes in its furious loops. Then, for a moment, the current seems to listen. The grit hangs in place. The crystal threads stop trembling. Somewhere beyond the wall, something answers with a low note that is almost the same as the mountain’s pressure, but not quite.

It may be in the dark shape that passes behind the stone. It may be the stone itself, learning to move.

When it comes into the pocket, it does not enter through any crack. A seam darkens; the mineral folds loosen; a long, pale shape gathers out of them, as if the mountain has drawn one of its thoughts into the water. There is no clear head. There are ridges like closed gills, and a thin fringe of crystal that trails behind it, chiming softly against the current. The rest of it is difficult to see. Its outline shifts with the wavering light, and sometimes the eye insists it is only a piece of the wall coming unstuck.

The little grazers know it. They turn toward it on their threads, their transparent bodies flashing once, like a handful of tiny signals. The pale tuft on the floor folds tighter. The creature does not eat either of them. It moves slowly through the violence, though the water around it is always in motion. Its passing turns the chamber’s roar into a deeper, more measured sound—one pulse, then another, as if the whole pocket were breathing with it.

It is the keeper of things that should have been carried away.

The three flat stones are arranged where it can touch them. The braided fiber is held in the seam by a thread of fresh mineral, not quite growing around it, not quite letting it go. The clay cup bears a smooth mark along its rim, too regular to be made by the current. The inhabitant tends these objects without seeming to know what they are for. It may have found them. It may have been here when they were placed. It may remember a hand that has long since left the mountain—or may only remember the pressure of that hand, preserved in the shape of the cup.

When something is dropped into the chamber, the inhabitant notices. It follows the object’s crooked descent, not by chasing it, but by changing the water around it. The thing wanders down, slides sideways, and eventually comes back near the place it began. Sometimes it returns to the hand that released it. Sometimes the hand is gone, and the thing settles beside the stones.

The inhabitant does not understand the chamber as a room. To it, the pocket may be a soft place in a vast body: a hollow where pressure gathers, where new rock arrives, where the mountain’s unfinished seams can be felt from the inside. It moves through cracks too narrow for any solid creature, spreading itself as a vibration through the stone and drawing back together in the water. The dark shape glimpsed beyond the wall may be its greater body—or only the shadow cast by a mountain that has not yet decided what it is.

At times it rests with its ridged side pressed to the floor. The water still surges around it, but the loose grains stop skittering. Warmth gathers in the corner and stays a little longer. The crystal threads shine faintly, each carrying a small trembling brightness down toward it.

Then the mountain shifts. A seam opens somewhere far above. The creature lifts—or the stone around it lifts—and the chamber rings like a cup struck underwater. The sound goes on after everything has grown still.

In that lingering note, the little life of the pocket holds its place: the grazers on their threads, the folded plant, the three stones, the cup, and the one who listens through them all.
