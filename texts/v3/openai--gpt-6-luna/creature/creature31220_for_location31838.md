The oldest inhabitant of the Hush has no face, and no name it can answer to.

It lies beneath the plateau’s dust as a dark, branching skin, spread through the cracks in the blue-black stone. What looks like one plant is many: colonies joined to colonies, roots crossing and re-crossing until no one can say where one ends. It grows by almost imperceptible degrees. A frond lengthens across a century; a fissure fills over the course of a life. The ground is its body, or its memory, or both.

It has no voice of its own. The wind gives it one.

When air passes through the tall reeds, each stalk sounds at a different pitch. The deeper notes travel through the rock, where the joined roots receive them. The colonies answer one another in pulses of light too faint for daylight and too slow for most eyes. At night, these signals move underfoot in scattered constellations. A patch glimmers; a patch farther away replies. The conversation may take hours to cross a basin.

The people of the Hush listen for it, though no two listeners agree on what it says. Some claim the root-lattice warns of the great tremors. Others believe it recalls every shift in the stone, and that its low, rolling chords are not messages but the plateau remembering itself. There are old people who sit beside the fronds for whole nights, hands resting on the ground, waiting for a note they heard as children. Now and then, one hears it again.

The animals have their own ways of reading the place. Small pale runners dart under the mats, their feet ticking as they hunt mineral nodules. They are nearly blind and know the world through vibration: a footfall, a moving shadow, the tremor of a grazing herd. Their narrow burrows thread between the roots without breaking them. When the ground sends a warning pulse, the runners disappear so quickly that the surface seems to swallow them.

The long-backed grazers travel in quiet companies, three or four together, cropping the fibrous growth and carrying its spores on the plates of their hides. Their weight presses shallow paths into the living skin. The plants close slowly behind them, leaving a faint seam in the ground that can be followed for generations. The grazers stop often, not merely to feed, but to listen. Their blunt heads tilt toward sounds too low for human ears. When they turn as one, the fronds turn with them.

Above, the wide-winged hunters drift along the ridges on the plateau’s steady updrafts. They are difficult to see against the pewter sky. Only their shadows give them away, passing over the mineral lace like a cloud crossing a dark floor. They strike without warning. When they fold their wings, the air cracks sharply, and every small creature beneath the growth goes still.

The people build as though they are guests who mean to be invited back. Their homes crouch among the outcrops, their walkways held above the mats on narrow supports. They do not cut roads so much as persuade the vegetation to leave a path. A settlement’s wind towers are tuned to the local fronds; their roofs catch the pressure of passing gusts and turn it into warmth, light, or a low note that carries to the next ridge. The best builders are also musicians, and the best musicians know how to mend a roof.

They speak of distance in sounds. A traveler might say that the western basin is three long moans away, or that the next settlement lies beyond the pitch where the reeds begin to beat against one another. Children learn the differences before they learn the names of the far places: the dry rattle that means a herd is approaching; the smooth, heavy chord that warns of a change in the rock; the sudden silence that makes every adult stop speaking.

No one owns the old paths. They are too strange for that. Some run straight over a hollow where the ground has sunk away, or climb a slope that no longer exists. The people who follow them take tuning forks, lamps, and food for several days. They say the paths belong to an earlier arrangement of the plateau, and that sometimes the world still fits it.

When the deep vibration comes, all the Hush attends.

The fronds lie flat in a wave that crosses the ridges. The grazers descend into the basins; the small runners vanish beneath the ground. The hunters wheel high above, their wings spread wide. In the settlements, lights are shuttered and machines are stilled. For a while there is no music—not even the faint beat of the wind through the reeds.

Then the vibration passes.

A finger’s breadth of new stone may have risen somewhere. A hollow may have altered its shape. A forgotten path may show itself beneath the mineral dust. The living mats begin to lift again, and their lights pass slowly from colony to colony.

The people return to their instruments. The animals return to their trails. The plants continue their patient work, loosening the rock and making it habitable, listening through roots that have been there longer than memory. Nothing in the Hush is truly silent. Some things merely take a long time to answer.
