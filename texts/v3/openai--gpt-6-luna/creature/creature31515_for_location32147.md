The plateau is inhabited, but not occupied. Nothing has claimed it as a home in the way a burrow or a reef is claimed. Its living things settle only for as long as the water permits.

The first to be seen are the fish: silver-grey bodies holding position in the shelter of a wall, all facing into the same invisible stream. They keep together so closely that the school seems like a single torn shadow. When the current changes, it breaks apart at once. The fish vanish through arches and along avenues, each following the water that carries it.

Closer to the stone are the flatter animals, the ones that can be mistaken for fallen pieces of the plateau. They lie against terraces with their edges pressed down, almost indistinguishable from the pale or dark slabs around them. Their eyes are small and set wide apart. Along their undersides, fine combs stir the scant living matter from the stone’s pores and seams. If a wall shudders, they lift themselves in a single slow ripple and drift away, leaving the architecture apparently empty again.

The long-bodied creatures do not rest. They pass through the high arches in wavering lines, feelers extended before them, testing the current. When the water runs hard, they stretch thin and travel quickly, like pale streamers caught in a flood. In stillness they gather themselves into loose coils beneath ledges, their bodies almost transparent except where the dim light catches the inner thread of them. They seem to hear the plateau’s hollow note. Sometimes, when it sounds, every feeler turns toward the same wall.

There are smaller residents in the seams: blind scavengers no larger than a hand, moving beneath the bridges and over the paving. They pick at whatever the water has left behind. A few carry shells, though none has made a home of one. They shelter under a lip of stone, then leave it without ceremony when the current begins to pull.

And there is something larger that comes and goes through the lower basins. It has a broad, flattened body, ridged in overlapping plates, with a fringe of delicate feelers trailing from its sides. Seen from above, it looks like a slab that has slipped from the old paving. Seen from near the ground, it is plainly alive: the plates shift against one another as it breathes, and the feelers move constantly, reading the water. It may remain in one basin through several turns of the current, nearly motionless, while smaller creatures pass over its back. Then the water changes, and it is gone—drawn through an arch too narrow for its apparent width, or carried down a passage that seemed to have no outlet.

None of them makes a nest in the chambers. None marks a wall or returns faithfully to the same shelter. Their lives are arranged by the plateau’s currents: feeding in one hollow, hiding in another, moving on when the water turns against them. They do not restore the old works, or understand them as works. A fallen tower is a shadow; a courtyard is a pocket of slow water; a doorway is simply a place the current passes through.

For brief moments, their movements give the ruins a kind of pattern. Schools thread the avenues. The flat-backed animals lift from the terraces together. The long-bodied ones gather under an arch, all their feelers tilted toward the sound inside the stone. Then the sea shifts, and the pattern loosens. The inhabitants scatter into the blue, leaving the plateau to its cold, patient shuddering.
