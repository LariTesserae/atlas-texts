What lives there does not have a body you could find.

At first it seems to be the vegetation itself: the interlocked stems, the roots drinking from the same dark water, the leaves turning their pale undersides toward a change in the air. But that is only its surface, the way a face is only the visible part of a person. The thing beneath it is slower, and more widely spread. It runs through the wet places and the warm ones. It thickens where a stem presses against a pipe; it thins where the water goes cold. It has been growing for so long that no one could say where it began.

It knows the place by contact. A drop falling into a root-bowl is a small event. A footstep is a pressure traveling ahead of the foot, through mud and wood and the trembling roots of reeds. The old machine is something it knows particularly well. Each turn of the flywheel sends a familiar knock through the ground. The knock passes outward, softened by moss, and is answered—not in sound, but in a minute change of tension among the stems.

The machine is not its heart. It is more like a tooth it has learned to keep, useful for feeling the shape of the world. The plants grow around the housing without smothering the wheel. Roots hold the bolts; leaves roof the moving parts; a thread of water keeps the metal cool. The slow clack belongs to the place as rain belongs to it. If the wheel stopped, the thing would not die. But for a long while—perhaps a season, perhaps a century—the plants nearby would keep making room for the sound that was no longer there.

It has no eyes, though light changes it. When the sun reaches a gap in the canopy, leaves lift toward the brightness, and somewhere below, fine roots alter their course. It has no ears, though it turns toward water and answers the creak of a closing stem. It senses by being touched, and by touching everything back. Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, are made of adjustments: a vine held away from a passage; a root lowered toward a seep; a dozen leaves all facing the same way.

That is why the empty iron frame matters. Nothing built it as an altar, and the thing does not worship it. The frame is simply a shape that has remained empty. Its outline exerts a pressure on the growth around it, as a gap in a hedge guides the wind. Leaves point toward it. Roots stop short of it. The plants do not fill the space inside, though they fill every space nearby. It is a direction the place continues to remember.

Sometimes, when a person enters, the thing notices only the disturbance: warmth where there should be none, weight on a path that was nearly closed, the abrupt displacement of water. It does not know the visitor as a visitor. It knows the visitor as a moving patch of heat, salt, breath, and pressure. A curtain of vine might ease aside, though it is hard to tell whether it has made way or merely sagged. A shallow pool might tremble before the foot reaches it. The old wheel may knock once, pause, and resume its patient pace.

If the person stays, the place begins to include them. Water beads on their sleeves. Roots rise against their soles, not enough to trip them, only enough to learn their weight. In the green chambers, the rustling gathers close around the sound of their breathing. Nothing approaches. Nothing watches from the dark. The attention is less like a gaze than like dampness: everywhere, impersonal, impossible to step outside.

It does not want the visitor gone. Wanting is too sharp a word for something that takes years to close a path. But it will go on growing after the visitor leaves. It will keep the warmth of their passage for a little while, then lose it among the countless pressures and temperatures that make up its world.

By then the water will have moved on. A leaf will have fallen into a pipe-mouth. The flywheel will turn, and the ground will receive its knock. In the broad green thickness, something will shift itself by the smallest degree, making room for what comes next.
