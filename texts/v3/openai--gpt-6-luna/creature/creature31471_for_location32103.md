There are no inhabitants in the way a town expects them: no crowds, no smoke rising from kitchens, no doors opening at the sound of footsteps. At first the plateau appears empty. Then you notice that the emptiness is attentive.

The halls are listening.

A low vibration passes through their root-pillars when the clouds change direction. Seed cases click in answer. Somewhere far across the meadows, a reed tower gives a single hollow note, and the sound travels through the woven paths beneath your feet. The structures do not move, exactly. They receive the weather and return it as music. What seems like architecture is one great, slow awareness spread through leaves, water, and root.

Its attention is not human. It has no face to turn toward you, no voice with which to ask your name. But if you stand still in a courtyard, the drops falling from the roof begin to sound unusually distinct. One lands near your left hand, another behind you, another just beyond the arch. The place has found your outline. It does not pursue you or welcome you. It simply includes you in what it is hearing.

The pale creatures in the pools belong to that awareness, or carry it from one place to another. They are narrow-bodied and almost transparent, with soft fins that trail like scraps of cloud. Sometimes they glide beneath a bridge; sometimes they hang motionless in the water, their eyes indistinguishable from the dark reflections of the leaves. They feed on the fine green film that gathers on submerged roots. When they leave a pool, the water does not ripple. When they return, you may only know because a distant wall gives a faint, answering hum.

They are the plateau’s messengers, though they carry no news that can be told. They drift between the structures, passing beneath causeways and through channels too small for anything larger. The roots feel their touch. The halls answer. In this way the separated places remain connected, not by roads alone, but by a continual exchange of pressure, water, and tone.

There may be others inside the green. In the thickest walls, something sometimes shifts behind the layered leaves: a shadow withdrawing from an opening, a pale joint folding away, the impression of a body too long and slender to belong to any animal you know. You never find a nest or a trail. The low plants close behind whatever passes, preserving no evidence. If these inhabitants are shy, their shyness is older than fear. They have no need to flee. The plateau has made room for them and will go on making room without changing shape.

Nothing here seems to be built or tended. Still, rain is guided into the roots; pools remain clear; paths hold firm beneath your feet. The work is performed by the place itself, or by the small lives moving within it, until there is no meaningful distinction between the two. A creature crosses a channel. A root takes up water. A wall resonates. These are not separate acts but parts of one unhurried attention.

If you remain long enough, you may begin to understand that the stillness is not absence. It is a kind of settled life, complete enough to have stopped announcing itself. The plateau does not grow toward a future. Its inhabitants do not gather to change it. They live in the continuance: water sounding in its chosen channels, pale bodies slipping through green reflections, roots listening beneath the paths.

And when you leave, the low leaves close over your footprints. The plateau keeps no visible memory of you.

But somewhere, in a hollow stem or a dark pool, a note sounds once and is answered from far away.
