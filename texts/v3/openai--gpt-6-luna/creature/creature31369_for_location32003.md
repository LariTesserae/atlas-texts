What lives there has learned not to trust a shape.

The long-winged grazers travel in close banks, their narrow bodies almost hidden by the copper grass. From a distance they resemble a current passing over the ground. They feed by lowering their beaks to the grass without breaking formation, and when the earth begins to shift, the whole flock turns—not away from the movement, but across it, as if reading a path through the change. Their wings are veined with a dull, stony sheen. At dusk, when the cracks beneath the plateau begin to glow, the flock’s undersides catch that light and shine faintly, like a second, lower cloud.

They do not fly far. They lift in a single sweep to cross a fault, then settle again wherever the land has become grass. Sometimes they alight on a slope that was a valley an hour earlier. They seem to know when it will settle. The smallest among them keep near the center of the bank, where the others’ bodies make a moving wall against sudden wind and rain.

Beneath them, the pale burrowers travel in families, though the word *family* may not mean much to them. They have soft, tapering heads and no visible eyes; their bodies sense pressure through the soil. They move in long branching paths, rising only when a fissure opens or a buried current warms the ground. Their trails can be seen from above as fine lines of disturbed earth—lines that sometimes meet, hesitate, and continue in a new direction after the landscape shifts. Where several trails converge, the soil is often unusually fertile. The burrowers leave behind seeds, spores, and small stones polished smooth by their passage.

The tall ferns are full of watchers. Some are feathered and quick, with bright eyes that seem to catch different parts of the world at once. Others are nearly transparent, their bodies visible only when they pass in front of a dark leaf or a glowing crack. They nest in the curled heads of ferns, in hanging root-tangles, and in hollows that may not remain hollows for long. They rarely call. When they do, the sound often comes from somewhere else: a thin chirring heard beneath a pool, or a low answering note from a ridge beyond the fog. They hunt by waiting for the world to misalign. A creature that steps where its shadow has not yet arrived is easy to catch.

Around warm vents grow the scale-leaved plants, layered in overlapping fans that open toward heat and close against the pressure of approaching storms. The oldest form rings so broad that grazers sleep inside them. New leaves emerge quickly, pale and almost translucent, then darken to copper and green as the ground’s mineral warmth passes through their roots. When a ridge trembles, entire thickets turn their leaves toward it. For a few minutes the plateau can look as if the vegetation is listening.

In the largest valleys live animals too large to be seen all at once. Their presence is known by slow changes: a mist column bending around something invisible, a fern forest parting without wind, a vibration traveling through the soles of the feet. They may be thick-bodied and low, with limbs buried in root and soil; or they may be the roots themselves, moving so gradually that the distinction has never mattered. Small creatures graze in the hollows of their backs. Pools gather in the depressions they leave. When one of these vast beings shifts, a valley may rise into a ridge, and the animals nearby wait—still, attentive—until the ground has finished becoming something else.

And under everything, running through the dark stone, is the plateau’s deepest inhabitant.

It has no mouth, no face, and perhaps no single body. It is the warm pressure in the rock, the mineral fluid moving through hidden channels, the recurring tension that opens one crack and closes another. It cycles. It gathers itself, releases, and gathers again. The animals feel its approach through their feet, the plants through their roots, the burrowers through the walls of their tunnels. They pause when it changes course. Then, when its vast work resumes, they go on feeding, growing, nesting, and migrating across ground that may not be ground by morning.

Nothing there appears to rule the others. The plateau’s inhabitants are joined less by hierarchy than by attention. Each waits for signs from things it cannot see: the shiver under the grass, the dimming of the cracks, the sudden stillness of a storm. They have no need to make the land reliable. They have learned instead to live in the brief intervals when it holds still—and to move when it does not.
