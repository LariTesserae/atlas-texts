At first, there is no one.

Then, after a while, the ridge seems to have one more notch than before. A shallow notch, low on the shadowed side, where the pale dust has gathered in a shape too narrow to be a crack. You might take it for a flaw in the casting. You might even touch it, if you could bear to put your hand into that grinding, steady dark.

The thing lives in the seams.

It is not large enough to have a body in any familiar sense. It occupies the pores beneath the grey-green tufts, the narrow channels under the basin, the hairline spaces where ceramic meets alloy. Its presence is distributed, though not evenly: a little in the ridge, more beneath the plateau, a delicate concentration at the place where the machine’s pulse enters the chamber. The filaments are its visible part. They tremble too slightly to be seen moving, drawing on vibration instead of water, on the residue in the air instead of light. They are not leaves. They are feelers, or mouths, or the exposed tips of something that has never had cause to rise.

It has been here long enough to mistake the machinery for weather.

The grind is its horizon. The whine is a change in pressure. The engine-pulse is the great, slow turning of the world. It does not sleep through these sounds, because there is no silence in which sleep could be distinguished. It has learned each frequency as a contour: the hard bright edge of the higher note, the broad weight beneath it, the tiny unevenness that arrives when a shaft passes its bearing. It knows the mountain by the way the mountain shakes.

It has no eyes. What would it look at? The country never changes. Instead it reads the dust. Every grain that settles is a small report from the hidden machinery: warm or cold, oily or dry, freshly shaken loose or long fallen. It reads pressure through the seams, and the faintest variations in the air. In its own way, it sees everything that matters.

When a maintenance hatch opens, the thing knows before the light reaches the plateau. A disturbance travels through the frame and into the ridge. The filaments tighten. Beneath the basin, some minute arrangement of matter shifts—not enough to stir the powder, not enough to make a sound that could be heard above the roar. The thing gathers itself toward the new vibration.

For a moment, it may seem that the little country is waiting.

It is not waiting for rescue, or company. It has no word for either. It is listening to the visitor’s weight, measuring the warmth of a hand against the cold cast stone, registering a pulse that falters and changes. These are rare events. They make no useful pattern at first. So it holds them, as it holds everything, in the countless fine passages beneath the artificial ground.

When the hatch closes, the pressure steadies. The stranger’s footsteps fade into the mountain. Dust resumes its patient settling.

The filaments loosen. The hidden thing returns to its listening—not lonely, because loneliness requires an expectation, and it has never expected anything beyond the next turn of the engine. It remains in the seams of its small, manufactured highland, alive in a way the makers may not have intended, keeping the only history the place can offer: every vibration that has ever passed through it, and the unbroken sound that continues now.
