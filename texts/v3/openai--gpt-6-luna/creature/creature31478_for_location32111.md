The people of the high country are spaced like everything else: a settlement here, a station there, and long stretches in which a traveler may see no sign of anyone but the road. They are not a folk of open plazas or crowded streets. Their cities are clusters of neighborhoods joined by causeway and rail, each built around a particular task—a quarry, a heat vent, a machine hall, a pass. A person may live their whole life within sight of the same dark ridge and still know only a small portion of the country between them and it.

They tend to be sparely built, with deep chests and a measured way of moving that wastes little breath. Their clothes are layered against cold nights and abrasive wind: close-woven inner wraps, hard outer shells, hoods with broad brims that shade the eyes and mouth. In open country they wear veils, not only against grit but against the dry air, which draws moisture away while a person is distracted by the view. In the settlements, where the stone holds the day’s warmth, people uncover their faces and speak more freely. Even there, their voices are seldom raised. The distances have taught them that a message is worth sending only if someone needs to receive it.

Water is precious, but it is not the measure of all things. They count heat, clean air, useful stone, and the condition of a road with equal care. Their machines are maintained as if they were members of a household: listened to, checked for small changes in pitch, and repaired before a fault becomes loud enough to announce itself. Children learn early to distinguish the ordinary tremor of a passing cargo train from the irregular shudder of animals moving under a floor. They learn which fissures are old, which are widening, and which should never be crossed alone.

The work is patient. Ore moves from the cuts in steady streams; metal is refined in enclosed halls; engine parts are replaced one at a time. Nothing is made quickly if it can be made to last. A settlement’s history is visible in its walls: an older foundation below a new tower, the outline of a sealed conduit, a strip of stone polished smooth where generations have brushed past. Their best craftsmanship is often mistaken for plainness. A cooling wall, a windbreak, a well-angled roof: each can be beautiful to someone who knows what it has prevented.

Beyond the roads, the animals make their own careful use of the country. The salt-sifters are small, pale creatures that travel in close groups, scraping mineral crust from the rock with comb-like mouths. They leave delicate tracks that the next wind soon erases. Beneath them, crack-burrowers click to one another through the stone. Their colonies are almost invisible until the ground seems to stir: little openings closing, little mounds appearing, then a whole patch of slope shifting by the width of a hand.

The broad-backed herds travel farther. At a distance they resemble loose slabs of moving rock, their dark plates collecting warmth as they cross the heights. They stop at mineral seams where smaller animals gather, and sometimes remain there for hours, nearly indistinguishable from the landscape. The people know better than to crowd them. They mark the herd’s passage on route boards and give it room, not from reverence exactly, but because the animals are old, strong, and have never shown any reason to make way for anyone.

In the high air, the sail-bodies drift through the seasons. Their translucent sails catch currents too slight to stir dust at ground level. They feed on the fine organic matter carried above the plateau, descending now and then toward warm stone. The people watch for them when the weather turns. A darkening of their sails can mean that cold air is coming; a sudden empty sky may warn of a larger change in the currents.

The largest creatures announce themselves without appearing. A line of deep impressions may cross a ridge, each filled with a small heap of grit. A vibration passes through a workshop wall. An animal’s shadow moves behind the dust-haze at the edge of vision. Sometimes one comes near enough to be seen: long-limbed, low-headed, with a back ridged like a fallen escarpment. It follows the warm seams and the smaller life they attract, then goes on, leaving the stone apparently untouched.

The people tell stories about these passages, though their stories are not always explanations. Some are warnings about roads that must be closed; some are songs for long journeys, where a companion’s voice helps mark the hours. They keep records too: the routes of herds, the shifting of dust, the slow retreat of a cliff. Their oldest records do not describe a world that changes dramatically. They describe a world that changes by so little that a person must be taught how to notice.

From far away, the plateau looks empty and the settlements look small. Up close, each holds a multitude of lives: people working in warm halls, animals passing beneath the crust, salt-sifters grazing at the margins of the road, sail-bodies travelling in the clear upper air. Nothing here gathers in a hurry. Everything finds its route, takes what it needs, and leaves the stone to remember the rest.
