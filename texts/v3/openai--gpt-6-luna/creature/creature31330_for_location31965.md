They are not quite separate animals, though each has a mouth, a shell, a place to hide. The pale ones cling beneath the waterline in crowded rows, their jointed feet fitted to the little ledges. The dark-shelled ones pass over them, over one another, over the stone, scraping a slow meal from the mineral skin. Threads tremble from the holes in the rock. Flat swimmers fold through the pools without making a wake. Each kind has its own small hunger, but together they seem to be doing one larger thing: keeping the basin occupied.

They know the water by its smallest changes. A bead forming on a metal knob, a trickle entering a channel, the weight of rain somewhere beyond the rim—all of these pass among them. When the basin fills, they spread outward, not hurriedly but with the ease of something returning to rooms it remembers. When the water sinks, they draw back into cracks and hollows, leaving the bare stone to shine. They do not flee the drying pools. They wait under the skin of the place.

The thrum gives them a different kind of instruction. It travels through the rock before it reaches the water. At its first faint pulse, the swimmers turn; then the shells pause, their dark rims lifted from the stone, and the threads pull taut in their holes. For a moment the whole basin seems to listen with them. Whatever produces the sound does not appear. It may be a machine far below, still working through its old motions. It may be the basin settling, or the water pressing against some sealed chamber. The creatures answer it all the same.

Perhaps they are fed by whatever the old ironwork once measured or carried. They worry the crust from the channels, sift the water, and pack themselves into every available hollow. The basin may be their whole world, though they behave as if they are maintaining it for someone else. Their movements have the patience of workers who have forgotten the task but remember its sequence.

The circular pool at the bottom is not theirs. Nothing touches it. The nearest animals keep to the outer stones, and even the threads withdraw from its edge. Its clear water holds their image: the shells, the pale clinging bodies, the swimmers turning among them. Yet the reflection is crowded beyond the count of what is present. In it, other creatures move in the same slow order—more of them, perhaps, or the same ones seen at a greater depth. They turn when the thrum comes. They wait when the water falls.

The basin’s inhabitants never look up at the rim. They have no need to. They live by the touch of water on stone, by the small tremors passing beneath them, by the return of rain. But when someone leans over the deepest pool, the reflected animals seem to notice first. Then, one by one, the real ones turn toward the sound of the watcher’s breath.
