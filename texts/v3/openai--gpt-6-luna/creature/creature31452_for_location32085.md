At first, the city seems uninhabited. Its doors stand open on empty rooms; its pools hold only sky and stone. Nothing watches from the terraces. Nothing hurries down the wet steps. Even the greenery keeps its place, leaf after leaf resting in the heat.

Then, somewhere beneath your feet, a channel carries water uphill.

It does so in a narrow groove between two stones, with the calm certainty of a road being followed. At the next crossing, a pool gathers a reflection of a balcony that is not above it. The wind passes through an arch and sounds one low note; from a passage behind you comes the answering note, though no wind enters there.

The residents are difficult to distinguish from the city because they have no separate bodies. They live in its arrangements.

Each is a particular way the place holds together: a line of water that runs against gravity, a staircase whose steps count differently from one landing to the next, a patch of cool shadow that persists beneath the fiercest sun. They are not the stone, or the water, or the green life threaded through the masonry. They are the intention among them—the exact relation that makes a door open onto a roof, or keeps a pool brimming forever at the same bright level.

They are many. One dwells in the deep cisterns, where the water is black and the sound of the falls arrives softened, as if heard through earth. Another inhabits the high bridges, spreading itself along their undersides in a tremor too steady to be called vibration. There are presences in the stairways, in the channels cut beside them, in the gaps between terraces where the visible distance grows impossibly wide. A few seem to belong to particular courtyards. Others occupy whole districts, or the interval between two distant cliffs.

They do not walk from place to place. When one turns its attention elsewhere, the city alters by a degree so small that it might be mistaken for a shift in light. A door gives onto a different landing. A stream finds a new seam in the wall. The same view across the valley becomes, without moving, a view across a greater distance. These changes are never random. They have the measured quality of someone making room.

The plants are not the residents, though they are close to them. Ferns and vines and the bent trees seem to have been given their particular places by a patience that understands both leaf and stone. They do not reach farther when no one is looking. They do not spread beyond their allotted shadows. Yet the allotment is so intimate that it can be hard to tell where a resident ends and its garden begins. A vine vanishes over a roof and reappears in a courtyard below, and the two spaces feel, for a moment, like a single thought extending through the city.

The residents communicate through sound. The roar of water is their shared voice, too vast to separate into individual utterances. But there are distinctions within it: a hollow beat under one bridge, a thin hiss along a garden wall, a note the wind finds only in a certain arch. Those who learn the city can hear a question in the change between two notes. An answer may come as a channel carrying water uphill, or as a staircase arriving at the right door.

They do not seem to need visitors, and they do not seem to resent them. When someone enters the city, a few passages become easier to find. Pools reflect the newcomer’s face without distortion. The wind, which has made its immense chord for ages, briefly acquires a clear space in the middle of it—as though listening.

Then the city resumes its sound. The channels keep their courses. Mist remains beneath the bridges. And the residents hold their places, not waiting exactly, but sustaining the conditions in which waiting could occur.
