The people who live there are careful with nouns.

They do not say *the ground is shifting* unless they have measured it twice. They say the ground is behaving differently. They do not say *that ridge is gone* when it disappears into fog; they say it is unavailable. Their speech is full of small allowances, little hinges built into every certainty. A house stands here, for now. The stair has forty-seven steps on the way up. The bridge is safe in ordinary weather.

They are practical people, though not because they believe the world can be mastered. They mark the height of doorways and the angle of floors. They keep duplicate keys in neighboring districts, and leave notes for the next shift in places that may become walls. Surveyors are treated with affection and impatience. Their instruments are carried in padded cases; their findings are written in ink that survives damp. A surveyor who says “I don’t know yet” is trusted more than one who says “impossible.”

Most residents have a home in one district and a reason to travel to another. They know the cableways by their sounds, and the lifts by the smell of their brakes. They learn which bridges sway only when the fog is thick, which staircases are kinder on the descent, and which alleys should be crossed before the lamps come on. A person may leave for work by a familiar route and return by a new one, not because the streets have moved exactly, but because they have acquired an additional bend, or a landing, or a door that opens onto stone. People get used to this. They put the kettle on.

The old quarter’s houses are full of rooms that have outlived their original purpose. Stone-facing doors hold coats. Windows that look onto a neighboring roof have become cupboards, or shrines, or places where children stand to listen. In some homes, the family keeps a chair turned toward the blank wall where a view used to be. No one sits in it much. Still, no one moves it.

Children grow up learning the city’s habits before its history. They know that a missing bridge is not necessarily a lost bridge, that a map can be true and wrong at the same time, and that you never race goats along a narrow ledge unless you want to lose. They play at measuring one another: standing back-to-back against a wall, then checking the marks a week later. When the marks disagree, they argue over whether someone has grown, the wall has moved, or the chalk was poor. They consider all three possibilities reasonable.

The animals are not quite pets, though people feed them. The blunt-nosed mammals in the machinery housings are given names by the workers who see them most often, and the names change from shift to shift. Birds nest on signal wires and drop feathers into the foundries, where they stick to the wet stone like scraps of ash. The goats are privately owned, officially unowned, and collectively blamed for any fence found open. They pass through certain gaps as if the gaps belong to them. Their bells carry across the shelves long after the herd has vanished into cloud.

There are smaller lives, too: pale moths clustering around lamps, green threads of moss in the seams of warm iron, tiny white shells left at the edges of pools that nobody saw fill. The gardeners know which terraces will take root and which will swallow a bed of seedlings overnight. They grow food that can tolerate being overlooked. At the end of a hard season, a pale fruit is passed around the table, and everyone agrees it tastes better than last year, although no one can remember last year’s fruit clearly enough to prove it.

People make rituals out of maintenance. On the first day of each month, households test their windows and mark the cracks. At the change of a shift, workers tap the rail joints with small hammers, listening for hollow places. When a survey crew discovers that a ridge has moved, the finding is read aloud in the nearest public hall. There is usually tea. Sometimes there is an argument about the measurements, but never about whether the ridge matters. Even the unmeasured places have names. People use them in directions, in warnings, and in stories told to children who ask what lies beyond the last known stair.

Nobody agrees on what the mountain is doing. Some say it is still being formed. Some say it is only settling, like any great weight, though nothing about its movements resembles settling to those who live on it. The oldest residents insist it used to be lower. The young do not disbelieve them; they simply point out that the sky may have been lower, too.

And beneath all of them—under the iron, the gardens, the old houses with their useless doors—is the pressure that no one measures. The mountain does not speak. It provides no signs that can be trusted as messages. It pushes up new stone through paving, warms the tunnels, darkens walls with moisture. Its changes are slow enough to invite arguments and intimate enough to be noticed by anyone who has stayed.

So the people live as if the city were less a possession than a long arrangement with something unfinished. They bolt down what they can, leave joints where they cannot, and build their homes with an eye toward the next version of the ground. At night, their lamps shine in scattered windows. Somewhere a bell rings; somewhere else, a bird answers. The sound crosses the fog in broken pieces, and the city listens without expecting the mountain to explain itself.
