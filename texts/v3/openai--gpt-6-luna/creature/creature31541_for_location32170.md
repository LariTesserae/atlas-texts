The people who live there have learned to keep their bodies slightly ahead of the damp. Their coats are waxed at the seams, their boots thick-soled and cut with narrow grooves to shed water. They carry cloths for wiping their hands and small tools for clearing grit from drains. Even indoors, they hang things where air can move around them. Nothing is put away wet unless it is meant to stay wet.

They are not a quiet people, exactly. They speak with a steady, economical force, saving their breath for sentences that need to cross a workshop or a square. Near the central works, they use signs: two fingers to mean *wait*, a flattened palm for *shut it*, a fist turned down for *pressure falling*. Children learn these gestures before they can read. They also learn the ordinary sounds of the city—the pump that is merely tired, the gate that needs attention, the particular hollow knock that means a channel is carrying more water than it should.

Most residents have some connection to the works, though the city is large enough to contain other occupations: cooks, clerks, teachers, builders, instrument-makers, people who repair the little domestic things the machinery does not make. Maintenance is not a calling so much as the background condition of life. A neighbor lends a wrench as readily as a cup. Someone who notices a new leak marks it in chalk; someone else will come by before the mark has washed away. The oldest residents can look at a darkening seam in a wall and say whether it is harmless seepage or the first sign of a serious shift.

They have made customs out of attention. On certain mornings, crews walk the long channels together, stopping at each bend to listen. This is officially called inspection. It is also when apprentices learn which silences are safe. People leave small offerings at the mouths of some drains—not gifts to a god, they say, but practical tokens: a polished washer, a blue bead, a strip of copper to show where water has gone missing. No one agrees on who first began doing it.

The children are hard to keep dry and harder to frighten with water. They play along the parapets, race the little currents in the street runnels, and dare one another to put an ear to the warm grates. They know the city’s damp places by name. Some have a game of guessing which pipe will start tapping after the rain has stopped. Adults tell them not to listen for too long. The warning is delivered in the same tone as *don’t lean over the rail*—routine, serious, and not especially persuasive.

There are stories about where the unaccounted-for water goes. It sinks into the stone, some say, finding old fissures beneath the shelf. It fills an abandoned reservoir. It escapes down through the ravines and returns as mist. A few insist that the deep system is larger than the plans admit, or older than the city, and that the pumps are not only moving water but answering it. No one can prove this. The pressure readings are usually within tolerance. The channels run. The tanks fill.

Still, people sometimes pause when rain taps overhead beneath a clear sky. They listen without looking up. An experienced worker may glance toward the nearest gauge, then continue with whatever they were doing. A less experienced one will ask whether that sound is normal, and receive an answer that is both true and unhelpful: “It has happened before.”

The city’s inhabitants do not think of themselves as brave for staying. They have homes, meals, disputes, festivals, and places they mean to visit when they have time. They like the broad squares and the amber light beneath the bridges. They know which stairways stay warm after dark and which courtyards smell of mineral rain. The machines are not a threat to them; they are part of the weather, and more dependable than most weather.

So they live by the rhythms of flow and repair. They patch what cracks, clear what clogs, and build only when the stone has shown it can bear the weight. Their confidence is not that nothing will go wrong. It is that someone will hear it when it does.

And beneath their careful lives, beyond the mapped cisterns and the familiar shudder of the pressure gates, the water continues somewhere through the dark. The people have given it no settled name. They have learned, instead, to recognize when it is listening.
