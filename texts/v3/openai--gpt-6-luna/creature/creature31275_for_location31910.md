At first they seem less like inhabitants than a condition of the plain: the way water is a condition, or warmth. Then one of the pale folds along a channel wall opens, and a small dark mouth takes in a thread of passing water. The fold closes again. Nothing else changes.

Most of them live pressed against something. A body fits the curve of a conduit; a shell occupies the hollow beneath a grille; a soft, many-limbed animal lies in the seam between two plates, where the warmth rises. Their shapes vary, but nearly all are broad or flattened, made for contact rather than speed. Some have shells scored with fine branching ridges that catch mineral dust. Others are nearly transparent, their organs visible as dim amber knots. Along their undersides, short feelers test the water, the metal, the bodies beside them. They seem to know their surroundings chiefly by touch.

There are solitary ones, though solitude is difficult to distinguish here. A creature may rest in a pipe-mouth without another body touching it, but the water that passes over it carries the same taste, the same warmth, the same faint vibration as the water passing over thousands more. Most live in heaps, not piled carelessly but settled together with an exactness that leaves narrow channels between them. A flank rises; the one beside it shifts. A series of small adjustments travels outward through the mass, scarcely disturbing the surface.

They feed without pursuit. Fine slots in the floors release pale grains, soft films, or something too small to see. The inhabitants draw water through fringed mouths and strain from it what the channels bring. Along the warmest housings, thin mats of life grow over the metal, then are grazed down by shell-backed animals that pass through the same places again and again. In deeper cuts, where the water moves more slowly, long jointed forms extend themselves from between the grates and collect whatever settles. They have no need to leave the water, and little reason to hurry within it.

The young are almost indistinguishable from flecks of debris. They gather in shallow basins, tiny translucent commas with bright points where their eyes will be. Currents carry them against the smooth banks, where they remain caught among older bodies until they are large enough to hold on. They do not seem to be guarded. Instead, the whole population makes a kind of shelter: warmth retained between close-set bodies, food filtered from the common flow, motion softened by the sheer number of living surfaces. A young creature can pass from one crowded hollow to another without ever entering open water.

Their eyes, where they have them, are small and dark. They do not turn toward distant shapes. They notice the approach of a foot or tool through the disturbance it makes in a plate, and answer with a tightening of the skin, a draw of feelers, a pulse passed along the channel. The response is neither alarm nor welcome. It resembles the way a valve answers a change in pressure. If the disturbance continues, some bodies make room for it; others settle more firmly into their hollows. Few flee. There is nowhere, in any useful sense, to flee to.

Now and then the inhabitants move together. A pressure change travels through the pipes, or a pump alters its rhythm by the smallest degree, and the animals begin to contract and release in sequence. The movement passes beneath bridges, through basins, along the long wet cuts. It may take minutes to cross a section of the plain. From above, it looks like a faint darkening of the water, followed by a slow return to its former brightness. Afterward, the creatures lie as they were. The machinery continues its measured work.

Whether the animals sense the machinery as machinery is difficult to imagine. They may know only that certain walls are warm, certain openings provide food, and certain vibrations precede a change in the water. Yet some of the largest bodies occupy chambers that seem to have been made for them. They lie coiled around submerged pillars, their pale backs rising and falling against the metal. When they shift, a pressure gauge in a nearby housing moves by a fraction. When they settle, it returns. The relation may be accidental, inherited, or so old that the distinction has ceased to matter.

There are inhabitants in places that appear uninhabitable: behind the narrow slots, within the dark mouths of the conduits, under the sealed-looking plates. Their presence is given away by a minute stirring in the runoff, by a row of tiny lights reflected beneath a grille, by the sudden absence of the fine film that had been growing on a pipe. Some are no larger than a fingertip. Some are too deep in the system to be seen at all. The visible bodies may be only the outermost layer of a population extending through every reservoir and passage beneath the plain.

They are not still, exactly. They breathe, sift, tighten, release, and grow. They shed thin skins that soften in the water and vanish into the channels. They turn toward warmer currents. They make room for the young and close over the spaces they leave behind. But their lives take place on a scale that makes movement seem almost like maintenance: a gradual tending of the same crowded positions.

At night—if there is night beneath the permanent grey—they gather around the lights. Not to gaze at them, but to occupy the warmth they lend to the water. The basins fill more densely. Small eyes appear in the dark beneath the platforms, catching the steady illumination one by one. Farther down, unseen animals continue to feed in the pipes.

The plain belongs to no single creature. It belongs to the multitude, each body fitted against the next, each life contained in the slow circulation of water. They do not rule the structures, nor appear to resist them. They make their home in every offered hollow, until metal and flesh share the same shape and even the emptiness between one machine and another is occupied.
