At first, the people of the High Fold are easy to mistake for part of the towns themselves. They move close to the walls, wrapped in layered wool and felt, their hoods drawn against grit and sudden cold. They climb the stairs without looking down, know which stones shift underfoot, and can read a day’s weather in the cloud caught on a far ridge. Their faces are often wind-reddened; their hands are broad and nicked from hauling stone, rope, and water. They do not hurry unless the weather gives them reason.

They live near one another because warmth and water are things best shared. A household may occupy only a few rooms, but its life spills into common passages, roof courts, and the landings between stairways. Doors open inward against the wind. Roofs are places to mend clothes, sort gathered grass, and watch the weather—not quite private, not quite public. People borrow tools without ceremony and return them where they will be found. A stranger arriving at a settlement is offered shelter first; questions can wait until the stranger has warmed.

Water gives the day its measure. At the communal tanks, people learn one another’s habits by the sound of their buckets and the hour they come to fill them. Children are taught early which cisterns are safe, which channels run after a cloudburst, and how to notice a new bead of moisture in a familiar crack. They know that a pool is not merely a pool: it may be a drinking place, a bird’s bathing hollow, or the last water for a stretch of trail. To waste it is to announce that you do not understand where you live.

The Fold’s animals understand the settlements differently, but no less well. The thick-furred little nesters tuck themselves into warm spaces beneath stairs and roofs, and are tolerated so long as they do not spoil stored food. Pale cliff-birds crowd the ledges outside town; people keep certain nesting faces untouched, and the birds in turn stir the air above the ravines with their morning flights. The long-legged grazers come nearest in hard weather, nosing around the outer courtyards for scraps. They are not owned. A child may learn to recognize an old grazer by its torn ear, but no one expects it to come when called.

The people leave room for all of them. A grain store is sealed against small paws; a ledge is left clear for nesting; a water trough may be set apart for animals when the rains fail. These arrangements are practical, but they have become customs, and customs in the Fold are seldom separated from survival. People speak of the animals as neighbors, though not as equals. They know that the birds will abandon a ledge before a rockfall, and that grazers sometimes gather where a hidden seep has surfaced. When the animals behave strangely, someone pays attention.

There is little need to raise the voice. In the narrow streets, words carry only a short distance before walls and turns catch them. People call to one another with taps on stone, whistles, or brief hand signs. A hammer’s rhythm can tell a neighbor whether someone is repairing a roof or cutting a new channel. In fog, a person may announce themself by dragging a stick along the wall. Quiet is not emptiness here. It is the way the Fold makes room for small warnings: water ticking into a basin, grit sliding across a roof, a flock lifting all at once.

Beyond the settlements, the people become fewer and farther apart. Those who travel between basins know the paths by their turns and textures: the pale shelf above the split ravine, the black stone that holds frost until noon, the notch where a rope bridge sways even when the wind seems still. They carry little, share what they can, and leave signs for those behind them. Some stretches remain unnamed, or have names used only by the people who cross them. The Fold is too large to belong entirely to anyone.

At dusk, the towns show themselves as scattered warmth: amber behind narrow shutters, a fire glowing in a roof court, the brief flash of a lantern moving under a covered stair. From far away, the people vanish into the stone and the animals into the dark between ledges. Up close, the whole country is busy with their traces: boot-polished footholds, feathers in the channels, small prints in cistern dust, smoke laid low by cold air. The inhabitants of the High Fold have not conquered its harshness. They have learned its habits, and, little by little, taught it theirs.
