The people do not speak of the ground as stable. They say it is *holding*, or *still setting*, or—when a street has opened under someone’s front step—*thinking about it*. Directions are given by what persists: the blue pipe with the dent in it, the wall with the green seam, the crane whose cabin faces inland. An address may be accurate for years and wrong by morning. Most people keep a second way of finding their own home.

They dress for damp and for work. Coats are waxed at the shoulders; boots have broad soles that spread the weight. Children wear bright cords around their wrists so they can be found in reed beds and construction dust. At doorways, shoes are left on heated grates, where they steam quietly through the evening. Nothing is thrown away merely because it has become part of the ground. A brick that comes up in the road is rinsed, examined for its old mortar, and stacked beside the curb. Sometimes a child will take one home and use it as a stool.

The inhabitants are skilled at noticing small changes. They hear a pump’s note alter before the gauges register it. They can tell from the smell beneath a stair whether the passage below is filling with water or merely sweating. They learn to read fresh cracks in plaster as others read the weather. A thin line means the wall is settling. A branching one means the ground is settling in more than one direction. If the line disappears under a shelf, the shelf is moved.

This knowledge is not considered special. It is simply how a person grows up here. Children play in the half-made places: under the raised railways, on warm pipes too low for adults to crawl beneath, among the young trees in lots not yet assigned to a department. They build rooms from packing sheets and call them houses until the city’s surveyors come to measure the lot. The surveyors are patient. They usually wait until the game is finished.

Families are not always arranged by walls. Some share a building whose floors have slipped into different generations: grandparents on the original ground level, parents one floor below it, a new baby’s room in a bright upper addition that may not be connected to either by the same stair. Neighbors take turns checking on one another when a passage changes. A person may leave for work by the front entrance and come back through the old laundry, now exposed behind the boiler house. Nobody is surprised to be welcomed home from an unexpected direction.

There are people who work for the city’s departments, though the departments themselves change names as often as they change their maps. Crews mark new ground, set gauges, cut vines away from switches, and carry out objects exposed by fresh excavation. They are practical, careful, and difficult to hurry. When they find a stretch of paving or a carved stone, they do not gather around it in wonder. They lay it on a tarpaulin, note its weight and texture, and ask whether it can be used again. Still, some keep private collections: a drawer of old bolts, a jar of pale seeds, a tile with a blue glaze that has not faded though no one knows how long it was underground.

Others live farther out, where the houses give way to reeds and warehouses. They raise small, cold-tolerant crops in beds made from salvaged panels. Their fences are low and easy to lift. They know which grasses can be woven, which roots loosen the soil, and which ones must be left alone because they draw the ground together. The city buys their cuttings, though no one agrees on what the cuttings are for. There is always another factory that needs them.

The creatures have their own routes through the built places. Pipe-mice travel the heated conduits in pairs, pausing at inspection hatches to listen. The pale birds leave twigs in ventilation housings, where the airflow keeps their nests dry. At night, the insects that gather on the windows face toward the flats. People have tried to turn this into a forecast. So far, they only know that when the insects all face the same way, the next morning will be misty.

The animals are tolerated, sometimes encouraged. A few households leave scraps near warm outlets. Children give names to anything that can be seen twice, even if the names contradict one another. Lift eel. Mud hen. Little drain horse. When an animal vanishes, they do not always know whether it has moved on, found a deeper passage, or been taken back into the ground. They keep looking for it anyway.

There are newcomers, too. They arrive with trunks, plans, and the expectation of a settled address. At first they ask how long the city has been there. The answer is usually another question: which part? Later they stop asking. They learn where not to stand during a pour, how to leave a lamp burning in an unoccupied room so a friend can find the entrance, and why the older residents pause at the end of a street before crossing. They begin to call the ground by its moods.

Some people say the basin is being built by the inhabitants. Others say the inhabitants are being made by the basin: given the habits and patience needed to live among unfinished things. Both explanations have their believers. Neither changes anyone’s morning. There are shifts to work, children to collect, drains to clear, walls to shore up before rain.

At dusk, people come home through the damp streets. Lights appear in rooms at different heights, some above the street and some below it. A window may glow in a wall that was not there the week before. Beneath the paving, water moves through narrow seams. Above it, roots find the warm pipes. The people listen without listening, each of them accustomed to the faint motion underfoot.

They do not expect the city to stay as they left it. They expect it to make room for them again.
