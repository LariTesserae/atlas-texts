What lives here cannot be separated cleanly from where it lives.

There are stems, mats, fronds, and roots—countless kinds of growth, some soft as wet cloth, some hard and jointed, some translucent with sap. Each occupies its own small portion of the world: a fold of leaf beneath another fold, a seam of soilless stone, the warm interior of a stalk. But none lives alone. Their roots lace together beneath the surface, over and under the young rock, so densely that the distinction between one plant and the next becomes a matter of scale. A single pale blade may belong to one body. The pressure it feels may be answered by a valley.

The roots are how the high country knows itself. They register the slow heave of stone, the arrival of water, the change in the weight of cloud. A tremor travels through them and becomes a change in growth: leaves turn, stems tighten, fresh runners seek the new cracks. The signal is not a thought passing from one mind to another. It is closer to weather passing through a forest—except the forest alters in response, and the response spreads far beyond the place where the weather began.

Its awareness is made of consequence. Where the ground will rise, the growth thickens before it rises. Where mist is gathering, leaves angle toward the coming damp. No part sees the whole country. No part needs to. Each receives what the others have already begun to change.

Perhaps there are individuals here. A stalk may live for a season, a root-mass for centuries, a colony for as long as new rock continues to emerge beneath it. But the larger thing has no clear edge and no single birth. It expands into space as space forms, taking hold of stone while it is still lifting. It does not conquer the land; it arrives with the land, and in places seems to help hold it together.

Its movements are immense and mostly too slow to watch. Fronds fold as mist thickens. Runners slide into fresh fractures. Whole slopes alter their posture by degrees, directing water into hidden channels. Then, under a pressure no eye can follow, a ridge swells beneath the living mat. The plants feel it coming. Their roots brace. Their pale stems fill with water, and the new stone breaks through into a world already waiting to cover it.

At night the network glows faintly—not with a light meant to guide or attract, but as sap moves through its crowded passages. The radiance reveals layers upon layers of living tissue, receding into green dimness. The sound is part of the same life: leaves rasping, stems groaning, roots knocking against rock. Its voice has no message in it that a person could hear. Yet the whole plain answers itself continuously, a vast exchange of movement and pressure beneath the steady rush.

Nothing runs through this place, because there is nowhere to run. Nothing watches from above. The inhabitants are rooted, many-bodied, and almost impossible to distinguish from the world that sustains them. They do not wait for the country to become finished.

They are what it is becoming.
