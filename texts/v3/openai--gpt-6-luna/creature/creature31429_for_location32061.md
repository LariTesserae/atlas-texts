At first, the place seems uninhabited. Nothing watches from the green chambers; nothing moves with the quick, separate purpose of an animal. Then a lane tightens gently around the sound of your footsteps. A pale leaf turns its wet underside toward you. Somewhere below, a root answers the pressure of your weight with a slow, wooden pulse.

The inhabitants are not separate from the city. They are its changing arrangements: knots of stalk and root that gather for a while into something like a body, then loosen back into walls, floors, and roofs. One may stand upright in a court, taller than a person, with a crown of narrow leaves that keeps opening and closing. Another may be only a dark swelling beneath the path, traveling by inches through the soil. Their shapes are never quite finished. A cluster that seems to have a head may be a flower turning toward light; a hand may prove to be a spray of searching roots, already entering the ground.

They have no faces, but they notice. They feel footsteps through the earth and changes in warmth across their leaves. They sense a newcomer as a disturbance in the weave: weight where there was none, breath altering the moisture in a narrow passage, light briefly blocked from a patch of green. In response, the city may open a way, close one, or grow a roof over the place where you have stopped. It is difficult to tell whether this is welcome, caution, curiosity, or simply the nearest growth answering a change in its surroundings.

Their thoughts—if thoughts is the right word—pass through contact. A root presses against another root; a stem leans into a curtain of leaves; a tremor travels outward, weakening and changing as it goes. The message is not a sentence but a redistribution: water drawn toward a dry court, a passage made firm beneath an unfamiliar step, a whole stand of pale stalks bending away from sudden heat. The deeper parts of the settlement know what the upper parts have felt, though not always at once. Sometimes a response arrives hours later as a new arch or a seedpod splitting above the place where you first paused.

There are individual lives here, but they do not stay individual. A narrow, upright being might loosen its roots from a wall and drift down the lane, its many small feet feeling for damp ground. It may stop beside a fallen trunk and unmake itself into the trunk’s softening surface. Days later, fresh shoots will appear there, bearing a slight inclination toward the route it took. What lived, what built, and what was built upon are not cleanly distinguished. The dead are not remembered as absences. They remain in the substance of what follows.

The inhabitants do not gather in crowds or occupy homes. They gather wherever conditions draw them together: in the courts where water lingers, in the passages where warmth collects, along the black seams in the earth. At times, many growths move in concert, and a neighborhood lifts, shifts, and settles as if some enormous intention had passed beneath it. At other times, each stem makes its own small adjustment, and the city’s motion is only visible in retrospect: a pillar leaning toward the sun, a bridge of roots thickened overnight, a square that has quietly become a garden.

Nothing here speaks, but the settlement is not silent. Its inhabitants knock and rasp and groan against one another; they announce themselves in the cracking of stems, in the patter of water released from leaf-cups, in the low vibration that travels up through the ground. When every leaf holds still, the pause feels less like peace than attention. The city is listening—not with ears, and perhaps not to anything in particular.

It does not seem to want visitors to leave, or to stay. It has no border between itself and the country beyond, only degrees of organization: the loose green spread at the edge, the crowded lanes, the dense interior where growth has learned to make rooms. You can pass through it without ever meeting a single inhabitant as a distinct creature. You can also be met everywhere: by a wall that bends slightly to admit you, by roots tightening under your feet, by a roof that opens above your head and then slowly closes behind.

The city lives as a multitude, and the multitude lives as the city. Its inhabitants are not hidden inside the place. They are the reason there is a place at all.
