At first, the upland seems to have only weather in it. Then a pale shape moves between two ribs of stone, and another follows—not a line of animals so much as a scattering, each keeping its own distance across the shallow valley. They are broad-bodied grazers with thick coats the color of old snow, dark muzzles, and feet that spread on loose scree. Their horns are short and blunt, worn smooth where they rub against rock. They crop what little grows around the springs: wiry grass, bitter leaves, the soft green threads that gather in warm cracks. They do not hurry. When the wind changes, the whole band lifts its head, listens, and moves on.

They follow no lasting trail. A route that was open in the morning may be glazed with ice by evening, or cut off by a new run of meltwater. The grazers cross where the ground feels firm, and stop where water remains liquid under a rim of frost. Their passage leaves shallow hoofprints in the gravel and clipped plants beside the warm seams. Sometimes the prints fill with clear water. Sometimes the next snowfall erases them before another animal comes by.

The hunters are harder to see. They walk on long legs, narrow-bodied and low-headed, with coats broken into gray and brown that take on the colors of wet stone. They travel alone or in small, unhurried groups, keeping to the ridgelines where they can watch the open ground. Their patience is not stillness exactly: a hunter may stand for a long time, then cross half a basin in a few quiet strides. When the grazers move, it moves too, not always closer. The land has many hollows and sudden edges, and the hunters seem to know that pursuit is wasted unless the ground offers its help.

Smaller lives gather where the larger ones pause. Dark birds perch in the lee of rock walls, their feathers puffed against the cold. They probe the mud around springs for insects and pick at what the grazers leave behind. Their calls are short and clean, but carry across the plateau until they sound farther away than the birds themselves. In the warmer seepage, fine-legged insects rise in wavering clouds. For a little while, they turn the gray air into a moving veil. Birds dart through it; the water ticks on beneath them.

There are lives that hardly seem to move at all. Mosses take hold in the seams where warmth reaches the surface, darkening the stone into soft, uneven patches. Low plants hunker behind pebbles, their leaves tough and close to the ground. In the wet hollows, pale films spread over rock and vanish under the next skim of ice. They make no bright carpet, no sheltered world beneath the grass. They hold on in small places, where mineral water, warmth, and a break in the wind coincide.

Even the stone has its habits here. Beneath certain hollows, pressure gathers until a spring exhales with a low hiss. Water runs over the black rock, gives up its warmth, and freezes again at the edges. Tarn ice shifts and groans. A fracture opens somewhere out of sight, and a faint tremor passes underfoot. The animals answer these changes without ceremony. They leave a hollow that has grown too wet, return to a spring that has opened, or stand listening while the ground makes one of its deep, nearly inaudible sounds.

Nothing living seems to claim the upland. The grazers do not remain long enough to wear paths into it; the hunters do not hold territories that can be marked against the next thaw. Birds return to the springs when insects rise, then are gone into the weather. Plants occupy the warmest cracks and yield their place to frost, water, or shifting stone. The life here is not sparse because it has failed to arrive. It is sparse because everything is still negotiating where it can endure.

So the plateau is inhabited in intervals: a band of pale backs crossing the valley, a hunter pausing above them, birds gathered at a steaming pool, green-dark growth tucked into a fissure. Between these visits, wind passes over bare rock and shallow water. The inhabitants are part of the upland’s unfinishedness. They come, feed, shelter, and move on, while the ground beneath them continues its slow, abrupt work of becoming.
