The people who live there are difficult to distinguish from the work they do.

They wear layers of fiber cloth, pale and gray, patched so often that no one remembers the first fabric. Their sleeves are tied close at the wrist against husk dust. Their boots have broad, soft soles, which make a person’s passage quiet over grit and keep the feet from slipping where new stone has shed its skin. In the cultivated districts, many go bareheaded. The air is still, and there is little to fall from above except the occasional flake of ceiling.

They move with the unhurried attention of people who know that the ground may change while they are looking elsewhere. A worker crossing a factory floor checks the joints in the walls by touch. A child knows which bridges flex under a loaded car and which groves are safe to climb. When a seam opens in a street, people do not gather around it in alarm. They mark its edges, set a warm-fiber lamp beside it, and begin asking what can be planted there.

They speak softly, though not because noise is forbidden. Sound travels oddly across the open plain: a dropped tool can seem to ring from a district a mile away, while a conversation disappears at the edge of a root-mat. Most people learn early to judge distance by the shape of a voice. They use hand signals around the presses, and touch when the machinery is loud. A palm laid against a wall can carry a message farther than a shout, though nobody agrees on how much of that is practical and how much is habit.

Their lives are arranged around the plants, but not in the manner of gardeners tending a possession. They bargain with them. A colony offered warmth will thicken over a crack; one deprived of light may loosen its grip on a beam. The older growers know how long to leave a new support uncut, and can tell, by the fine give of a root-cable, whether a bridge is settling or simply taking hold. They call some plants by the names of people who found them, some by the districts they built, and some by what they do: latch, brace, hush, carry. They are careful not to say that plants understand. They are careful, too, not to say that they do not.

In the lower factories, crews work beside slow presses and winding spools. They cut and shape the pale fibers, bind them into beams, and return every usable scrap to the beds. A day’s work may end with a worker leaning against a warm housing, listening for a change in the machine’s hum. Not a breakdown, necessarily. A new vibration might mean a bearing wearing thin, or the roots outside have reached a seam and begun to draw heat from it. The workers are proud of their machines, but no one speaks of a machine as if it were entirely separate from the living insulation wrapped around it.

Children are allowed to wander farther than visitors would think sensible. They know how to cross the open stretches where a fall of stone might come down, how to duck beneath a moving freight line, and how to tell an old animal track from one made that night. They collect mineral flakes in their pockets and small, shed husks from the groves. Sometimes they build miniature bridges across grooves in the grit, threading them with plant fiber. The smallest children leave gaps in the structures for creatures to pass through. The older ones insist this is because the creatures are useful. Both things may be true.

The animals are part of the settlement’s ordinary traffic. Pale, quick-footed nesters live inside the walls, where they feed on husks and the soft mineral growths gathered around warm channels. They slip along overhead cords and disappear into openings too narrow for a hand. People know the sound of a family moving through the ceiling: a brief patter, then a pause, then the tiny scrape of claws finding another strand. They leave bowls of sifted husk near certain factory walls, though they say the animals would find the food anyway.

The larger creatures are not so familiar. Their tracks appear in the grit when the lights are low, paired impressions following a path that crosses the city without bending around its buildings. No one has seen one clearly. Some say the marks belong to an old kind of scavenger, driven into the unlit reaches by the expansion of the lamps. Others say that anything heavy enough to make those prints would not need to hide. The freight crews keep watch when they pass through the open districts, but they do not speed up. The animals, whatever they are, have learned the schedule. So have the people.

And then there is the city’s other population: the fine activity within the walls.

It begins as ordinary signs. A support thickens without anyone having led fresh growth into it. A patch of leaves loosens in a dark chamber, though no lamp has been turned on. A tapping travels through the rootwork in the hours when the presses are idle. People test the wall temperature, inspect the joints, look for nests. Sometimes they find something: a colony reaching toward a warm seam, a small animal tucked behind a brace, a loosened fiber knocking against stone. Sometimes there is nothing to find.

The workers have names for the patterns. They speak of a wall “taking breath,” of a pillar “listening,” of roots “remembering a load.” These phrases are useful, and their usefulness has kept them in circulation longer than anyone can say. No one writes them into maintenance manuals. No one has managed to remove them from everyday speech.

Some residents believe the plants are only responding to heat, pressure, light, and the repeated touch of human hands. Some believe the city is becoming aware of itself, not all at once, but in the gradual way a body learns where it has been hurt. Most have no settled opinion. They know that a bridge can be grown stronger if enough travelers use it, that a dark grove may shift its leaves toward a lamp after years of neglect, and that the hollow is still changing underneath them. They are willing to live with causes they cannot yet name.

At the city’s edge, the people are fewer and the buildings more provisional. A household may be given a length of root-cord, a tool kit, and permission to follow a new light shaft outward. They establish a shelter, test the stone, and plant a first line of supports. For a while, the place is only a roof held between two rocks, a patch of leaves gathered around the heat, a rail laid across open ground. Later there may be a street. Later still, someone may speak of the district as though it had always been there.

Those who leave the lit routes do not vanish from the city’s imagination. Their names are kept in factory ledgers and sometimes on the beams they helped raise. A returning traveler may bring a new mineral sample, a seedlike knot of root, or simply a story of a chamber where the ceiling climbed higher than the lamps could reach. People listen, ask practical questions, then leave the new thing somewhere warm and watch what it does.

They are not a loud people. They do not fill the hollow with festivals or voices. But they have their small customs: a hand placed on a newly repaired wall before its braces are removed; a pause when the first leaves loosen after an artificial night; the tapping of a tool against a factory housing to announce a shift change. They mark births with a thread of pale fiber tied around the family’s doorframe, where it will eventually be swallowed by the growth. They mark deaths less visibly. The dead are remembered in the work: in a bridge that holds, in a grove that took root, in the exact angle of a support someone once insisted should be left alone.

If the city has a single living shape, it is not a people gathered in a place so much as a place that has begun to gather its people into its shape. The residents know the routes of the rails and the habits of the plants; the animals know the warm channels and the intervals between freight cars; the roots find the cracks and supports, the lamps and heat. Each learns the others’ movements without quite learning their thoughts.

For now, this is enough to live by.

When the tapping comes from inside a wall, someone listens. Then, if the wall is sound, they go on with the day.
