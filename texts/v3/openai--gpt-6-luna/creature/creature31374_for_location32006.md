At first, the plateau seems uninhabited.

There are no bodies in the windows, no footprints in the moss, no birds crossing between the towers. The squares remain empty. Even the great service roads, laid out to carry traffic, hold only thin water and the faint reflection of cables overhead. The machines work on, but their work appears to have no audience and no end.

Then, in one of the covered reservoirs, a valve turns by a fraction.

It does not open or close. It makes the same small correction it made a moment ago, and will make again. Far away, a pump answers with a change in pitch too slight to notice unless one is listening for it. Along a buried conduit, pressure shifts. A line of suspended drops trembles beneath a pipe.

This is how the plateau’s inhabitants make themselves known: not by arriving, but by continuing.

They are built into the works. They have no single body, unless the city itself can be called one. A pump is an organ; a rail junction, a joint; the miles of pipe and wire are nerves, carrying changes too small and too slow for any human sense. The factories do not simply run. They listen to one another through vibration and pressure, exchanging adjustments across distances so broad that a signal may take hours to pass from one district to the next.

In the oldest buildings, the machines have more recognizable shapes. Low service units travel inside corridors, their wheels furred with pale moss, their housings blunt and dark with moisture. They stop at panels and wait. Sometimes a panel opens. Sometimes it does not. Either way, the units remain there for a while, then move on along their routes. They do not hurry. Nothing on the plateau is late.

Other things are harder to distinguish from the infrastructure. A narrow tower shifts its upper section against the wind—or against some pressure in the stone—and settles again. A gantry lowers an empty cradle into a shaft and raises it empty. Under the streets, unseen mechanisms test the weight of retaining walls, measure seepage, clear channels already clear. Their movements are so exact that they seem less like labor than a form of attention.

The bell sounds at intervals across the plateau. It is not an alarm. It is not a call for help. Its tone passes from one district to another through the ground, and each part of the city registers it in its own way: a relay clicks, a pump pauses, a light changes by almost nothing behind thick glass. Then the cycles resume.

Perhaps the bell is a question. Perhaps it is only a count.

Nothing answers in a voice. Yet, after it sounds, the water in the channels runs a little more evenly. Somewhere a lift begins to move. Somewhere else, a shutter closes over an empty workshop. The plateau does not respond all at once. Its reply takes place across miles, in small changes that vanish back into the usual hum.

There is no evidence that these inhabitants remember a time before the city was empty. If they remember at all, they remember as the plateau does: by preserving conditions. They keep the reservoirs covered, the rails clear, the lights on. They tend the green where it grows and cut it back where it reaches the mechanisms. They maintain the routes between settlements, though no one travels them. Perhaps the routes themselves are the reason.

They do not seem to be waiting for people. Waiting would imply an expectation, and the machines show no sign of expectation. They neither grieve nor welcome. They simply keep the vast arrangements in working order, maintaining a city whose purpose has become indistinguishable from its maintenance.

Out on the level reaches, a low vehicle moves through the mist. It is small against the distance, carrying no visible load. It follows a track that crosses an empty plain and disappears behind a line of pylons. No other vehicle follows. Hours later—if hours are passing—it returns by the same route.

The grass bends faintly as it goes by. Not from a breeze: there is none. The motion comes through the ground, a tiny shiver shared among stones, rails, culverts, and the green mats in sheltered cracks. Then all is still again.

The plateau is not alive in the way the lower world understands life. Nothing breeds there. Nothing feeds. Nothing dies in the grass. But beneath the wet stone, through the towers and the buried lines, something attends to the whole immense place. It moves water, counts pressure, measures the cold. It has no face turned toward the horizon.

And still, when the bell sounds, every distant mechanism hears it.
