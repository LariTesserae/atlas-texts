The people who belong to the roof are easy to mistake for visitors. There are so few of them, and they leave so little behind. A shelter is only a windbreak of leaning slabs; a fire is a dark ring of stones; a track disappears where the rock changes color. They do not build to last. The country has taught them that anything fixed is only waiting to be moved.

They live in scattered households, sometimes no more than two or three together, keeping to the green basins and the sheltered folds beneath the rim. Their bodies are spare and weather-browned, their hands broad-fingered and heavily callused from climbing. They move carefully, but not timidly. A ledge that looks impossible to cross from below is, to them, a familiar path with one loose step and a place to pause. They carry little: a skin for water, a wrapped blade, a few lengths of cord, and small flat vessels for catching the moisture that gathers beneath overhangs.

They know where water will be before it appears. They read the cloud combing over the high rim, the dampening of certain stones, the faint green in cracks where no rain has fallen for weeks. After a storm, they walk the channels while they are still running, filling their cisterns and marking any pool that has become clouded with grit. A pool is not simply a pool. It is a place to return to, unless the cliff above it has begun to speak differently.

They listen to the stone. The country makes too much noise for conversation to carry far, so the people speak close together, in low voices and with their hands. But they can pick apart the sounds that matter: the dry click of a new fracture; the shuddering murmur of a slope preparing to slip; the hollow note that means a ledge has lost its support. They do not always know what the rock will do. They know when it has changed its mind.

Their young learn to be still before they learn to climb. They sit near the edge of a sheltered hollow and listen while an elder names the sounds passing through it: wind in a crack, water beneath stone, grit shifting in a channel, a distant fall. The lesson is not to understand every sound, but to discover which ones can be ignored. A person who startles at every rumble will never sleep here.

There are other lives on the roof, though most are seen only by their traces. Small, dark creatures come out after rain to drink from the shallow runnels, then vanish into crevices before the sun dries them. Pale insects shelter under loose flakes of stone. Far below the rim, broad-winged hunters circle on the rising air, almost motionless until they fold themselves toward some unseen target. In the wet hollows, ferns uncurl quickly, and the bent trees hold their leaves close against the wind. The people take only what is ready to be taken. They have watched too many green places disappear beneath a fall of rock to believe that abundance will remain.

At night they sleep lightly, beneath overhangs or within the low mouths of caves. They can hear storms before the clouds reach them: a softening of the wind, then a long hush, as if the whole upland has drawn breath. Sometimes they wake to the sound of rain running through channels that have been dry for months. Sometimes to a collapse far away, its rolling clamor arriving after the danger has already passed.

By morning they may find the land altered. A familiar ledge gone. A new hollow holding water. A trail interrupted, a shelter buried, a ravine opened wider to the sky. They do not call this ruin. They gather their things, look for the next sound, and move on across the old stone.
