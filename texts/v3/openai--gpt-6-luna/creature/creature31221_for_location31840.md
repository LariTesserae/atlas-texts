The people of the stone are easy to mistake for shadows that have learned to walk.

They are small, though not childlike, and wrapped in layered cloth the colors of dust, clay, and old leaves. Their hoods are broad enough to shade their faces from the fierce light. Beneath them, their eyes are dark and bright, accustomed to reading the trembling of a vane or the faintest movement in a curtain of dust. They carry narrow baskets, folded tools, and little stone markers. They do not hurry. Nothing here can be hurried for long.

The wind is too loud for ordinary conversation. The residents speak with their hands, with a touch to the sleeve, with a finger drawn across the palm. When words are needed, they lean close, mouth to ear. They have learned to hear other things: the change in pitch as air moves through a tower, a bridge’s small shiver before a gust, the dry click that means a roof vane has caught. Their children learn these sounds before they learn the names of streets.

They live among the old rooms, though few rooms seem to belong to only one family. A shut door may open for someone who knows where to press; another may stay closed through a lifetime. Inside, there is little furniture. Clay shelves hold cups, seeds, tools, and pieces of writing too old to be read aloud. Some residents sleep in the houses; others sleep in warm niches in the stone, close to the routes where the air passes most steadily. They leave offerings of mineral salts and fallen leaves in the cracks—not to a god, they would say, but to keep the cracks kind.

Their work is mostly attention. Each day they walk the courts and move the small markers that show the wind’s paths. They mend a stair, clear a slot, turn a vane by a finger’s breadth. They gather the papery blossoms when they have dried and sort the fine grains according to color and weight. The plants are not planted so much as persuaded: their roots are guided through cracks, their fans trimmed where they crowd a passage, their seeds carried to ledges where the sun falls at the right hour. A resident can tell, from the angle of a leaf, how the chamber’s weather has changed.

They have no word for stillness that means silence. The city is never silent. When the winds align and the great openings begin to sound, the residents pause where they are. Some put a hand on the wall. Some close their eyes. The smallest children are held against a grown body so they can feel the low notes through the ribs. They say the city is breathing. They do not say that it is asleep.

There are not many of them. Whole streets may pass a season without a door opening. Yet no one calls the place empty. A figure appears in a sunlit court, pauses to read the dust, and is gone beneath a bridge. Somewhere a door shifts on its stone hinge. Somewhere a dry green orchard folds its leaves against an unseen gust.

Under the paving, pale many-legged creatures move through the roots. The residents leave them room. Perhaps the creatures keep the root-felt loose; perhaps they have always been there. Once, long ago, someone carved one on a wall, with a careful line beneath it that may be a name or a warning. The carving has softened until it resembles a natural mark in the stone.

The people do not think of themselves as guarding the city. They are simply the ones who are here when the wind turns. Their grandparents were here before them; their children will learn the routes, the signs, and the old ways of listening. Outside the seam, the pale stone takes the sun and gives nothing away. Inside it, small hooded figures cross the bright courts, keeping time with the air.
