At first, the Upper Hollow seems to be inhabited by ordinary things: little warm-bodied animals under the leaves, dark-winged creatures in the branches, something heavy pacing the rim. They have the practical shapes of animals—backs, feet, mouths, fur or feather—but the darkness makes it difficult to decide where one ends and another begins. A pair of bright eyes might belong to a small creature crossing the path, or to something much larger watching from farther away.

The small ones are not shy so much as untroubled by being seen. They pass close to a visitor’s boots, pause to sniff the rain on the leather, and go on. Their feet know the basin’s soft places and its sharp ones. They take no notice of the old structures, except that they never cross the paths of water which bend around them. Over time, they make paths of their own: narrow, damp trails through the leaves, all leading somewhere and none seeming to lead out.

Some are quick, though they never look hurried. A claw-tick, a wing-brush, a brief stirring of the grasses—and then the creature is gone. Later, perhaps, it is there again on the opposite side of the basin, dry-backed and moving at the same unhurried pace. If it remembers having been seen, it gives no sign. There is nothing plaintive in the calls they make, nor anything welcoming. They sound like statements, made to the dark and left there.

The larger animals are harder to place. One travels the rim with its back to the basin, as if inspecting the mountain rather than guarding the Hollow. It is broad enough that its passage can be felt through the wet stone. No one has seen it descend. Yet now and then, in the low ground, a bellow rises from beneath the pools, and the water wrinkles without rippling. The rim-walker stops when it hears this. It may stand motionless for hours. Then it continues on, having apparently received an answer.

The central tree is also inhabited, though not always by anything one can point to. Its low branches are crowded with small shapes that are not birds. They cling to the warm leaves and turn their heads together when the basin falls silent. In the mist, their outlines soften until they seem to be knots in the wood. If approached, they scatter without flapping, slipping from branch to branch, and the tree’s leaves tremble long after they have gone. Its flowers open among them, pale and velvety, releasing their cellar-sweet smell. The creatures feed on the flowers, or perhaps only touch them with their mouths.

There are larger hollows in the tree, but none deep enough to enter. The trunk is warm even after cold rain. Sometimes a visitor placing a hand against it feels a slow movement inside: not a pulse, exactly, but a shift of weight, as if something on the far side of the tree had turned over in its sleep.

The old structures have no evident inhabitants. Nothing nests in the squat tower. No animal shelters beneath the arch. Even moss stops short of their smooth surfaces. But their presence can be felt in the way the living world makes room for them. A fern unfurls toward a pin in the stone, then grows around it without touching. A runnel changes direction over the years, as though it has been gently asked to. The animals’ trails shift too, first by inches and then by whole turns of the basin.

The pins sometimes move. If a person watches one, it will remain quite still. The fraction of a degree by which it has turned becomes apparent only later, when the paths and pools have begun to favor some new arrangement. The small animals adapt at once. The larger one on the rim pauses, listens, and continues. Whatever understanding passes between them is too slow to resemble a signal. It has more in common with the way water finds a seam.

Perhaps the Hollow’s inhabitants are the animals, and the tree, and the life under the moss. Perhaps the structures are only the old remains of something that no longer lives there. But when the calls rise together, they have the cadence of a place speaking through many mouths. When they stop, the basin seems to be listening.

A visitor can stay long enough to learn the faces of some of its creatures. One small, blunt-nosed animal will come to the same stone shelf each evening. A dark-winged shape will cross the tree’s lowest branch whenever the mist thickens. The rim-walker may pass at the far edge, its body lost in the unmeasured dark, while the ground carries the quiet weight of each step.

Then, one evening, the blunt-nosed animal does not come. The branch remains empty. The calls begin from somewhere under the earth, and the rain runs upward along the mineral seams. By morning, if morning can be said to arrive, a new path has appeared through the grasses.

The Hollow’s residents take it without hesitation. They have been here longer than the path, and will probably be here after it changes again. Nothing in their manner suggests that they own the place. They simply belong to it, with the slow certainty of water in stone.
