The people who live here are mostly known by their hours. They arrive before the weak light has made much difference, or leave when the lamps are already shining on the wet pavement. They wear boots that have learned the puddles, and jackets that smell faintly of oil even after they have been hung up. They know which doors stick in the heat, which grates take water slowly, which motor has begun to complain. Their voices rise over the machinery for a moment, then sink back into it. They do not seem to own the place so much as keep it in working order.

There may be homes on the streets beyond the sheds, though few windows invite a look inside. A lit kitchen window, a curtain pulled against the damp, a bicycle leaning beneath an eave: small signs that the district is not only a workplace. The people here have learned to live with its sounds. A pump’s knock can be slept through. A change in that knock cannot. In the morning, someone will notice the chain at the basin has gone quiet, or that the water at one outfall is running cloudy, before anyone has thought to look.

The animals have their own shifts and familiar routes. Gulls take the roofs first, claiming the warm ridges and the best view of whatever comes loose near the water. They are noisy, quarrelsome, and entirely at home among the stacks. Swallows work the lower air, turning sharply beneath the sheds to catch insects in the vapor. Frogs call from culverts no one sees into; their voices make the drains seem occupied even when the streets are empty. Small fish gather where the outfalls breathe into the basin, flashing briefly in the tea-colored water. Rats travel the retaining walls with the certainty of people who know every shortcut.

The insects belong to the heat. They gather at lamps, cling to the undersides of pipes, rise in soft clouds from the wet ground when footsteps pass. By morning they are gone from the light, but not from the place. There are beetles beneath the broken brick, larvae in the standing channels, small lives turning the district’s leaks and scraps into something usable. Moss works patiently in the shade. Reeds bend at the basin’s edge, scarcely more than a fringe, but they hold their ground.

And then there is the machinery. It does not live, exactly, but its continued attention makes it difficult to call it dead. Pumps lift water. Belts carry weight. Motors thrum behind slatted doors, each keeping a different measure. The pump house has its blunt, regular knock, like a heart that has been given a job and intends to do it. Workers listen to these sounds without ceremony. So do the rats, the frogs, and the fish, each in its own way.

Nothing here seems to wait for a great event. The inhabitants know the small changes: a pipe beginning to sweat, a gull missing from its usual roof, a drain running a little too freely after the rain. They come and go, feed, mend, call, sleep, and begin again. The place holds them without warmth or welcome, but with a kind of reliable room. Between water and machinery, they have made themselves at home.
