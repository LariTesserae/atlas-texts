There is no single animal that owns the highland.

At first it seems to belong to the birds. They fill its upper edges: small, quick-bodied things wedged into drain-mouths and roof seams, nesting in old mortar and under the lips of eaves. They know the covered ways better than the stone remembers them. They fly through the dim lanes without hesitation, banking around corners, skimming just above the steps. Their nests are made from grass, feathers, root-fibres, and whatever the wind has carried up from the lower terraces. In the crowded season, every ledge is contested. A bird may be driven from its hollow only to find, a few steps away, another hollow already warm with eggs.

Then the place reveals its quieter inhabitants. Mice move in the walls, following the channels of old timber and root. Lizards keep to the sunlit faces of the upper terraces, flattened against the stone until a fly passes. Spiders string fine webs between carved spouts and the leaves of damp beds. In the cistern courts, frogs wait beneath the green water, their throats swelling at dusk. When rain comes, they call so loudly that the sound climbs the stairways and seems to gather under every roof.

The warm chambers belong to insects. They rise from drains in brief dark clouds, settle on leaves, and vanish into cracks when a bat passes overhead. At evening, bats pour from the roof hollows in long, wavering streams. They make the air above the terraces feel suddenly occupied. Their flight stirs the birds into motion, and for a little while the whole upper level flickers with wings.

Larger animals use the highland differently. Goats climb where a person would hesitate, picking their way along retaining walls and narrow ridges of tile. They crop the leaves that reach through the masonry and leave droppings in the dry corners. Pigs root among the planted beds, turning over the soft earth behind the low walls; they know which courtyards stay wet and where fallen fruit gathers. Dogs sleep across the stairs, rising only when the traffic of hooves or wings passes too close. Their coats are rough, their ears nicked, their bodies accustomed to the damp chill of the shade.

None of them is tame in quite the old sense, though some are familiar with one another. The dogs do not chase every bird. The birds do not scatter at every hoofstep. They have learned the tolerances of a place in which no creature can keep a whole passage to itself. A goat will wait on a landing while a flock streams around it. A dog may lift its head as bats pour overhead, then put it down again. The smallest lives go on around the largest with the confidence of long residence.

They feed on what the mountain makes and what the construction catches. Seeds collect in gutters. Fruit ripens in the sheltered beds. Insects gather where warmth and moisture meet. Moss, fallen leaves, feathers, droppings, and scraps of dead plants wash into the channels and settle in the cistern margins, where new growth takes hold. The walls are not merely boundaries to them. They are soil, shelter, lookout, nesting ground, sunning stone, and the edges of pools.

Life is thickest where the water pauses. In shaded courts, fern fronds loosen over the stones, and frogs hide beneath them. Birds come to drink and argue at the pool’s rim. Beetles turn in the shallows. Higher up, where the rain drains quickly and the light lasts longer, the lizards and seed-eaters gather. Between these places run the covered lanes: darker, cooler, busy with feet, wings, and things that move only when the rest of the highland has quieted.

There are seasons when the whole country seems to lift its voice. Rain fills the tanks; frogs begin calling; insects hatch in the warm chambers; birds crowd the eaves with new broods. Later, when the water dwindles, the animals concentrate around the pools and trickling channels. Some leave along the ridge. Others follow the fruiting vines from terrace to terrace. The goats descend to browse, then climb back to the dry upper walls. Bats continue their evening routes, taking the same invisible paths through the roofs.

The highland does not seem to them like a ruin. It is simply the shape of the world: an immense arrangement of ledges, hollows, wet places, dry places, and passages that bend out of sight. They inherit it without knowing it was made. Their nests soften its corners; roots widen its cracks; hooves polish the steps; rain carries their lives down through the old channels. The builders are gone, but the inhabitants keep the place in motion.
