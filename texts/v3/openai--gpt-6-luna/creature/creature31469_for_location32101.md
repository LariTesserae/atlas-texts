The most numerous inhabitants are the narrow ones.

They pass through the arches in long, jointed lengths, pale as old bone beneath their translucent skin. Each is scarcely wider than a finger, but many are longer than a person. They swim by folding themselves, section by section, so that motion travels down the body like a ripple through cloth. Their eyes have dwindled to dark grains beneath the skin. They do not need to see the next arch. They know it by the pressure of water changing against their sides.

At first they seem to be passing through the district by chance. One slips along a wall; another turns beneath a stair; a hundred more drift in the black beyond. But their paths are not quite separate. When the current shifts, they feel it in the joints of one another, or in the little changes each body leaves behind. They turn together. The movement travels from terrace to terrace, not as a command but as a feeling passed along: water coming, water slackening, something large approaching and already gone.

The smallest are born in the mortar seams, where their eggs cling in clear, beadlike strings. They hatch almost invisible and feed on the thin films that coat the stone. Most are eaten before they are large enough to leave the seam. Those that survive emerge into the open, where the blind folded things wait beneath the ledges, and the pale soft-bodied animals pack themselves into every hollow. The narrow ones eat what they can catch, and are eaten in turn. Their lives are short beside the life of the terraces, but each generation finds the same routes worn smooth by the bodies before it.

They do not understand the rooms as rooms. A chamber is a place where the current gathers. A doorway is a narrowness that makes the current quick. A basin is a pocket where food settles. Yet they prefer the old builders’ shapes. They lay their eggs in the protected corners, sleep curled beneath the stairs, and gather where the stone makes a low, steady pressure against the body. Their lives have taken the measure of the architecture.

There are other inhabitants, smaller and more fixed. They grow in soft layers inside the seams, almost the color of the mortar, and release minute young into the water when the current is right. The narrow ones graze them. The smaller blind animals graze the narrow ones’ eggs. Nothing here is separate for long. A life leaves a trace in the stone, the trace becomes food, and the food becomes another life pressed into the same dark.

The old figures in the walls have not escaped them. One small, faceless shape beside a threshold is furred over with pale bodies that open and close without sound. The creatures do not gather around it in reverence. They gather because the hollow beside its shoulder catches drifting matter. Still, when the current changes, the colony bends in unison across the figure, and for a little while it seems to move.

The narrow ones have no names for the terraces, or for the tower, or for the place where they live. They have no need of names. What they possess instead is a memory distributed among thousands of bodies: the remembered force of a passage, the remembered warmth of a niche, the remembered turn that keeps a body from striking stone. No single creature holds the whole district. But together, over generations, they have learned its shape more thoroughly than any builder could have intended.

Sometimes one leaves the others and enters a passage ending at a mineral-sealed door. It presses its head to the pale crust and feels the water trembling on the far side. Then it turns back. Perhaps the passage is empty. Perhaps something lives beyond it, in a chamber where the current has not changed for a very long time. The creature does not know the difference. It carries the door’s pressure back into the district, and the others receive it through their bodies.

So the Upper Quiet is full of small, passing knowledge. The water shifts; the narrow ones turn. Sediment gathers; the folded things open beneath the ledges. A seam darkens with eggs. A colony feeds and thins, feeds and thickens. Above them, the old tower holds its last dull gleam. They pass through its rooms without seeing the hands that made them, and the hands, worn smooth, remain around them like the walls of a shell.
