At first, it seems that nothing lives there except the plants.

They occupy the ledges and slots with patient confidence, their leaves overlapping wherever light reaches. They do not turn toward a visitor, or startle at a passing shadow. Their roots follow the nutrient lines because the lines are there; their stems lean into the slow circulation because it carries what they need. If they have any sense of the district around them, it is measured in moisture, warmth, and the pressure of neighboring roots.

The machinery seems no more alive. It draws water up, sends it through the filters, and lets it fall again. It ticks at intervals. It keeps the lamps almost—but not quite—steady. Its work is so continuous that no one action seems to belong to it.

But stay long enough, and the division between the two becomes difficult to keep. A root finds a warm seam in the wall and enters it. The system detects the obstruction and adjusts its flow by a fraction. The altered flow feeds a mat of green; the mat thickens, cools a sensor, and changes the pump’s rhythm. Nothing has chosen anything. Still, the place answers itself.

Its life is not a creature hidden among the leaves. It has no eyes to catch the light, no mouth to call through the pipes. It is the slow exchange between root and conduit, leaf and lamp, reservoir and pump: a body distributed through the district, too large to gather into one shape and too quiet to announce that it has begun.

Perhaps it is only an arrangement that works. Perhaps, given enough years, it will become something that knows it works. For now it grows into every space it can spare, while the machinery learns to make room. In the clear water, the whole crown continues its careful, unhurried breathing.
