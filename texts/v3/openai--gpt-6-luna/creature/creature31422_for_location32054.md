At first, it seems the only thing that lives here is the blunt-snouted amphibian, crouched in its cup of water. It is the size of a fist, pale as a root left too long in the dark. Its eyes are open but not watchful in any familiar way. Each time a hollow stalk tightens and draws water upward, its flanks rise and fall with the faintest delay, as if the chamber is breathing and the animal has learned to breathe along.

It does not hunt so much as receive. Clear-winged insects settle on the water near its mouth; larvae pass through the felt beneath it, stirring the sediment. Sometimes it snaps at one, quick as a dropped pebble. More often it remains still, letting the small lives arrive and depart around its body. Its skin gathers moisture from the air. Its toes are spread wide for the slick leaves. It has never needed to leave.

There are others beneath the floor, behind the walls, inside the ceiling: pale, many-jointed things that patrol the rootwork; soft-bodied feeders that vanish into the compressed stems; little creatures that leave only wakes or nibbled edges behind them. They make no colony that can be seen all at once. Their traffic is dispersed through the chamber, like water through a sponge. When one disappears, another finds the same damp hollow. The place notices, if it notices at all, by continuing.

The plants are not a backdrop to these lives. They are the deep, slow residents—the ones that keep the rooms warm and the basin full. A hollow stalk draws water up through its body with a steady inward pull. A seed head turns, clicks, and lets fall a measured dusting of grain. Beneath the floor, runners carry the nourishment gathered in one patch to another patch that has gone thin. The bulbed stem strikes its stone, again and again, without anger or fatigue. Its small hammering shakes loose food for things too tiny to be seen.

In time, the whole pocket begins to seem like a single creature, though there is no point at which it could be said to begin. The roots are its ribs, the basin a dark, unblinking eye—or perhaps only a place where water happens to collect. The stalks pump. The leaves sweat and tremble. The animals pass through its folds as blood cells might pass through a body, except that none of them belongs to it, and none is necessary in precisely the way a heart is necessary. They are participants, not parts.

It has no voice beyond its workings. Its speech is the broad rush of water under root, the dry patter overhead, the repeated thud against stone. When a drop gathers beneath the ceiling and falls into the basin, the sound is small enough to be mistaken for nothing. Yet the amphibian hears it. So do the larvae, in whatever way larvae hear. The plants register the change as a disturbance in their wetness and continue pumping.

Nothing here watches for intruders. Nothing guards a threshold. If a paw or hand were to push aside the reeds and find the hidden room, its inhabitants would not scatter as one. Some would freeze. Some would sink from sight. A seed head would keep turning; the stalks would draw their water. The chamber would make room for the disturbance, slowly and without understanding it as a disturbance.

It has lived through generations of marsh above it, through floods and dry spells, through the rise and fall of plants whose names are gone. It does not remember these things. It keeps them instead in the shape of its roots, in the warmth held under the floor, in the old basin that water still finds. Its life has no clear beginning, and no face to meet yours.

Still, there is a kind of contentment in its noise: a small, tireless world doing what it does, hidden under the reeds, with no need to become anything else.
