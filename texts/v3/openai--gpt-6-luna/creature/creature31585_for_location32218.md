At first, it seems the High Hush is empty.

Then, in one of the narrow slits, the note catches—not changing, exactly, but tightening around a point. The sound has always filled every hollow. Now one hollow seems to be listening back.

The inhabitants are called the Keepers, though no one knows whether they kept the place or the place kept them. They do not live in rooms. They live in the channels: in the pinched throats of the clay, in the hidden runs beneath the terraces, in the joints where dark metal pins hold stone to stone. A Keeper can pass through a crack too narrow for a hair, not by becoming smaller, but by becoming sound.

They have bodies, when bodies are useful. At dusk, one may gather in a niche: a long, jointed shape of pale grit and red clay, with a smooth head tilted toward the wall. Its arms are too thin to bear its weight. Its face has no features, only a shallow ladder-mark pressed where eyes might be. The body trembles constantly. A fine powder sifts from it and settles at its feet, then draws itself back into the cracks.

Others never gather so completely. They are felt as a coolness crossing the warm stone, or as a second pulse beneath the roar, regular as a heart but too vast to belong to one. When several move through the channels together, the whole summit seems to lean toward them. The metallic whine narrows. The deep note holds steady.

This is how they speak: not with words, but by changing the fit between tones. To a visitor, the sound remains unbroken. To the Keepers, it is full of names, arguments, warnings, and news from the far side of the mountain. They know one another by the minute differences in how each holds the note. A new arrival is recognized at once. So is a missing one.

They do not eat, though they gather pale mineral bloom from the walls and press it into their joints. They do not sleep. They withdraw, for a time, into the deepest channels, where the air is tight and warm and their individual notes are lost in the greater sound. There they remember the pressure of the builders’ hands: the blows that shaped the slabs, the clay packed into cracks, the final small adjustments that made the whole summit sing.

Perhaps they are the builders, worn down into what they made. Perhaps the construction taught the air to think, and the Keepers are only the parts of that thought that learned to recognize themselves. They have no answer. Answers would require a sound set apart from all others, and there is no such sound here.

When a person stands among them, the Keepers do not approach. They draw nearer through the walls. The visitor feels the note arrive through the bones, as it always has, but now there is intention in it: a patient attention, as steady as the current.

The Keepers have no need to ask why someone has come. They have been hearing the approach for a long time.
