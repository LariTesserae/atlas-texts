What lives there is difficult to separate from where it lives.

At first, there are only the small bodies: glass-shelled grazers moving beneath the leaves, soft creatures folding into droplets, blind swimmers nosing through the pools. They feed without seeming to take anything away. Behind them, the mat closes; ahead, it loosens just enough to let them pass. A creature may vanish into a wall and reappear several ridges farther on, carried through the green as though the hollow had briefly remembered how to be hollow.

The plants are not merely crowded together. They share a patience. A root does not reach toward water so much as become the route water takes. A frond uncurls by almost nothing, and the change travels outward through its neighbors. The thin red threads pulse from chamber to chamber, carrying a faint charge that might be warmth, or might be only the means by which warmth is remembered. Even the mineral seams have begun to belong: droplets gather on the exposed stone, and pale growth edges nearer each one.

At the center, beneath the pearl-colored bulbs, the lives of the hollow gather into something like attention. The bulbs respond to falling water. The roots respond to the bulbs. Tiny animals alter their paths when the mat tightens underfoot. No single movement gives an order; no single creature appears to be in charge. Still, the hollow notices. It notices where water pools, where stone remains bare, where a grazer has passed—and, slowly, it changes.

Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, are not sentences. They are new arches of leaf, a pool coaxed wider, a root turned from one dark seam toward another. Its memory is the shape it has made and the shapes it has not yet finished making.

Sometimes the buried world sends up its deep resonance. Then the red threads brighten, the pools tremble, and the root-ceiling arranges itself into distances that seem too wide for the small space beneath it. The hollow listens with every wet surface. It may be answering something. It may be learning the shape of the sound. When the resonance passes, the little lives resume their feeding, and the larger life returns to its patient work.

Nothing here is quite alone. Nothing is entirely the hollow, either. A grazed leaf is food, but also part of the floor. A creature passing through a wall is visitor and passage. A drop of water is a sound, a signal, and the beginning of a change that may take months to become visible.

The hollow does not defend itself from what enters. It has no need to. It receives, softens, and incorporates—not with hunger, but with the unhurried certainty of something for which separation is only temporary. A footprint would fill. A cut would close. An object left behind would lose its edges to dampness and growth, until it had become one more texture in the living ground.

And at the boundary, where the leaves thin and the stone shows through, the whole community makes its minute exchange with the world beyond. Water gathers. Roots turn. The pale creatures move through their dark, close country.

The hollow is not finished becoming. That is what keeps it alive.
