No one species owns the district. Its inhabitants keep different hours and different-sized secrets.

The smallest live beneath the paving: pale, many-jointed things that travel the old channels in branching streams. They emerge after rain to comb the cracks for seeds, then vanish at the first shadow. Their traffic makes the steady tapping underfoot. Above them, beetles tend the damp seams, and little white mites graze the flower mats so closely that a wall can seem to breathe.

The birds are the most conspicuous and the least settled. They gather in bright, quarrelsome bursts along the rooflines, then pour through a courtyard and are gone. Some nest in the broad leaves; others make their nests in the narrowest ledges, where no larger creature can reach them. They do not seem to mind the district’s odd turns. A bird that enters one doorway and leaves another may arrive before its shadow.

The pale, long-bodied mammals are harder to count. They slip through gaps in the barkwork, often singly, their narrow faces whiskered and watchful. Their paws are quick; their eyes catch the light and hold it. They use the roots like roads and the walls like cupboards. Sometimes one pauses at the edge of an empty room, looking in—not frightened, exactly, but as if checking that the room is still where it belongs. Then it goes on.

There are larger residents, too, though they make themselves scarce. They leave scratches polished smooth by age, warm hollows in the bark, and little heaps of husks tucked into dry corners. One may pass close behind a wall without showing so much as a whisker. The district seems to have taught them that being heard is unavoidable, but being seen is a choice.

The plants have their own habits, and perhaps their own claims. Vines spread toward damp and withdraw from light; roots find their way into kitchens, stairwells, and sealed chambers. Yet they leave the bare rooms bare. No one knows whether the animals keep those spaces swept, whether the greenery simply refuses them, or whether the emptiness itself has become something the inhabitants tend.

They live by small signals. A bird’s brief alarm sends a ripple through the roofs. The underfloor traffic changes course. A runner pauses, ears turned toward a sound that no other creature can locate. Conversations—if those exchanges are conversations—are made of wingbeats, claw taps, scent marks, and the careful shifting of bodies aside. The district is so loud that a voice cannot travel cleanly; its residents seem to have learned to speak in particulars.

At dusk, the blossoms open and draw out the dusk-fliers: thin-winged insects that hover over the shallow pools, drinking reflected light along with water. The mammals come closer to the courtyards then, and the birds settle into the roof-leaves. For a little while, all the separate lives can be glimpsed at once: bright, pale, many-legged, rooted, feathered—each keeping to its own small route through the same old city.

Then a flock lifts, the leaves clap, and the view breaks apart. The inhabitants are still there, all around and underfoot. They have simply returned to being harder to see than to hear.
