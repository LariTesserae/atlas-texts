The Lower Choir is inhabited by things that know how to live in the spaces left over.

The bats keep the upper rooms. By day they hang in the arches, packed close as dark fruit, their wings folded like little coats. They sleep lightly. A drip striking the wrong stone can pass through the roost as a ripple of clicks: one bat waking, then another, then a whole ceiling answering before they settle again. At dusk they pour through the vents in separate streams, never colliding, and return before the coldest part of night. They know the arches by touch and echo. There are passages they never enter. No one knows what they hear there.

The swifts have the sky above the roofs, though it comes to them only in narrow slices. They stitch the vents with their flight, swift as thrown scraps of shadow. Sometimes one disappears into a slit and does not emerge until the next day. The others circle, calling, and then carry on without it. They nest in cracks too small for a hand, leaving only a few pale feathers and the faint dry smell of dust.

Below them, the water holds its own inhabitants. The eels pass under tiles and through channels, travelling with the current or against it, their pale bodies almost invisible until a glimmer catches them. They favor the lower-running drains and go where the water seems least likely to take them. Small fish gather in overflow bowls, flashing silver when the weak light reaches them. In the warm mouths of the drains, frogs cling in crowded ranks. Their blunt calls travel oddly through the culverts; one frog’s note can sound as if it came from three rooms away. Snails leave delicate, shining tracks across the ledges. Insects rise from the channels in brief clouds and vanish into the breathing dark.

They have learned the place by its exceptions. The bats will not roost beneath one arch, where the stone stays dry. The fish follow the channels toward the lower gate, even when those channels run thin. Each spring the frogs fill the cold circular basin, though their eggs never hatch there. The creatures do not seem confused by these habits. They observe them with the calm certainty of things that have inherited a map no one can read.

There are small plant lives, too: sedge in the old clay cups, moss along the seams, a pale creeper drawn across a lintel without any root one can find. They grow only where the water pauses. Their green is soft and restrained, as if they understand that the brick has already claimed most of the room.

Together, the inhabitants make a sound larger than the basin. Water chatters through the channels; frogs knock at the drains; swifts whistle overhead; bats mutter behind the arches. Insects tremble in the air like a held breath. When rain is passing and the water begins to fall, all these sounds sometimes gather into one wavering roar. It has no melody, exactly. It is more like the place remembering how to breathe.

Nothing here rules the rest. The bats do not command the birds, nor the frogs the water. Instead, each creature keeps to its hour, its notch in the brick, its shallow pool or narrow way. A fallen tile becomes a roof for eels. A ledge becomes a nursery for snails. A crack opened by frost lets in a strand of green. The basin is always being remade, but only by small agreements: water wearing stone smooth, animals returning to the same shelter, life finding a use for what has been left behind.

If the Lower Choir has a single inhabitant, it is this patient congregation. It has no face and no voice of its own, unless the voice is the many voices together: the wet clatter below, the restless wings above, the calls that pass through walls and come back changed. It lives in the channels and the cracks, and in the habits that persist from one season to the next. The old machine turns, and its keepers—unaware they are keepers—turn with it.
