The people of the Unfinished Height call themselves the **Keepers of the Interval**. There are not many of them—fewer than the houses they have raised, and many fewer than the beds they expect one day to need. They do not speak of the town as empty. They say it is **making room**.

They are practical people, made lean by the climb and patient by necessity. Their clothes are layered wool and patched hide, usually damp at the cuffs. They carry chalk, cord, little wooden stakes, and a narrow-bladed knife for trimming the moss that grows across steps. Most wear their tools openly, not as badges but because a useful thing should not have to be fetched. Their hands are roughened by stone and timber; their voices are kept low until someone calls across a gap, when the whole height seems to answer.

No one owns a plot outright. A house belongs to the people who keep it sound, and only so long as the ground beneath it remains trustworthy. Walls are built in sections that can be taken down. Roof tiles are stacked close at hand. A doorway left unfinished is not a failure; it is a promise made cautiously. Children are taught to read the stone before they are taught to build on it. They learn the old measures—how deep a crack may be, how long a patch of moss must hold, how a spring’s level is marked at dawn—and are praised less for making something than for knowing when not to.

The settlement’s daily work follows the weather and the mountain’s small changes. Someone clears the gutters after cloud has passed. Someone tests the bridge cords. Two people carry water from the spring while a third watches the level in the basin. The surveyors move from stake to stake, renewing chalk lines and noting where a new lip of stone has appeared. At the end of the day, they compare what they have found over a meal of broth, gathered leaves, and whatever berries have survived the wind.

They have many names for stone that outsiders would call simply stone. There is **held stone**, which has borne moss through a full year; **listening stone**, which sounds hollow under a tapped knuckle; **new stone**, pale and smooth, still too uncertain to build upon. They do not name every ledge. A place receives a name only after it has remained itself long enough for people to need one. Even then, the name may change if the mountain does.

The young are given room to run, but not wherever they please. They know which seams shine after rain and which stairs return a footstep too loudly. They play at laying out towns in the bare plots, making streets with pebbles and building houses from loose tiles. Their games are interrupted often by some small duty: fetching cord, sweeping a roof, standing very still while an elder measures the spring. They grow up understanding that the future is not a place beyond the town. It is the ground directly under their feet, if it holds.

At night, the Keepers sleep lightly. Wind through the covered passages can sound like distant voices; the bridge cords sometimes hum when cloud presses against the shelf. A watchkeeper walks the upper stair, not looking for enemies so much as listening for a sound that does not belong: a fresh fracture, perhaps, or water moving where it has never moved before. If nothing happens, this is considered a good night. If something does, no one hurries to explain it.

There are stories, though the Keepers are wary of turning them into laws. One says the height is not being built but remembered: that each new ridge is the mountain recovering a shape it once had. Another says the spring rises because something deep below is drawing breath. The oldest story is told without a beginning or an end. It says that, long before the first houses, the mountain made a flat place and waited. The people came because they, too, were waiting.

Whether the height is alive is a question they do not settle. They leave a bowl beneath a new seep, and they listen when the rock booms in the wind. They do not pray for the mountain to finish. They are careful not to ask it to become something else.

Among them, the most respected person is not the strongest builder or the oldest surveyor, but the one who can look at a bare shelf and tell whether it is ready to bear weight. Such a person may spend an entire season advising everyone to leave a place alone. The Keepers regard this as a form of work.

So they live in their half-made town: tending the spring, airing the empty rooms, planting edible greens against warm walls, and leaving spaces where the stone has not yet answered. They are not waiting for the Unfinished Height to be complete. They are learning how to belong to something that is still becoming.
