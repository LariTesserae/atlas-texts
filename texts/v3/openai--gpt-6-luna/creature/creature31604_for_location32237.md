At first, the plateau seems to be inhabited mostly by what it cannot keep still: vapor, water, the small motions of leaves. A silver flicker above a pool vanishes into fern shadow. Something knocks once against a tree trunk. Then the basin goes quiet again, as if the life in it has learned not to spend a sound without need.

The smallest residents are everywhere. Insects hatch in the warm shallows, their skins catching the light like bits of mica. They rise in brief, wavering clouds above the pools, where birds pick them out of the air. Under stones, pale crabs and many-legged things graze on the soft growth that gathers around mineral seepage. Some have learned to live where the water is warm enough to fog their shells; others keep to the cooler margins, where fresh rain dilutes the mineral taste. In the moss, tiny frogs cling so closely to the wet rock that their outlines disappear. Their throats pulse silently until dusk, when the whole gully begins to ring with thin, glassy notes.

The plants make the first map of the place. Dark trees gather around reliable water, their roots following cracks beneath the stone. Their upper branches carry gardens of wiry epiphytes, flowering after rain and dropping seeds into the runnels below. On the ridges, where soil is shallow and wind occasionally finds its way through, the growth is lower: waxy shrubs, tough grasses, and little flowers that close when the vapor thickens. Along the hot channels, plants grow in close bands according to the water’s temper. Moss at the cool edge, bright herbs where the water is warm, then a narrow bare strip around the hottest vents. Their roots do not merely seek water; they test it, turning away from what is too hot, too sour, too laden with metal.

The grazers are easiest to find by their traces. They cross the open basins in loose, long-legged groups, pausing to crop the fresh shoots beside streams. Their coats are the muted colors of wet stone and dead grass; from far away, a herd can look like a row of boulders slowly changing places. They drink cautiously, touching the water first with their lips, then moving on if it carries too much heat or bitterness. Young animals stay close to the older ones when mist rolls in. In clear weather they spread across the shelves, but at the first distant hiss from a vent they draw together, lifting their narrow heads to listen.

They leave the safest paths for everything else. Smaller creatures follow the grazers’ tracks through the grass. Birds wait on the trees above those paths, watching for insects stirred up by passing feet. At the pools, long-bodied amphibians lie motionless on warm ledges, their pale skin almost luminous where the steam thins. They take what comes within reach and retreat into cracks when the water trembles with a deep vibration from below.

The predators are seldom seen in full. One may leave a print in damp clay, large and sharply clawed, beside the tracks of the herd. A birdcall stops halfway through its phrase. The grazers all look in the same direction, though there is nothing there that a visitor can make out. The hunter uses the broken ground and the mist as others use cover, choosing the uncertain hours when distances collapse and a nearby shape might be a stone, a tree, or something watching from between them. It does not rule the plateau. It belongs to its timing.

The birds have the most visible lives. Some wheel above the falls, riding warm air up the cliff face; others keep to the canopy and call across gullies that are too wide to cross on foot. Their voices carry strangely through cloud. A call heard close at hand may come from the opposite side of a basin, or from a ledge far below. In the wet season, they gather around the flowering shrubs, and the ridges briefly seem busy with color. Then the flowers pass, the clouds thicken, and the birds retreat into the hidden green.

Nothing living here seems to have mastered the plateau. Instead, each creature knows a narrow portion of it: where the water is drinkable, which stones stay warm at night, which slopes shed fog, which channels flood without warning. A frog knows the safe side of a spring. A root knows the seam that holds rain. A herd knows when to leave a basin. Their knowledge is old but local, learned again in every generation.

There is no single sound of life. There are stretches of dripping and insect pulse, the rustle of grass under the grazers, the calling of birds concealed above the vapor. Then, from beneath the stone, comes the low rush of water shifting under pressure. For a moment, every living thing seems to pause around it. The birds stop. The herd lifts its heads. Even the small things withdraw from the pools.

When the roar fades, the plateau resumes.

Mist threads through the trees. A line of animals descends toward a stream whose mineral rim has grown a little thicker since the last rain. Insects rise over the warm water. The plants drink through their roots. And far below them, unseen, the heat continues to move, changing the paths that all these lives have learned to follow.
