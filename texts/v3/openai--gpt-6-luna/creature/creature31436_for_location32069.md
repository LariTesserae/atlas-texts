At first they look like frost caught in a net.

The pale insects on the overhead mesh are so tightly packed that the wires seem furred. Each is no longer than a grain of rice, all legs and fine, translucent joints, with a narrow body that holds a faint blue light where the machine’s hum passes through it. They cling head-down in the pipe mouths, where condensation gathers, and drink from the beads before they fall. When a droplet swells too large, they do not flee. They loosen their grip together, just enough to let the water pass through them and down into the channels, then close over the wet wire again.

They have learned the machine’s intervals. A pipe ticks; a ripple moves through the mesh. A thousand small bodies turn toward the sound, though none can know where it began. Their legs comb the metal. The rasp they make is not a signal, or not only a signal: it is how they keep one another’s position in the close dark. Each insect knows the pressure of its neighbors better than it knows open space. The ones at the edge of a cluster are restless. The ones at its heart scarcely move at all.

In the channels, nearly invisible animals comb the water. They have soft, clear bodies and fans of hair that turn the film of condensation toward their mouths. They gather where the ceramic has been left rough, in the tiny manufacturing pits, and keep those hollows clean. When the flow thins, they contract into bright threads. When water returns, they spread themselves through it again, each animal making a small current for the next. They are not separate from the water so much as its way of being used.

The white colonies on the walls seem, at a distance, like flowers that have forgotten to be plants. Their fans open and close against the ceramic with a slow, patient pulse. Up close, each is made of hundreds of minute mouths joined around a common pale stalk. They take what the channels bring: scraps shed by the combing animals, the faint mineral taste of the basin, the dead and loosened bodies of the insects. Their soft fronds shiver when the metal ribs vibrate. In that shivering, tiny young break away and settle a little farther along the wall, where the damp has not yet been claimed.

Nothing here hunts in the open. There is no room for pursuit, and no open country to escape into. Some of the larger insects pick their way along the ribs, feeding on the smallest things that stray from the water. Their jaws are hidden beneath their narrow heads; what they leave behind is a clean gap in the moving skin, filled almost at once by others. The wall colonies close around intruders and digest them slowly. The combing creatures take what the hunters miss. Every body, living or dead, passes into another mouth, then into the water, then back into the channels.

They reproduce by division, by shedding, by being carried a few millimeters in a bead of water. There are no nests in the manner of nests; the mesh itself is a crowded nursery, and every pipe lip a damp threshold. The young are pale even among the pale, small enough to be mistaken for a glint on metal. They learn the paths by following the movements of others, and the old paths are never abandoned. The same circuits pass over the same bolts, around the same pipe mouths, down the same ceramic grooves. A creature that strays far from them soon finds itself with no water, no contact, no answering tremor from the packed bodies. It turns back.

There may be no eyes among them. Or there may be eyes too simple to distinguish from the wet shine of their skin. They know the world by pressure, temperature, taste, vibration: the slight change in the air before a droplet swells, the difference between a live body and a dead one, the machine’s steady note and the smaller notes made by each other. Their understanding of the place is intimate and incomplete. They cannot know that the ribs are metal, or that the basin has been made. They know only that some surfaces conduct the thrum and some hold water; that certain seams stay cold; that when the ceiling beads, the channels will soon run.

As individuals, they are brief and unremarkable. A small white mouth opens, closes, and is gone. One translucent insect loses its grip and is carried into the basin. A thread of water divides around it; the combing animals take what remains. Yet the population does not seem to thin. There are always bodies in the gaps, always a rasp in the mesh, always the slow opening and closing on the walls. Each life is replaced before its absence can become a change in the sound.

Perhaps, in the long stillness, they have become something larger than a multitude. Not a mind with a center, not a single animal stretched across the machine, but a shared attention: the whole skin turning when the pipes tick, the channels adjusting as water comes and goes, the colonies opening to the thrum and closing around what it carries. They have no need to imagine the weather beyond their ceiling. Their weather arrives as beads. Their world is no wider than the paths between one wet seam and the next.

And still they make room for the young.

Along the roughest terrace, where the ceramic ends in a sharp unfinished lip, the oldest colony has spread into a pale, many-layered fan. Its outermost mouths open toward the falling droplets. Beneath it, the smallest insects cling in a fine veil, and below them the clear combing creatures stir the shallow channel. None can see the whole arrangement. None could leave it and carry it elsewhere. But together they occupy every surface, every trickle, every hidden pocket of shadow, until the manufactured hollow has become theirs—not by changing its shape, but by filling it so completely that the machine’s cold, constant breath passes through living bodies before it returns to the pipes.
