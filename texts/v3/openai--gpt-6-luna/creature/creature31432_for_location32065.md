The people of the High Quiet are easiest to find by the small changes they make.

A gutter that has been cleared of leaves. A drooping bridge bound with fresh fiber. A cup of water set on a threshold where no house can be seen. Follow these signs and, after a day or a season, you may find a dwelling: a hollow opened between two great roots, its floor padded with moss, its roof stitched from leaves. A family may live there for generations. From the outside, the hollow looks less like a home than a place the forest has chosen not to fill in.

They are few, or seem few. The High Quiet stretches too far for its people to gather easily. A traveler may pass through a whole country’s worth of terraces and meet no one, then discover that three households have been living in the walls around them, listening to their footsteps. The people are slight and sure-footed, with broad, soft soles and hands stained green-brown from the plants they tend. They wear layers of woven fiber, often in colors close to bark or wet leaf. Their hair is kept short, bound, or allowed to grow together with fine climbing vines; in old age, it can be difficult to tell where one ends and the other begins.

They speak, but not much at a distance. A voice goes soft quickly in the High Quiet, so they have learned to do most of their talking close together: a touch at the wrist, a tilt of the head, a hand resting on a wall to feel the tremor of someone moving on the other side. They also leave messages in knots, in the turn of a leaf, in stones set beside a runnel. A stranger might see only a bit of maintenance. Someone who knows the place can read: *water is low; take the upper way; a child has gone ahead.*

They do not think of themselves as owners of the country. They say they are *staying in it*. Their work is to keep passages open where they are needed, and to let them close when they are not. They loosen roots around old drains, bind cracked beams, gather fallen leaves for the deep beds. Sometimes a whole stairway is left to disappear under growth. Sometimes, after many years, someone clears a single step in it again. Neither act is treated as a victory.

Children learn the routes as they learn songs: by doing them with someone older, turning at the right root, waiting under the right arch, keeping the rhythm of their feet through a stretch of corridor that might otherwise lengthen. They are warned not to trust a map that has not been walked recently. They are also warned not to hurry after a familiar voice. Voices travel strangely, and the country has plenty of things that can imitate the sound of someone you miss.

The animals are their neighbors, though not always welcome ones. The little pale burrowers sometimes undermine a floor; the climbing mammals steal soft growth from the beams. Birds are listened to carefully. If all the birds in a grove turn toward the same shade, the people do not go there to see what frightened them. They leave food on the far side of the path and wait. No one claims this always works.

Above the terraces, people watch the great drifting creatures pass through the dimness. Their bodies shine faintly, veined with light, and some are large enough to carry gardens of their own. The people do not hunt them. They gather the translucent husks that sometimes fall, and use them to cover lamps or mend a torn window. They say the creatures follow water that has forgotten its route.

The people have no word for the country’s deepest inhabitants—or, if they do, they do not use it casually. They know that certain chambers remain warm when the rest of the structure cools. They know that water sometimes climbs a wall toward a place where no channel has been cut. They know that, on occasion, a doorway they have sealed for safety will be open again in the morning, though the roots around it remain unbroken. They leave such places alone. The High Quiet is old enough to have earned its privacy.

When a bell sounds, the people stop whatever they are doing. A hand stills over a knot. A child goes quiet beneath the leaves. Far apart as they may be, they listen together.

No one hurries toward the sound. They wait for a second note, which may come in a moment, or in a year, or not at all. Then, when the dripping resumes and the leaves shift softly overhead, each returns to their small work: mending, tending, carrying water along its remembered way.

The bell is not a summons, they say.

It is how the High Quiet tells them it has noticed they are there.
