The inhabitants are easiest to mistake for adjustments in the city.

A handrail warms along a stretch of bridge, though no one is near it. A door opens onto an empty landing and waits. Behind a translucent wall, a narrow shadow pauses beside a pool, then continues upward. Only after a while does it become clear that the shadows are people.

They are slight, not from hunger but from the shape of their lives. They have learned to move where there is little room to turn, to place their weight on the narrowest walkways without testing them. Their clothes are close-fitted and matte, dark as wet stone. At the wrists and throat, fine bands of silvered material meet the fabric; these are fasteners, perhaps, or instruments, or something their bodies require. Their faces are difficult to read at a distance. They do not cover them, but they keep them angled toward the rock, the bridge, the person they are speaking to—not often toward the open air.

They travel alone, usually. When two meet, one waits while the other passes. There is no impatience in this; a crossing can take as long as the mountain takes to settle. They greet each other by laying a palm briefly against the rail. The warmth travels away under the hand, down through the supports, and is gone.

The city gives them little that is not necessary. Each dwelling is a narrow room behind a sealed door, with a sleeping ledge, a basin, and some means of fastening oneself in when the stone moves. The rooms are arranged along the walls in small clusters, but the clusters are not neighborhoods so much as pauses in the ascent. Some doors have not opened in years. Their seals are intact, their thresholds clear. Others open only for a hand placed against a particular patch of wall.

They eat together, though rarely in numbers. At certain hours, faint vapor rises from vents near the tanks, carrying the mineral scent of warmed water and something faintly bitter. Figures appear on the upper decks and take small bowls from a counter set into the rock. They eat without haste, sometimes standing, sometimes watching the daylight shift across the chamber. No one wastes water. The last drops are tipped into narrow channels, where they vanish without a sound.

There are children, but they are seldom seen. Once, a small figure runs three steps along a high walkway before an older one catches them by the back of the coat. The child does not struggle. Together they stand at the rail, looking down into the shadowed basin, while the older one’s hand rests over the child’s. Then they go on, climbing.

The residents do not seem to fear the mountain. They attend to it. A tremor passes through the chamber, too slight to disturb the hanging drops, and somewhere a person stops speaking. Others stop too. For several seconds they listen—not with their heads tilted, exactly, but with their whole bodies held still. Then a cable shifts, a door eases shut, and they resume what they were doing.

They know which sounds belong to the city and which do not. The small tick of water is familiar; the thin scrape of a door is familiar; the low note of wind at the opening is something else. When that note comes, people turn toward the light. Some stand beneath it with their eyes closed. Others place a hand against the rock, as though feeling for a second, deeper sound beneath the first.

They speak quietly, and not always aloud. At times a person will look toward an empty bridge and wait. A line on its edge brightens. The bridge extends by a little, taking them to the next ledge. Whether they asked for it, or the city anticipated them, is not obvious. Perhaps it is not obvious to them either.

No one tends the green-black film on the wet stone. No one pursues the pale many-legged thing when it skims the edge of a pool. The residents let certain lives pass without naming them. Their attention is reserved for changes that matter: a channel running too slowly, an ice fan forming where it should not, a tower leaning against the new weight of the mountain.

They have been there long enough to seem less like visitors than a property of the seam. Yet now and then, in the narrow daylight, one of them pauses with a face turned toward the slit above. The whitened sun catches the side of it and makes the skin briefly translucent: veins, a pulse, the delicate motion of an eye.

Then the light moves on. The person lowers their gaze and continues along the wall, while beneath their feet the city makes its quiet corrections and the mountain, almost imperceptibly, makes room.
