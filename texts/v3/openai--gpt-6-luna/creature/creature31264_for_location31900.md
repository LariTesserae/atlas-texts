The Bell Pocket is inhabited by its answers.

At first they seem to be only echoes: the scatter of a footstep, the low murmur returning from a lane you did not enter. But the answers do not always wait for a sound. Sometimes, before anyone moves, a small tap crosses the upper roofs, followed by another farther off, as if someone were walking a familiar route in the dark. The city gives back what it has heard, though not always in the order it received it.

These are the listeners, if they need a name. They have no bodies that stay in one place. They gather in the hollow parts of the city—in plugged arches, stairwells, roof gaps, and the space beneath the central bowl. A listener may be no more than a faint second note following a drip. Several together can make the impression of a door opening, a passage being crossed, or a crowd withdrawing just around a corner.

They know the city by its resonances. Each stair has its own small complaint; each bridge, its thin wooden-sounding tremble, though no wood remains. The listeners travel along these differences as other creatures might follow a scent. A sound that falls into the pool spreads through them, and for a little while they are everywhere the ripples reach. Then they draw back into the old lanes and hollows, leaving the water still again.

They are not the dead who built the houses. They do not remember faces, or names, or why the basins were made. Yet they have inherited something of the builders’ habits. They pause at thresholds. They circle the pool at the same points where a path once met the curb. Now and then, when a visitor stands quietly in a doorway, the listeners make room: a series of soft, receding taps, as if a household were passing deeper into its rooms.

The rare pale creatures beneath the paving are different. They are small, folded, and almost transparent, with soft feelers that test the wet seams. They retreat from light and touch, and are seldom found twice in the same place. The listeners attend to them. A drop striking near one will be answered by several delicate ticks, so quiet that a person may mistake them for water. Whether the creatures hear the listeners, or merely disturb them, is not known.

The central note belongs to neither. It is made by water, stone, and the hollow bowl together. The listeners gather around it, though never inside it. They keep its rhythm—not perfectly, but faithfully enough that the chamber seems to breathe with the sound. When the note is missed, the whole city waits. Then the next drop falls, and the old paths open again.

Nothing in the Bell Pocket asks to be seen. Its inhabitants reveal themselves by returning what you bring: a step, a whisper, the scrape of a hand against stone. If you stay long enough, you may hear a second set of footsteps keeping pace with yours. If you stop, they stop too.

And if, just before you leave, you hear someone walking on ahead, they are not leading you anywhere. They are only showing you that the city has noticed you—and has already begun to remember the shape of your sound.
