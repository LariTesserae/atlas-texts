The crews call them sumpbacks, though nothing about them belongs to a sump. They are animals of the open bog, and the name is only what people could think of when one first began breathing beneath a pump house.

An old sumpback lies in a flooded hollow now, with rain ticking on the water around her. She is longer than a person, low to the ground, and so broad across the ribs that reeds lean against her when she passes. Her skin is dark, slick, and ridged with old scars. Along her flanks, folds of sensitive flesh open and close with each breath, testing the pressure of the air. Her eyes are no more than sealed pits. They have been useless for generations.

She knows the hollow by what touches her.

A beetle walking across a floating leaf sends a small, sharp tremor through the water. Rain makes a thousand fine impacts, but she can distinguish it from the slower shudder of a fish under the peat. Farther off, a pump hammers at its familiar pace, and the vibration arrives through the ground, passing into her broad feet and up through the bones of her jaw. She does not hear the pump as a sound. She feels its weight in the world.

When she moves, she does so carefully. Her feet spread wide over the bog’s skin; her claws find the old roots underneath. The water parts along her sides. Reeds bow, then clatter back together behind her, and small animals scatter ahead—not because they smell her, but because the earth has begun to answer her steps.

Sumpbacks eat what the wet country offers: snails, soft roots, the pale grubs that live beneath moss, and the small blind fish that gather in warm channels under the stone. They feed slowly, working food between ridged plates in the back of the mouth. Hunger is rarely urgent here. There is always something living in the water, and the water is nearly everywhere.

They are not solitary, exactly. Adults keep far apart, but their territories meet in pools and channels, and at certain times of year the whole plateau seems to pass messages between them. A sumpback lowers its throat against the water and releases a long, low pulse. The sound is too deep to be heard cleanly in the air. It travels beneath the bog, through the pools and flooded cracks, arriving as pressure against another animal’s ribs. A reply may come from a mile away, or from beyond the next shelf of stone.

The old female knows many replies. Her own calls are uneven now, worn thin at the edges by age. Still, she can tell a neighboring animal from a stranger, a courtship answer from a warning, and a young one’s panicked burst from the tremor of a collapsing peat bank. The young are born in warm shallows, small enough to hide in the woven roots. At first they answer every vibration. Their mothers teach them, by turning away or pressing them into the mud, which sounds are rain, which are food, and which should be feared.

The machines are part of that teaching.

When she was young, this pump house worked harder. Its pistons struck the same interval hour after hour, a dry, regular knock carried down through the stony rise. The sumpback learned the rhythm before she learned the contours of the pool. She would rest beneath the foundation while the machine ran, feeling its beat through the stone. Now the pump has stopped. Water fills the casing, and moss thickens over its warmest seams. The beat is gone, but the place still holds its shape in her body. When she crosses the hollow, she angles toward it without knowing why.

Sometimes she presses her throat against the old pipe that runs down into the marsh and sends a low note through it. The pipe gives the note back in a long, hollow shiver. It is not a call meant for another sumpback, though one may answer. Nor is it grief, as the crews sometimes say when they hear her. It is an examination: the animal asking the emptied machine what remains inside it.

The plateau changes around her. Pools deepen. Channels silt over. New pumps begin their pounding farther along the ridge, and old ones go quiet. Herds of smaller animals migrate between basins, their movement felt first as a faint unrest in the water. The sumpback follows these changes by scent, pressure, and the sound of reeds shifting against one another. She has never known the edge of the high country. To her, the world ends only where its messages do.

When rain crosses the plateau, she feels it approaching as a soft roughness spreading through the ground. She settles lower into the warm water. The rain reaches her in thousands of separate taps, drumming along her back, filling the old pump casing, and running down through the hidden stone. Beneath it all, far away, another sumpback answers.

She lifts her throat from the pipe and replies.
