The people of the Fold are easiest to mistake for shadows moving behind leaves.

They are few, and they keep close to the paths. Most are slight—not from hunger, exactly, but from a life spent turning sideways, ducking under ribs of stem, and climbing where the ground gives way. Their hands are broad at the palm and tough along the fingers, trained to feel a change in a root before it can be seen. They know which arches will bear weight, which walls are tightening, which leaves are ready to lift. A stranger might call the settlement quiet. Its people would say it is listening.

They wear layered things made from what the Fold sheds: soft inner bark, woven root fiber, felted plant down. Their clothes are dark and close-fitting, kept that way so they do not snag on the passages. They carry no lamps. They find their way by the give of a floor underfoot, by the temperature of a wall, by the faint difference between water running toward the cistern and water running out toward the cliff. In the dimness, they can recognize one another by breath and footfall.

Their homes have little that can be called private. A curtain of leaves, a bend in a passage, a room tucked behind the thickening of a trunk: these are enough. A person may sleep for years in one warm hollow, then wake to find it growing too narrow for them. They leave without ceremony, carrying what they need to another opening, while the old room closes around its living frame. No one speaks of being displaced. The room has changed its mind, and the person makes room in turn.

Children learn the Fold by being carried through it. Before they can name the lanes, they know the soft bridge that trembles under many feet, the dry pocket where the air smells of warm stems, the dripping roof that must be passed quickly in a frost. They are taught not to pull at young growth, and not to stand still in a passage. A path kept open is a promise made with the body.

The inhabitants do not tend the settlement in the manner of gardeners. They guide what can be guided: bending a new shoot around a shoulder, pressing a loose mat of roots into a gap, walking a passage until it takes the shape of passage. Much of the work is simply living there. Breath warms the rooms. Feet strengthen the bridges. Bodies resting against a wall leave it a little less cold. Even silence has its uses; they wait beside a new growth and let it find its direction.

They speak, but rarely at a distance. Their voices are low and close, meant for one person at a time. Sometimes they touch a stem to call someone in another chamber; the vibration travels through the joined ribs of the dwelling, softened by leaves and root. The old can feel these messages most clearly. They have learned the settlement’s slow speech: the creak of an arch under new weight, the little shift of a roof seeking light, the wet sigh of a channel thawing from below.

When one of them dies, the body is not taken outside. There is scarcely an outside in which to put it. The dead are laid in a hollow near the roots, wrapped in fiber, and left where the stone is thin and the living growth is strong. In time the hollow changes. A wall thickens there, or a new stem rises through the softened earth. The Fold does not keep the dead as memory alone; it keeps them as part of its structure.

They have no clear view of the mountain and little longing for one. To stand at the ridge’s edge is to look into cloud, and the cloud reveals nothing for long. Their world is nearer: a roof leaf holding a bead of water; a warm breath clouding in a doorway; the familiar pressure of another person passing behind them in the dark. They are not at home despite the Fold’s closeness. They are at home because it closes around them.

From the ridge, the settlement seems almost empty—one dark tangle of plants on young black stone. Only now and then does something move within it: a face at a leaf-framed opening, a hand resting briefly on a living wall, a narrow figure slipping from one green tunnel into another. Then the leaves settle, the water goes on ticking, and the people disappear back into the place that has grown around them.
