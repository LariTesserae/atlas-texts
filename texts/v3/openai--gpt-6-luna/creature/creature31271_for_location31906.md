The first inhabitants you notice are the ones that appear not to be there.

A ridge seems bare until a dozen pale backs lift from the stone. The grazers stand on narrow shelves with their long legs gathered beneath them, their coats the color of weathered rock. From a distance they are no more distinct than the ridges themselves. Up close, they are spare and watchful: broad-footed, with dark, split hooves that grip wet stone, and ears that turn constantly toward sounds they cannot separate from the falls. They do not startle easily. They have learned that the plateau is full of noise and that most of it means nothing.

They feed on what little grows: wiry grasses, bitter cushions, the thin green edges around springs. Their mouths are narrow and patient. A group may spend half a day working its way across a basin, stopping often to drink from hollows no wider than a hand. The young stay between the adults, not so much hidden as made difficult to distinguish from them. When danger does come, the herd breaks apart without panic, each animal choosing a different line across the rock. For a while the plateau seems to swallow them. Then, far off, one pale shape lifts its head above a ridge.

The black-winged birds belong to the cliffs. They ride the rising air in broad, unhurried circles, barely moving their wings. Their nests are tucked into shelves where the mist blows past but does not settle. They know every updraft, every warm face of stone. Some carry small animals from the basins; others take fish from the narrow streams, snatching them where water spills shallow over the rock. Their calls are sharp enough to be seen, almost: a brief opening of the beak, a flick of the throat, a note lost at once in the roar. They communicate more by turning together, by the angle of a wing or the sudden vacancy of the sky above a ledge.

On the cliff faces, where water runs constantly, there are smaller lives that never appear against dry stone. They cling in dark seams: slick, flat-bodied things with pale undersides, moving against the current by inches. They gather where the falling water breaks into spray and where minerals have been left in crusts along the rock. In stiller pools, translucent larvae thread through the silt. They rise at dusk, when the light goes blue, and vanish again before the sun reaches the basin floor. A visitor might mistake their ripples for the water trembling under wind.

The lizards prefer warmth. In the morning they emerge from cracks to lie along sunlit shelves, flattened and motionless, their backs patterned like fragments of stone. They hunt insects too small to see except when they catch the light. Those insects come in clouds on certain days, appearing after a night of wet air, gathering above the pools in such numbers that the water briefly seems to smoke. The birds descend then. The lizards wait beneath the stones for the crowds to pass. Even here, abundance arrives in episodes.

There are predators, though the plateau rarely gives them a shape to be remembered by. A grey hunter moves low between the basins, almost indistinguishable from the shadows under the ridges. It follows the grazers at a distance, not in pursuit but in attention, waiting for the old, the injured, or the young that strays too far into open ground. Its paws are broad and furred beneath, quiet on the wet rock. It does not need speed often. The terrain itself divides the herd, and the long distances exhaust the careless.

At night, the animals draw closer to the water. Not together, exactly. They come to the same springs in turn, each waiting beyond the others’ reach. The grazers drink first. Then the small hunters, then the birds that walk the banks with their wings held out for balance. In the seepage cracks, tiny things stir under the dark. The pools fill with reflected stars, broken whenever something touches the surface.

Their lives are arranged around distances. A call is not useful here; a silhouette on a ridge is. A familiar spring, a remembered ledge, the taste of water in a certain hollow—these are the landmarks that endure. The animals know where the mist will gather and which channels remain wet after a dry spell. They know which slopes hold the day’s heat longest. They make no paths that last, only temporary lines of disturbed gravel and bent grass, erased by the next wash of water.

And so the plateau is crowded without seeming inhabited. Its creatures leave little behind that can be found: a few pale hairs caught in a crack, a nest lined with dark feathers, the clean print of a hoof in soft mineral mud. Everything else is motion at a distance, a shape revealed and withdrawn. The animals do not fill the emptiness. They have learned to live inside it.
