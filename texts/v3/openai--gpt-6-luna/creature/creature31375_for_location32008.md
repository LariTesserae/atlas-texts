At first, the small pale things between the stones seem to be all that lives there. They lie low in the cracks, jointed and almost leaf-shaped, and when the light shifts they disappear into the stone. A few can be seen moving—though never quite from one place to another. One is beside a wet hollow; then it is on the far side of it, with no trail between. They do not startle when approached. They turn, slowly, toward the sound of a person’s steps.

They are not the plateau’s only inhabitants. They are only the part of them that can be seen.

The old stone walls shelter the rest. Their doorways open on bare rock, a shallow pool, or a drop with no bottom in sight. Look through one for long enough and the space beyond may acquire a depth it did not have before: a dim chamber, perhaps, with a pale shape resting against its far wall. It is difficult to tell whether the shape is sitting, or whether the wall has simply taken on a more attentive arrangement.

The inhabitants have no fixed height. Some are no larger than the pale things in the cracks. Others seem to fill a doorway without standing in it. Their surfaces are made of the same pale stone and dark bands as the plateau, crossed by fine seams of dampness. There are suggestions of limbs, but no reliable number; an arm glimpsed at the edge of sight may turn out to be a ridge continuing behind them. Their faces, if they have faces, are smooth and shallow. Now and then a hollow appears where a mouth should be, black with still water.

They do not walk much. They shift by degrees, like the horizon: a little nearer, a little less distinct, then present on the other side of the basin. Sometimes a traveler finds their own footprints already waiting ahead. The inhabitants stand beside these impressions, looking down—not in surprise, but with the patient attention of people watching a familiar thing arrive.

They know the constructions. Each low wall, each roofless room, is a kind of dwelling, though none keeps anyone inside. The one with the reflecting pool is used by those who need to see stars; its water shows a sky the plateau has not yet reached, or perhaps one it has already passed through. The doorway over the escarpment is used by those who have somewhere else to be. They may stand in it for years. No one asks whether they have gone.

The roar is their common voice, though it does not carry words. Under it, they speak in single cracks from the ridges, in rings from the wet hollows, in grinding from beneath the shelves. A pebble dropped on the plateau gives them something to answer. Its report returns from empty directions, altered each time. The inhabitants listen to every version. They are especially attentive to the last echo, which sometimes arrives before the first.

Their smaller shapes—those pale, leaf-memory things—gather near damp seams. They grow where the plateau’s impossible lines cross, and where breath hangs in the cold. Perhaps they are young. Perhaps they are only the visible edges of the others, where an inhabitant has pressed close to the surface of the world. The dark birds overhead are also theirs, though the birds do not travel. Their cries come from wherever the plateau needs them to come from: above, below, or from the stones just behind a listener.

They are not waiting for visitors. Waiting implies that something might happen when the wait is over. The inhabitants have been here so long that arrival and departure are merely two more contours in the rock, worn smooth by repetition.

Still, they notice a person.

One may appear beside a basin after the traveler has passed it, not following, exactly, but keeping the same distance in the way a distant ridge does. It will not block the path. It may look at the traveler’s breath hanging in the air, then at the place where that breath slowly loses its shape. If greeted, it may make a sound from somewhere beneath the ground: a blunt, low note, almost like agreement.

Then it will be gone, or else the traveler will have moved around it without noticing.

Later, in a shallow pool, the traveler might see the figure standing in the reflection. The reflected sky will hold too many stars. The figure will not be looking at the traveler, but at a point just beyond them—as if watching the place from which they came, or the place where their footprints will eventually appear.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not seem lonely. They have the roar, and the cracks, and the small lives in the seams. They have their finished walls and their endless patience. If they have a purpose, it is not to guard the plateau or explain it. They keep it company. They remain with it in the long stillness, listening as the stone holds its shape, the water holds its dark, and the land continues without going anywhere.
