At first, the plateau seems to be inhabited by nothing larger than its shadows.

In the cracks, little dark animals wait with their feelers tucked close. They are quick only when they must be: a sudden flicker from one shelter to another, then the rock is bare again. In the lee of the ridges, translucent films cling to the stone. They barely stir, though the current combs them constantly. Pale clusters of shells mark the places where food gathers, where water turns, where a narrow cut draws a steady stream up from the depths. The life here is written in small signs. To read it, you have to learn the currents.

Then the shoals arrive.

They pass over the high ground in broad, shifting veils, thousands upon thousands of silver bodies turning together. From below, their scales catch the thin daylight and scatter it across the rock. A valley brightens, dims, brightens again. The shoal follows no visible leader. It bends around the pinnacles and streams through the passes, breaking into ribbons where the water quickens, gathering again beyond. Its small members feed among the animal films and shell beds, and on the tiny lives stirred loose from the stone. When danger comes, the whole multitude changes direction at once, like a thought moving through a body too large to see.

Beneath them travel the long ones.

They are pale, slow, and seldom seen whole. A flank passes beyond the edge of sight; a broad head rises out of the blue and tilts toward a sound no other creature seems to notice. Their skin bears old scars and the faint marks of lesser animals that have made homes upon them. They cross the plateau by routes so consistent that the small creatures seem to anticipate them. On certain shelves, the shells are open before the long ones arrive. The shoals thin and lift. Even the water seems to make room.

Their calls travel through the rock. Each is a deep, unhurried note, less a voice than a pressure that moves through the body before it reaches the ear. One call may come from far below, where the plateau’s edge falls into blackness; another answers from a ridge beneath the faint daylight. The notes overlap with the ceaseless rush of water, and with the hammering of machines. No one can tell whether the long ones are speaking to one another, calling their young, or simply sounding the shape of the world around them.

They have learned the machines, at least.

Small animals live in the warmest seams of the pressure station, though warmth there means only a slight easing of the cold. Shells gather in its pipe joints. The gantry’s shadow draws the shoals close, and the long ones sometimes turn beneath it, answering the turbine’s steady pulse with a note so low that the metal seems to hum. Nothing has taken the installations over. Nothing has made them useful in any deliberate way. But life has gathered around them, as it gathers around a ridge or a cut in the stone. The lamps are just another kind of light, the cables another kind of current, the machines another sound that has always been there.

Perhaps that is how the plateau’s inhabitants understand time. Not as a succession of new things, but as returning paths: the shoal through the pass, the long ones across the shelf, the tiny creatures retreating into cracks as a storm’s shadow rolls overhead. The rock changes by degrees too small to matter to a life that moves with the current. A fracture opens. A shell bed shifts. A pylon leans farther into the blue. The animals adjust their routes, and the plateau remains.

There is no single creature that belongs to the place. Its inhabitants belong to one another, and to the water’s repeated motions. The small lives read the stone. The shoals read the small lives. The long ones read the sounds beneath them. Together they pass over the raw ridges and through the ancient basins, appearing suddenly in their thousands, then dissolving into the open water until the plateau seems empty again.

But it is not empty. It is waiting for the next return.
