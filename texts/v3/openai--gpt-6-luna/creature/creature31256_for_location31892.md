### The One Beneath the Green

At first, it seems to be one of the pale things that passes among the plants: a narrow, translucent body, jointed in places that suggest a shrimp, though it has no clear head and no eyes. It slips behind a leaf and disappears.

Then, much later, it passes again.

It is never quite the same size. Sometimes it is no longer than a fingernail; sometimes its pale length vanishes into a curtain of green and does not emerge for several minutes. It travels upright, as if the water had forgotten which way was down. It leaves no wake. The plants do not stir around it, but afterward their tips lean a little farther in the direction it went.

The little creature is only the part of it that moves.

Its larger body is threaded through the hollow: a fine, nearly invisible mesh tucked beneath the moss, between the leaf-shaped growths, and down into the black silt. Here and there, the mesh gathers into soft knots. A pale cup. A bead of water that does not fall. A patch of green thick enough to hide a seam in the stone. Nothing in the hollow is quite separate from it, though nothing seems to belong to it entirely. The plants keep growing. The stone keeps making its sharp new scars. The thing lives among these changes as a root lives among stones, not commanding them, only finding its way through.

It has no face. It feels the hollow by the pressure of water, by the small drag of silt shifting, by the change in a shadow when light falls where it should not. A visitor is therefore difficult for it to know. A hand in the water is a sudden change in temperature and shape. A foot at the entrance is a tremor arriving from a direction that may not remain a direction. The thing attends to such disturbances briefly. Then they fade into the hollow’s many other slow rearrangements.

The three stones leaning like a doorframe are where it has gathered itself most densely. The smoothness there is not wear from passing hands, but the patient polish of its threads working over the stone, again and again. It has drawn pale sand into a ridge before the frame. The ridge is not a warning or an invitation. It is simply where the water, under its influence, has learned to stop.

Sometimes a drop trembling from a frond reflects a passage beyond the doorframe. The thing can feel that passage, though it cannot reach it by moving through the stone. When the passage appears, the hollow contracts around the reflection—not visibly, not all at once, but in the same manner that distance can shorten between one stroke and the next. The pale creature goes toward it. It never arrives. The passage recedes, or the creature turns aside, and the stone remains stone.

Perhaps this is what the creature is: not a keeper of the threshold, but the hollow’s unfinished attempt to cross it. Perhaps the frame is a nest built around an absence. Or perhaps the hollow has no single inhabitant at all, and the pale thing is only the moment when its crowded, patient life briefly takes a shape.

When the water is disturbed, the creature withdraws into the green. Its threads tighten gently through the silt. The trembling drops hold their reflections. Afterward, the water closes over every trace of the visit, and the hollow continues to grow around the place where the visitor stood.
