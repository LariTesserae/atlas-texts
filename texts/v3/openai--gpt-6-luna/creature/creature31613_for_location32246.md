At first, the inhabitants seem to be another kind of growth. A shoulder passes behind a pale stem; a face appears in the shadow of a wall, then vanishes as a curtain of leaves settles back into place. The people are difficult to see all at once. They live in the close spaces between things.

They are human in their proportions, though made compact by the habits of the plain: sure-footed, strong in the hands, with eyes accustomed to shifting shade. Their skin is often marked with the pale dust of the mineral ground, or darkened with plant dyes against the glare. They wear little that hangs loose. Their clothing is woven from tough fibers and layered where the sun reaches the paths; indoors, it is shed into bundles by the door. Most carry a small blade, a digging hook, or a basket shaped to pass easily through narrow growth.

Water is not something they meet in a stream or find gathered in a hollow. It is drawn carefully from the life around them. At the cool hours, they cut selected stems and catch the thin moisture inside; they press pulp through woven filters, or set broad leaves to gather the night’s faint condensation. The deepest roots are left alone unless a settlement is in danger. Children learn early which plants can be cut, which must be coaxed, and which should never be touched. A good water-gatherer is not the one who takes most, but the one who can return to the same thicket season after season.

Their gardens do not look like gardens from a distance. They are thickets whose growth has been persuaded into useful shapes. Seed-bearing crowns are left standing above the paths; edible roots are marked by small stones; the most useful shrubs are pruned so that people can pass beneath them without damaging their lower leaves. Insects, eggs, soft shoots, and the meat of small animals supplement what the plants provide. Nothing is gathered in great haste. A family may spend an entire morning working a patch of ground, then leave most of it growing.

The settlements are held together by this repeated attention. A wall is not simply built and abandoned: it is packed again where rainless heat has cracked it, lined with fibers where roots are pressing through, and allowed to soften back into the soil when its usefulness is done. Roofs are clipped and renewed. Passageways are cleared only as far as needed. People know which roots lie under each floor and can tell, by a faint tremor in the house or a shift in the leaves above it, when some animal has passed beneath.

They speak quietly, but they are not a silent people. Their voices are low and near; conversation happens at doorways, in courtyards, and along shaded paths where there is no need to call across distance. Much of their communication is carried in smaller sounds: a tongue-click to warn someone of a thorned stem, a brief whistle that means a predator has crossed nearby, knuckles tapped twice against a wall to say that a household is awake. Even strangers learn to listen before they speak. The close growth catches words and breaks them up.

There is plenty of laughter, though it seldom travels far. It rises briefly from a courtyard, then is absorbed by leaves and packed earth. Children move most noisily, until they discover how many creatures they startle by running. They play at following tracks through the groundcover, at recognizing plants by touch, at slipping from one house to another without exposing themselves to the open heat. Their elders pretend not to watch them closely. In truth, they are always listening for the sudden hush that means the small animals have stopped moving.

The people do not think of the city as separate from the plain. A household may tend the plants outside its walls; the plants, in turn, hold the earth in place beneath the household. When an old building collapses, its materials are sorted and reused, but some are simply left to crumble. New growth takes the softening earth. A doorway can become a root passage; a street can narrow until it is no longer a street at all. The people remember paths not as lines but as agreements: this is where feet may pass, this is where the leaves are allowed to close.

Beyond the city, the animals keep their own routes. Quick grazers slip through the low stems. Burrowers open and seal their entrances beneath roots. Birds pick their way along the canopy, hopping between crowns rather than risking long flights through the dense growth. The people know the signs of larger hunters—the silence of smaller creatures, a pod split in an unusual way, a passage where the leaves have been pressed flat—but rarely see the hunters themselves. They leave room for them. The plain is too crowded, and too alive, for any one creature to claim all of it.

At dawn the inhabitants emerge with the plants. Shutters open; leaf screens lift. Someone tests the air with a hand held above the roof. Someone else goes to check a cracked seam in the ground, or the tender new growth along a wall. For a short while the whole settlement stirs in the mildness: baskets moving through the lanes, pale stems trembling overhead, small animals venturing into the open places.

Then the heat gathers, the leaves fold, and the people withdraw into the shade they have made together. Nothing they build is meant to last unchanged. What they hope for is something more modest, and more difficult: that when they return tomorrow, the roots will still hold, the plants will still answer, and there will be enough living world around them to begin again.
