What lives there is the garden.

At first it seems to be only growth: moss thickening the stairways, fronds suspended from the ledges, pale stalks holding their small green lights in the dim. Nothing looks back. Nothing stirs at an approaching sound. The plants do not flee or gather. They simply occupy the places where water, light, and old soil allow them to continue.

But the growth has spread so long through the same arrangement that it begins to seem less like a collection of plants than a single, dispersed life. It runs along the channels and settles in the basins; it keeps to the old terraces where packed earth remains, then crosses the broken walls and joins one district to the next. Roots work through the joints in the stone. Soft mats conceal the edges of the paving without erasing its lines. In some courtyards, the original beds are still legible beneath the vegetation, as though the plants remember the shape of their planting even if nothing in them remembers being planted.

It has no center. There is no great trunk or hidden chamber from which the rest depends. Its oldest parts are low, dark, and nearly indistinguishable from the silt. Its newer growth rises wherever the water is faintly brighter. Thin leaves lean with the current, each touching the next. When a frond breaks loose, it drifts through the avenues, catches against a wall, and begins another slow life there. The garden does not need to reclaim the ruins. It has been growing among them for so long that the ruins are part of its conditions.

There is no mouth in the green beds, no creature moving beneath them, no hunter or scavenger to disturb the fallen leaves. The plants make their own quiet cycle. They grow, shed, soften, and become the brown sediment from which more growth rises. Nothing is eaten. Nothing is chased. The only appetite is for the slight warmth of the brighter water and the traces of nourishment held in the old soil.

In the deepest districts, its faint light gathers along the tips of narrow stalks. It does not brighten the rooms; it merely gives darkness a few delicate borders. From far down an avenue, the glow might resemble windows. Nearer, it resolves into hundreds of tiny green points, none shining strongly enough to cast a shadow. If the water changes, the lights change with it, slowly, too slowly to be watched as an event.

The garden does not know the builders’ plans. It has no memory of hands, or of the work that made those terraces level and those channels straight. Yet its life follows the places they prepared. It is held by old beds, guided by the walls, divided and joined by the channels. The builders shaped the water’s path; the plants have inherited the path. What began as cultivation has become a kind of persistence—not intention, exactly, but a form that lasts because the world around it keeps repeating the conditions of its beginning.

It does not rule the drowned plain. It does not transform it all at once. A blocked channel shifts the current; a fallen wall changes where the silt settles; a little more darkness, a little less, and growth thins or gathers. The garden answers without haste. It can take years to fill a stairway, centuries to bind two courtyards together. It is patient in the way stone is patient, except that it continues to make more of itself.

So the place is not empty. It is inhabited by a life with no single body and no need to announce itself. It lies over the old roads, threads through the architecture, and gathers in the sheltered basins. The city is its inherited ground. The water is its weather. And beneath the green, beneath the silt, the first stones remain level and aligned, still holding the beds in which it began.
