At first, the herds appear to be stones the plateau has not quite finished making.

They are low and broad, built close to the ground against the cold. Their bodies move beneath overlapping plates of dark mineralized hide, and frost gathers in the seams between them. From a distance, a herd is less a collection of animals than a slow alteration in the plain: a shelf of rock shifting across a basin, a darkening of the frost, a faint disturbance traveling from one ridge to another. Only when they draw near do their parts resolve—the blunt head, the small recessed eye, the heavy feet set wide apart, the mouth working continuously against the stone.

They graze with the patience of weather. Their lips are divided into tough, flexible combs that draw lichen, salt, and the thin living films from damp rock. The mouth does not so much bite as read the surface. A grazer presses its muzzle to a ledge, tests it, then scrapes away a layer almost too fine to see. The mineral goes into the animal; what it cannot use returns to the land as pale grit and dark, nutrient-rich pellets, scattered along the migration routes. In a basin where a herd has passed, the exposed stone looks faintly polished. Lichen will return, slowly, in stains and threads. The animals will return too.

Their bodies are built to keep water. Some have hollow chambers beneath the breastbone where cold air drawn through the nostrils is warmed and its moisture caught. In the morning, beads form inside these chambers; the animal swallows them, or lets them run into a lined pouch beneath the throat. The pouch can be seen swelling slightly on long, dry crossings. Others carry branching ridges along the skull, fine as the exposed roots of a tree. They gather the weak electrical charge before a storm and pass it through the body in measured pulses. At such times the herd becomes strangely attentive. Heads lift, ridges angle toward the clouds, and a faint shiver moves through their plates—not fear, but adjustment, as if each animal were listening with its whole frame.

They do not travel in tight masses. Their spacing is deliberate, each body keeping the ground it needs while remaining within the reach of the others’ signals. A migration flows across the plateau in loose ranks, opening around hollows, dividing at a ridge, and joining again far beyond it. The old paths are not followed blindly. They are remembered by scent and vibration, but each season they are tested against new frost, new water, the changing taste of the stone. A route may shift by miles while still belonging to the same ancient passage.

Their young are smaller, smoother-backed, and poorly equipped for the open cold. They travel in the lee of adults, where the pressure of moving bodies alters the wind. When a storm approaches, the herd closes by degrees, not into a wall but into a pattern of overlapping shelter. The young disappear among the legs and plated flanks. Once, in the dimness before snowfall, a calf’s pale face can be seen between two adults, its mouth moving at the rock without finding much to take. An older animal lowers its head and leaves the calf a freshly scraped patch. It is not a gesture one can be certain of. It may be habit, or the arrangement of bodies may simply have made it so. But the calf feeds there, and the elder moves on.

Under the herd, and in its wake, the smaller life is busy. Pale burrowers slip through fissures in the basalt, their bodies soft and narrow, their forefeet edged with hard plates. They do not dig so much as widen what is already open. They harvest the lichen fragments the grazers miss, carry them into cool chambers, and leave their droppings in the deeper seams where water gathers. Their burrows form a second map under the visible one: branching, interrupted, full of little rooms in which moisture, warmth, and waste are exchanged.

In the shallows of the black basins, tiny things turn beneath the skin of ice. Their bodies are almost transparent, except for a dark organ that holds the minerals they have sifted from the water. When the surface thaws, they rise in a trembling film. When it freezes, they settle into the mud and wait, their life reduced to a sealed, nearly motionless knot. The burrowers feed on them; the grazers feed on the crust they leave; scavengers feed on what the grazers cannot carry. There is no abundance that remains unused for long.

The scavengers are the most visible animals, though they are seldom close. They circle high above the plateau on dark, narrow wings, using differences in air pressure that are too slight to trouble a standing creature. Their heads are bare and metallic-looking, their eyes pale against the cloud-shadow. They follow the herds without appearing to follow them. When an animal dies, they descend only after the body has cooled. Then they settle in an uneven ring, picking clean the soft tissues and opening the plated hide at its seams. What they leave is taken by burrowers, insects, and the weather itself. In a year or two, the place may be marked only by a slight change in the lichen.

There are other lives harder to separate from the ground. Beneath overhangs, where condensation collects in beads, fine threads of growth cling to the stone. They brighten after a wet spell and darken again as the moisture goes. In cracks, small jointed forms wait with their legs folded inward, emerging at night or during the brief thaw. A stone may shift and reveal dozens of them, each turning once toward the cold air before vanishing into the nearest seam. Their numbers are easy to miss until a basin crusts over with movement: the frost lifts, settles, and seems to ripple as the bodies beneath it turn.

Nothing here gathers around a single center. The plateau’s life is dispersed across distance and depth. A herd may take days to cross the view. Beneath it, a thousand small animals work a handspan of ground. Farther down, water slips through basalt and feeds life that never sees the sky. The same substances pass among them in altered forms: salt drawn from a ridge, ground beneath hoof, carried in the gut, returned to the earth; a bead of water condensed in a warm chest, swallowed, excreted, taken up again by a hidden seam.

This is why the animals seem less like visitors than like a property of the highland itself. They do not stand apart from the stone. Their bodies have taken on its colors; their migrations follow its old glacial scars; their weight has made paths that resemble the channels of vanished water. The plateau is not gentle to them, but neither is it merely hostile. It provides a narrow range of things, spread thinly over an immense area. The animals have answered by becoming patient, exact, and difficult to hurry.

Even their sounds belong to the rock. A herd passing nearby produces small, dry clicks as the plates along its flanks touch. The mouths make a faint rasp over stone. A calf may give a low, breathy call, quickly lost in the thin air. More often, their presence is felt before it is heard: a pressure through the soles, a slow vibration running along a shelf of basalt. An animal pauses, head raised. Others pause after it, each at a different distance, until stillness has spread outward through the herd. Then, without any visible command, they begin again.

They are not unchanging. The great ridges on their skulls grow and break; old animals carry pale scars where the branching plates have snapped. Herds divide, exchange members, and lose their routes when a basin stays dry too long. In some generations the grazing fronts become denser; in others they thin and leave whole valleys to lichen. Their work is neither perfect nor permanent. It is simply carried out at a pace that makes a century feel like a single passage across a slope.

When the cloud ceiling lowers, the herds turn. When frost deepens, they cross toward the damp seams. When the storms pass, they spread out again, each animal lowering its blunt face to the rock. From a ridge, the plain soon looks empty. Only the faint grooves remain, and the darkened patches where bodies have moved.

Then a stone shifts.

Then another.

And the highland begins, once more, to travel.
