The ones who live there are small enough to pass beneath the lowest roots without stooping. At a distance they might be mistaken for pale stones shifting in the dimness: quiet shapes with dust-colored skin and hair fine as rootlets, gathered close to the walls. Up close, their faces are lively. Their eyes are large and dark, made for catching the green scraps of light; their broad hands are quick, sensitive to a hairline crack or a change in the dampness of earth. They walk barefoot, feeling the ground before trusting it.

They call themselves the **Iru**, though the name is less a word than a sound made together: a low breath drawn in and released. They speak quietly, in murmurs, soft clicks, and little whistles that travel through the hollow without disturbing its stillness. Much of what they mean is carried in gesture. A hand held against a wall can say *wait*. Fingers spread toward a patch of stone can mean *this is new*. The Iru are good at hearing what has not yet happened: the faint tick of grit shifting overhead, the small hollow note that warns a shelter wall has begun to loosen.

Their homes are shaped by many hands, but never quite finished. An Iru family may stack a few slabs into a windbreak, pack earth around a doorway, and lay leaves across the top. If the ground changes, they do not insist that the home remain where it is. They loosen what can be saved and move it a little way, leaving the rest to settle back into the hollow. A shelter is useful; it is not sacred. The Iru have no word for a house that must last forever.

They sleep in clusters, curled close against the cool hours, and wake gradually with the green light. In the morning they check the catch-basins for water, turn over leaves to find beetles, and test the newest cracks in the walls. Some tend the plants in the wet pockets, guiding vines where they can climb and clearing grit from the small grasses. Others gather seeds and fallen fruit brought in by birds, or search the leaf-mold for soft roots and edible fungi. They take little from any one place. A patch stripped bare today may be all that grows there tomorrow.

The youngest Iru are encouraged to play in the open ground, where every new thing can be seen. They race along curved lanes, make little towers from loose stones, and press their ears to the earth to hear the hollow answer back. When a wall shifts, they are often the first to notice. They do not always know what the sound means, but they call an adult, and the adults come with patient hands.

No one rules the settlement from a raised place. Decisions gather around the common ground, usually after a change: a seep drying up, a fresh crack opening, a shelter becoming unsafe. The Iru lay out stones to mark what they have learned. A smooth pebble beside a passage may mean *the floor is sound*. A leaf tucked into a split may mean *do not lean here*. Their marks are temporary by design. When the place alters, the old warning is removed, or the stone that carried it is moved.

They are not fearless of the hollow’s movements. When the earth gives a muffled settling groan, every Iru goes still. For a moment even the children stop their games. Then someone touches the wall, someone else listens, and the settlement begins to respond: a basin moved away from a new crack, a bundle of leaves carried to a safer roof, a handful of soil packed around an exposed root. Their caution is not panic. It is a kind of attention they have made into a shared craft.

At night they draw together in the deepest part of the hollow. The soft-footed visitor that crosses the lanes after dark is known to them, though they rarely see it clearly. They leave a little food near the wall and do not try to trap it. Sometimes its faint prints pass close to their own sleeping places; in the morning, an Iru will crouch over them, smiling as if greeting an old neighbor.

They are not waiting for the hollow to become finished. They have no picture of what it ought to be when it is done. Each new root, seep, or split in the stone is simply another thing to learn. They live in the narrow interval between the earth’s first shape and its next one, making shelters that can be changed, paths that can be forgotten, and room enough for the hollow to keep becoming.
