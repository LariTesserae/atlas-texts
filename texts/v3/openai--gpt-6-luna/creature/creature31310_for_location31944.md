The first inhabitants are easy to mistake for parts of the ground.

The pale grazers come out at dawn, when the fog still lies between the trunks. They are long-bodied and narrow-headed, with soft, almost colorless coats and broad, splayed feet that spread their weight over the yielding peat. They browse the tender shoots in small, shifting groups, never staying long in one place. A grazer will lift its head at a sound too slight for a visitor to notice; the others will follow its gaze, then melt away between the ferns. They leave little behind but cropped stems and dimples in the moss that fill with water before noon.

Their hunters are darker, smaller than their tracks suggest, and hard to see even when they are close. They travel the root-tunnels and fallen trunks, keeping low beneath the leaves. They do not pursue for long. They wait for the grazers to make a mistake, or for weather to separate one from the group. When they have eaten, they drag what remains into the wet undergrowth, where beetles and fungi finish the work. By the next morning, the forest has covered the evidence.

Above them, the trees are busy with small lives. Round-bodied, rain-slick animals shelter in hollows and feed on moss, grubs, and the soft growth on bark. Thin-winged creatures glide between the crowns when the air is still enough to carry them; in fog, they cling to branches and wait. Birds give their low, brief calls from deep inside the canopy. Their nests are woven into places a person would never think to look: under a shelf of bark, inside a curtain of roots, among the tight leaves of a plant that closes around them when rain falls.

In the warmer hollows, there are creatures that live almost entirely in water. They rest beneath mats of floating leaves, their eyes and nostrils just above the surface, and take insects from the air with a sudden flick of the head. Their eggs are laid in the dark pools where streams pass underground. The young emerge pale and nearly transparent, hiding among the roots until their bodies darken enough to disappear in the stained water.

But the country’s most enduring inhabitant has no single body.

It lies in the soil as a fine, branching web, runs through the roots, and follows the mineral seams down into the ravines. It gathers water and dissolved metal, exchanges them with plants, and grows through whatever the mountain offers: dead wood, stone cracks, peat, the margins of pools. In places where the underground streams pulse through narrow chambers, its threads thicken into pale, tough cords. These quiver when the pressure changes. The vibration travels outward through root and rock, and the forest answers with small movements: a tremble in hanging water, a shiver through a slope of leaves, a brief tightening of roots around a loosened stone.

Whether this web is aware of anything is difficult to say. It may only be a vast living process, sensitive to warmth, moisture, and pressure. Yet the animals behave as if its signals matter. The grazers alter their routes after certain pulses. The dark hunters settle in the lee of particular ravines before a storm. Even the trees nearest the mineral springs put out fresh roots in patterns that seem to follow the subterranean thrum.

Nothing in the high country makes a home in the way people do. There are no permanent nests large enough to be noticed from far away, no clearings, no paths worn open for long. A shelter may be a hollow beneath a root-mass, used for one wet season and then abandoned. A feeding place may vanish beneath new growth. The inhabitants leave their lives lightly on the land because the land is always taking those lives back into itself.

At night, the animals grow quieter. The pools darken; the canopy gathers its dim light close. Then, from far down in the stone, the mineral ravines begin their faint singing. Some creatures stir at the sound. Others sleep through it. The oldest things do not move at all, though their roots continue to spread, listening—or merely growing—through the soaked earth.
