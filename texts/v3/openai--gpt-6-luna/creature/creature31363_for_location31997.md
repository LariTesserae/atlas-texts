At first there is no one to see.

There are only the things that belong to the hollow: snails with their pale mouths pressed to stone, frogs packed into the root-dark, salamanders laid flat in the runnels. They do not look toward the entrance when you come in. They do not need to. The moss gives under your weight; the water keeps its small, unhurried course.

Then, after a while, the hollow seems to notice that you are there.

Not by turning or opening eyes. A bead of water gathers above you and hangs, brightless, from the rock. Somewhere beneath the leaves, a frog shifts once. The sound that might have been breathing draws a little nearer—not louder, only nearer, as if the space between one breath and the next has narrowed.

The creature that lives here is difficult to separate from the place it inhabits. It may be the dark beneath the shelves, which receives the water and gives it back. It may be the pale threads woven through the moss, passing unseen from root to root. It may be the slow agreement of all the small bodies: the salamanders holding still, the frogs crowded together, the insects resting beneath leaves that never quite fall. Perhaps each is a piece of it. Perhaps the hollow has gathered them around itself as a body gathers warmth.

There is a place, deep in the basin, where the roots cross beneath a mat of leaves. Nothing grows there taller than a thumbnail. The stone is black with wet, and smooth as if worn by a hand. If you crouch beside it, you may feel the cold of the rock change—not becoming warm, exactly, but less separate from your own skin.

The living things make room for you. This is not welcome, and not warning. It is simply how the hollow accommodates what enters it. Moss parts beneath your foot and rises again. Water closes around the print. A salamander’s flank moves once, so faintly you cannot tell whether it has breathed or whether a bead of water slid across its back.

The hollow has no face, but it is not blind. It knows your weight, the heat of your blood, the brief quickness of your thoughts. It does not ask you to stay. It does not ask you to leave. Its attention is the same as the damp in the stone: complete, patient, and everywhere.

By the time you climb back through the broken rim, you may think you were alone in there.

The hollow will have made room for that thought, too.
