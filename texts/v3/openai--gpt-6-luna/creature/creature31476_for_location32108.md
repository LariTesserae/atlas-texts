At first, the plain seems to contain only small lives: many-footed things ticking across the mud, narrow bodies threading the reeds, soft shapes passing beneath the water without a ripple. They leave no tracks for long. The rain fills them in; the ground closes over them; the dark keeps no visible record.

But the small lives do not scatter when something larger approaches. They draw close to it.

The larger creatures have no use for eyes. They are low and broad, their weight distributed across a hundred pale, jointed feet, or perhaps across folds of soft body that open and close against the mud. A ridge can pass beneath one of them without changing its course. It finds its way by pressure: the faint sinking of water into a crack, the tremble of a turning engine, the minute tap of another animal moving three channels away. Its feelers comb the ground ahead. Now and then it raises them into the warm rain, tasting what has fallen through the air.

They are hard to see even when they are near. Their skin takes on the color of wet stone; mud clings to their backs in a thin, dark crust. If one brushes a hand, it feels neither cold nor slick but faintly warm, with a texture like the inside of a reed. It does not flee. It pauses, considering the pressure of the touch, then moves on with a patient, crowding shuffle. By the time the water settles, it seems to have been part of the ground.

Smaller creatures live in the furred mats and under the brittle mineral crust. They gather around the larger ones, picking at whatever the passage has loosened. They also gather around the machines. In the seams of a pump, beneath the warm curve of a pipe, something grows in crowded layers: pale threads, soft beads, minute shells that ring faintly when the machinery turns. The rust is not merely rust. It is a living stain, kept damp and fed by the slow circulation of water. It creeps along the rivets, then holds its place for years.

The machines have their own inhabitants, though none appears to have made a home there deliberately. Little bodies nest in the housings and ride the turning gears. They know the cycles of each mechanism. When a chain lifts, they withdraw into the metal; when it sinks, they emerge again, tapping the links with tiny hard limbs. Their sounds are too slight to distinguish from the movement of water, but the larger creatures feel them through the earth. Sometimes one pauses beside a structure and lays its broad body against the wall, listening through its skin.

Farther down, below the soft ground and the channels that come and go, there is something that moves so slowly that it can be mistaken for a change in the plain. Its passage raises a ridge by a little, lowers a basin by a little, draws water through a seam and leaves it there. It may be an animal, though the word fits it poorly. It has no known boundary. The smaller creatures travel over it without knowing they have crossed its back. The larger ones turn aside from certain stretches of ground, not in fear, but with the quiet courtesy of one living thing making room for another.

They do not call. Nothing here needs to announce that it exists. A click travels through water; a tremor arrives along a ridge; a body brushes another body in the dark. These are enough. When the single heavy knock comes from beneath the flooded ground, every small movement pauses. The broad creatures flatten themselves into the mud. Beneath them, the hidden life goes on shifting, though more carefully than before.

Then rain touches the plain. Water gathers in the hollows. The engines turn, the animals begin to pass again, and whatever made the knock settles into its long, unhurried silence. The inhabitants do not wait for light. They have never expected it.
