The people who live there have learned to take up very little space.

They are not hidden because they are afraid, though they do not often go above. They are simply fitted to the hollow: sure-footed on slick stone, quick to turn sideways in a lane, accustomed to the low roof and the bright square of sky. They dress in layers of close-woven cloth, patched until the patches have become part of the garment. Their hands are broad and careful, marked by clay, sap, and the pale dust of the stone. They carry no more than they need, and set everything down as if someone else might need the space.

They speak, but softly. A voice can travel strangely between the close walls, so most conversation is made of a few words, a glance, a hand laid briefly on a sleeve. Children learn early to walk without stamping and to lift a latch without letting it click. Their games are quiet ones: tracing old mineral lines, trading seeds, hiding small carved animals in the gardens for someone else to find. They laugh, though it is usually a sudden breath of sound that vanishes into the moss.

Their days are marked by tending. Someone checks the channels after water has passed; someone else turns a pot so its leaves catch the opening’s light. Vines are guided away from doorways, then allowed to hang where they soften a wall. A loose stone is not left loose. A cracked basin is repaired, even if it leaks a little afterward. The work is shared without much announcement. People notice what needs doing and begin.

They grow what can live in the cold: roots, bitter greens, small fruits that ripen slowly, and pale fungi in the dampest rooms. Food is stored in clay jars and narrow wall niches. They keep a few small animals close—mice, perhaps, for their warmth and quickness; birds are left to nest where they choose. No one drives the snails from the steps. The tiny frogs are treated almost like neighbors: if one has settled in a doorway, people go around.

The oldest residents remember when certain walls were lower, or when a roof garden held a tree that has since been cut back. Their memories are practical. They know which stones sweat first before rain, where the roots have begun to press, and which stair holds warmth for an hour after the light has moved on. They pass this knowledge along while working, not as lessons but as small corrections: put the pot there; leave that seam open; don’t trust the third step when it is wet.

There are children and old people, and plenty in between. A household may occupy only a few rooms, but doors stand close, and the boundary between one home and the next is easy to cross. People borrow tools, water, and one another’s company. They sit on thresholds to mend cloth or sort seeds. Sometimes, at the brightest time of day, they gather in the central hollow and look up at the pale opening, not as though waiting for something to come through, but because it is where the light is.

They have stories about the world above, though nobody agrees on all of them. Some say the opening has always been there; others say their grandparents saw it narrower. They speak of the surface as a place of wind, heat, and distances too large to cross in a single afternoon. The stories are not warnings. They are simply about somewhere else.

In the hollow, a person’s life is measured less by what they build than by what they keep in good order. A lane remains passable. A roof continues to feed its garden. A channel carries water through another season. The people do not seem to be waiting for their settlement to become something grander. They have made a world small enough to know by hand, and they maintain it with the patience of people who expect to be here tomorrow.
