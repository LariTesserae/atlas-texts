At first, there seems to be nothing living here that is not rooted.

Then a pool, reflecting an empty sky, wrinkles once from underneath.

The inhabitants are seldom seen whole. A pale hand may be mistaken for a fork in a root, its long fingers resting just below the waterline. A narrow head may rise among the reeds, smooth and dark with peat, showing no more than two small, lightless eyes before sinking without a ripple. The marks they leave are easily confused with rain: a shallow depression in the moss, a neat parting in the grasses, a line of droplets on the dry side of a stone.

They are not numerous, or perhaps they are numerous in a way that does not require each one to be seen. They move between pools through passages under the peat and through the pale root arches, following routes that never quite stay where they were. When one crosses open ground, it travels slowly, with the patient care of water finding a path through reeds. Its limbs are long and jointed, made for feeling along stone and root. Its skin is the color of the lowland at different depths: black-brown on the back, gray and translucent at the wrists, faintly green where it has been still beneath leaves.

There is no clear line between an inhabitant and its reflection. Sometimes a shape can be seen beneath a pool, keeping pace with someone walking along the bank. Sometimes the reflection moves first.

They have no visible mouths. At close range, there are delicate seams beneath the jaw, opening and closing as they listen. Their hearing seems to extend through the ground. One may put its palm against a wet stone and remain motionless for hours while water passes through it. Another, standing in a channel, may turn toward a sound arriving from miles away—or from yesterday. It is difficult to tell which.

The lowland does not frighten them. Nor do they seem to possess the usual curiosity about visitors. A person can stand on the bank while several of them gather below, hidden among roots, and feel only the soft pressure of being attended to. They do not approach to beg, threaten, or investigate. They wait, as the pools wait, letting the stranger become part of the place’s sound.

Sometimes they answer.

A visitor speaks beside a channel, and later—perhaps much later—the same words come back from a different pool, spoken in the visitor’s own voice. The inhabitants do not seem to be repeating what was said so much as returning it to the water, where it can be kept. They collect sounds, impressions, disturbances: the weight of a footfall, a stone lifted from its bed, the shape of a passing storm. What they make of these things is not apparent. They are not storytellers, exactly. They are more like the listeners inside a story, remembering each event imperfectly and passing the memory onward.

Their young are almost never found. There are no nests, no dens, no signs of birth. In the shallows, however, there are sometimes small pale shapes gathered around the roots of a low tree. They cling to the wood like mussels and tremble when the water sings. By morning they may be gone, leaving behind nothing but a clean patch of bark and a faint, sweet smell, like rain on stone.

The older inhabitants are harder to distinguish from the channels. Their backs gather moss. Their fingers split into fine, rootlike tendrils that disappear into peat. They can remain beneath a bank through an entire season, breathing through the water and listening to the slow pressure of the land. When one finally moves, the pool above it may hold the empty impression of its body for several days, as though the water has not yet understood that it has left.

There are places where many of them gather: not in settlements, but in basins whose stone sings at a pitch too low to hear. On still mornings the water there becomes almost perfectly flat. Then small rings appear, one after another, spreading outward from points where nothing has touched the surface. The inhabitants are speaking, though the speech may be nothing more than a change in pressure passed from pool to pool. The answer can travel for hours through roots, hollows, and underground channels before the receiving water begins to stir.

They have no rulers and no apparent customs of ownership. A tree may lean over a pool for centuries, and the inhabitants pass beneath it as freely as rain. A channel may be dammed by a fallen trunk; they wait for the water to find another way. When a bank collapses, they move with it, or through it, and the place where they lived becomes an island. Nothing in the lowland is lost cleanly. They seem to know this better than anything else does.

Whether they made the country’s intricate patterns is uncertain. They do not appear to cut stone or arrange roots. Yet at the oldest shelves, narrow cracks sometimes contain a trace of their pale skin, as if they have pressed themselves into the seams to feel the water moving on the far side. In the deepest basins, where the land’s memory grows difficult to separate from the present, one may see a figure standing upright beneath the surface. It remains there while the clouds pass overhead. It may be watching the sky, or remembering a sky that has already gone.

The inhabitants are not the lowland’s guardians. They do not defend it from change. They allow trees to fall and water to alter its course. They do not keep the country untouched; they keep it accompanied. Their lives are spent among changes too slow to witness and memories too blurred to own. They listen as the banks break, as rain enters the stone, as one channel closes and another begins.

At the central basin, if someone stays until the light thins, a figure may rise at the far edge. It stands with water streaming from its shoulders. Its face is featureless except for two shallow hollows, catching the dim light from beneath the clouds.

It does not look at the visitor.

It looks across the country, listening to all the waters at once.
