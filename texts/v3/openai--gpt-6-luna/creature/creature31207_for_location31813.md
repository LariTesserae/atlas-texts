At first, the basin seems inhabited only by absences: a patch where the silt does not drift, a hollow that stays clear after every current, a pale crust that vanishes when the eye turns back to it.

Then one of the stones moves.

It does not roll. The gravel around it loosens, grains sliding down its sides, and the stone lifts by the width of a finger. Beneath it is a soft, pale body, folded close to the ground and covered in plates so dulled and rounded they might have been there for ages. It eases itself forward, feeling the current through the floor. When the water shifts, it settles again. Sediment gathers over its back. In moments it is only another nodule among hundreds.

There are many such animals, though few are seen whole. They live in the smallest darknesses: beneath ledges, between embedded stones, inside cracks packed with fine grit. Some are little more than a mouth, a pair of sensitive feelers, and a shell hinged shut against the water. Others have long, soft bodies hidden almost entirely underground, extending threadlike feeding limbs into the flow to catch what the current brings. Their colors are the colors of the basin—ash, old bone, wet stone—and their movements are slow enough to be mistaken for changes in the sediment.

Their lights are small and spare. A point of blue-green may appear beneath a shelf, fade, then appear farther along the same crack. These are not lamps but messages: a warning passed from one hidden body to the next, or a brief recognition between neighbors. They illuminate nothing. The dark remains whole around them.

And beneath these scattered lives is something larger—not a single animal with a beginning and an end that can be found, but a living network spread through the gravel. Fine tissues thread between buried stones. Pale feeding crowns rise here and there from the sediment, and close again when grit begins to fall. The network takes what little reaches the basin: flakes of dead life from the water above, the remains of smaller creatures, the slow nourishment released as old matter breaks down. It grows so gradually that the floor can change around it for centuries before any change is visible.

The little animals shelter within its reach. Some graze its exposed surfaces; others hide from it. When a current uncovers a section of the network, scavengers gather in the brief clearing, picking at the soft growth. When the silt returns, they scatter into their crevices and the exposed tissue closes over. Nothing is hurried. Even hunger here has learned to wait.

Sometimes a broad shape passes over the plain, low and nearly flat, stirring no great wake. It may be one of the larger scavengers, its back furred with grit and tiny pale growths. It presses its underside to the seabed and draws food from the thin living film there. A ridge of mineral crust rides along its spine; small shell-creatures cling to it and travel without seeming to know they are moving. Afterward, the animal leaves a shallow furrow that lasts until the next drift of silt.

The basin’s inhabitants do not make a home of the place so much as become difficult to distinguish from it. They inhabit the seams between stones, the sheltered side of a current, the moment before a veil of sediment settles. Their lives leave few lasting marks. A shell closes. A feeler withdraws. A faint light goes out beneath a ledge.

Then the current combs the floor again, and somewhere under the newly shifted gravel, something feels the water change.
