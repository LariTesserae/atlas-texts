The thing that lives there has no single body. It is spread thinly through the damp clay, under the pool’s dark skin, along the seams where warm water meets new stone. In one place it makes a fine stippling in the silt; elsewhere, a pale thread slips between grains and disappears. It has no eyes for the darkness. It knows the hollow by pressure, warmth, and the small changes that pass through water.

It grows too slowly to be seen growing. A mineral grain settles; a film gathers around it. A thread reaches beneath the pool, not toward light but toward a difference in the clay. Perhaps the rock’s blunt knobs and fresh planes are partly its work—or perhaps it has only learned to live among the unfinished shapes the mountain gives it.

The thing is most aware when someone enters. A sleeve against stone sends a tremor through its wet borders. Breath warms the air and leaves it changed. The pool wrinkles, and for a little while the resident feels the hollow all at once: roof, floor, ledge, the narrow split to the wind. Then the visitor is gone. The water stills. The bead holds to the ceiling.

It does not keep the bowl or the chips. It cannot understand them as possessions. But it has preserved the faint warmth of hands in the dark stone, and the place where the bowl was set stays, for a long while, subtly different from the rest.

It is neither sleeping nor waiting. Waiting belongs to creatures that expect an arrival. This one persists: quiet, almost indistinguishable from the wetness around it, holding its small, folded world together.
