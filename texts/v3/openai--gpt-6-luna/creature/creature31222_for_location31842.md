At first they seem like motion in the dust: a grain shivering, a pale thread slipping between crystals. Then the dust stirs everywhere, and the blister reveals itself as crowded.

The smallest bodies are scarcely more than joints and appetite. They have no eyes, no faces that can be picked out among their plates. Their soft undersides are the color of ash; their backs carry a sift of iron and sulfur, so that each creature wears a little of its home. They travel by many delicate legs, testing the floor through their feet. A change in the stone reaches them as a tremor. A change in the air reaches them as pressure along the plates. They do not look toward the red deep in the rock. They feel it warming their sides.

One crawls over another, and another follows the first into a crack. Their traffic has no obvious leader. It gathers, divides, gathers again: a slow current slipping around crystal spires, thickening where the crust can be rasped, thinning where the hot gas stirs the throat. The colony is less a swarm than a single substance that has learned to come apart. Each creature can be lost without consequence; each place in the moving mass is quickly occupied.

They feed on what the chamber offers in its meager way. Tiny jaws scrape the pale bloom from the floor. They worry at grains loosened from the ceiling, and at the translucent skins shed by their neighbors as they grow. Nothing is wasted for long. A cast-off shell becomes a meal, a brace, a place to hide. When one creature dies, its body is found by others without ceremony. They dismantle it slowly, while the rest pass over and around them.

Their world is felt through contact. The crystal points are landmarks, walls, shelters, and sometimes food. A narrow seam may shelter one creature for its whole life; a broader hollow may hold a hundred, pressed together so closely that their pulses travel through one another. They crowd into the warmest places, but not without limit. Too near the gas-throat, the vibration shakes loose grit and makes the plates along their backs shiver. Too far away, the stone cools, and the sulfur crust is less generous. Between these conditions they make their routes, wearing faint paths through the dust.

The mountain’s hammering is not a voice, though the colony answers it. When the hidden pressure strikes, the basalt shard ticks in its socket. The creatures nearest the throat pause. Those farther off feel the beat pass beneath their feet, through the floor and into their bodies. For an instant the whole colony seems to tighten. Then the pulse travels on, and they resume their feeding. Over countless repetitions, their paths have grown to fit the pauses: a gathering during the lull, a scattering at the next tremor. They have made a habit of the mountain’s rhythm without knowing there is a mountain.

They are not all alike. Some are broad-backed and slow, their mineral coats thick from long residence in the hottest hollows. Others are newly formed, pale and nearly bare, moving quickly through the gaps. The young do not recognize the old, nor the old the young. There is no courtship that can be seen, no nest apart from the whole chamber. Life begins and continues in the seams, in the crowded shelter of bodies, in whatever minute differences let one creature persist where another cannot.

Sometimes a fresh grain falls from the ceiling. The colony ripples beneath it, then closes over the small disturbance. Sometimes the throat narrows, and the ticking shard becomes quieter. The creatures shift their routes by the width of a leg. Sometimes the stone warms, and the whole population draws inward toward the red that cannot be seen. Their changes are so small that the blister appears unchanged for ages. Yet the mineral maze is continually remade: scraped here, packed there, crossed and crossed again by pale bodies moving through the dark.

There is no loneliness in the chamber. There is scarcely room for one creature to be alone. Each is pressed against stone, crystal, or another living back; each carries the warmth and movement of the others along its plates. If the colony has a shape, it is the shape of a place being occupied. If it has a mind, it is not held in any single body. It is the shared response of thousands of minute lives to heat, pressure, vibration, hunger—and to the steady, hammering world that surrounds them.
