At first, the moving patches on the flats seem to belong to the weather. Then one lifts its head.

The grazers travel in loose bands, long-legged and narrow-bodied, with dark joints and coats the color of old grass. They feed by cropping the low mats close, moving almost constantly, their small herds joining and separating without any visible signal. When they cross open stone, their hooves make a dry ticking sound. When they pass over the softer ground, they leave shallow paths that fill slowly with dust and seeds. The young stay in the middle of the moving bodies. At a distance, the whole herd can vanish behind a low rise and leave behind only its noise: a rumble that gathers in the gullies, then drains away.

There are larger animals, too, solitary ones that follow the ravine rims. They have blunt, heavy heads for pulling at the thorned shrubs and powerful shoulders for climbing broken shelves. Their coats are thick along the back and thin beneath, where the air is warm. They stand for long periods beside the stone bowls, not drinking so much as taking their time. The smaller grazers give them room. Nothing about this is dramatic. The large animals are not kings of the plateau; they are simply difficult to hurry.

Predators are harder to see. A tawny cat lies flat in the grass until the wind changes. A lean, pale runner crosses a saddle at dawn, head low, its tail balancing every turn. The hunters rarely pursue far across the open ground. They use the folds and seams of the plateau—the places where a shallow dip becomes a blind, where a patch of shadow meets a track. Sometimes the grazers scatter, a quick shiver running through the herd, and then close again. The cat remains where it was, watching. On a land this broad, an unsuccessful hunt need not look like anything at all.

The cliffs are full of birds. Some nest in cracks too narrow for a hand; others build their nests on ledges where the wind should take them. Broad-winged scavengers ride the warm air rising from the shelves, turning without a flap until they find something dead below. Smaller birds work through the mats in restless companies, prying seeds from dry stalks and insects from beneath leaves. Their calls travel cleanly over the stone. One short cry can seem to come from every cliff at once.

Most of the plateau’s life is lower than the eye first expects. Insects rasp from the plant cover, from the seams in the rock, from places that appear entirely bare. Some come out at night to feed on fallen seeds and softened leaves; others spend their lives beneath the mats, where a little dampness persists after the surface has dried. Tiny beetles make black paths around the pools. Winged insects rise in brief clouds when the sunlight reaches a warm hollow. Their sound gathers into a constant, fine-grained roar, broken by birdsong and the passing herds. When the wind drops, it can seem that the ground itself is speaking.

The plants make shelter as much as food. Cushion growths collect grit around their roots. Broad, fleshy leaves hold dew in their folds. Wiry grasses bend flat beneath the grazers and lift again after they pass. In the shaded cracks, moss and lichen spread slowly over stone, and small pale flowers open wherever water lasts a little longer. A single seep supports a dense congregation: insects, birds, the hoof-marked mud of visiting animals, and roots reaching down through the cracks. The pool may be no wider than a person’s outstretched arms, but its visitors arrive from across the surrounding miles.

The largest trees hold their own small worlds. Birds pick through their flattened crowns. Small climbing animals nest in hollows in the trunks and emerge at dusk to feed on leaves and fruit. Beneath them, the ground is darker and softer, thick with droppings, seed husks, shed feathers, and the remains of things eaten. The trees do not make a wood. They make islands, and the paths between them are measured in days of travel for the smallest creatures and in a few hours for the herds.

People are another kind of passing animal, though they leave more durable traces. They live in small settlements sheltered from the prevailing wind, and spend much of the year moving between known water and grazing places. They mend roofs, clear paths, gather fuel from fallen branches, and build their walls from stones that have already come loose. Their knowledge is practical and exact. They know which pools persist after a dry season, which gullies hold mist until noon, and how long a herd will remain near a particular slope before the plants there are cropped too short.

They do not try to make the plateau yield all at once. The herds are hunted in season, and only in numbers the land can bear. Plants are gathered from different patches, leaving roots and seed heads behind. A path may be used for generations, then abandoned for a while as growth closes over it. The old wall across the saddle may guide people, animals, and wind, but no one can say who first laid its stones. Each new repair is small enough to disappear into the old work.

At dusk, the grazers move toward the sheltered folds. Birds return to the cliffs; the hunters begin to travel. Insects keep up their steady rasp, and the pools darken until they hold only scraps of reflected sky. The people close their doors against the cooling wind. Far beyond the huts, another herd crosses a ridge, its many footfalls rolling over the plateau like distant thunder.

Then it passes out of sight, and the high country goes on living—beneath the mats, inside the cracks, along the cliff faces, and in the long paths that no single creature owns.
