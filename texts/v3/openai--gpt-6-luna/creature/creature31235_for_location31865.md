At first, the life in the town seems to belong entirely to the lake. A school of fish pours through a doorway; something pale folds itself beneath a bench; a broad-backed grazer noses along a carpet of plants, leaving a path that closes behind it. The rooms are busy with small movements, but nothing appears to claim them.

Then, in the shadow of a lintel, a pair of eyes opens.

The people who live here are slender, soft-bodied creatures, longer than a child and shorter than an adult human. Their skin is the muted grey-green of stone under water, mottled with darker flecks that make them hard to distinguish from the walls. Fine frills fan from the sides of their necks. Their hands are narrow and deft, with webbing between the fingers; their feet spread wide against stone, gripping by suction when the current stirs. They do not hurry. Even when startled, they seem to fold into stillness rather than flee.

They live in the old houses, though not quite as the houses were meant to be lived in. A room with a roof of fronds may be a nursery. A roofless courtyard, enclosed by low walls, may be where food is sorted, or where the oldest members settle to feel the lake’s slow changes through the stone. Doorways are kept clear, but only just: a mat of plants is useful for hiding in, and a passage that narrows over generations becomes a better passage for those small enough to use it. They repair what they need with braided roots, pegs, and strips of tough stem. Their work is quiet and difficult to tell apart from the growth around it.

They eat from the town. Soft plants, shell-creatures, eggs, and the little animals that gather in sediment make up much of their food. They also tend the living beds, thinning one patch, encouraging another, carrying small stones to anchor roots where the current has worried them loose. Fish feed in the channels they leave open. Grazers are allowed into certain plazas, where they crop the growth short. The relationship is not tame, exactly. The animals come and go as they please. But the inhabitants know which trails will bring a grazer back, and which walls are likely to hold its weight.

They speak by touch and vibration. A knuckle tapped against a stone can carry down a lane; a palm laid flat against a wall can feel the answer from somewhere out of sight. Their voices, when they use them, are thin clicks and liquid trills, easily lost among the knock of fronds and the ticking of fish in the reeds. Much of their conversation is carried in small motions: a lifted hand, a shift of the head, a light brush of fingers along a companion’s shoulder. In the darker rooms, faint bands along their flanks brighten and dim, not enough to illuminate anything, but enough to mark attention, alarm, or recognition.

The young are more restless. They chase the small fish through empty halls, darting between the squared shadows of old pillars. They climb the terraces and tumble through the plants, their gill-frills flaring whenever they are excited. They learn the town’s paths by playing in them, and learn its dangers when an elder catches them by the wrist before they follow a crack in the masonry too far. The old know where the shelf ends. They do not often go there.

At the central rise, one of the oldest inhabitants tends a patch of plants around a low, worn platform. Their movements are so slow that, from a distance, they seem to be another growth among the stones. They clear a narrow ring of sediment, set a broken stem upright, and rest their hand against the platform for a long time. Perhaps this place mattered to the people who built it. Perhaps the gesture has simply been repeated until it matters now.

No one here remembers the town above water. Its rooms are not ruins to them, and its lanes are not the remains of someone else’s life. This is where they are born, where they gather, where they make space for the young and bury their dead beneath the soft plants. They do not think of the stone as a human thing swallowed by the lake. They think of it as ground that holds warmth, catches sound, and gives roots somewhere to fasten.

When the upper water brightens, the inhabitants emerge from their shadowed rooms. They drift into the lanes among the fish, moving with the unhurried certainty of creatures who belong to every depth of their home. The old town does not wait for them to return to life. They are its life now.
