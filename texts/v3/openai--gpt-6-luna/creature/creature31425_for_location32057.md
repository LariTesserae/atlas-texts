At first the people are mistaken for stones.

They lie low against the basin floor, wrapped in gray hide or simply still, their backs slick with rain. Their bodies are broad and compact, built close to the ground: thick thighs for crossing soft flats, long hands for feeling along submerged cracks. Their skin is pale and pebbled, darkening where it stays wet. A clear inner lid slides over each eye when they dive. They can remain beneath the water longer than a visitor thinks possible, feeling their way through the channels by touch and by the small changes in pressure against their faces.

They do not try to keep dry. Their shelters are places to wait out a hard flood, to sleep above the coldest water, to store the few things that must not be soaked. The rest of their lives happens in the shallows. They move with the changing level, taking the newly exposed shelves when the water retreats and abandoning them without complaint when it returns. A causeway is useful until it sinks. Then it is a causeway beneath the water, and useful in another way.

The basin feeds them by revealing itself. They gather shell-creatures from the rock, scrape edible films from the pools, and trap the long-bodied swimmers in narrow channels. Their hands are quick, but never hurried. They know which shells are empty, which are young, and which pools must be left alone until the next turning of the water. A child learns first to distinguish the harmless movement in a flooded crack from the movement that means to bite.

They speak in small sounds that travel well through stone and water: clicks, soft whistles, and low notes held until another voice answers. The shell strings on their poles are not decoration. Their clatter tells them when the air has shifted, when a familiar wind comes over the shelves, when something large has passed nearby. Near the deeper seams, speech becomes gesture. A hand raised above the water can be seen; a palm pressed to the stone can be felt by someone on the other side.

Their histories are kept in places rather than objects. A notch in a ledge records a high flood. A channel cut into a basin’s lip shows how an elder once solved a problem that has not occurred for many seasons. The marks weather down. Someone cuts them again when they are needed. No one seems troubled that the record will eventually vanish. The water is part of the keeping.

They make little distinction between a settlement and a feeding ground. Shell-creatures crowd the stones around their warm vents; wings gather where the shallow water draws insects and smaller life. The people know the habits of their neighbors intimately, though they do not pretend to command them. They move nests out of the path of a rising sheet of water when they can. They leave certain pools untouched. They drive away a prowling swimmer only when it comes too near the young.

The young are small and watchful. They spend much of their first years in the shelter’s shallow basins, learning the sounds of home: water ticking under stone, shell against shell, the hollow boom that means a buried passage is filling. Older people teach them by taking them out into the fog and asking them what they hear. There is nearly always an answer. A distant flock turning. A stream finding a new path. Someone returning across the flats, still out of sight.

When they gather, they do not make a bright or imposing spectacle. They sit on wet stone, shoulders touching, faces turned toward the channels. Their voices overlap with the basin’s own until it is difficult to tell which calls belong to people and which to the winged things wheeling beyond the mist. They seem less like masters of the place than its most attentive listeners.

And when the water rises, they go with it: into the shelters, along the submerged causeways, down through passages known only by hand and memory. For a while the flats are empty of them. Then the water draws back, and small shapes emerge from the fog, carrying tools, shells, and one another’s children, ready to begin again where the stone has been uncovered.
