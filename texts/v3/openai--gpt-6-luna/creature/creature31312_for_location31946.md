The people are easiest to mistake for absences.

A traveler may cross a basin and notice only a low wall, a pale shape crouched in its shade, a few stones newly turned. Much later, looking back, they may realize the shape had watched them pass. The people keep no settlement that can be seen all at once. They live in scattered chambers and shallow excavations, in the lee of mesas and among the old walls, close enough to find one another and far enough apart that each hollow keeps its own weather.

They are not numerous. A household may consist of two or three generations, or of people with no kinship beyond having shared a dry season. Their shelters are made by improving what is already there: a lintel shifted to widen a strip of morning shade, a floor scraped smooth, a narrow channel cleared so that the next brief rain will reach a basin instead of slipping away. They build little above ground. A roof would catch the wind, interrupt the light, and make a promise of permanence the plateau has no use for.

They know the rock by its small differences. One person can tell where water will last by the sound of a footstep; another reads the dusk insects, or the color that appears on a ridge before fog arrives. Children learn these things by following adults on long, quiet walks. They are taught not to ask whether a hollow is empty, but what it is doing. A basin can be full of crabs beneath a dry crust. A seam can be holding coolness. A patch of lichen can mean that the stone has been wet, or that it remembers being wet often enough to keep its color.

When fog comes in from the west, people travel. They set out before it reaches the basins, carrying shallow cups and woven mats that lie flat against the stone. They collect what beads on the shaded faces and leave much of it for the smaller creatures. No one needs to say whose water it is. There are places where a person may drink, places where the animals drink first, and places that are not touched at all. These customs are not written down. The plateau enforces them more reliably than a law could: a careless season is long, and a spoiled hollow may not recover quickly.

Their clothes are light and close-woven, usually the color of dust or dry lichen. They cover their heads at midday, but leave their faces exposed to the moving air. Their eyes are often narrowed against brightness, and their hands are nicked from stone and thorn. They move without haste, yet rarely waste a movement. A person bending to pick something up may also be checking the dampness under a ledge, the direction of the wind, and whether a reptile has been disturbed. The gesture is one thing to an outsider and several things to them.

They eat from the plateau’s brief abundance: insects, eggs, small burrowing animals, the tough seeds of low plants. They take only what can be spared, or so they say; the distinction between what can be spared and what must be left is a matter of careful argument. At evening, people sit near the mouths of their chambers and mend things that do not look broken yet. They trade news as observations: the west fog arrived early; the dark stones in the lower basin stayed wet; the birds have begun standing farther apart. From these remarks, a season can be understood.

Some people have never gone beyond the plateau’s edge. Others leave for the lowlands and come back with new tools, new words, and an uneasy sense that the rest of the world changes too quickly. Those who return do not tell many stories about distance. They speak instead of water that runs away downhill, of green that grows over everything, of mountains with sharp edges. The children listen, then ask what happens to all the creatures when the rain comes.

At night, the inhabitants sleep lightly. The ground gives them news: a faint settling beneath the chamber floor, the scrape of grit, the ticking of a small animal in the wall. Sometimes the people wake when the basin goes still. They do not always know why. They lie quiet until the hidden tremor passes through the stone, then listen as the plateau begins again.

In the morning, their chambers look empty. The old walls hold their shade; nests fill their corners; the lichen climbs without interruption over the stones. Only a cup left to catch the fog, or a clean narrow path through the grit, shows that anyone was there at all. The people have lived among these hollows long enough to understand that being at home does not require leaving a mark.
