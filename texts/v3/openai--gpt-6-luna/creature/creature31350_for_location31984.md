At first, the inhabitants seem to be part of the stone.

They are found in the seams between old blocks, where warmth gathers: flattened bodies the color of mineral dust, folded close around themselves, filaments tucked in. They can remain there for days without moving. A passerby might mistake one for a chipped piece of masonry until the filaments stir, feeling the pressure of a footfall through the wall. Then the creature opens—not quickly, but with the unhurried care of a thing that has never needed to hurry—and shifts itself deeper into the crack.

The city’s larger inhabitants are harder to mistake, though not by much. They are narrow, jointed, and low to the ground, with dark plates overlapping their backs and softer, pale tissue hidden beneath. Their limbs end in broad, sensitive pads. They do not so much walk as place themselves: one foot, then another, each set down without waste. Their paired tracks in the powder are exact enough to look stamped. Sometimes a trail stops in the open country, ending at a face of unbroken stone. It may be that the walker entered a concealed passage. It may be that the stone opened for it.

They have little use for eyes. A face, when they have one, is a shallow arrangement of pits and delicate feelers. They read the world by touch, warmth, and vibration. A hand held against a pipe tells them whether it is carrying heat; a palm against the floor can distinguish a familiar machine from a failing one. They know one another by the small differences in the rhythms they make—the drag of a heel, the pause before a threshold, the pattern of pressure left on a stair.

They speak rarely in the way outsiders would recognize. Most of their conversation is made through contact: a tapping on a wall, a tremor passed along a railing, a filament laid briefly against another’s wrist. Voices exist, but are kept low and used sparingly. To raise one is to make a sudden thing in a place where suddenness has almost disappeared.

They are not numerous. A few may occupy a whole district, with long intervals between encounters. Some remain inside the maintained enclosures for years, tending pressure vessels, clearing vents, renewing seals. Others cross the causeways to the industrial compounds, carrying tools whose shapes have been worn smooth by use. They move on errands whose beginnings are no longer remembered. The old systems need tending; the systems that matter are tended. Whether anyone knows what would happen if they stopped is uncertain. Perhaps the inhabitants know and choose not to say. Perhaps the knowledge has passed beyond the life of any one mind.

Their bodies change slowly. A young one is paler, its plates thin enough for warmth to show through them. It stays close to the foundations, where the mineral-colored creatures gather in the cracks. With age, the plates thicken and darken. The oldest become so still that dust settles over them, and so thoroughly shaped to the city’s recesses that they can be overlooked even by their own kind. There are doors beside which one has rested for generations. Its neighbors know it is there by the warmth it leaves in the stone.

The shallow cuts on the metal tags are part of their memory. They are not words, exactly, but records of touch: a count of inspections, a warning about a pressure change, the mark of a route no longer safe. Each generation adds to them. Some tags bear more cuts than there are living inhabitants to interpret them. The marks are handled, traced, and passed on, even when their original meaning has worn away. An old tag may be kept simply because it has been kept.

They have few possessions. What they do keep is placed with care: a tool beside a shaft, a broken fitting near the machine from which it came, a small polished stone set into the wall at a junction. Their rooms contain little that can be separated from the work of staying alive. Yet they have ways of recognizing one another that seem to exceed necessity. A warmed patch in a wall is left undisturbed for a particular sleeper. A route is kept clear for someone who no longer walks it. Now and then, one pauses beside another and lays its filaments gently against the other’s plates. Nothing is said. The pause is enough.

When the deep vibration passes through the plateau, the inhabitants do not look up. They feel it in their feet and along their bodies. Some answer with a soft sequence of taps against the nearest stone. Others remain still, listening. The vibration may belong to the machinery beneath the city. It may come from deeper under the plateau, from some system too old to have a name. To the inhabitants, the distinction may not matter. The pulse is part of the world’s condition, like warmth, darkness, and the pressure of stone.

They do not appear to expect the city to end, or to improve. They do not lament the absence of water or the lack of light. Their lives are arranged around what continues: heat in a buried line, a door that still seals, a channel kept clear. When one of them dies, its body is carried to a warm foundation and placed in a narrow chamber. Its plates are left intact. In time, mineral gathers around them, and the chamber becomes another part of the wall.

From the outer causeway they are invisible. The city looks empty, its towers and capped stacks rising without purpose into the blackness. But, within it, a hand is pressed to a pipe. A filament tests the air at a sealed door. Far below, a creature shifts its weight in a crack, and somewhere on the plain a new pair of footprints begins—each dent the same depth, each step placed with patient certainty.
