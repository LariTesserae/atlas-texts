The people are easy to miss at first. From the ridge they seem like another pattern in the stone: a shutter opening, a figure passing along a bridge, a pale sleeve against a wall. Up close, they are neither as still nor as solemn as the town suggests. They hurry when there is reason to hurry, laugh in the narrow lanes, quarrel over small things, and carry on conversations from one level of a house to another without raising their voices. The walls keep sound well. Everyone knows who is speaking.

They are built, in a way, for the steepness: compact in their movements, sure-footed on worn steps, able to turn sideways in a crowded passage without breaking stride. Their hands are roughened by stone, rope, and the lids of jars. Children learn early which walls are safe to climb and which channels must be left clear. They learn the turns of the lanes by walking them, and the turns of water by watching for the faint darkening on an uphill wall.

Water makes a quiet measure of the day. A household sends someone to the public tap at its appointed hour; a cup is filled, a lid replaced, a place yielded to the next person. People notice leaks as readily as they notice a stranger. A bead of water gathering where it ought not to gather will bring someone with a tool, and often someone else with a jar. The town has little surplus, but what it has is accounted for without ceremony. An old person may be trusted to judge whether a cistern is ready to be sealed; a child may be sent to check whether a channel is running. No one calls this a lesson.

They do not speak often of the emptiness beyond the wall. They know it well enough: the pale faces of the mountain, the gap to the next ridge, the long distances where a person can be seen and not reached. Their attention stays nearer. They know which roofs shed rain badly, which stair holds frost longest, which neighbor has begun to walk more slowly. A repaired lintel is admired. A new room is noticed. When a house changes hands, people remember not only who owned it but where the morning light fell, and whether the cellar stayed cool.

There are families who have lived in the same few rooms for generations, though rooms are borrowed, divided, joined again. A marriage may bring two households into one stairwell; an empty chamber may be opened to a cousin returning from a lower valley. People leave sometimes. They come back, or they do not. Those who return are not asked at once what the world was like elsewhere. First they are given a place to sit and something to drink. Later, while a roof is being mended or a meal is set out, they tell what they saw.

The young are more restless than the stones, and the old less fixed than they appear. Children race over bridges and invent shortcuts through buildings whose owners have known them since they were small. They scuff the lanes, are scolded for wasting water, and grow up to scold someone else. Old people sit in the sun-facing courts, turning their faces toward the light when it comes. They can remember a shutter that is gone, a family whose rooms were sealed, a rain that lasted nearly a whole day. They tell these things plainly, without making the past sound larger than it was.

There is tenderness here, though it rarely announces itself. A door left unlatched for someone coming home late. A bowl carried across a square. A patch of roof fitted before the next cloud reaches the shelf. People know who lives alone and who should not carry a full jar up the upper steps. Help is offered as work, not as pity. They may grumble while giving it.

In the evenings, the town gathers itself inward. Fires are kindled; smoke climbs straight through the clear air. A few voices drift from the square, then are folded away behind stone. The residents’ faces hold the last light on the upper floors, while their hands move in the dimness below: mending a strap, sorting grain, pressing a shutter closed against the cold. They do not seem to be waiting for the mountain to provide more. They have learned to live with what reaches them, and to make a life from the spaces between one measured cup of water and the next.

And still, sometimes, they stop to look out. Not with longing exactly, nor with dread. The ridge across the gap is there; the cloud rests at the edge; the bare slopes keep their distance. Someone leans on the parapet a moment longer than necessary, then turns back toward the roofs, where a neighbor is calling softly from an open window.
