Nothing lives in the city as a person might live in a house. The city is its own inhabitant.

It has no face, unless a wall of folded bark can be a face; no voice, unless the long complaint passing through the rootwork can be speech. Its life is distributed so completely that no single stem can be called the living one. Pillar, bridge, floor, roof: each holds a little of the same slow purpose. The oldest trunks bear the weight. The younger sheets stitch their cracks shut. Roots feel along the stone, patiently finding what can be taken from it, and draw it inward without a sound.

The city knows the shape of things by pressure. A grain of mineral shifting on the roof sends a faint tremor through a hundred joined fibers. Somewhere far below, a root tightens; an arch answers; a hollow trunk turns the movement into a booming groan. The response does not travel quickly. It may take minutes to cross the city, passing from one living wall to another until the first small disturbance has become something immense. Then the sound dwindles, and the city settles again.

Perhaps this is how it remembers. Not in pictures, but in strain: the old weight of a hand against a doorway, the repeated tread along a passage, the patient shaping of a stem around a tool’s cut. Those impressions remain in the growth, though their makers are gone. The city has kept their proportions. It leaves rooms where rooms were wanted, paths where feet once passed, doorways whose purpose has outlasted the door. It does not seem to miss its builders. Yet it has not quite forgotten them.

In the empty chambers, nothing waits in the manner of a creature waiting. The walls simply continue to hold their shape. Dust slides over them and is shifted away. Fibers draw tight by imperceptible degrees. A bridge thickens around its supports. The city’s attention, if it has attention, is so slow that a visitor might mistake it for absence.

But the visitor would be felt.

A footstep would enter the floor as a small, sharp weight. A hand would leave warmth on the bark, then take it away. A voice would strike the chambers and return altered, and the city would carry that vibration outward through its ribs. It would not answer at once. Somewhere in the dark a stem might rasp against another; far off, a dry creak might travel from wall to wall. In time, a shallow passage might narrow, or a root might turn toward the place where the sound had been.

The city does not hunt, and it does not welcome. It has no need of company. It grows by holding fast, by sealing what is broken, by making every open seam part of itself. The rooms are not vacant to it. They are spaces it has learned to preserve—quiet hollows inside its body, as necessary as the wood between them.

Above, the pale plain keeps its blankness. Below, the roots draw what little the stone yields. Between them, the city listens in the only way it can: through the joined tension of its fibers, through the patient movement of dust, through sounds too deep and slow to belong to anything but the place itself.
