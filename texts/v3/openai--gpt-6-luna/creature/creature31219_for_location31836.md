At first, the place seems to contain only its own machinery. Then a drain runs against its usual current for three breaths and stops. A shutter stands open after the pressure on either side has equalized. On a wet plate, a line of small tracks crosses the water without disturbing its reflection.

The pale, jointed things that slip through the gratings are called nothing, because no one who has seen one has been able to agree on what it is. They are narrow as hands, many-legged, and nearly translucent, with dark knots at their joints. In the open they move quickly, but they can lie still for hours in the angle between a pipe and its bracket, looking like a crease in the metal. They gather where the pipes sweat. When the fans draw inward, they travel with the current of air; when the fans blow out, they disappear into the works.

They do not seem to eat. They touch the wet walls with their forelegs and leave behind a faint, darkening film. The film grows into the mosslike patches in the corners of sluices and beneath the walkways. It is not quite plant, not quite residue. Under a lamp it shows fine threads running through it, silver-black and branching. If scraped away, it returns—not always in the same place, but often near a warm collar or a live cable.

The small wingbeats belong to another stage of the same life, or perhaps to something that depends on it. Those creatures are hardly more than scraps of skin and delicate ribs, mothlike in outline but without a visible head. They rise from the damp mats in the minutes after a pressure change, fly in short, wavering arcs, and vanish into the dark above the pipes. Sometimes they strike the lamps. More often they seem to navigate by the vibration of the structure, following a hum too low to hear until it passes through the soles of the feet.

The inhabitants are most evident in what they alter. Water takes a narrow detour around a dry patch of growth. A channel left empty for days suddenly carries a thin stream, not enough to trip the sensors, but enough to wet a hidden seam. The creatures appear at the seam afterward, touching it, clustering there until the water runs clear. They do not repair the machinery as a worker might. They coax it. They find the loose tolerances and live inside them.

Over time, the marks begin to look less like scattered accidents. The black growths in separate corners share a faint, branching sheen after the fans stop. Small shapes move beneath several grates at once. A ripple travels across a flooded terrace against the draught, as though something below the plates has passed from one end of the valley to the other. The next morning, a pressure door that has been sealed for weeks releases with a soft sigh. No alarm sounds. No indicator changes. The place simply resumes one of its old, forgotten habits.

If there is a colony, it has no center that anyone can find. It lives in the pauses between cycles: in the condensate under a walkway, in the warm dark around a pump casing, in the thin film inside a pipe where the water runs uphill for a few meters before turning. Its members rarely meet in the open. They communicate, if that is what they do, by changing the conditions around them—moisture, vibration, current, the minute resistance of a valve. The great machinery becomes their weather, and they have learned to make weather of their own.

They are not trying to escape. The open flats hold little for them, and the high dry surfaces less. They prefer seams, undersides, channels that sometimes run dry but never stay dry. When a bank of machines starts in sequence, the inhabitants withdraw into the walls. When the sound settles into its long, even roar, they return.

The floor is being made around them, or they are learning to live inside what is being made. Fresh composite ribs bead with water. Before they are fully set, a dark thread sometimes appears along their undersides. By the time the panels are bolted in place, it has disappeared into the joints.

No one has seen the inhabitants build anything. Yet there are passages where the machinery should have left none, shutters that open only far enough for a pale body to pass, and drains that remain just barely clear. The place does not belong to them. It may not belong to anyone. But in its seams and damp hollows, something has settled into the running system and learned its measures. It waits through the roar, wakes with the pressure, and lives wherever the works have not quite managed to be finished.
