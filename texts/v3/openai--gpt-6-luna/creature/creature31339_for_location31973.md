They live by listening.

The crabs hear through their feet. A tremor in a pipe reaches them as a change in the floor; a shift in the rain travels up the walls and through their soft shells. They pause when the water alters, one pale claw lifted, and wait for the others to understand. Then the whole colony resumes its small errands: scraping films from the ceramic, carrying crumbs of living matter into the cracks, laying eggs in the warmest seams.

They do not hurry. Their bodies are almost translucent at the joints, and in the tea-colored light their shells hold a faint iridescence, like the inside of a wet stone. When they molt, they leave their old selves tucked into crevices. The next crab along eats what can be eaten. Nothing is wasted long enough to become a problem.

The insects are less patient. They hang in crowded clusters beneath the ceiling, their long legs gathered close, their wings ticking whenever the vent changes pitch. At intervals, one lets go and skims the pools. The blind fish rise to meet it, quick as a thought. They know the channels by touch, and vanish into the slots when the water runs too fast. In the dark they gather to spawn, scattering their eggs where the current will carry them into the sheltered hollows.

The amphibians keep to the warm breath at the chamber’s upper end. They press flank to flank, thumb-sized and damp, each one fitted against the next until the knot seems less like a gathering than a single listening creature. When the water shifts, they answer with a soft, descending call. The crabs stop. The insects stir. Even the fish hold still in their slots. The call passes through the catchment, altered by every pool and pipe, until it returns as something broader and quieter.

Perhaps this is how they learn the place: not by sight, and not as separate animals, but through one another. A crab feels the amphibians’ warning in the ledge. The fish feel it in the runnel. The insects hear it in the wet ceramic. Each knows only a little, yet the little is enough. Together they recognize the ordinary changes—the rain’s familiar weight, the pump’s deep pulse, the vent’s warm breath—and the rare ones, too.

They have no need to look toward the door. When it opens, the air alters. The crabs flatten against the seams. The insects close their wings. The amphibians tighten together, and a ripple of stillness travels down into the pools. The blind fish turn toward the new vibration: a stranger’s step, heavy and uneven, carrying the dry sound of the outer station.

For a while, the catchment listens to this arrival. Then the rain continues. One crab resumes its scraping. A fish slips through a slot. From the warm ledge comes the first small note of the amphibians’ call, and the others take it up, each in its own way.

By the time the door closes, the stranger has become only another tremor among many. The catchment keeps him briefly in its shared attention, then lets him go.
