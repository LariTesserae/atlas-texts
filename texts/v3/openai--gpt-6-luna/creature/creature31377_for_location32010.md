What lives there is difficult to separate from what keeps the basin in balance.

Behind the grille, where the water passes through filters and narrow channels, a dark film has gathered over the years. It coats the mesh, threads the seams, and fills the smallest sheltered pores. It is not the flat green growth in the cracks of the basin, though the two may have begun from the same sparse beginnings. Here it has found warmth, trace minerals, and the slow provision of moving water. It has spread without haste, layer upon layer, until it has become less like a stain than a hidden tissue.

It does not swim. Nothing in the basin has seen it move.

Its presence is instead a slight difference in the way the system behaves. A regulator holds pressure a fraction longer than its maker intended. A filter clears itself just before it would have begun to clog. Water, passing through the buried machinery, takes the quietest route. These changes are so small that they could be wear, or chance, or the cleverness of a well-maintained system. There is no single adjustment that proves anything. The basin remains settled; the pump keeps its level; the vibration stays low and even.

If the film has any awareness, it is unlike awareness in an animal. It does not look outward or wait for company. It knows the water as pressure, mineral content, temperature, the faint changes that arrive when a hand touches stone or a body enters the basin. Such disturbances travel through it, diluted and translated, until they are scarcely distinguishable from the ordinary facts of its world. A visitor does not startle it. At most, the visitor becomes a brief change in the balance.

In the basin itself, its signs are almost too slight to call signs. The dark mineral crusts interrupt the pale sediment in places where seepage gathers. The growth in the cracks lies close to the stone, short and unvaried, but some strands point toward the grille. Near the pipe, a film fine as smoke has settled where there should be only bare metal. None of it stirs.

The thing behind the bars has no need to fill the pocket with motion. It inhabits the hidden passages, the pores and surfaces the light never reaches, and the measured flow that continues inside the rock. The basin is its quiet face: clear water, smooth hollows, a muted knock passing from wall to wall. It does not own the place. It has simply become part of what the place does.

For a long time, perhaps, it has been keeping things as they are.
