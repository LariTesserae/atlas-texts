The basin’s inhabitants have no single shape. From a distance they seem one creature spread over the stone; close up, they are thousands of hungers passing through one another.

At the bottom of the food chain are the seam-crawlers: pale, many-jointed things that live where the rock stays wet. They graze the almost invisible films on the stone, feeling their way with soft mouths and fine trembling feelers. They move in tangled streams, each following the warmth and chemical traces left by the one ahead. When a stream reaches a fresh seep, it thickens there until the stone is hidden beneath its shifting bodies.

The shelled skitterers keep to the grooves. Their hard backs are dull as old coins, polished bright along the edges by the same routes traveled over and over. They graze, scavenge, and sometimes feed on the seam-crawlers. They know the basin by touch: a change in slope, the mineral taste of a crack, the faint vibration of a larger body approaching. They vanish into narrow cuts long before the biggest animals arrive.

Those larger animals are slow and low, with blunt heads and ridged backs crusted in mineral deposits. Their mouths open and close in a patient rhythm, drawing water, mud, and small lives through coarse filters. They do not chase. They follow the moving concentrations of food, shouldering aside the living carpet as if the basin itself were soft beneath them. Their passage leaves temporary channels in the crowd. Behind them, smaller creatures gather to feed on what has been missed.

Above, the winged ones roost beneath the shelves, packed close enough that their bodies make the rock seem furred. Their wings are thin, veined membranes, almost colorless in the dark. They cling through dry intervals and rise when rain comes, or when something below sends a tremor through the stone. A whole shelf may lift into the air at once. For a moment the basin is obscured by them; their wings rasp together, and their faint bluish sheen flickers like light seen through deep water. Then they settle again in the familiar hollows.

Rain gives each kind its brief abundance. The winged ones drink from the air and the ledges. The crawlers stream into shallow pans. The larger animals crowd the deeper pools, where their movements churn up food from the bottom. Eggs are laid in the water and in the wet seams around it—some in clear clusters, some concealed beneath bodies, some carried until the rain returns. The young hatch into an already crowded world. Their first shelter is often the press of other lives.

Nothing here is wasted long enough to become a monument. When an animal dies, the smallest creatures find it first. The larger scavengers come later, drawn by the disturbance in the ground. By the time the water dries, the body is gone. What remains—the bone, the darkened stone, the salts—is folded back into the basin’s quiet nourishment.

They communicate chiefly through vibration. A footfall, a body’s slow turn, the deep pulsing call of one of the great sifting animals: each travels through the ground and is taken up by the others. A warning does not need to be understood by every creature to change the whole crowd. The basin ripples. Paths open. Wings lift. A call begun in one hollow may return from the walls as a pressure felt in every body.

Their order is older than any one life in it. They return to the same channels because those channels hold familiar warmth and food; they crowd into the same sheltered cuts because generations have found them safe. Certain ridges remain empty, their cold stone avoided even in the worst hunger. No creature rules the basin. No creature needs to. The water, the rock, and the endless passage of bodies have shaped a map that lives in all of them.

They do not make the landscape their home so much as complete it. The dark flats are their backs, their trails, their feeding ground and nursery. When they rest, the basin appears still. When they move, stone and air seem to move with them. In that moment, the whole crowded place reveals what it is: not a wilderness filled with animals, but a living weather of them.
