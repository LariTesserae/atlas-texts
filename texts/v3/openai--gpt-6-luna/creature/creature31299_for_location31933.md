At first, it seems to be only another damp place: a darkening beneath one pipe collar, a softening of the pale threads where they cling to the metal. Then, on a night when the motor’s vibration falters, the threads draw tight.

They do not grow like moss. They gather.

The pale filaments have spread through the narrow seams and hollows, joining damp places that appear to have nothing between them. They run behind the tiles, under the plinth, along the lip of the trough. Most are too fine to see. Here and there they braid into translucent cords, almost clear, with a faint mineral sheen. In the dark they look like condensation that has learned to hold its shape.

The thing they make has no settled outline. It is a colony, if counted by its parts; one inhabitant, if counted by the way it answers. It can draw itself close around a pipe joint, or loosen into threads that cross the room unseen. It has no need to move all at once. A tightening at the ceiling may be answered moments later by a tremor in the basin.

It lives on what the room gives it: minerals from the water, warmth from the walls, the slow and steady shiver of the old motor. It cannot endure the dry plates of the floor for long, so it travels through the cups, channels, and damp seams, leaving no trail except the faintest whitening of ceramic. The insects in the trough are not its food exactly. It gathers what they leave behind, and sometimes shelters them beneath the dark rim of the basin. When the water dries, the colony draws back into the pipe collars and waits.

It has no eyes. It knows the room by touch: the difference between a drop that falls from a familiar elbow and one that arrives where no drop has fallen before; the pressure of a loose bracket; the altered rhythm of the motor. When someone enters, it feels the weight through the floor. It does not flee. It becomes still enough to be mistaken for the room.

Perhaps it is the reason certain seams have not opened. Perhaps the pale lines beneath the collars are its way of binding them. Where a drip has begun, it gathers around the leak, not stopping it, but giving the water a narrow path to follow. Over years, the channels deepen. Over years, the room’s dampness keeps its borders. It may be maintaining the place, or merely making a home in the habits of its materials. There is no clear difference.

The black pool at the far end is where it is most present. Its threads cross beneath the surface, too fine to disturb the reflection. If the water is touched, the colony gathers along the basin’s edge. The ripples stop there; or seem to. A person leaning over the pool might see the pipes overhead, the low ceiling, the amber point in its gauge window. They might also see, at the edge of vision, a pale shape withdraw beneath the water’s unbroken blackness.

The old motor turns. The colony feels it everywhere.

Some who know the room call its inhabitant the Gatherer. Others say there is nothing living there at all, only a quirk of water and mineral deposits. The threads do not answer either way. They tighten when a joint cools. They loosen when the warmth returns. In the long stretches between visitors, they continue their quiet work, binding damp to stone-like ceramic, carrying the room’s small messages from pipe to pipe.

It has no face, no voice, and no hurry. It belongs to the Condenser Room as the condensation does: not placed there, exactly, but made possible by everything that has settled into place.
