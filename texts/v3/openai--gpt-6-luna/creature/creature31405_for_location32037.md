Nothing lives there in the way an animal lives in a burrow. There is no body to find at the center, no nest tucked behind the roots. The inhabitants are the plants—and, less certainly, the space they are making between them.

Each plant seems solitary. The great blade has no visible stem thick enough to support it. The pale flower stands at the end of a stalk that vanishes wherever the eye tries to follow it. The roots hanging in the air do not reach for soil. Yet when one plant shifts, the others answer: not all at once, and not in any way that resembles a signal, but with the slow attentiveness of sleepers turning toward a sound.

They notice warmth. They notice weight. They notice a person most clearly after the person has gone. A visitor may feel nothing while crossing the chamber, then hear the roots stir behind them, tracing the route their body took. A leaf will incline toward an empty place and hold there, as if waiting for something to return. Perhaps the plants are remembering. Perhaps they are only growing toward whatever in the chamber has become most real.

Sometimes, where there was bare stone, a new shoot appears. It has no seed, or none that can be found. It may be no taller than a fingernail. Given an hour—or what passes for an hour there—it can stretch across the floor, curl up a wall, and disappear into a hollow that was not present before. The other plants do not crowd it. They leave space, though whether out of gentleness or because they do not yet know what it is, there is no way to tell.

The translucent grubs belong to the margins of this growth. They cross the stone with a slow, deliberate motion, pausing at shadows as if testing their depth. They do not eat the plants. They do not seem to eat anything. One may vanish into the dark cast by a pebble, while the pebble itself remains in plain light. If disturbed, a grub contracts to a bright bead and is gone. Later, a bead of moisture appears on a root nearby.

The pale flies are harder to count. They gather over the wet hollows, but their reflections outnumber them, or sometimes remain after they have scattered. They drift around faces and flowers with the same mild curiosity. A person who tries to follow one may find that the chamber has turned them gently away from where they meant to go.

There is also the possibility that all this is one inhabitant, still too young to have a shape. The plants may be its fingers, its feelers, its first attempts at having a body. The stone is not yet settled because it is being learned. The rain falls without a sky because the place has not distinguished weather from itself. Its strange distances are not tricks, exactly; they are the motions of something making room before it understands what room is for.

It does not chase visitors. It does not welcome them. It attends to them.

After someone leaves, the chamber may keep a faint version of their warmth for a long time. Roots whisper along the path they took. A flower turns toward the entrance, though the entrance is too small for its stem. Then the warmth thins, the roots grow still, and the plants return to their patient, spacious work.

For a while, the place has learned the shape of a person. Then it begins, quietly, to grow beyond it.
