The people are easiest to see at the edges of things: a figure crossing a high bridge in the hour before the lamps go out, a face behind a pane set level with the cliff, a small group waiting beneath a lift while the mountain’s warmth rises through the soles of their boots. From the open slopes, the city seems almost uninhabited. Its inhabitants keep to the sheltered passages and the inner sides of the ridges, where the wind is broken and the stone gives back heat.

They are not a silent people, exactly, but they have learned to make little noise without thinking about it. Thin air punishes haste. A person climbs at a measured pace, pauses without embarrassment, and speaks in a low voice that carries farther than expected in the dry heights. Doors close softly. Tools are set down rather than dropped. Even the children, who run where adults walk, learn which bridges hum underfoot and which stairways end in sudden exposure to the wind.

They are lean from the long climbs and the close quarters, their faces often dry from the air. Many wear layered clothing even in the warmth, because heat changes sharply between sunlit ledges and shadowed passages. At the wrists and throats, fabric is fastened close to keep out mineral dust. Their homes are compact, fitted into the mountain wherever a room can be made: sleeping alcoves behind service walls, kitchens warmed by pipes, windows that open not onto streets but onto immense distances. Some families have lived in the same carved rooms for generations. Others move often, following repairs, new lifts, or the latest survey of a wall that has begun to shift.

They have a particular way of speaking about time. A building is not old or young so much as settled, watched, or still changing. A road may be called temporary for a century. A crack is given a name when it first appears, then checked at regular intervals, like a neighbor who needs attention. The people who measure the ridges carry instruments along the shelves and mark their findings on metal plates. Their maps are thick with corrections. Children learn early that a line drawn on paper is only an agreement with the mountain, and agreements here must be renewed.

Work is threaded through the city’s quiet. People tend the heat ducts, guide quarry cuts, clean condensation from the undersides of bridges, and move water from one brief catchment to another. There are furnace keepers who recognize a change in the machinery by the faint alteration of its vibration. There are lift operators who can tell, from the weight of a carriage, whether someone is carrying a reservoir tank or only a week’s provisions. Much of this work is done out of sight, behind walls or beneath platforms. The city’s inhabitants seem to belong to its hidden mechanisms: at certain hours, a hatch opens, a lamp changes color, a distant motor begins to turn, and somewhere inside the stone someone has answered a signal.

Water gives the day its smallest ceremonies. At dawn, people set shallow cups beneath bridges to collect the night’s condensation. When rain falls, even briefly, roofs and ledges acquire a sudden crowd. Neighbors come out with pans, hoses, and patched containers, working quickly before the sun takes the water back. A child might be allowed to keep a little pool in a glazed bowl, not for drinking but for watching the mineral colors gather at its rim. Rain is not a festival here. It is a task that happens to be beautiful.

The inhabitants do not treat the mountain as hostile, though they do not trust it. They rest one hand against walls as they pass, feeling for warmth, vibration, or a new tremor in the stone. In some districts, residents tap the rock beside their doors before leaving home—not superstition, they insist, but a useful habit, a way of noticing whether the wall sounds as it should. At night, in the deepest blocks, the mountain’s heat travels through the floors and the people sleep close to it.

There are places where they gather to look outward. The high terraces are kept mostly clear, not as parks—there is nothing to grow there—but as open ground from which the next ridges can be watched. On certain days, survey crews set up their instruments while passersby stop to study the horizon. They know the distances are deceptive. They know that the neighboring tower, bright on its far shelf, cannot be reached by walking straight toward it. Still, they look. The ridges are their weather, their history, and the measure of what may one day be built.

As for the small pale animals at the pools, the people have not agreed what to call them. Some say they are animals that have lived in the cracks longer than the city; others think they emerge only after rain. The children leave scraps of mineral salt near the walls. The adults tell them not to. On rare occasions, a dark shape circles above a vent stack, and the whole district pauses to watch it vanish into the glare.

The people record these things. They record the changing seams, the unseasonable rain, the animals that may or may not have crossed a terrace. They keep their lamps lit and their machinery turning, making a home in a place that has not yet acquired the habit of remaining still. Their lives are close and practical, shaped by warmth, height, and the careful sharing of scarce water. But they are not merely waiting for the mountain to finish. They have begun to regard its unfinishedness as the ordinary condition of the world.
