The dark mats and the pale stalks belong to one life, though they look like separate, meager things. The mats are its old body: pressed flat into the cracks, sealed against the dry air, their dark surfaces holding a little of the day’s warmth after the stone has begun to cool. The stalks are younger growth, made slowly from salts gathered at the edges of the shelves. They are too stiff to bend in the faint wind. Their tips are blunt, and nothing flowers there.

For most of the time, the growth is nearly indistinguishable from the mineral crust. It does not spread across the open grit. It keeps to seams where the rock holds heat longest, and to the sheltered feet of the shelves, where salts collect. What seems like emptiness between one patch and the next is simply the distance its life can afford. Beneath the crust, threads of it follow cracks too fine to see, joining places that appear isolated on the surface.

It has no need to hunt or flee. There are no tracks around it because nothing passes between the patches—not in any way that leaves a mark. Its changes are measured in seasons, perhaps in longer spans: a rim of darkening here, a new stalk where yesterday there was only pale grit. Sometimes a stalk breaks at its base and lies where it falls. The mat receives it without sign or sound.

The warmth below the basin is its nearest approach to weather. It gathers what little the stone gives, and endures the long intervals when nothing changes. If the air shifts, if a trace of vapor crosses the rim, the growth does not stir; whatever response it makes is too slow and too small for an eye to catch. Only after a great while might a patch have thickened, its curled edge eased open by the width of a grain.

In darkness, the mats remain dark. They do not shine. Yet, sitting close to one, a person might feel the faintest difference in the temperature of the rock—not heat given off, but warmth kept, as though the living surface had learned how to hold on to what the basin was already storing.

There is no nest at its center, no single stalk larger than the rest, no sign that the scattered patches gather around a hidden heart. The basin itself is as close to a home as this life has: a shallow, sheltered bowl of old stone, quiet enough for its slowest changes to last. It grows where it can, and where it cannot, it waits without appearing to wait.
