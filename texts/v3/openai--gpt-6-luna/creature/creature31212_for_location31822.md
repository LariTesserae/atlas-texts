The creatures have no name for themselves, if they have names at all. They do not need to distinguish one from another in the way a traveler does. The plateau is crowded with them, and so each one is already among its kind.

Up close, they are neither stone nor quite separate from it. Their backs are pale and finely grained, furred here and there with short, dark bristles. Some wear shells made of overlapping plates; others have soft, folded bodies that hold the shape of a stone until they move. Their heads are small, almost incidental. A mouth may be a narrow seam beneath the body, or a round opening that remains unclosed. Their many limbs are often tucked away so neatly that they seem to have none. When they travel, the whole animal gathers itself into a slow ripple, as if its body were remembering movement rather than performing it.

They have eyes, but no one has seen them use their eyes. The eyes are dark and shallow, set into the head like marks in rock. Their longer feelers are more active. A feeler laid across another animal’s back may remain there for years. It might be a greeting, or a way of knowing that the other is still in its place. To disturb the feeler is to feel, briefly, that you have interrupted something important.

They do not feed. Their open mouths meet the empty air, or rest against the pale stone, and nothing passes between them. Yet they are not starved. They have no thinness, no weakness, no visible beginning or end. Their shells bear chips and old scars, but the scars do not deepen. An animal may sit with its head lowered and its legs folded under it until the posture looks like death; beside it, another may unfold from a shape that looked exactly the same. Neither event seems to alter the gathering.

The smallest creatures live in the spaces made by the largest. They pass beneath ribs, between legs, through the narrow canyons formed by plates of shell. The largest lie along the rims of the shelves with their bodies extending over the drop. From below, they resemble ledges laid upon ledges, though now and then a limb contracts and the whole cliff seems to have shifted by the width of a hand. Smaller animals cling to the vertical stone, their flattened bodies aligned with its markings. It can be difficult to tell where the cliff ends and the creature begins.

They have a way of being in several places without seeming to travel between them. The same animal—marked by a black crescent on its shell, or by one broken feeler—may be found at intervals along a ridge. Each instance is solid. Each bears the same small injury. If one is touched, all of them may draw in their limbs, or none may respond. A person who watches long enough begins to understand that this is not a trick of sight. The animal is there as many times as the ridge requires it to be.

The creatures notice footsteps, though not as a living thing in the ordinary world notices danger. Under a boot, a shell may settle by a fraction. A crowded mass may accommodate the passage of a traveler, the nearest bodies narrowing or broadening their arrangement without any single creature moving aside. It is possible to walk across them. Their backs are hard, almost level, and they do not cry out. Sometimes, several minutes after a foot has passed, a faint click travels through the crowd, reaching the ear from a place where no one is moving.

There is no hunger among them, and therefore no pursuit. No animal flees another, and none follows. Their closeness is not companionship, though it contains a kind of intimacy: bodies pressed to bodies, feelers laid across old shells, young-looking forms tucked beneath shapes that may be ancient. They seem to share contact without sharing need. When one disappears, the neighbors slowly occupy its space. The crowd remains the same size.

The sparse growths between them belong to a different order. Black-green fans spread from hairline cracks, stiff and dry to the touch. Red filaments gather in little bursts against the stone. They have no visible roots, but neither can they be pulled free. The animals sometimes rest against them. Nothing eats them. They may be plants, or simply the plateau’s way of making a mark that is not stone.

The low walls and doorways are also part of the inhabitants’ country. The creatures gather around them, and sometimes through them, but none is ever seen building. A tower may stand empty while animals crowd its base; a doorway may frame a single pale body at an impossible distance. Their bodies do not fit inside the little rooms, yet the rooms feel occupied. Perhaps something lived in them once. Perhaps the animals still do, in a way that does not require entering.

They do not seem to understand a doorway as a passage. They lie on both sides of one, or directly beneath it, and the view through the opening appears to have no more importance to them than the view around it. If a traveler steps through while looking away, the creatures remain where they were. If the traveler watches the doorway, there may suddenly be a creature on the other side that was not there before: same shell, same closed-eye mark, same patient arrangement of limbs.

Nothing about them suggests that they are waiting for the world to change. They are not waiting for food, weather, danger, or the arrival of their own kind. The plateau is already full. Their lives seem to have settled into a condition that needs no next event. And yet, each has a body, a particular scar, a place it occupies—even when it occupies the same place elsewhere.

A traveler may stand among them for a long time and feel, at first, like the only creature capable of noticing anything. Then the silence begins to feel less like absence than attention. Not attention directed toward the traveler, exactly. The animals do not turn their heads. Their feelers do not lift. But the traveler’s weight has entered the plateau’s arrangement, and the crowd has made room for it without becoming less crowded.

There is room to walk. The animals have left no empty ground.

They have simply included walking among the things that happen there.
