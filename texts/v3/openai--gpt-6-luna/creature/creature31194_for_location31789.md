Nothing here seems to live alone.

The smallest inhabitants form a silverish dust on the ridges. Each is a jointed sliver with a mouth at either end, though it feeds with neither so much as worries at the stone, tasting the salts and metals with fine, trembling feelers. They gather in cracks by the thousands. When the water changes temperature, they click once, all together. The sound passes along a ridge like a thought. Then they loosen themselves from the rock and stream into the shallows, where they become almost impossible to distinguish from the dark, moving sheen of the water.

The larger things feed on them. They travel in schools beneath the flooded flats, their mineral armor overlapping in smooth, worn plates. A school may be shaped like a long ribbon one moment, a circular knot the next. There is no leader that can be seen. Each creature turns when the water presses against it differently; the turn travels outward until the whole school has changed direction. Their bodies are pale where they have been sheltered beneath one another, and black along the ridges of their backs. Now and then one rises through the water film, exposing a row of legs folded close to its sides. It tests the air, though perhaps not for air, and sinks again.

On the ridges, the crust colonies are always working. They creep over the stone by increments too small to watch, their ribbed surfaces tightening and loosening in repeating waves. Their many tiny mouths rasp the rock. The loosened mineral feeds the little slivers; the slivers, in turn, feed the armored schools. If the colony is broken, it does not bleed. It releases a cloud of minute swimmers that join together in whirling bands, searching for the shape of the missing whole. Given enough time, they begin to scrape a new one into being.

Then there are the animals that move across the open flats. They are low and broad, with a shell that seems folded rather than closed. A line of dark, soft tissue shows beneath its rim. They drag themselves forward on many blunt limbs, each planted and lifted in a different rhythm. The furrow behind them fills with water from above. Sometimes they halt and open their shells, exposing not a body but a pulsing hollow, lined with pale filaments. The smaller creatures enter it and leave it, feeding or sheltering. The shell closes slowly over them. Whether they have been eaten is difficult to tell.

Farther beneath the plain live the ones that make the tides.

They have no clear outline. Their backs pass under the water as long dark ridges, then disappear beneath the black mineral shelves. A single one may occupy an area so vast that its movement is mistaken for a change in the land. When it draws in water, the basin’s shallows lean toward it. When it releases its breath, vapor rolls low across the flats and every bead on every stone shivers. Its passage sends the schools turning, the crusts tightening, the shell-backed grinders pausing with their limbs raised. Nothing flees. Nothing follows. The whole basin makes room.

Their old trails are channels worn into the stone, deep enough to hold water even when the rest of the plain is nearly dry. The small animals feed in these channels. The great schools travel them. The shell-backed creatures grind their edges down, year after year, into sediment fine as soot. In this way the deep bodies leave nourishment behind them without ever surfacing. They seem less like animals moving through the basin than organs moving through a body too large to see.

The inhabitants know the basin’s oddness by sensation. A school will scatter before a sound has been made. A line of small creatures will climb a ridge when the water above it is still. The broad animals may stop, fold themselves shut, and wait through an hour that has not yet happened. When the immense cycles shift, the creatures do not panic. They turn, all at once or in succession, and pass into some region of the plain where distance has become uncertain. For a while, the water lies empty. Then other bodies arrive, carrying different plates, different rhythms, different ways of feeding. The basin does not seem to notice the exchange.

Nothing in it appears to look at the sky. Its inhabitants attend to pressure, warmth, vibration, the weight of water against stone. Their world is made of currents and impacts. They recognize one another by what their movements do to the water around them: a scrape, a pulse, a brief interruption in the cold. They communicate without calling, or call in frequencies too low to rise out of the plain.

When the grinding roar comes, the smallest creatures cling to the ridges. The schools sink. The shell-backed animals seal themselves beneath their plates. Far under them, the great bodies turn in the dark, and the water goes flowing in its new direction.

Afterward, the basin is busy again. The little slivers return to the stone. The schools spread beneath the film. Somewhere below, something breathes. The plain continues to feed itself, slowly and without pause, as if every creature were one moving part in an ancient machine that has never learned to stop.
