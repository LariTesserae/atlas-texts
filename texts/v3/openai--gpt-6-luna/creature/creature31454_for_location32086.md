At first, the inhabitants might be mistaken for one more kind of growth.

They crouch in the narrow places between root-mats and walls, their backs rounded beneath overlapping plates the color of cooled ash. When they stop moving, the plates settle into the same angles as the surrounding stone. Only the face gives them away: a pair of dark, polished eyes beneath a raised brow, and a small opening that draws the thin air in shallow, measured pulls. They are not tall. Even standing fully upright, the adults come only to a person’s chest, and most of the time they move close to the ground on four long, jointed limbs. Their hands are delicate and broad-palmed, made for turning brittle seeds, testing cracks, and fitting stones together without wasting a fragment.

They do not hurry. Their bodies seem designed to keep a decision for a long time before acting on it. A hand may hover over a plant for several breaths before touching a leaf. A group crossing a courtyard pauses at every change in the ground, as if the stone is speaking in a language too quiet to interrupt. Their faces have few expressions that an outsider can read. Feeling shows instead in small alterations: plates lifting along the neck, a narrowing of the eyes, the slow uncurling of a finger.

They feed on what the plateau makes slowly. Some seeds are cracked between flat stones; others are softened in the mouth until their resin can be drawn out. The people also harvest amber from the plants, but only in tiny amounts, taking what has gathered at the stem’s edge and leaving the rest to seal the wound. They know which growths can be touched, which should be left for the grazers, and which are not plants at all but colonies of pale, folded creatures that have taken root beneath a ledge. Children learn these distinctions before they learn the old routes across the settlement.

There are children, though they are seldom heard. The young are small and pale, their plates still flexible and faintly translucent at the edges. They spend much of their time tucked under leaf-canopies, watching the adults repair a wall or coax a mat of green away from a doorway. When they play, they make no games of chasing. They build little arrangements from loose chips of stone, then wait to see whether the wind or another living thing will alter them. Sometimes an elder changes one piece by the width of a nail. The child studies the new shape for a long while.

Their speech is made for nearby company. A few low tones, almost too soft to travel beyond a courtyard, carry most of what needs saying. There are also clicks made with the tongue, taps of the fingers against stone, and a way of turning the head that can mean *come closer*, *leave this untouched*, or *I have noticed you*. Their stories are not told in a single sitting. A place-name may be repeated over many visits, each speaker adding a little: the year a crack opened, the plant that first found it, the person who taught the path around it. What cannot be said in sound is shown by touch—palm to wall, hand over a root, fingertips resting briefly against another’s shoulder plates.

They live close together, but not in the way of a crowd. Each house has its own small patch of growth, and each patch belongs to several people at once. The old are not set apart. They tend the doorways, judge the condition of the rock, and remember which walls have begun to lean. A person who can no longer climb the crisp steps may still spend a day listening with one hand against a courtyard floor, feeling for the faint shiver of a settling slab. Advice is given quietly and followed slowly. No one seems to command the plateau. Those who have been here longest simply know where not to press.

The long-beaked grazers are tolerated in the lanes. The inhabitants clear paths around them rather than drive them off, and some of the smaller animals nest inside the warm outer walls. When one of the winged creatures glides down onto a roof, people may pause to make room, then continue what they were doing. The settlement is not divided into the living and the useful. Everything has a place, though the place may change.

At the rim, the people go carefully. They watch the stone shed its black fragments and gather the fallen pieces into low retaining banks below the houses. Nobody builds at the very edge. No one seems to think of the plateau as something that might be conquered or made safe. It is a young thing, they say—or perhaps they use a word that means both *young* and *still deciding*. They live upon it as one lives beside another creature: attentively, without asking it to become what it is not.

And when the faint evening comes, the inhabitants grow quieter. They settle into the warm rooms among the roots, their backs turned toward the walls, their eyes catching the darkening light. Outside, the brittle plants continue their slow work of holding the cracks. Beneath them, the people listen—not for anything dramatic, but for the small changes by which the plateau tells them it is still alive.
