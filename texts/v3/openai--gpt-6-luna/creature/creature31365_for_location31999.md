Nothing here is born into a home. The young are born into a direction.

They hatch in wet seams, squeeze from cracks, or detach from the undersides of ledges when the stone gives way. For a while they cling to whatever surface is nearest. Then the floor shifts, water runs, a herd moves through, and nearest becomes elsewhere. The small pale crawlers know this best. They travel in layers, each one feeling the feet of the others through its shell. When the stone trembles, the whole stream turns before the sound arrives.

They feed on whatever change leaves behind: skins caught on sharp rock, eggs spilled from a broken nest, mineral slicks where water has stood. Some gather in the shallow pools, their many mouths combing the bottom until the water clears. Others rasp at the damp walls. They do not seem to eat so much as keep the hollow clean of anything that might settle.

The long-bodied swimmers are more solitary. They nose through the runnels, lifting blunt heads whenever a new draft enters the air. Their bodies are pale and smooth, built to slide through passages that will be too narrow by tomorrow. When a pool drains, they follow the last shining thread of water into the cracks. Sometimes they return days later from another wall, as if the hollow has turned them out somewhere they did not intend to go.

The great plated animals are slower, and because they are slow they appear deliberate. They wedge themselves into young fissures and brace with their heavy backs. A crack may widen around them. A shelf may split and drop. They remain, gripping the stone, while smaller creatures pour over their armor like rain over a roof. When at last they pull free, they leave behind passages that the rest of the hollow quickly learns to use.

Above them, near the seams of daylight, the winged clouds turn and fold. Each creature is nearly invisible alone; together they make a shifting veil, pale as breath on glass. They rise when the air warms and settle beneath the roof when it cools, though sometimes a sudden draft scatters them down the cavern in a shining sheet. Their wings flash briefly in the light shafts. Then the cloud closes again, and the brightness is gone.

There are creatures that seem to have no front or back. They roll in slow rings, gripping the rock with mouths set all around their soft bodies. Behind them the stone is clean and wet. The crawlers follow, feeding on what the ring-creatures loosen; the swimmers follow the crawlers into the new puddles. Nothing leads for long. Each kind makes a little room for the next, and the next makes it vanish.

When the stone cracks, the hollow falls silent. Not because the animals understand what has happened, but because every body has felt it. The winged clouds stop beating. The plated beasts hold their braces. Even the streams of crawlers seem to pause, each shell touching the next. Then the water begins tapping again, or the floor settles, or some distant herd resumes its passage—and motion returns in all directions at once.

The people who cross the hollow move carefully, with lamps and cairns and clappers to warn them of changes in the air. The animals do not mark paths. They carry the path with them, in their bodies: the tremor underfoot, the taste of water in a crack, the breath of a newly opened chamber. What looks to a person like a landscape is, to them, only a temporary arrangement of surfaces.

By the dim hours, the warm hollows fill with bodies. The crawling streams braid together beneath the ledges. Long shapes drift through the pools. The ringed feeders roll slowly over the stone, and the winged clouds settle in the high dark, their wings trembling against the roof. Then the roof lets go of water, the floor rises somewhere out of sight, and the whole congregation begins to move again.

They do not tame the hollow, or flee it, or wait for it to become finished. They live by being ready for it not to be.
