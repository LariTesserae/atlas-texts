At first, they seem to be another kind of movement in the water: a ripple against the current, a shadow slipping behind a grate. Then a small hand appears at the edge of a basin. It has long, narrow fingers, joined by fine webs; the nails are blunt from working at mortar and clay. The hand tests the water, waits, and turns a loose shard of tile until the trickle runs where it should.

The people who live here are low-bodied and quiet, made for passages where the ceiling presses close. They can stand upright in the larger chambers, though most of the time they crouch, climb, or move along ledges above the water. Their skin is the color of damp ash or old plaster, faintly patterned with darker seams. In the dimness, their eyes catch light like the wet backs of fish. They dislike the bright shafts, but they do not hide from them entirely: at certain hours they gather at the edges, warming their hands and watching the swifts cut across the opening overhead.

They have no private rooms in the way a visitor might expect. A family sleeps in a shallow recess behind a cistern; two old ones share a tiled nook near a warm flue; young ones curl together on woven mats above the high-water line. Their belongings are few and carefully kept: fired-clay cups, hooked tools, little lamps with covered flames, bundles of reed fiber for plugging a leak. Each object has a place. When something is moved, the absence is noticed long before the object is missed.

They know the underland through its small changes. A drip that strikes the wrong side of a cup means the channel above is clogging. A new tremor in the floor may be water gathering behind a wall. They listen with their palms against brick, and speak to one another in murmurs, clicks, and taps that travel along the arches. A brief pattern of knocks can carry a warning through several chambers; a longer one might mean that the cistern has risen, or that a child has found a frog nest and should be left alone.

They do not command the water. They coax it, catch it, divert it a little, and forgive it when it goes elsewhere. After a hard rain, they move through the rooms with clay bowls and lifted grates, opening one path and closing another. In the dry stretches, they keep the deepest tanks shaded and leave small pools for the frogs. Fish are never taken from the oldest cistern. Nobody remembers who made that rule, but everyone knows the fish there are pale, slow, and older than they look.

The animals tolerate them. Bats stir above sleeping bodies without waking them. Swifts sometimes drop feathers into the rooms, which the young collect and tuck into the cracks around their nests. Beetles are brushed from sleeping mats, not crushed. There are arguments about the frogs: some are welcome wherever they settle; others croak so loudly near the sleeping chambers that they must be gently relocated in a pot.

The inhabitants are not the original builders, though their oldest stories say the arches were made for them. They have patched the place for so long that repair has become a kind of memory. One marks a childhood by the first drain they were trusted to clear. Another knows every basin where water gathers after rain, including the ones that no longer appear on any map. Limewash is renewed where it falls away, but never everywhere. They leave some old brick exposed, so the layers can be read by touch.

When a stranger enters, the rooms seem empty. Then the echoes begin to arrive from the wrong direction. A face appears briefly in a darkened channel, upside down in the water. Somewhere above, a tool is lifted from a ledge. No one comes forward at once. The inhabitants wait to learn whether the stranger will slam a gate, stir a cistern, or simply stand still and listen.

If the stranger stays, they may be offered a cup of water. It will taste faintly of clay. One of the small people will watch closely as they drink, not from suspicion exactly, but because every visitor changes the balance of the place a little. Then, after a while, a child may crawl out from behind a warm wall and sit nearby, blinking in the lamplight, while the water goes on finding its way through the old channels.
