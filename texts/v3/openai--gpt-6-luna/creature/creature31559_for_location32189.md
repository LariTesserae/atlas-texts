The district’s inhabitants are easiest to see from the corner of the eye.

A figure will be standing beneath a covered passage, dressed in dark, closely fastened clothes, one hand resting on the wall as if feeling for a pulse. Look straight at them and the passage seems empty. Look away, and they have moved on—not quickly, but by a route the district has quietly made available.

They are people-shaped, though the shape is not always the same. Some are narrow as doorways; some seem too tall for the rooms they enter. Their faces are plain and difficult to hold in memory. You may recognize the way one pauses at a stair, or the small bright bead sewn near another’s collar, but later be unable to say whether you saw the same person twice. Their bodies are warm beneath the cold, and leave prints in frost. They breathe. They sleep. In the morning, a cup left beside a bed may contain water shaped exactly like the cup.

They live in rooms that fit them for as long as they need them. A room may be reached through a door in a familiar lane one day and through a cupboard behind a stranger’s kitchen the next. The inhabitants do not seem troubled by this. They carry little: a length of pale cord, a smooth stone, a folded piece of metal that might be a key. These things are not tools so much as assurances that something can be held, measured, or remembered.

They speak rarely. When they do, their words are quiet and practical: *wait here; this wall is still moving; come back when the water has settled.* More often they communicate by leaving things in places. A bead placed on a stair means the step has changed. A line of salt drawn across a threshold asks that the room beyond be left alone. At the lowest square, where the streets briefly settle into their proper positions, they sometimes gather without greeting one another. They stand around the unmarked circle, listening through their feet.

They know the district’s habits, but not its rules. They know which passages tend to return them to the same place, and which ones become unfamiliar if crossed too confidently. They can tell when a wall is about to acquire a doorway. When a new foundation begins as light in the air, one or two will wait nearby, watching without impatience. If a room appears, they may enter it and sit down, as though it had been expecting them.

Perhaps it has.

There are smaller lives, too. The clear beads at the ends of the white stalks tremble when someone passes, though the air is still. Green stains on the stone sometimes gather in the shape of a curled animal and move to another wall. Now and then the inhabitants find prints in frost that begin and end where no sensible path can be made. They do not follow them far.

The district itself may be the oldest inhabitant, or the only one. It holds the people as it holds water: imperfectly, in whatever shape is available. In return, they give it their attention. They walk its lanes, return to its squares, remember the rooms they have known. Where one of them stands, a line may remain straight a little longer. Where several meet, the surrounding walls seem to lean less.

They are not building the city. They are helping it stay possible. The district provides them with passages, shelter, and the occasional dry corner; they provide the district with the repeated thought of a place being lived in. Neither seems quite complete without the other.

When the upward-falling drop vanishes above the roofs, the inhabitants do not look up. They feel the square shift beneath them. One turns toward a street that was not there a moment before, and the others follow at an unhurried pace, carrying their small, uncertain belongings into the next version of home.
