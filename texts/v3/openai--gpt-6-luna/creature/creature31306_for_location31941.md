At first it seems the hollow is empty, because its inhabitants have learned to become part of its darkness. Then one shape loosens from the ceiling. Another answers it. Soon the stone is moving.

The bats hang folded along the roof and ledges, close enough that a sleeping body rests against its neighbors. They are small, dark, and warm, their thin wings tucked around them like coats. Awake, they know the hollow by touch and echo: the tooth of a shelf, the narrow turn before a fissure, the updraft lifting from below. They do not need to see the whole chamber. A click, a wingbeat, a breath returned from stone is enough to tell them where they are.

Some are old enough to have worn familiar hollows into the roost. Others are new, still clumsy when they climb, their ears too large for their faces and their cries sharp with insistence. The adults shift around them without ceremony. A crowded ledge is not a hardship here; it is simply the shape of safety. When the young wake hungry, the whole ceiling stirs, and sleep passes through the colony in ripples.

They leave in the dim hours, slipping from the cliff one by one or in loose, sudden streams. Against the open sky, each is briefly visible: a dark stitch crossing the pale air above the valley. They feed far from the rock, where the insects rise, then follow the mountain’s currents back. Near the cleft they gather their bearings by sound. From inside, their calls mix with the calls of those already home until the whole chamber seems to be speaking with one many-throated voice.

The bats are not the only ones who know the warmth of the fissures. Pale, close-clinging creatures occupy the deeper seams, scarcely distinguishable from mineral crust until they stir. Dark birds make nests in the narrowest openings, darting through spaces the bats approach more carefully. The birds are quick and sharp-voiced; their young call from the stone as if the mountain itself has developed a nervous appetite. Insects move where the warm air and damp collect, feeding on scraps, old feathers, and the innumerable traces left behind by so many bodies.

Nothing here belongs wholly to itself. The bats’ droppings feed the insects and the moss; insects feed the birds and bats; the moss takes what water the ceiling remembers to give. The cleft’s inhabitants have no need to understand this arrangement. They know only the ledge that holds, the crack that shelters, the draft that leads outward and home again.

When the colony takes flight together, the hollow loses its stillness all at once. Wings beat the air into a gust. Bodies brush past the stone and one another, quick and certain, while startled birds flee deeper into their seams. For a few breaths the roost is all motion and sound. Then the departing stream thins, the last wings pass the lip, and the ones who remain resettle among the rocks.

Their quiet is never complete. There is always a small claw finding purchase, a sleeping wing opening and closing, a call returned from somewhere deeper in the mountain. The warmth rises. Water gathers and falls. And above the immense drop, in a darkness too crowded to be lonely, the lives of the hollow wait for the hour to go out and the hour to return.
