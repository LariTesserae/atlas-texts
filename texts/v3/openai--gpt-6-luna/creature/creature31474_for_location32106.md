At first, the basin seems uninhabited. Then, in the shadow of a stone lip, a bead of water trembles against the rhythm coming up through the ground.

Something small uncurls beside it.

The creatures are low and blunt, scarcely larger than a thumb joint, with bodies the color of wet slate. Their backs are faintly ridged, their undersides soft enough to draw close against the rock. They have no need to hurry. In the daytime they wedge themselves into the narrowest, coolest seams, where their bodies lose little moisture and the deep machinery reaches them as a steady pressure. When the air dampens, they come out to graze on the nearly invisible life that gathers around mineral stains. They leave only pinprick tracks in the dust—marks so slight that a passing drop of water can erase a night’s work.

They are not silent, though. When one finds a fresh bead of condensation, it taps the stone with its forelimbs: a few dry clicks, almost nothing beside the racket below. Others answer from the fissures. The sound passes poorly through air but well through rock, and so the basin’s scattered hollows seem, for a moment, to be exchanging faint knocks beneath the greater noise. Whether the creatures are calling to one another or merely testing the stone, no one could tell. They have learned the timing of the vents. They emerge in the pause before each warm breath and retreat when the rim begins to hum.

There are larger inhabitants, too, though their existence is easier to infer than to witness. In some of the long furrows, the dust lies differently: pressed flat along one edge, lifted in a fine ridge along the other. A shallow pan may be found empty at dawn, its water drawn away without a track leading from it. Far below the crust, something moves slowly between the seams, not often enough to disturb the surface except where the stone is already weak. It may be a solitary burrower, or many small lives moving through connected cracks. The basin offers no clear distinction.

The buried machinery shapes all of them. Its heat keeps certain fissures habitable; its vibration carries news across distances too great for scent or sight. The small creatures gather near the old housings, where condensation forms reliably, and avoid others where the stone shivers too sharply. They do not enter the metalwork. They do not mend it. But they have arranged their lives around its ancient labor so completely that a silent basin would be, to them, an alien one.

At long intervals, perhaps once in many seasons, one of the larger things comes up through the crust. It does not rise whole. First the dust stirs in a shallow line; then a smooth, dark back appears, ridged like the rock itself. It rests beneath a warm vent while vapor beads along its hide. The small creatures leave it a wide, precise circle. After a while it sinks again, and the furrow begins to soften under the slow fall of mineral dust.

Nothing here is plentiful. Life persists in pockets, in pauses, in the thin dampness left behind by a passing rain. The inhabitants do not make the basin feel alive in the way a forest does. They make it feel inhabited in the way a sleeper’s room feels inhabited: by faint movements, by a warmth that has been kept, by the sense that something unseen has been listening to the same sound for a very long time.
