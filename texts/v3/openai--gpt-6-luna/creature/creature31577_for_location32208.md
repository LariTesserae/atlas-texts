At first, the hollow seems to be inhabited by almost nothing. A clear-winged speck crosses the light. A leaf trembles though the air is still. In one of the stone’s hairline cracks, something pale withdraws from the sun.

Then the place begins to move.

The smallest residents travel in shining drifts along the damp seams: bead-bodied crawlers, translucent mites, threadlike things that cling to the undersides of leaves. They pass over the rock in loose streams, dividing around dry patches and meeting again where the stone darkens. Seen from above, their migrations might be mistaken for shadows of clouds. Seen close, each is a tiny, deliberate journey: toward a flower’s warm mouth, away from a crack that has begun to dry, into the shelter of a fern’s curled fist.

The plants are full of other lives. The dark flowers open in the heat, and minute visitors crawl down into them, setting off their faint, uneven chime. Some emerge dusted in pale grains. Others do not emerge at all until the next warming of the stone, or until the flower closes and carries them with it into darkness. Beneath the thick mats, soft things feed and shed their skins. The old leaves are not dead so much as becoming a slower, lower kind of life.

All of it listens.

A click in one crack is answered by a rustle from the far side of the hollow. A tremor travels through the plant mats; leaves turn toward one another, their polished surfaces catching a little light. The creatures have no need to see across the bowl. They read it in vibration: the faint scratch of a crawling body, the wingbeat of something passing overhead, the small bright ring of a grain of stone falling from the rim. Their chorus changes with these messages, though there is no single caller and no single reply. The hollow speaks by being occupied.

Now and then, the long-legged grazer comes down from the rim. It is too slight for the scale of the mountain, a narrow body carried above the plants on jointed limbs. Its feet touch the shelves without crushing them. It eats slowly, choosing the thickest growth, and pauses often with its head lowered, as if listening through the stone. Wherever it passes, the tiny residents scatter from its steps; afterward they return, gathering in the furrows its feet have left. The grazer may be feeding on the mats, or pruning them, or merely keeping a path open through the bowl. The place has had a long time to make use of what the grazer does.

The three dark posts are part of this life, though nothing living claims them. The moss stops short of their bases. The small crawlers cross their shadows but rarely their surfaces. When moisture gathers, faint lines appear on the posts and the chorus rises into a pitch the plants seem to feel more than hear. The inhabitants respond without approaching: flowers open; the mats tighten; the pale grasses fold inward. Then the dew hangs briefly above the stone, and the whole hollow waits beneath it.

When the beads begin to drift toward the cracks, life follows their passage. Filaments descend from leaves. Crawlers pour into the narrow channels. The grazer lifts its feet and stands motionless until the last of the water has gone below. No creature seems to command the others. Still, each knows when to move, and when to make room.

They are not one animal, nor a colony with a center. They are a multitude of small appetites and sensitivities, held together by the bowl: bodies that eat, shed, flower, click, listen, and turn toward moisture. Their oldest habit may be the chorus itself. Perhaps it began when the first life found warmth in the summit hollow; perhaps the posts once taught the stone a rhythm that the inhabitants have since taken for their own. Either way, the hollow’s sound belongs to them now.

In the long intervals between wettings, they grow quiet but do not sleep all at once. A few creatures continue their passage through the cracks. A flower closes by degrees. Beneath a leaf, something makes a small, dry rasping noise. The grazer waits at the rim, its thin legs outlined against the sky.

Then the mountain cools. Water beads in the seams, and a faint thrumming rises from the stone.

The hollow answers.
