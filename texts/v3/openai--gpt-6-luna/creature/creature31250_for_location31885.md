Nothing living lives in the Cup. But something remains there.

It has no body apart from the place: dark ribs, pale slabs, the narrow seams between them. It is not the mountain itself, which is too vast and indifferent, but the mountain’s attention gathered into one small hollow. It knows the touch of water by the paths water takes. It knows cold by the clear ice that stays hidden in its deepest cracks. It knows the sun by the sharpness of its shadows.

It does not move when watched. Its changes are smaller than watching: a grain loosened, a fissure opened by frost, a flake carried away on the wind. The Cup’s inhabitant is made of those changes, though none of them belongs to it alone. It has no face, but every surface bears its weathering. No voice, but it speaks in the tick of dripping water and the muted click of tightening ice.

The old stone stacks are not its work, perhaps. They were made by hands long ago and left behind. Still, the resident has kept them—not untouched, but not entirely undone. A little water runs along the carved groove and gathers in its shallow round basin. The stones settle by fractions. Their edges soften. Whatever purpose they once served has worn away, while the arrangements themselves persist.

When mist arrives, the Cup darkens and beads. For a few hours, its inhabitant seems almost to breathe: water gathers, threads downward, waits in hollows. Then the cold closes over it. Light breaks the ice. Meltwater slips into the seams, and the hollow is dry again.

It is not patient in the way a person is patient. It does not expect anything. It simply continues: sorting water from air, grain from slab, old edge from fresh fracture. Under the immense, hard brightness, it keeps the Cup—neither guarding it nor owning it, but becoming, very slowly, the shape that remains.
