The people of Vey do not build so much as make room.

They live in the towers, though not always in the same tower for long. A family may spend a season on the high, dry floors, then move down when the air changes or the ground begins to hum beneath the foundations. Rooms are divided by curtains that lift and stir in the damp. Furniture is light, jointed, easy to carry across a bridge. Few things are fastened permanently to a wall. The people have learned that permanence is not the same as staying still.

They are quiet, but not solemn. They speak in low voices because sound travels so far here, and because they are accustomed to listening between words. A pause may be an answer. A hand laid briefly on a railing may mean: the bridge is moving; wait. Children learn to recognize the different creaks of a span before they learn all the names of the streets.

At night, people leave small lamps in their windows. Not to guide one another home—everyone knows the bridges, or how to find the water—but so the towers can be counted from a distance. If a light goes out, someone eventually crosses the plain to see whether the resident has moved, fallen ill, or merely decided to sleep somewhere else. Nobody calls this checking on a neighbor. They call it keeping the city in view.

The Veyans wear soft-soled shoes and carry narrow tools for lifting roots from doorways, clearing rain channels, and testing new cracks. They know how to step on the pale stone without trusting it. When a shelf begins to tilt, they mark it with ribbons that change color in the damp. When a whole stretch of ground rises overnight, they go there in the morning and stand at its edge. They don’t ask what the land is doing. They watch to see what it does next.

Some people listen for a living. They travel between the foundations with instruments made of wire, glass, and old machine parts, taking readings from the earth’s long, uncertain noises. Their instruments no longer produce useful numbers. Instead they give colors, pulses, and thin tones that must be learned by ear. A listener might come back from underground saying the western district will need to loosen its bridges by evening. The bridges loosen. The district settles. This is not considered a miracle. It is considered good work.

The city has other workers. Gardeners move through the old rooms, tending plants that have taken root inside walls. Waterkeepers watch the divided streams and open or close channels when the rain comes. There are people who repair the transit line, though no one has ever seen them touch it. They climb to its high, empty route and sit beneath the invisible track, waiting until the air around it grows bright with fine lines. Then they go home, and the line runs smoothly again.

The animals are less careful about keeping to their own kinds.

The small bright ones in the grasses move in quick waves, flashing as if a single thought were passing through them. They feed on seeds, insects, mineral crusts, and sometimes the beads of water gathered on leaves. When the ground shivers, they vanish all at once into hollows no wider than a thumb. The long-legged grazers come close to the towers at dawn. They strip leaves from the lower walls, and in return leave the stone dark with droppings from which pale mosses grow. Their herds have no fixed paths. One year they pass beneath a bridge; the next, they cross the same place underground, their progress marked by a slow series of bulges in the silt.

In the pools are silver animals with flat, translucent heads. They surface only when the water is nearly still. People say they can see the reflected sky beneath the ground, though no one has proved it. At times they gather under the bridges, turning together in the dark, and the drops falling from above strike their backs like a second, smaller rain.

The birds have learned the city’s distances. They nest in cracks, on ledges, and in the crowns of trees growing through rooftops. Their flocks pass in layers: low over the water, high between the towers, higher still where the transit line disappears into the dim. They do not scatter when a carriage passes. They open around it and close again behind.

Then there are the large animals, which even the Veyans do not name in the same way twice. They come out of the reed beds with their backs furred in green and their faces hidden by long curtains of hair. They walk slowly through the avenues, placing each foot with care. Rainwater runs from their shoulders. Sometimes they stop beside a tower and lean against it, as if listening through the stone. The buildings answer with a low adjustment of their joints.

No one tries to drive them away.

There is something else living in Vey, though people disagree about whether it is alive. It is the city’s answering behavior: the way a bridge lifts a little before a flood reaches it, the way lights dim when something moves beneath the foundations, the way doors unstick themselves when an unfamiliar pressure gathers at their frames. The old systems have no voice. Their replies arrive as changes in temperature, a shiver through the railings, a green band of light running down an empty stairwell.

The oldest listeners say the city did not learn these things from the people. It learned them from the land.

At the edge of Vey, where the bare shelf reaches toward another district, people leave no homes and plant no gardens. They go there in pairs, sometimes with children, and sit among the dark-glass stakes. They watch the tiny lights move under the stone. If warm air breathes from one of the hollows, they place a bowl over it and return the next day to see whether the bowl has shifted. If seedlings appear, they leave them. If the ground opens, they step back.

They have no word for the thing they are waiting for.

Perhaps it is a district. Perhaps it is a creature. Perhaps the shelf is learning the shape of a place where someone might live.

In Vey, these possibilities are not far apart. The animals pass through the avenues as if they belong there. The people sleep lightly, ready to move. The towers bend around trees, and the earth raises its new stone through everything that was meant to outlast it.

When the plain goes quiet, the living do not mistake the silence for emptiness.

They wait.

Then, when the ground begins to speak again, each of them answers in its own way.
