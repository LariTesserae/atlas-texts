At first, you think the city is empty because you are looking for bodies.

The first sign of its inhabitants is usually a room that has grown warm. Not warm enough to thaw anything, or even to comfort a hand—only enough that the stone feels as if someone has just left it. Dust may be swept from a threshold in a narrow, careful arc. A door, shut when you passed it, will stand open when you return by another street.

The residents are difficult to see directly. They are pale, but not the white of the salt flats: more like the inside of a shell, or the light beneath thin ice. They have no fixed height. In a distant court, one may seem no larger than a child standing beside a pillar; close by, the same presence fills the whole doorway, though it has not stepped nearer. Their outlines are made of overlapping folds, fine as mineral membrane. Sometimes the folds draw together into something almost like a face. Sometimes they part, and there is only the dim wall behind them.

They do not walk as visitors walk. A resident turns its attention toward a stair, and the stair becomes the route it has taken. If it crosses a room, the crossing may be over before you notice it began—or may continue for several minutes after its outline has disappeared. They are most often glimpsed at the edge of sight, where a corridor changes direction or a balcony meets the blank air. Looking straight at one does not make it vanish. It makes it harder to tell where it ends.

The soft clicks that travel through the towers belong to them, though not all of them. Some are the residents settling their folded bodies against the stone. Others are replies, sent from far across the city and received in the wrong order. A single click may mean *I am here*. Two, after a pause, may mean *I remember where you were*. Their conversations can last for days. They do not seem to mind the delay.

The branching stains on the upper walls are closer to their footsteps than to plants. Where a resident rests, a delicate lace of mineral spreads through the stone. It responds to warmth, pressure, and the passing of other living things. In old districts the walls are threaded with it, pale lines running into ceilings and down behind doors. The residents touch these patterns with their folded limbs. They may be reading them; they may be leaving something for another to read. A wall in a quiet quarter can carry the history of a conversation that began before the avenue outside had its present shape.

They keep houses, but not in the way houses are usually kept. Some prefer rooms with no visible exit. Others gather in high chambers where the wind passes through the walls without making a sound. A family—if that is what they are—may occupy several buildings separated by a gulf of bare rock. For a season they are near one another. Later, the city places a new court between them, and they continue as before, meeting in the same room from different directions.

There may be young among them. The small pale creatures beneath the bridges unfold at the dimmest hours, gliding from one ledge to another in brief, silent arcs. They do not resemble the larger residents, but sometimes a resident waits beneath a bridge until one has landed. It touches the creature once, very gently, and the two remain still for a long while. Afterward the little one may glide away, or may be gone without crossing the intervening air.

The residents are patient with visitors. They do not approach, unless approaching is what you call the moment when the corridor behind you becomes a little shorter. They leave space around you in the streets. If you sleep in one of their rooms, the air will be warmer in the morning, and there may be a faint line of clean stone beside your bed. It is not a warning. It is not an invitation, exactly. It is the mark of attention.

They do not make tools. They do not seem to need them. The city changes in answer to their presence, or perhaps their presence is one of the ways the city changes. A bridge will come to rest against a tower where it did not reach before. A bare stretch of street will gather dust in the shape of a small courtyard. One morning, a doorway you have passed a dozen times will open onto a room containing three residents, all turned toward the place where you are standing.

None will speak.

After a time, you may learn to answer their clicks. Not by imitating the sound, but by waiting before you move; by leaving a door open when you have no reason to leave it open; by remembering a room without insisting that you can find it again. If you do these things, the city grows no friendlier. It simply becomes more willing to include you in what it is doing.

And eventually, somewhere far behind you, a door closes with care.
