The people of the plateau are difficult to separate from the work of keeping it habitable. They move along covered walks with hoods drawn close, carrying baskets through the rain and stepping around the slow, dignified traffic of birds. Their clothes are patched against spray and grit. Their boots leave dark prints on the stone, soon blurred by water. Most know the channels by sound: a clear rush means a catchment is running well; a hollow knocking below the street means a drain has taken on something it should not have.

They grow up learning where not to stand. A ledge that looks empty in the morning may be crowded by noon; a roof may belong to the birds for a season, then return to human use after the young have flown. The old have names for these shifting borders, though few are marked on maps. Children learn them by following adults, and by being scolded when they forget. There are gates to close, walkways to cover, and whole streets to surrender without complaint. A bird can be shooed from a doorway. A thousand cannot.

The city’s trades follow the lives above and below it. Fishers work the sea far beneath the rim and haul their catch up by lift and cable. Sorters handle feathers, shell, salt, and the mineral crust scraped from channels and cisterns. Millwrights keep the pumps turning; masons mend walls where water has worried a seam wider. Some workers climb to the nesting terraces to clear dangerous blockages or repair the frames built around them. They do so quietly and in season, with gloves, masks, and the practiced caution of people entering another creature’s crowded home.

The birds are everywhere, but they are not all alike. Great seabirds claim the upper ledges in squabbling ranks, shoulder to shoulder, while smaller ones flick through eaves and ironwork. They know the city’s routines. They wait by processing yards for scraps, nest in the warm hollows around old pipes, and cross the avenues where generations of people have learned to make room. From a distance, the flocks rise as one pale confusion. Close by, each bird is alert to its own small territory: a gutter, a chimney, a patch of wind-sheltered roof. Their cries are not a single sound but a constant argument, carried from one part of the basin to another.

Underfoot, quieter lives occupy the water and loose stone. Pale burrowers tunnel beneath the nesting shelves, feeding on what falls there. Long, narrow scavengers slip along gutters and vanish into the drains. In the old quarry pits, blind fish turn through mineral-dark water, and thick-shelled crawlers cling to submerged walls. The people know many of these animals only by evidence: a ripple in a cistern, a clicking sound inside a drain, the sudden clean sweep of a channel after some unseen mouth has passed through it.

No one here thinks of the plateau as tame. The city is not laid over the rock so much as caught in its folds. Water chooses its routes; the birds choose their ledges; people build around both, then rebuild when either changes its mind. The oldest residents speak of neighborhoods by the names of vanished roosts or buried channels. They remember when a certain roof was quiet, or when the runoff ran elsewhere. Their memories are small against the age of the stone, but the city is made from such memories: adjustments, warnings, repairs, and habits handed down until they seem as permanent as the terraces.

At night, the inhabitants—human and otherwise—continue their ordinary work. Pumps throb beneath the streets. Water ticks from the bridges. Birds mutter in their sleep, or wake to call across the basin. Behind lit windows, people mend nets and tools, eat in kitchens clouded with steam, and listen to the rain find its way through the city. The plateau’s great lives are visible overhead; its smaller ones move in cracks and dark water; and the people, for all their engines and stonework, live among them as another settled species, learning when to build, when to wait, and when to give the street back.
