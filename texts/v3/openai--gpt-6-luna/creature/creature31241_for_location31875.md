The people who remain here are mostly those whose work is measured in levels, pressures, and hours until the next tide. They live above the floodline in narrow blocks attached to pumping stations, or in apartments built into the upper floors of old warehouses. Their windows are small and often sealed against damp. They know the city by its sounds: the change in a pump’s pitch, the knock of a gate that has taken on too much silt, the sudden hush when power fails somewhere along the basin.

They walk the causeways in waterproof boots, carrying tools in bags that have gone soft at the seams. They rarely hurry. The water will arrive when it arrives. At shift change, two workers may meet beneath a pipe bridge, exchange a few words, and continue in opposite directions without looking back. Some have lived here for years without seeing the flats at low tide. Others know every exposed channel and the safe places to stand when the mud begins to move.

There are fewer of them than the buildings seem to expect. Whole blocks stand empty, their doors swollen shut, their lower rooms rinsed clean and filled again by successive floods. In the vacant places, the city’s other residents have taken up their routines.

Crabs come first along the edges: through grates, around the feet of flood gauges, beneath the lips of concrete stairs. They crowd into the warm drainage channels when the water is cold, and retreat beneath broken slabs when the tide slackens. They are not shy. A worker crossing an apron may find the crabs parting before their boots, then closing behind them like water over a stone.

The fish arrive in larger numbers and with less warning. When a channel begins to darken, people at the pumps glance up from their instruments. Shoals fill the water from bank to bank, their movement making a single surface that wrinkles and shines. They pass beneath bridges, through sluices, and around the pilings of idle docks. Some are small enough to vanish in the turbulence from an outflow; others turn beneath the lamps, broad backs briefly silver, then gone. The operators know which gates to leave open for them. They know, too, that the fish will find another route if a gate stays shut.

The birds wait out on the flats. For hours they may appear as a thin line along the pale rim of a settling pond, each one standing still in the damp light. Then a feeding shoal comes into the shallows, and the line breaks apart. The birds lift together, wheel over the channels, and settle again somewhere farther down the basin. Their cries are sharp and brief. The sound travels farther than it should across the open water.

None of these creatures owns the city. They pass through it, gather in it, leave it bare again. But the people who tend the pumps have learned to regard their arrival as a kind of weather. A sudden raft of fish under the lock means the tide has turned. Crabs moving inland across the service road mean the lower channels are filling. Birds rising from the flats may signal nothing at all, or may be the first visible sign that the water has begun to move.

After rain, when every surface is dark and shining, the inhabitants seem to change places. The workers draw back behind metal doors. The crabs come across the steps. Fish press into the canals, and birds follow them overhead. For a while the city belongs to the water’s traffic: a passing, feeding, gathering life that uses every flooded basement and open gate as though it had been built for it.

Then the tide falls. The channels pale. The fish slip away, the birds stretch out along the distant flats, and the crabs disappear into their cracks. A worker comes back to the lock and checks the gauge. The pumps resume their low, patient rhythm. The city is quiet again, though never empty.
