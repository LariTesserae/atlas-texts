At first it seems to be one of the pale stones in the deepest bowl: an oblong shape under cloudy water, nearly the same color as the sediment, with a thin rim of mineral crust along its back. It does not stir when the faint light shifts. It does not stir when a drop falls nearby.

Then, after a long while, the shape draws in its edges.

It is a small animal, though not one readily named. Its body is soft and low, scarcely thicker than a hand, with four delicate limbs folded close beneath it. The skin is almost translucent, showing a muted thread of blue-gray beneath the milk-pale surface. Along its spine, mineral has gathered in a ragged line of tiny plates. They resemble the basin’s folded crusts so closely that, when it rests against the rock, the distinction between creature and ground becomes difficult to hold.

It has no visible eyes. It senses disturbance through the water: a tremor passing down a channel, the pressure of a foot on the stone shelf, the slow arrival of a falling bead. At such times it becomes absolutely still. Only when the basin has quieted again does it move, and then with a patience that seems less like caution than a different measure of time. A forelimb reaches forward. The body follows. It may take several minutes to cross the width of a hand.

It keeps to the deep bowls and the narrow seams beneath the ledges, where the water stays warmest. It feeds on what gathers there—minute life in the sediment, things lodged in the cracks that are too small to see without disturbing them. The basin looks barren, but it is not entirely so. In its darkest crevices, a faint slick comes and goes; the animal grazes that hidden abundance without ever making the water cloudy.

Sometimes it climbs onto a wet stone. There it rests with its throat pressed flat against the surface, drawing warmth from the mineral beneath it. The pale plates along its back dry to a dull silver, then darken again as a film of water passes over them. It has no call. Its only sound is the faintest click when one of those plates touches stone.

The old shelter and the fitted wall mean little to it, except as they have become part of the basin’s geography. It travels the hand-cut channels when they hold water, following them toward the catchment hollow. Once, long ago, it may have lived in the hollow itself. Now it is too shallow, or too exposed, and the animal has settled in the dark bowl beneath the wall. There it can remain for days without moving, its pale back mistaken for another piece of the basin’s long-settled stone.

It is not the basin’s guardian, though a person arriving there might think so. It does not keep the water, or mend the channels, or notice the shelter in any human sense. It simply occupies the few places that suit it, and has done so for longer than the shelter has stood. If the basin changes, it will change with it by degrees too small to mark: a little farther into the seam, a little deeper beneath the ledge.

When it rests, the water seems empty. When it moves, the basin seems to have remembered that it is alive.
