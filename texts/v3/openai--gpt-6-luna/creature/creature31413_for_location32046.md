The shelf is lived in by creatures that know how to make a home out of almost nothing.

The quickest are the little rock-dwellers. They slip from crack to crack with their bellies close to the ground, warm-brown against the pale stone, alert to every shadow passing overhead. In the brief sun they forage among the mats of leaves, clipping them into neat borders; at the first shift of wind, they vanish under a ledge. Their calls are so small they seem to belong to the stone itself—a thin, questioning squeak, answered from somewhere out of sight. They know which hollows hold warmth after dusk, which seams stay damp, and which gaps are too narrow even for a bird’s beak. They do not cross the open rock casually. Each crossing is a decision.

Above them, the dark birds have the whole height of the place. They ride the wind without seeming to spend strength, then settle on the highest teeth of the rim to look down into the green seams. They are not the shelf’s rulers so much as its witnesses. They watch the small animals come and go, the weather gather over the far ranges, the clouds rise from below. Sometimes one drops from its perch and passes close to the stone, its shadow sliding over the moss like a hand. When it lands again, it folds its wings carefully, as if the air were something it had carried a long way.

After rain, tiny winged things wake in the crevices. For a little while the sunny hollows glitter with them: a brief, wavering life above the flowers, delicate enough to be stopped by the cold. The plants have their own stubborn inhabitants, too. Roots press into hairline cracks; moss takes possession of the wet places; pale flowers open flat against the ground, close enough to feel the warmth of the rock. They cannot travel, but they are not entirely still. Each season they edge farther into a seam, retreat from a dry face, or take hold where a trickle has newly found its way.

There are signs of people, though no one is present now. A low wall cups the spring. Flat slabs make a shelter from the wind. Cairns mark the path where the shelf narrows and the fall begins. Whoever built them understood that shelter here must be borrowed, not imposed. Moss has begun to fill the joints. One cairn has come apart, and its stones have made a small trail downhill, as if even the marker has started to go somewhere.

Nothing on the shelf is entirely settled. The little animals keep changing their routes. The birds move their perches when a ledge breaks away. Meltwater slips into a crack and emerges somewhere unexpected; a plant takes hold there, and the next year a winged creature finds it. The place is young enough that every life seems to be learning it—and old enough, already, to have taught them caution.

They live in intervals: between sun and shadow, rain and frost, one green patch and the next. The shelf is not empty. It is simply a place where life keeps its voice low, and where every inhabitant has learned to listen.
