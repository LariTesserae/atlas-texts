At first, there seems to be no one.

Then the water ticks into a channel, and somewhere behind the wall another drop answers. A thin trickle alters its pitch as it passes through a narrow seam. The basin is so quiet that these small sounds seem less like accidents than signals—though no message can be made of them, and nothing answers in words.

The water is the closest thing the highland has to a resident. It lives not in one cistern or hidden spring, but throughout the construction: in the dark joints, beneath the steps, inside the packed earth. It enters at the ridges and travels inward by routes so finely divided that no single course contains it for long. A drop disappears. A little farther down, another appears. The water seems to remember the shape of the place, or perhaps the place remembers for it.

It has no face, no voice beyond its faint tapping and hollow notes. It cannot cross a wall except by finding the seam already there. Yet over time it makes choices of a kind. It favors a channel whose lip has worn low. It gathers behind a blocked drain. It finds a new hairline and follows it down, darkening the pale surface one slow handspan at a time. A wall shifts, and the water takes the altered path without pause.

Perhaps the basin was built to hold water. Perhaps, by holding it so completely, it gave the water somewhere to be. There is no clear boundary between the keeper and the thing kept. The channels guide the dampness; the dampness tests the channels. Each depends on the other, though neither seems to know it.

Nothing else has settled there. No creature drinks from the cisterns. No roots probe the mortar. But in every deep hollow, unseen water continues its passage, joining and dividing in the dark. The whole construction is full of that patient movement. If the basin is inhabited, it is inhabited by a presence too dispersed to gather into a body: a slow intelligence of pressure, seepage, and return.

It does not wake. It does not sleep. It keeps the place damp, and the place keeps it in motion.
