The first to settle there is the green.

It does not arrive as a forest, or even as a proper meadow. It spreads close to the ground: a dark, joined skin of moss and creeping stems, threaded with sedges that lift narrow blades wherever a shaft gives them enough light. The plants take what the roof brought down with it—old soil, fragments of root, pockets of trapped leaf mould—and make a thin living world on the spoil. They grow around stones, over wheel ruts, between sleepers. In places they cover the floor so completely that the ground seems to have been upholstered in shadow.

Their roots are small and patient. Some hold fast in grit. Others find cracks in the newly exposed rock and follow them upward, toward the cold soil above. There they meet the dangling roots that have come down through the roof breaks: pale, bare things, still reaching for a surface they cannot touch. Where the two kinds meet, the roots braid together. They make no sound. They do not need to. The galleries carry enough noise for everything.

Under the green is a finer life, almost invisible. Damp seams support films of fungus and pale threads that bind grit to grit. They extend from one leak to the next, stopping where the stone dries, then beginning again at another dark patch. Around pools, they make soft rims that hold the water’s edge. In the warmth of a pipe or pump housing, they gather more thickly; when the machinery cools, they slow, leaving a faint whitish dust on the metal. Nothing here spreads quickly. Even decay proceeds by inches.

The insects belong to the wettest places. They are minute, soft-bodied, and seldom seen except when a lamp catches them moving over a wall. They cluster in the fine moss beside a drip, where the stone stays cool and the water comes regularly. A few live among the roots, feeding on whatever the plants and fungi leave behind. They keep to the shadows and are gone when the ground begins to tremble with a start-up motor.

Moths come down the shafts by accident. They spend their short lives circling the work lights, then rest on the warm casings with their wings folded like scraps of ash. Sometimes one is found in a pool, its reflection still hovering above it. The galleries are not a place for much flying. There is too little light, too much dust, and nowhere to go when the fans pull the air toward the workings.

The plants are the only inhabitants that seem to occupy the whole place. They make no distinction between a natural ledge and a concrete footing, between a fallen beam and an old rail. Their shoots work along the metal where a little grime and moisture have collected. A vine enters a split in a cable housing and emerges from the other side; moss softens the base of a compressor no one has used in years. They do not reclaim the works in any grand rush. They simply continue, placing a leaf here, a root there, while the machines keep their routes clear.

When the lamps are on, the green appears almost black. Under a roof break, it takes on a deeper, fuller color, as if the weak daylight has reached something buried inside it. The leaves turn toward the shafts, though the shafts are narrow and far away. They are not reaching for warmth so much as for the fact of light: a pale permission to grow a little taller. Below the lamps, where the yellow falls flat against the stone, the sedges remain short and tough.

The machinery has become part of the climate. The fans draw the same cold air across the leaves day after day. Pumps give the roots a pattern of water: a long dry interval, then the steady ticking of a leak. When the crusher starts, dust settles over everything, pale as a second frost. When it stops, drops can be heard striking a pool, and the stems nearest the water shift faintly in the returning stillness.

There may be workers passing through, but they are not the place’s settled life. They come along the rails, check the gauges, clear a blocked screen, and leave again. The green does not follow them. It keeps to the abandoned benches and the edges of the routes, to all the small margins the machinery does not quite need. If a boot crushes a patch, the stems lie flat. The next wet spell darkens them, and from the rooted edges new growth begins.

Nothing here has mastered the other. The fans have not made the plants disappear; the plants have not stopped the fans. Water is lifted out of the workings while moss gathers at the places it escapes. Cut vegetation travels away on the conveyor, and fresh vegetation continues to form beneath it. Each life is local, modest, and unfinished.

So the galleries are inhabited, though not crowded. A low green body lies across the floors, with its roots reaching into the cut stone and its smallest tenants tucked close to the damp. Above it, machinery turns and thuds through the rock. The plants endure the noise without answering it. They take the light where it falls, the water where it leaks, and the few inches of soil the buried world has left them.
