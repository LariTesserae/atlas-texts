The people who live there are not all alike, though the city has pressed a common shape into them. They move with a slight readiness, as if every floor might tilt or every dry passage might become a channel. They know where to put their weight on old walkways, how long a gate takes to close, and which wall will give a warning shiver before it gives way. A stranger sees people constantly looking down. A resident knows they are listening.

Most speak with their hands. Their signs are quick and economical, visible in the narrowest street: two fingers drawn across a palm for *wait for the lock*, a knuckle tapped twice for *water rising*, a hand held open against the chest for *I have you*. In the lower districts, where mist blurs gestures, people use small lamps clipped to collars and sleeves. A white blink means *coming through*. A slow amber pulse means *the route is uncertain*. Children learn these signals before they learn to read, and can carry on whole arguments without making a sound.

There are spoken languages, too, though voices are used sparingly. People speak close to one another, faces nearly touching, or send a sentence through a speaking tube whose far end emerges in another room, another block, sometimes another generation’s building. The oldest residents can identify a pump fault by the way it changes the pitch of a room. They can tell, from the returning vibration in a railing, whether the machinery below is merely strained or in danger of stopping. They call this listening. No one calls it superstition.

Clothing is made to get wet, dry slowly, and get wet again. Layers of close-woven fabric cling to the body beneath jackets of treated hide or rubberized cloth. Sleeves fasten tight at the wrist; pockets have drainage holes; important documents live in sealed tubes worn against the ribs. Shoes are broad-soled, with grips that can be turned inward or out depending on the surface. People who work the lower channels carry a second pair tied at the hip. Their hands are often ridged and toughened from ropes, ladders, and wet stone, though even those who never enter the water keep a hook or line within reach.

The city divides them less by street than by depth and access. Some families live high, above the ordinary reach of flooding, close to the rooflines and shafts of sunlight. They repair cranes, inspect vents, and maintain the slatted screens that keep debris from the upper channels. Others live at the level where water is a daily negotiation. They know the gatekeepers, the pressure crews, the market stewards who decide when a plaza must be cleared. Below them are the people who work in the machinery’s shadow: filter-cleaners, salvage divers, fault-listeners, and those who go into flooded buildings when the route maps are already obsolete.

No one can afford to think of these occupations as distant from one another. A blocked filter in an upper district may bring animals into the lower passages before nightfall. A careless diversion of water can flood a market three neighborhoods away. Each block appoints people to watch its local gates, but the watches are linked by messengers who travel along the roofs and through the service corridors. Their reports are brief, practical, and rarely complete. *South stair sealed. Pressure falling. Something large beneath the old bridge.* The city has little use for certainty that arrives too late.

Children grow up in groups that are not quite schools and not quite households. They learn to swim early, not for sport but because a familiar street can become unfamiliar depth between one hour and the next. They learn to climb without disturbing loose masonry, to read the concentric marks in the plazas, and to recognize the harmless animals from the ones that can split a glove or take a finger. In play they imitate the pumps: crouching in a line, rising and falling together, making the whole group breathe in a slow mechanical rhythm. The game is older than anyone remembers. Adults sometimes watch them and tap the beat against a railing.

The water animals are part of ordinary life, but never entirely ordinary. Blind fish gather beneath warm grates, and people leave narrow openings for them when repairing a channel. Armored scavengers are driven out of kitchens with long poles and no particular panic. In the lower streets, families keep their doors fitted with screens that can stop small animals and let the harmless water through. There are rules about feeding the broad creatures that rise from the cisterns. Most residents obey them. The ones who do not are remembered—not as daring, but as foolish.

Some districts mark the passing of a person by returning something to the water. A work-glove, a nameplate, a strip of cloth is set in a current chosen by the family, then watched until it vanishes beneath a grate or catches somewhere out of sight. Elsewhere, names are cut into stone above the high-water line, where they will remain until the building shifts or the water reaches them. People do not expect permanence from a wall. They make the mark anyway.

The city’s inhabitants are practical about change, though not indifferent to it. When a block begins to move, they pack what can be carried, seal what cannot, and mark the old doorways so no one mistakes a buried entrance for an open one. Neighbors exchange labor: one household braces a walkway while another moves the sleeping platforms; a group of children carries messages between crews; someone brings hot food in a covered pot that will not spill if the floor jolts. Afterwards, they learn the new route. They complain about it, sometimes for years. Then the complaint becomes a saying, and the saying becomes part of the route.

What binds them is not the hope that the city will become still. They have never known stillness. It is a more local trust: that someone is watching the gate; that the lights will answer; that, if the current takes a familiar shape in the wrong direction, a neighbor will notice. In the permanent thunder, a hand on the shoulder can be heard. A lamp seen through mist can be enough. A familiar knock against a pipe can travel farther than a shout.

And when the buried stone trembles and the water runs backward, the inhabitants do not wait for the city to explain itself. They look to one another, read the signals passing from hand to hand, and begin to move.
