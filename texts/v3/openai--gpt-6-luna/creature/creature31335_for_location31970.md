The first thing that lives there is the wetness.

It keeps no shape for long. It beads under the concrete, threads down a root, collects in the cups of the floor, and disappears into cracks too narrow for anything else to enter. The others depend on it, though none of them own it. Even the moss grows as if listening for its return: small dense cushions around the pools, green paling toward their edges, ready to dry without quite surrendering.

The mites travel in shining lines. They cross the bare clay one after another, so small that a change in the light can make them seem like a trick of the surface. They follow channels left by earlier water, and sometimes the line parts around a grain of sand, divides, and joins again farther on. The flattened beetles keep to the undersides of broken concrete flakes. Their backs are copper-dark, nearly the color of the old bolts. When the machinery hums, they press themselves flat and wait for the vibration to pass through them.

In the water cups, the pale shrimp-things make sudden, bright strokes. They are almost invisible until they move. Then they flick through a reflection of sky that cannot belong to the space above them. They feed on what settles, on what the trickle brings, on whatever the moss gives up. Sometimes one rises toward the surface and hangs there, trembling, as if it has mistaken the reflected brightness for a way out.

The lizard lives in the warmest crack. It has a narrow head and a body the color of dried mud, except where its skin catches the damp and turns almost black. It does not chase the little animals often. It waits. The others have learned the stillness of it so completely that they pass its resting place without alarm, stepping over its tail, climbing across its back when the crack is crowded. Only when the winged insects rise does the lizard become wholly awake. Its tongue flashes; a wing vanishes. Then it settles again into the heat, no more than another dark seam in the wall.

At certain hours—if hours can be counted here—the water cups hatch. The winged insects come up in a brief, churning cloud, filling the hollow from floor to ceiling. They brush the concrete and the roots, catch on the rusted pipe, settle in the moss, lift again. Their wings make a sound finer than rain. For a few minutes the whole pocket seems to be moving at once. Then the cloud thins. The survivors fold themselves against whatever surface will hold them, and the lizard closes its eyes.

The roots are slower inhabitants. They enter through the wall’s smallest failures, pale at first, then darkening with the mineral stain. They do not appear to seek the pools, yet they bend toward them. One has grown through the eye of the brass wheel, and another follows a crack toward the warm plate. They take what the concrete releases and hold the mud where it wants to crumble. Their tips are tender; their paths are not.

No one in the seam understands the old machine, but all of them feel it. The low hum gathers in the floor, passes through the water, and reaches the bodies tucked beneath flakes of metal. Once every few hours the hidden motor turns. The roots tremble. A bead on the ceiling swells, hangs, and sometimes falls. At other times it disappears before touching anything. The beetles wait out the vibration; the shrimp flick away from it; the lizard opens one eye.

The pools remember a larger place. Their surfaces show pale sky, grasses, a moving light, though the chamber has no opening wide enough for any of these. When the motor runs, the reflections break into strips. The inhabitants move through those broken skies without looking up. They have learned the ceiling by touch, the walls by scent, the floor by every change in its tilt.

Rain brings strangers and rearranges the old residents. It pours through the seam in a brief, cold rush, carrying grains of mud and fragments of leaves. A channel may open where yesterday there was only a damp mark; a beetle’s shelter may be swept away; the shallow pools may join, then separate. The shrimp ride the new current. Mites cling to the clay. The moss darkens. By the time the trickle dwindles, the hollow has become slightly different, and every creature begins again to learn its routes.

Nothing in the seam has enough room to be separate from everything else. The lizard rests where water gathers. The beetles hide under the concrete that sheds its grains into the pools. The roots hold open the cracks through which the mites travel. Even the old machinery contributes its slow warmth and occasional shaking. Their lives overlap without becoming one life.

The pocket is still forming around them. Mud lifts; concrete parts; water searches the gap. The inhabitants answer each change at their own scale: a mite turns aside, a root lengthens, a beetle settles beneath a new fragment, a lizard shifts its weight in the warm crack. For now, the seam is narrow enough to shelter them all. For now, there is always another dark place to enter, another drop gathering overhead, another faint pulse moving through the wall.
