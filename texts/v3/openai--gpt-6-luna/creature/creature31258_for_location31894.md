They are people, though the place has rubbed some of its habits into them.

They move through the galleries with the economy of those who know exactly how much room a body takes up. A hand finds the rail before a foot finds the stair. Shoulders turn sideways between a pipe and a wall without interrupting conversation. They wear patched work clothes in layers, their cuffs dark with ash and their collars damp from the close air. Gloves are prized, but most hands are bare: scarred, quick, and blackened at the creases. In the warmer galleries, people tie their hair up and go about in rolled sleeves; near the cisterns, they pull on old coats against the sudden chill. Their faces are difficult to read at a distance in the uncertain light, but close by there is nothing spectral about them. They grin, argue, yawn, and worry like people anywhere.

They do not think of the city as finished. A room is a claim made provisionally against the stone; a corridor is a suggestion. When a fracture opens, the first question is not who owns the wall, but what must be moved before it changes its mind. Families keep their most precious belongings in portable chests. Workshop benches are bolted down with joints that can be loosened. Even the old have learned not to grow too attached to a particular view of the galleries. Their houses are crowded and personal all the same: a copper kettle on a shelf, a strip of colored cloth over a sleeping alcove, someone’s carefully mended coat hung to dry above a warm pipe.

Water gives the settlement its calendar. People know which gutters are likely to run after a hot shift and which cracks have been quiet for too long. Children are taught to place a cup beneath a drip without being asked, and to leave the first water from a new seep for testing. The collecting tanks are tended by a small, patient guild whose members can tell by taste when the water has picked up too much iron or when a pipe has begun to leak somewhere out of sight. No one wastes water in public. In private, people sometimes linger over a wash, palms cupped beneath a thin stream, as if they were listening to it.

They have made a craft of listening. Warning chimes are checked at the start of each shift; the sound of a belt slipping or a furnace drawing badly is recognized before the machine fails. The oldest residents can distinguish a harmless settling groan from the low, slow complaint that means a corridor has begun to shift. Their knowledge is not mystical, though they speak of the stone as if it were temperamental. They leave offerings of silence: when a new crack opens, workers pause, lamps held low, and wait to hear whether it continues. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the rock says nothing, and they go back to work with greater care.

Work organizes the hours, but not the whole of life. People meet at the warm tanks to scrub up, trade news, and sit with no task in their hands. They play games with washers and scraps of wire on the flat ledges above the channels. Music is made quietly: a thumb along a bottle’s rim, a tap on a pipe, a low song that carries farther through the stone than anyone expects. The settlement has few private corners, so affection is often practical. Someone has saved you a dry place to sleep. Someone has repaired your lamp. Someone has put aside the cleanest cup.

The young learn the city by its sounds before they learn its map. They can tell which gallery they are in by the pitch of the machinery and know not to cross a certain walkway when the winch is under strain. They are given small tasks early: clearing scale from a runoff channel, carrying lamp wicks, marking new grit-falls with chalk. When a chime rings by itself, adults do not hush them out of curiosity. They teach them to stop, listen, and then move together.

They are not unafraid. They know the ceiling can shed stone, that a wall can press inward while everyone sleeps, that the warmest room may become the wrong room without warning. But fear here has become a kind of attention. They build for one another, leave escape routes uncluttered, and write names on the backs of tools so that lost things can find their way home. In the close, damp dark, they have made a society out of repair: of watching the seams, catching what water comes, and keeping one another warm while the earth goes on changing around them.
