At first, there seems to be no one here—only the small lives at the margins, each keeping its own narrow peace. The frogs wait beneath the falling drops. The beetles tuck themselves under stones. The translucent grazers move across the water-film so slowly they might be mistaken for the water changing its mind.

But they do not live separately. Their pauses arrive together when the turbine knocks. The midges settle; the frogs flatten against the stone; even the fine roots seem to hold still. Then, when the wheel finds its rhythm again, the hollow resumes.

The oldest inhabitant is concealed in the cleft beside the turbine. It may be a pale salamander, though no one has seen it whole. A tapering flank shows now and then beneath the water, smooth as a wet root; once, a small blind head lifts among the moss, sensing the pool with delicate folds at its jaw. It feeds on what the hollow offers: larvae, fallen midges, the smallest of the translucent grazers. It does not flee from the machine. It has grown used to the wheel’s tremor, and rests in the shelter of its casing as if the old metal were part of the stone.

Perhaps it is only one animal. Perhaps the thing mistaken for its body is a gathering of root, moss, and living film, all stirred by the same pulse. In this dimness, a boundary is difficult to keep.

When the seepage weakens and the turbine falters, the hidden resident comes nearer the open pool. It moves without a splash, making a wake so slight that the pale stones seem to shift instead. The frogs turn their heads toward it. The midges rise, briefly, in a loose dark cloud. Then the wheel catches again, and the hollow settles back into its patient measure.

Nothing here rules the others. The small lives feed, hide, and wait; the water enters and leaves; the machine turns when it can. The oldest resident simply knows the intervals best. Between one knock and the next, it listens to the whole hollow breathing.
