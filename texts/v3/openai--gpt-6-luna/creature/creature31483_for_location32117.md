The people who live there are difficult to see all at once.

From the upper walkways, they appear as small, deliberate movements among the gardens: a figure crossing a bridge with a crate of figs; someone leaning over a cistern to test the water; children running along a wall wide enough to be a street. Lower down, they gather in courts where the light arrives in narrow bands, or pass through the service tunnels with lamps swinging at their hips. Their lives are spread across so many levels that no one person sees the whole of them. A neighborhood may know the people three terraces above better than those on the far side of the ridge.

They are skilled at finding a way through. Every child learns the obvious routes first—the stairs, lifts, bridges, and marked passages—and then the private ones: the narrow steps behind a screen of vines, the old rail corridor that emerges beside the orchard, the door in a retaining wall that is only opened when the pumps are being serviced. Directions are given by landmarks that keep changing slowly: *up past the blue tiles, take the stairs where the fig roots have cracked the third landing, cross beneath the high bridge before the bells*. A stranger asks how far it is. A resident asks which level they mean.

Most people know something about water. They can hear a pump straining, smell the mineral dampness that comes before a leak appears, and tell by the sound in a gutter whether it is carrying rain or a blockage. The work is not always dramatic. Someone clears a filter; someone patches a channel; someone carries a bucket to a roof garden when a lift is broken. But these small tasks run through the day like a second system of streets. Water is measured, shared, argued over, and celebrated. After a good rain, people step outside to watch it travel.

Gardening belongs to almost everyone, though not everyone calls themselves a gardener. A cook tends herbs beside a kitchen vent. A group of neighbors cares for the pear trees on their shared roof. In a lower court, children grow beans in cracked ceramic pots and insist that the vines are doing well, even when they are not. There are people whose work is to know the depths of soil above a roof, the weight of a mature tree, the routes its roots might take through an old wall. They are listened to carefully. No one wants to learn the hard way what happens when a terrace is overloaded.

The levels give people different weather. At the top, the air is broad and bright; on a clear day the hills of construction can be seen stretching away in every direction. Lower down, warm air gathers in courts, and a summer storm may be heard long before its rain reaches the narrow streets. Some residents speak of the heights as if they were another country. Others rarely go above the orchard shelves where they grew up. They carry home the sounds of their own part of the place: lifts groaning, birds beating from beneath a bridge, the rush of water in a wall.

Animals make their own maps. Goats know where the best growth is coming in; rats know which service doors fail to close; swifts know every open pocket of air. People know this, and mostly let it be. A family may leave fruit on a ledge for birds, or keep a small lamp lit to draw insects toward a sheltered garden. At dusk, when bats emerge from the ventilation arches, conversations pause—not out of reverence, exactly, but because the sky has suddenly filled with something alive.

The old residents can tell how the place has changed by remembering what used to be in the way. A bridge was added. A staircase was diverted. A factory roof grew into a park, then into a place where people met after work. The youngest children think the city has always been this green, the walls always this thick, the terraces always rising beyond sight. Their elders know that none of it is finished.

And perhaps that is the closest thing the country has to a single resident: the ongoing work of keeping it habitable. People, plants, animals, pumps, roots, and walls hold one another in place. The place is not a wilderness tamed, nor a machine that has forgotten its makers. It is a lived-in height, continually tended—so vast that no one can command it, and so familiar that someone is always on their way to fix a dripping pipe.
