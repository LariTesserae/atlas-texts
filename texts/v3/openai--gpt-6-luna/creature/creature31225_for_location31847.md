At first, it seems that no one lives there.

Then the ringing begins to sound less like a property of the material and more like a way of being. The place is not empty. Its inhabitants are simply difficult to distinguish from their houses.

They are close-set presences, each held in a corner, a seam, the underside of a ledge. Not hidden behind the walls: made of the same pale substance, or perhaps made of the slight differences between one fitted piece and the next. A rounded ridge belongs to one; a narrow court belongs to another. Where the lanes braid together, several of them overlap so completely that no border can be found.

They have no faces to turn toward the brightness. They do not need to look. Light arrives through every surface, and every surface gives it on again. They have no feet, because there is nowhere to go that is not already part of them. What we might call their bodies are roofs and buttresses, stairs that lead nowhere, hollows held clean and dry for ages. Their stillness is not the stillness of sleep. It is their full, chosen form.

Each has a note. Not a sound it makes and then stops making, but a tone it keeps: thin, hard, exact. Together the notes make the continuous ringing of the whole enclosure. No one voice can be picked out. To listen closely is to hear not individuals, but the impossibly crowded fact of them—each distinct, none separate. They do not speak in turns. They are always speaking, and have nothing to add.

Perhaps they were shaped by hands that meant to make a dwelling. Perhaps the hands made only walls, and the inhabitants gathered in the joints afterward, as a meaning gathers around a word. The distinction has grown old and useless. The made things have worn smooth; the inhabitants have become inseparable from what was made. Nothing here remembers a time before the fitting, or expects a time after it.

In the deepest seams, the gray-green lichen writes a few small strokes. These may be names, or the residue of names, or simply the only dark color the inhabitants can bear to have among them. It does not spread. The pale city has no need to grow.

So they remain: a people without motion, living by being perfectly placed. They know one another as pressure knows a boundary, as a note knows the note beside it. Their world is small enough to close in a fist, yet no part of it is lonely. Every ledge rests against another. Every hollow is contained. Every voice is already answered by the shape that receives it.

They are not waiting for anything.

They are keeping the place whole.
