At first, the inhabitants seem to be the things that have made a home in the machinery.

The birds in the cross-braces know which valves are about to burst before the gauge needle moves. Moths gather on cabinets whose interiors have gone cold, as if remembering a warmth the machines no longer provide. The pale geckos slip through the condensation channels with their throats pulsing, drinking what gathers there. In the shallow tank, the eyeless creatures turn whenever the Works shudders. They are not frightened by the turbines. Nothing here is frightened by the turbines for long.

Then there are the repairs.

A new bolt appears in a housing too high to reach without the crane. A length of pipe, split by winter, is sleeved in metal and sealed with a bead of ceramic paste. Once, on a morning when the gardens have been combed by wind, every fallen leaf is found tucked back among the stems. The work is neat, but not quite human-neat: a little too much care given to a hidden seam, a little too little to a place where any visitor would look.

The keepers are seldom seen all at once. One may be glimpsed through the frosted glass of a control room: a narrow shape standing very still, one hand against the wall, listening. Another crosses a court behind a veil of steam. In the distance, a door closes softly, though the noise of the Works should have swallowed it.

They are small and spare, built—or made—by the passages they inhabit. Their coats are patched from insulating cloth and old curtain material, their cuffs dark with mineral dust. Their fingers are long, blunt at the ends, good for turning fasteners in cramped places. Some wear thick plugs in their ears; others have learned to speak with a hand on a companion’s wrist, passing words as pressure and rhythm. Their faces are difficult to remember. The noise has taught them to watch the whole body rather than the mouth.

They sleep in the warm galleries behind the settling tanks, in rooms that have been subdivided from chambers no longer needed for anything else. Small lamps burn there, shaded green. The walls are crowded with objects that look like refuse until their purpose becomes clear: lengths of wire sorted by gauge, ceramic fragments arranged by tone, seed packets pressed flat beneath glass. On shelves, alongside tools, stand jars of water from different parts of the circuit. The keepers taste it, or hold it to the light, and put it back.

They do not seem to believe that the Works belongs to them. They behave more like guests who have been asked to stay until a difficult task is finished. They know which stairs should not be used in rain, which housings can be leaned against, which pools must never be drained. They leave food for the bats but not for the birds. They let the pale geckos hunt. When the little eyeless things gather in their trembling tank, the keepers lower a hand into the water and wait until the creatures have dispersed.

Some of them are very old. Not old in the ordinary way: one has been seen for years at the same window, with the same gray scarf, though a visitor who returned after a decade said the face seemed younger. A child was once heard laughing in the abandoned cooling chamber. No one has admitted that there are children.

At night the keepers move through the Works by routes that are not marked on any plan. A light passes behind the ceramic walls. A ladder shifts in the darkness. The crane turns a few degrees over the drop. By morning, a valve has been eased open, the gardens have been mended, and the repaired leaf is shining with condensation.

Whether the keepers maintain the Upper Works, or whether the Upper Works makes keepers to maintain itself, is not a question they answer. They listen to the pipes. They listen to one another. And when the whole summit gives its deep, unceasing shudder, they place their hands against the walls until it passes.
