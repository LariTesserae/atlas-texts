They are called the seam-born, though no one knows whether they were born in the seams or merely found them first.

At a distance they appear to be one creature in many pieces: a dark, restless skin laid over the hollow. Up close, they divide into kinds. The smallest are pale and nearly flat, delicate as flakes of mica, their legs working constantly along the mineral crust. They gather the thin films that form where heat and salt meet, scraping them from stone with mouthparts finer than a needle. They are the first to feel a change in the seams. When the rock cools, they turn together; when a warm pulse passes beneath them, they shine faintly and begin to feed.

The hooked ones come after. Their shells are thick and dark, their forelimbs made for levering scales of crust from the floor. They crack open the mineral plates and expose the softer layer beneath. They also crack open whatever has stopped moving. They do this without haste, and without any apparent distinction between a stranger, a nestmate, or one of their own dead. The hollow gives up what it can; the seam-born take it back.

The largest seldom cross the open floor. They live folded against the walls, where their long legs brace them in the fluted stone and their heads remain hidden beneath overlapping plates. When the multitude shifts, these great bodies shift too, slowly enough to seem part of the wall itself. They may be old. They may be many creatures joined beneath a single shell. The hollow offers no easy way to tell.

None of the seam-born has eyes in the way surface animals do. Their shining points are sensitive patches set into shell and limb: receivers for pressure, heat, and the minute tremors of the rock. A footstep above the basin is a distant weather. A hand laid on basalt is a sudden, intimate arrival. They know the weight of one another, the passage of warmth, the dry click that means a seam has opened—or that something has died.

That click is answered by stillness. The smallest cease feeding. The hooked ones hold their lifted claws in place. Even the black veils on the walls stop descending. For a little while the whole hollow listens. Then a shudder travels through the floor, and the bodies begin again, rearranging themselves around the new information. They have no leader. Their order is older and more immediate than command: one creature feels, the next receives the feeling, and soon the entire basin has turned.

The human-shaped chamber is part of this order now. Its narrow doorway remains open because bodies pass through it in both directions, though no one could say why they choose that way rather than another. The nest inside is warmer than the surrounding rock. At its center, generations of seam-born have worn a shallow depression into the floor. The oldest shells are fitted into the chamber’s cracks, not as decoration, nor perhaps as anything intentional. They make the walls more insulating. They hold the heat. A human looking in might mistake the arrangement for a shrine.

If the seam-born remember the makers of the pillars, they remember them as pressure and warmth, as heavy creatures who came and went. Perhaps their ancestors fed on the residue those creatures left behind. Perhaps they were already here, small as grit, beneath the crust. The old constructions have lasted long enough to enter the hollow’s habits. Nothing living has to understand a thing in order to keep it.

They do not flee when a stranger enters. They make room, but not very much. The first bodies to touch a boot withdraw; those behind them fill the space at once. The floor seems solid until it shifts beneath the weight, until a pale sheet of many-legged life parts around the sole and closes again over the print. If the stranger stands still, the seam-born gather near. If the stranger moves, the pressure runs through them ahead of each step.

The hollow does not belong to them in the way a burrow belongs to a fox. They are less its inhabitants than its moving surface: the part that senses, feeds, and changes shape. The mountain warms; they rise. The mountain cools; they climb. Their dead become food, shelter, and ground. Their living bodies cover the stone so completely that, in places, the basalt is no longer visible.

And still the hollow is not full. There are cracks no light reaches, seams too narrow for even the smallest bodies, and depths in the mountain whose heat has never yet risen to the basin. The seam-born wait above them, pressed close beneath the mineral scales. They wait without expectation, or with an expectation so old it no longer resembles thought.

When the mountain stirs, they will know. They will feel it first in their limbs, then in the floor, then in the dark beneath the floor. And the hollow, which seemed to be holding its breath, will begin to move.
