The hollow is lived in, though it gives little sign of being occupied.

A small tortoise has made its slow circuit among the shelves and ruined stones. Most days it stays hidden beneath a low overhang, where the rock holds a little coolness and the entrance is narrow enough to keep off the wind. Its shell is the color of dust-darkened limestone, scored and muted; when it tucks itself against the wall, it looks less like an animal than another lump the weather has rounded.

It comes out when the light is mild, or after rain, moving from one thin patch of grass to the next. There is no hurry in its route. It knows the damp seams, the few leaves that survive close to the stone, and the shallow pools where water lingers longest. Sometimes it drinks. Sometimes it simply rests with its head extended, feeling the day through its skin. Then it withdraws again, and the basin appears empty.

Smaller lives persist in the cracks: a few hardy roots, pale threads of moss, perhaps tiny creatures that keep to the wettest darkness. They do not make the hollow lively. They make it habitable. The tortoise does not seem lonely, or aware of being the last of anything. Its world is the sheltering ledge, the warmed stone, the short distance between food and refuge. It has no need to cross the rim.

The old enclosure offers it a second shelter. Its stones have slumped into the natural shelves, leaving a gap low enough for the tortoise to pass through. Rain gathers there and drains away; grit shifts under its feet. The animal uses the place without recognizing it as something made by hands.

When it rests, nothing moves. When it walks, the sound is scarcely more than a faint scrape over grit. The hollow remains much as it was: a basin of pale stone, holding a little water and a little life, with enough shelter for one slow creature to return, day after day, to the same quiet ground.
