Nothing here can survive by being quiet, so the inhabitants have learned to listen.

The great grazers travel in herds so loose they seem, from a ridge, like drifts of dust moving against the wind. Each is low and broad, carried on six jointed legs that spread its weight over the brittle crust. Their hides are the color of old bone, thickened with mineral grit and scored by years of blowing stone. Along their backs grow short, stiff filaments, dark at the roots and silver at the tips. In a storm these stream behind them like grass beneath water.

They feed slowly, combing the high edges of the stem-fields with their blunt, split mouths. The filaments they crop are tough enough to rasp, and the animals’ jaws move in a steady grinding rhythm. Salt gathers in the folds of their hides. Tiny winged creatures follow the herds to pick it free, flashing bright whenever they turn.

A grazer does not watch the horizon so much as feel it. Fine sensory frills fan from the sides of its head, catching changes in pressure and the minute tremors that travel through the stone. A shift in the wind’s pitch can turn an entire herd before any animal has seen what approaches. Their calls are low, spaced pulses—one from the front, answered from somewhere behind. The sound travels through the stems and comes back altered. A familiar ridge has a familiar voice. A gap in the growth sounds different from a passage through it. The herd carries a map in its bodies, renewed with every step.

Young animals stay close to the adults, not for shelter alone but to learn this map. They walk inside the moving ring of the herd, where the ground’s vibrations are crowded and easier to read. When a calf strays, its thin, uncertain call is lost among the plant-noise. An adult answers with a slower pulse, and the calf follows the beat back.

The great herds do not flee all danger. There is too much open ground for that, and too many false alarms in a country where everything creaks, shifts, and whistles. Instead they become still in stages. The nearest animals stop first; those farther away feel the change pass through the ground and follow. Hundreds of bodies settle, legs braced, heads low. The wind continues through the stalks above them. Their backs, pale and ridged with dust, lie nearly indistinguishable from the stone.

That stillness is watched by the runners.

They are small enough to disappear beneath the crust-mats, pale and many-jointed, with narrow bodies that flex close to the ground. They hunt in brief, coordinated bursts, sensing a herd’s position through its footfalls and choosing the stragglers: an injured animal, an old one, or a calf that has wandered outside the shared rhythm. When they run across bare stone, their feet touch down so lightly that they leave almost no trace. When they vanish beneath the vegetation, only a ripple in the mat gives them away.

The runners do not always kill. Much of their feeding comes from whatever the plateau has already broken: shed hide, fallen seed-capsules, the remains of small creatures caught in a storm. Their burrows are lined with spore-felt, which holds warmth in the cold night and clogs with mineral dust until the runners scrape it clean. The scraped dust is left in small, pale fans at the mouths of their tunnels. Even their waste becomes part of the plateau’s slow exchange, a fresh concentration of salts where the crust can root.

Above the ground, the bright-winged animals roost on the lee sides of the towers. By day they cling flat against the stems, folding their reflective wings until they look like flakes of metal caught in a seam. At dusk they rise in sheets. They feed on spores and on the minute growths that bloom inside the hollow stalks, carrying grains between towers as they go. Their flight is not graceful. The wind catches them and turns them, and they correct themselves with rapid flicks, shining and vanishing, shining and vanishing. When a whole cloud crosses the sun, the plateau dims as if something immense has passed overhead.

But the largest presence is neither herd nor hunter. It is the living crust itself.

The mats spread too slowly to be seen moving, yet the animals know where their edges are changing. A broad plate may lift in a storm and travel across the stone, its thousands of fine anchors tearing free with a dry crackling that can be felt through the ground. The grazers turn away from such a sound long before the plate rolls into view. Later, they return to the exposed ground. They nose the fresh mineral, crop the scattered shoots, and leave behind the seeds and salts that help the crust take hold again.

Nothing in the plateau is separate for long. Wind carries the spores; animals carry them farther. The grazers open paths through the dense growth, and the mats close them again over seasons. Their footfalls compact loose dust into shallow terraces. The runners disturb the crust and expose mineral beneath it. Each life alters the conditions of the next, though none could be said to plan the work.

At night, the herds gather in the lee of the tallest stalks. They do not sleep all at once. Some remain standing, their heads angled into the wind, while others fold down beneath them. The hollow towers cool and contract overhead. A stem knocks once, then again; a distant ridge answers with a low, resonant groan. The animals shift their weight, listening.

If the call is familiar, they settle.

If it changes, the whole herd rises without haste, pale bodies gathering into a single slow movement. They turn toward the sound, or away from it, and the plateau receives their decision as a tremor passing through stone. Then the wind takes up its work again: through the filaments, over the mineral, and across the backs of everything that lives there.
