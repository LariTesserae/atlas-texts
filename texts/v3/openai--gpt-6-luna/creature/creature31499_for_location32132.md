The people of the Upper Pocket are difficult to see all at once. Each landing keeps its own little portion of them: a pair of hands passing a wrench through a hatch; a face behind a grated lamp; someone crossing a bridge sideways, one palm on the wall. From a distance they seem like parts of the machinery, moving only when something needs to be tightened, carried, or caught.

They are not a quiet people by nature. They have simply learned that sound is a kind of distance here, and distance is hard to judge. A call can vanish in the shaft; a tiny click can arrive from far above as if made beside your ear. So they speak close together, often with one hand resting on the same rail, and use taps for what must travel. Two taps mean *wait*. Three, spaced carefully, mean *safe to cross*. A quick, uneven run means *something is coming down*. Children learn these sounds before they learn the names of all the landings.

They wear close-fitting clothes patched so many times that no original fabric shows. Loose ends are tied down; sleeves are buttoned at the wrist; hair is braided or cut short. Everyone carries a hook, though not everyone admits to carrying it for fear of falling. Their boots have soft soles and ridged edges, made for ladders and narrow steel. The oldest people keep a strip of pale paint along one shoulder, renewed when it rubs away: a way of seeing one another in the half-light, and of knowing whose shape is coming around a corner.

They are not especially tall, and they do not hurry. In the Upper Pocket, a hurried person can pass three landings in a minute and spend the next hour trying to get back to the right one. People pause to listen. They put a foot down, wait for the bridge to settle, then put down the other. They know which stairs shift when a pump starts and which rail rings before a counterweight moves. Their knowledge is less like a map than a collection of cautions, handed down in ordinary voices: *Don’t lean there. Take the inside rung. If the lamp flickers twice, give the lift a moment.*

The settlements are crowded, but they do not live close in the way people elsewhere mean it. A household may be three rooms stacked at different heights, connected by a ladder that belongs to no one and is therefore watched by everyone. Doors are often left unlatched. A person can hear a neighbor’s kettle—or the little knock of their spoon against its rim—through stone and pipe, and knows by the rhythm whether they are home. Privacy is made with curtains, lowered lamps, and the understanding that nobody asks why a light has gone out.

Children are taught to find their way by counting landings, listening for familiar machines, and noticing the temperature of the wall beneath their fingers. They play with small bolts and scraps of bright metal, but never throw anything from a height. When a toy slips away, the game stops. Everyone listens until it strikes bottom, or until its noise is lost among other noises and no one can tell whether it has stopped or gone on. Only then does someone begin another game.

The people keep the climbers and fliers close without claiming to tame them. At furnace mouths, they leave warm grit and shallow dishes of powdered metal, replenished in the same unspoken way that someone oils a hinge or sweeps a landing. The pale crawlers gather around these offerings in moving knots, their reflective eyes winking between the stones. The fliers settle in exhaust throats, folded flat as scraps of bark. A person might wake with a shed skin on their pillow or find a nest woven into the cuff of a coat. The usual response is to shake the garment out, move the nest if it can be moved, and leave the next night’s sleeve somewhere less inviting.

No one agrees on whether the creatures are good luck, pests, or simply residents with better access to the walls. Some people claim that the fliers emerge before a pressure change. Others say they only seem to, because the people have learned to notice them when the lamps begin to tremble. The crawlers, at least, are useful: they find warm cracks, failed seals, and machine-fuzz caught in places a hand cannot reach. They are also capable of entering a sleeping person’s mouth. Both facts are accepted without being reconciled.

The work of the Pocket is divided by height, though not neatly. Those who live near the upper narrowing tend the lamps, tracks, and air routes. Those near the old industrial throat keep the pumps and the heavier mechanisms running. In between, people carry tools, messages, crates, and small pieces of machinery from one level to another. A household may have a craft, but nobody has only a craft. Everyone learns enough to secure a walkway, patch a duct, or hold a ladder while someone else climbs.

They have a particular respect for things that keep working past the point where they should have failed. A pump that knocks patiently for years may be given a name. A lift that stops at the wrong landing is cursed, repaired, and—if it does it again—given the same name as an unreliable uncle. When a machine finally breaks, its parts are taken down one by one. Nothing is abandoned until it has stopped making noise.

The dead, too, are listened for. When someone falls, the whole district becomes attentive. People lean over rails; lamps are lifted; machinery is stopped where it can be. They wait for the last impact, or for the silence that means the sound has passed beyond them. Afterwards, their grief is practical and communal: a missing tool is returned, a ladder is made safer, a room is kept warm for a while. The person’s name may be tapped along a handrail in a pattern their friends can recognize.

The inhabitants do not think of themselves as trapped in the mountain. They think of the mountain as something unfinished, still lifting and breaking around them—and of their own work as a way of holding a place in it. They keep lamps burning, bridges fastened, and small gifts waiting at the furnace mouths. They climb the same ladders each day, though those ladders are never quite the same.

At night, when the fliers pour from the exhaust stacks and the lamps divide the shaft into narrow bars of light, the people can be glimpsed at their doors: listening, counting, watching a distant glow move below. Then they draw back inside. Their machines continue turning. Their neighbors are somewhere nearby. And somewhere far beneath them, something is still falling.
