At first, you think nothing lives in the district but the small animals. Then you notice that the animals keep making room.

A pale thing slips from a pipe and stops beside a wall. Its narrow head turns—not toward you, but toward the empty space behind you. The birds fold their wings tighter. A curtain of stems draws back from a doorway that was not there a moment before.

Someone comes through.

They are tall and slight, though their height is hard to judge. Their bodies have the spare, vertical look of the plants: pale limbs, long fingers, joints that seem to bend only after the rest of them has moved. Up close, they are not made of stems. Mostly. Their skin has the matte, damp look of new stone, and beneath it something faintly green shifts like light beneath water. They wear no clothes that you can identify, only strips of old material grown or fastened around the shoulders and waist. A length of wire hangs at one hip. A seed is tucked behind one ear.

There may be several of them in the district. There may be only one, seen at different distances.

They do not hurry. They walk the streets as if remembering them into place. At a crossing, one pauses and puts a hand against a blank wall. Somewhere inside the block, a motor starts. At another, they kneel beside a puddle and let the reflection settle. The face in the water is not always theirs. Sometimes it is older, or younger, or turned away. They wait until it looks back.

The residents tend the machines, though not in the way a person tends a machine. They sit beside the rusted press while its jaws open and close, and place nothing between them. They touch the transparent tubes; the clear fluid inside changes direction. They walk around the seed-pressing apparatus and leave behind a few seeds, though no one sees them carry any. In the morning, shoots appear in the sheets of soil.

They rarely speak. When they do, the sound is small and dry, like a click from cooling metal. Their answers are not always meant for the questioner. A resident may turn its head toward an empty stairwell, and a minute later the stairwell will acquire one more step.

The animals know them. The wall-climbers pass over their shoulders without fear. Birds settle inside the hollows of their ribs, or seem to, until the resident shifts and the bird is revealed to have been perched on a pipe behind them. The soft-bodied grazers follow at a distance, feeding on the green film that gathers where the residents have rested their hands. None of the animals gathers close for long. Each has its own route, exact and solitary, and the residents do not interfere.

There is one presence the others respond to differently. At long intervals, a large animal crosses the far streets. You never see more than a portion of it: a pale flank between buildings, a slow bend of something like a neck, a foot settling without sound. Plants incline toward its passing. Motors wake in sequence. Puddles tremble, their ripples all moving the same way. The residents stop wherever they are and face the track it leaves behind.

They do not follow.

Afterward, the district feels briefly more complete. A doorway holds its shape. The bridge no longer seems to end in the wrong place. Even the shadows fall where they should. The residents resume their small tasks until the certainty drains away.

There are signs of ordinary life, though nothing stays ordinary for long. A shallow dish sits on a ledge, filled with rainwater. Three pale seeds lie beside it. In a low housing block, a light appears behind one window, then another, as if someone is moving from room to room. You may hear a chair scrape. You may hear someone laughing once, very softly, from beneath the floor. The next time you pass, the building has no upper story.

The residents seem neither abandoned nor entirely arrived. Some are more finished than others. One has a shadow that keeps pace; another casts none until it passes beneath the arch. One has a face, though the features blur whenever you try to remember them. A smaller figure follows a few steps behind, leaving prints in the dark ground that fill with water before you can count them.

They do not appear to be waiting for a visitor. They do not appear to be waiting for one another.

Still, the district makes room for them. A corridor lengthens at their approach. A wall sprouts a handhold. A roof opens to let rain through. Their homes, if they have homes, are not places they return to so much as arrangements that gather around them for a while: a warm patch of floor, a pipe that hums, a curtain of leaves drawn aside.

Perhaps they are the district’s inhabitants. Perhaps they are what the district has managed to make of inhabitants so far.

At dusk—though dusk is difficult to distinguish from the rest of its darkness—they stand among the plant-columns, spaced widely apart. From a distance, they look like more of the same: thin pale uprights, almost lost among stems and old beams. Then one turns its head. Another raises a hand. Between them, the air remains open.

Not empty. Kept.
