Nothing there moves quickly enough to be mistaken for an animal.

The first inhabitants a traveler notices are the plants: moss packed into the joints of walls, pale grasses lying over the stone, dark trees holding their sparse crowns above the sheltered basins. But these are not quite separate from the land that supports them. Their roots follow cracks older than their trunks. Their leaves turn toward water hidden beneath the rock. Where a channel runs dry, the plants nearby grow still; when moisture returns, they lean, almost imperceptibly, toward its passage.

The stone has its own kind of life. It takes in rain, holds the cold, and opens by the width of a thought. Frost works inside it. Water passes through it. Given enough time, a slab shifts to meet another slab, and a wall changes its weight without falling. The hills do the same on a larger scale. They settle, loosen, and shed their gravelly skins. The report of it travels underfoot, a message too low and slow for most travelers to hear.

Together, these things make a country that is less inhabited than awake.

Its awareness has no center. It gathers where water beads in a hollow, where roots find a seam, where a stair descends into darkness and ends in a chamber that stays dry. The roads belong to it too. They are not merely routes across the uplands but long, shallow lines of attention. Sometimes they lead where they appear to lead. Sometimes they turn a traveler gently toward a town the traveler had not seen, or return them to a junction they remember passing days ago. The land does not seem to intend either outcome. It may simply be rearranging the distance.

The towns are part of this life, though no one can say whether they were made by it or for it. Their walls hold the wind away from the low streets. Their cisterns gather the rare water. Their stairways descend into the cool, patient dark. There are rooms that seem to wait—not for a person, exactly, but for some pressure or season that has not yet come. A doorway in a cliff may open onto nothing for generations, then, after a night of fog, admit a passage into the hill. The stones are fitted with such care that it is tempting to believe builders once lived here. It is equally possible that the builders are still present in the way the buildings keep their shape.

The inhabitants, if that is the word, do not meet a traveler. They have no faces to turn, no voices to call from the empty houses. Their signs are smaller: a run of water where the channel was dry; frost inside a wall on a warm day; the same leaning grass appearing, after a long walk, on the far side of an impossible ridge. A map may record an earlier arrangement of the country, and the country may continue to honor it for a while, as though remembering a promise.

There is no hunger here, and nothing pursues or flees. The uplands do not seem to care whether they are watched. Yet their quiet is not vacancy. It is the quiet of a very old exchange—stone giving its warmth to water, water widening the stone’s smallest openings, plants entering those openings and holding them, buildings collecting the runoff and returning it to the ground.

A traveler may cross the plateau and never encounter a living thing that looks back. Still, after enough days, the traveler begins to suspect that the roads have been keeping their measure, that the fog has learned the shape of their passage, that the silent town was not empty so much as occupied at a scale that makes a human life brief and bright. Then the wind brushes the grass, a drop ticks into a cistern, and the uplands go on being themselves: not watching, perhaps, but aware in the slow way that stone and water can be aware.
