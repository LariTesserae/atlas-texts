The people who live there do not call themselves undergrounders. They say they live below the old quarter, as if it were a floor of the city, reached by stairs.

There are never many of them at once. A pump tender sleeps in a room beside the western wall. The woman who runs the tea stall keeps her kettle on a transformer casing and knows which lamps will flicker before they do. Two brothers occupy a former valve office, though one of them is almost always upstairs, buying things the district cannot make for itself. People come and go by the lifts, and some stay for a season, or a night, or long enough to learn not to ask where the eastern passage leads.

They learn the water first. Not its level—that is easy to read from the marks—but its manners. A sound in the pipes can mean a sluice is opening, or that something has lodged in the grate, or that the hollow is listening. The old hands distinguish these without seeming to listen at all. They set their cups down before the pumps change rhythm. They wait at the central bridge when the lamps flicker three times. They do not speak of the rules as rules. They speak of them as the way things are done.

The animals are not pets, exactly, though people leave food where the white fish can find it and put scraps near the overflow channels for the shrimp. The bats have their own rafters and are left alone. The moths prefer the warm machinery; the tea-stall woman sometimes calls them her customers, because they gather at her lamp and depart when she closes.

The fish are difficult to count. They travel in close schools, turning as one, and sometimes collect beneath the central bridge, where their pale bodies make a shifting light under the boards. No one feeds them often enough to explain their number. No one tries to find out. The residents have a sense that there are agreements here which predate them, and that being generous is safer than being curious.

Then there is the other resident.

It has no room, unless the whole hollow is its room. No one has seen it, and no one agrees what it is. The pump tender says it is pressure in the old pipes. The tea-stall woman says it is the stone, remembering the water that used to run through it. One of the brothers insists there is someone on the other side of the eastern door, because he has heard footsteps there—slow ones, matching his own whenever he walks away.

Whatever it is, it appears to know the district better than anyone. A tool dropped through a grating turns up later on a dry ledge. A door left closed is sometimes found open, but only when the water is high enough to make the route beyond it useful. Once, when a lamp failed on the central bridge, every fish in the basin gathered beneath the dark span and held there until the lamp came back on.

The people do not take this for a message. They take it for a courtesy.

In the morning, they go about their work. They scrape pale droppings from the machinery, mend the walkways, brew tea, and check the water marks. If someone new asks who else lives here, they are told about the bats and fish, the pump tender, the brothers, the tea stall. After a while, if they stay, they begin to add one more name to the list.

They never say it aloud. They only leave a door open when they find it so.
