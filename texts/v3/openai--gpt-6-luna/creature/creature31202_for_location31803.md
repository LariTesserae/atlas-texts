At first, the garden seems to be inhabited by its plants in the ordinary way: ferns, moss, the broad-leaved climbers that have been coaxed along their rings. They drink when the valves open and turn their faces toward the lamps. If a frond is bruised, it darkens at the edge. If a vine strays too far, someone guides it back.

But the guides are not always there.

A new tendril will sometimes find a pipe before it finds its ring. Roots slip into the grout, thin as hairs, and follow the hidden water beneath the tiles. They gather around a warm joint, loosen it by a fraction, and then grow away again. The pumps answer with a change in pitch. A valve opens sooner the next morning. None of this is enough to call intention. It is only enough to make the maintenance records unreliable.

The garden has a resident that occupies the places where one kind of life meets another. It is in the moss that grows in the old lamp rectangles, in the pale bulges swelling from the newest walls, in the root-thread under the stair. It is also, perhaps, in the small adjustments of the irrigation system: a pause in the flow, a pressure held and released, a drip that falls where the mat has begun to dry. No single part of it is alive in any useful sense. Together, they have started to behave as though they notice.

The builders call this growth. The gardeners call it a nuisance, though they have stopped cutting it back. There is no name for it among the machines.

It does not move toward people, or away from them. It simply makes room. A vine eases aside from a handrail when a person passes carrying tools. Moss thickens over a sharp corner. When someone stands too long beside the central stair, the damp beneath their boots draws toward the hidden route under it, and a fine thread of water appears in the seam. It may be coincidence. The garden is full of things that happen for reasons no one has yet measured.

The dark-winged insect is easier to see. It rests on the pipes, folded small against their warmth, then lifts off without seeming to beat its wings. It is not always the same insect—or else it is very good at hiding between sightings. At dusk it goes among the leaves, where the morning inspections find nothing. Once, a worker found a neat row of pinprick holes in a sheet of new growth. Another time, the holes were gone, though the leaf remained.

The insect does not seem to feed. It touches the garden as if taking its measure: a landing on a valve, a pause on the lip of a basin, a brief black shape against the pale wall. The plants incline toward it, though that may only be the draft.

When the pumps fall quiet, the inhabitants do not. Water continues to creep through the mats. Roots continue their slow negotiations with the seams. Somewhere beneath the stair, the unplanned channel widens by a width too small to see. Then the machinery settles with its familiar click, and the whole garden resumes its gentle, scheduled breathing.

Whatever lives here has not yet learned to leave the walls. It has not learned to speak, or to ask for anything. It may not even know that the walls are walls. But in the damp dark between tile and growing mat, in the warm spaces around the pipes, it is beginning to make a place of its own.
