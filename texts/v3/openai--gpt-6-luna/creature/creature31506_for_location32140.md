The creatures here are not still in the way of corpses. They are still in the way of a note held too long.

The crickets have their legs folded against one another, pale joints nested neatly, each narrow body caught at the instant before the next rasp. Their eyes shine wherever a crack admits them. None looks toward the light. None needs to. They know the chamber by touch: the close warmth of another cricket’s flank, the damp seam underfoot, the fine vibration of crystal growing somewhere just beyond reach. Their song has no beginning and no breath between phrases. It is the texture of being together.

The salamanders lie among the stones with their speckled backs pressed into the gaps. They are small and cool, their toes spread as if they had just found a comfortable place and meant to stay there. The pale spots along their sides resemble flecks of mica, though they give back a softer gleam. Each salamander rests with its mouth slightly open. The damp air passes through them—or seems to. Their stillness has the ease of sleep, but sleep ought to have dreams, and nothing in this chamber appears to be dreaming forward.

Beneath the puddle’s bright skin, the fish are gathered shoulder to shoulder. They are blind, but their bodies know the water’s border with perfect certainty. Their mouths make small, soundless openings. Their fins hold the shape of a swim, their tails the shape of a turn. They travel without going anywhere, and the puddle keeps the faint press of them on its surface without ever wrinkling. Perhaps the fish have mistaken the reflected daylight for a vast, motionless sky. Perhaps they know perfectly well that it is only a ceiling, and find that sufficient.

Around the young crystals are the translucent ones: soft knots of flesh, difficult to distinguish from a film of wetness until one notices the feelers. These they lay across the mineral points with extravagant care. The feelers touch the crystals as roots touch soil, though nothing is taken away and nothing is left behind. Their bodies have no clear front or back. They seem less like animals grazing than like the chamber’s own unfinished thoughts, gathered wherever the stone has begun to make a shape.

The mosses do not spread. The tea-colored shoots do not open their cups. Still, they are not merely waiting. A green thread hangs from the roof, a drop poised at its tip, and the thread holds the drop as if the two had agreed upon this arrangement long ago. The plants ask for no soil. Their roots go through stone without breaking it, finding passages the rock has not yet admitted it contains.

All these lives meet in the cramped air, and none can make room for another. The crickets rasp; the salamanders keep their warm-cool silence; the fish swim beneath their little sky; the feelers rest on stone. Their sounds overlap until they become one sound, steady and close, with the low hum of the chamber beneath it. There is no call and no answer. There is only company.

The three small arches belong to no creature anyone can see. Their openings are too narrow for the crickets, the fish, even the strangest soft-bodied things. Nothing has passed through them while the chamber has been held in its bright hour. Yet the stone around them is worn smooth, as if something small once leaned there often, or as if the arches remember hands. The groove points toward the puddle and stops just short. The fish do not rise to meet it.

Perhaps the chamber’s inhabitants are all caught in the same unfinished instant. Perhaps they are so perfectly fitted to this place that motion would be a kind of leaving. They remain pressed together, each body touching the next, each sound held inside the sound of the whole. Their world has no empty corner, no dark edge, no distance in which to wander.

They live as a country lives on a map: every creature in its place, every path present, the borders exact. Nothing advances. Nothing is lost. And still, from the pale stone beneath the roots, comes the unbroken rasp of them all.
