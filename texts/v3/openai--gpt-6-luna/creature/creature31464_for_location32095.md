The first inhabitants are the things that look like the place itself.

They stand in the dark as trunks, walls, and branching columns, their outer surfaces layered with old growth. Up close, the rootstone is not stone but a dense mesh of living fibers, some fine as hair, others thick as cables. It tightens where the current presses hardest. It sends new stems into empty spaces and quietly seals passages that have ceased to matter. What might be called a building is only a portion of a much larger body: one outgrowth among thousands, joined below the terraces in a web too extensive to map.

No single trunk seems to possess a mind. Yet the plateau responds. When a current carries warmth from the abyss, the higher fronds turn, almost imperceptibly, toward it. When grazers strip a meadow too closely, growth thickens along its edges. Pressure travels through the rootwork as a faint tremor, branching and fading, carrying news without words: a passing body, a damaged crown, an abundance of food. The inhabitants do not gather to decide what should be done. Their decisions take the form of growth, and may take centuries to become visible.

The animals know this body intimately. Small translucent fish pass in and out of its lattices, vanishing through seams too narrow for larger creatures. They feed on the soft films that cover the roots and leave behind faint trails of brightness. In their schools, turns ripple like thought. A single fish may dart; the whole school may change direction, making a loose silver shape that folds through the branches and is gone.

The wall-grazers move more slowly. They travel in broad, orderly bands, scraping the dark growth from the columns and terraces. Their mouths work continuously, while their bodies cling to the living architecture as if it were a hillside. They do not eat everything. Narrow paths remain untouched, winding between feeding grounds—old routes, perhaps, though none of the creatures that use them could remember their beginnings.

The great animals above the terraces are harder to distinguish from the growth. They hang among the high branches, wide-mouthed and motionless, filtering small life from the water. Some have blunt heads and delicate, comb-like fringes; others are long enough to curl around a trunk. Their dim bellies glow when the water is disturbed. They may remain in the same hollow for years, letting the current pass through them. Now and then one releases its hold and drifts to another crown, moving with the slow certainty of a leaf falling through still air.

Then there are the travelers—the largest of the plateau’s animals. They move along the broad corridors, turning their shoulders past the old pillars. Their shapes are difficult to see whole: a ridged back, a fan of fins, a pale underside passing through blue-green light. They touch the trunks as they go. Perhaps they feed there. Perhaps the contact is a greeting, or a way of reading the tremors that travel through the roots. The architecture neither yields nor recoils. It holds the passage open.

The creatures have no visible need to hunt one another in the open. Life is gathered in the seams, in the fronds, in the dark films on the walls. Hunger is met by patient grazing, by filtering, by drifting through places where tiny bodies collect. Even the largest animals seem less like predators than moving parts of the plateau’s rhythm. They arrive, pass through, and return to old corridors. Their journeys do not make the place feel busy. They make it feel inhabited.

At the highest ridge, where the meadow has been cropped short, the smaller animals scatter in brief, wavering clouds. Beneath them, fine fibers draw water through the roots. The fronds bend under the feeding and slowly rise again. The ridge is not a lookout, though it offers the longest view. Nothing stands there to survey the distance. The plateau has no need to be watched from above; its awareness, if it can be called awareness, runs through the whole living mass.

There may once have been separate beings here: plants, grazers, wanderers, builders. The distinction has blurred. The animals carry the plateau’s seeds and spores; the plateau shelters the animals, feeds them, and alters itself around their habits. Each species lives within a pattern older than any individual. A creature born in one hollow may spend its whole life within sight of the same trunks, never knowing a boundary between habitat and body.

Nothing is waiting for a command. Nothing is preparing for a migration that will transform the world. The current passes through the same channels. The grazers return to the same walls. The rooted things grow, erode, and grow again so slowly that change resembles permanence.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not rule the dark, or fear it, or seek to escape it. They live as though the dark were simply another part of their bodies: a depth around them, a water pressing close, a silence full of familiar motion.
