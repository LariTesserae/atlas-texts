The people who live there have learned not to ask a place to hold still.

They are not born with the city’s whole shape in mind. No one is. A child’s first map is a set of sounds: the tremor that means a transit car is crossing overhead, the soft ticking behind the wall that belongs to a harmless colony, the three hard knocks in the rail that tell a household to move uphill before the water comes. Later come the names of bridges, districts, and animal passages. Later still, the knowledge that a street remembered from childhood may now run beneath a basin, or have been lifted a hand’s breadth by stone growing in the night.

The inhabitants dress for damp and for change. Their clothes are layered, close-fitting, and quick to shed water; their boots grip wet metal and pale mineral crust. Many wear small instruments at the throat or wrist, though these are less for speaking than for listening. A faint vibration travels farther through the city than a shout. People tap messages along railings, doorframes, and the heavy ribs of buildings. There are whole families who can recognize one another’s patterns from a floor away.

They move carefully, but not timidly. In the lower districts, they know which spans can bear a crowd, which steps are slick with chemical film, and which apparently empty gaps are used by things that emerge only when the fog grows dense. They know where not to put a hand. They know when to wait. This patience is not the same as passivity. When the city shifts, people go to work: loosening anchors, opening a passage, unsealing a building’s skin so a migration can pass beneath it. The labor is ordinary enough to have its own jokes, tools, and songs.

Some tend the machines that keep the city balanced. Some harvest the thin food-film from the water and hot cracks, working with strict limits and long records of what can be taken. Others study the animal movements, though “study” can mean sitting for hours in a dark room, feeling the rails tremble and trying to tell one season’s passage from another. Their knowledge is practical, but never complete. The largest creatures still cross the ridge unseen. A sound may be a call, a warning, or simply the noise of a body turning somewhere in the fog.

Children learn the old paths of the animals alongside the routes between home and school. They are taught not to shine lights into the upper cloud, not to block a spillway because it looks unused, and not to call every clicking noise an alarm. Some children can imitate the courtship bursts of the jaw-clickers so accurately that the grown-ups make them stop. When the young ask whether the animals understand the city, the answer depends on who they ask. An engineer says the animals have learned its rhythms. An elder says the city was built inside their lives, not the other way around.

The animals, for their part, do not seem to distinguish the city from the rest of the plateau. They pass under its bridges, graze its warm seams, shelter in its hollows, and gather on its roofs as if these were simply new features of the stone. A migration can turn a district into a living underside: bodies pressed along the bridge ribs, feelers stirring in the dark, the whole structure humming with their weight. People wait behind sealed doors until the passage is done. Some leave water in the channels. Some leave nothing. The city’s oldest customs are practical ones, and the difference between hospitality and survival is not always clear.

There are residents who have never gone beyond the last maintained platform, and others who work at the edge of the open plateau, where the stone is still forming and the machines can only make temporary promises. They return with their sleeves crusted white, their voices hoarse from the pressure in the air. They bring back measurements, mineral samples, and stories about animals seen only once: a pale shape bending over a new ridge; a broad, folded body resting in a basin that did not exist the year before.

People disagree about what the city is becoming. Some call it a refuge. Some call it a bargain made with the mountain. The children mostly call it home, though they say the word with the assurance of people who know home may move while they sleep.

At night, they keep their rooms dim. They gather inward around warm pipes and small, shielded lamps, while the darkness beyond the walls fills with rasping, clicking, and the low calls passing through the rock. When the rails begin to sing with a distant migration, people pause their conversations. They put a hand to the floor and feel the city listening with them. Then, when the vibration fades, they go on.

They live there by attending to what is changing: the water in the seams, the pressure beneath a walkway, the new note in an animal’s call, the slow upward insistence of stone. They do not mistake endurance for permanence. To belong, in that place, is to keep making room.
