The first inhabitants are easy to mistake for weather.

A sheet of small silver fish moves across the plain, changing shape as it passes between the pylons. From a distance it is a flicker in the water, then a single bright cloud, then nothing. Up close, each fish keeps a little space from the next. The school bends around a tower without breaking, splitting at its broad collar and joining again on the far side. They seem to know the basin’s currents as thoroughly as the floor knows its seams.

In the channels, the larger swimmers travel more slowly. They have blunt heads, heavy shoulders, and narrow tails that beat with unhurried force. Their bodies are the color of wet stone, except along the flanks, where pale markings show when they roll. They move in loose groups of five or six, following the deep water between the low walls. When a pump changes pitch, they turn before the current shifts. Whether they hear the machinery or feel its pressure through their bones is hard to tell. Perhaps the distinction has never mattered to them.

The small fish gather where the built landscape offers shelter: under ledges, against the feet of towers, in the quiet seams behind gates. There they feed on whatever the water brings, and scatter when a maintenance vehicle passes, its lamps washing them briefly white. They return almost as soon as it is gone. Even the enormous machines have become part of the daily arrangement—dangerous, but familiar, like a sudden current or a shadow crossing the sun.

There are living patches, too, though none is allowed to become a forest. Dark cultivation fronds stream from their rails, folded and stretched by the water. Small fish thread through them. Larger ones come to browse, then move on. Above, beneath the floating frames, slender roots or feeding lines hang in orderly curtains. The animals visit these places in season and leave them again. Nothing here can spread far without encountering a boundary.

No one can say whether the basin’s inhabitants are wild. They were brought into a world whose currents can be started and stopped, whose food-bearing growth is trimmed, whose rivers pass through gates. But they have made their own map of it: the sheltering shadows, the clean channels, the places where the water turns cool and quick. They know which engines shudder before a release, which pylons gather the little fish, which open stretches are safest for crossing.

Their lives are not untouched by design. Neither are they entirely contained by it. The basin provides a world, and the animals answer by living in it—schooling, feeding, resting in the long blue intervals between one cycle and the next. When the pumps begin their low change of note, the fish turn through the water like a thought passing from one body to another. Then the gates open, the current gathers itself, and the inhabitants move with it.
