The oldest inhabitants are the plants, though no one agrees on when a stand of them becomes one thing rather than many.

Each stalk responds to light, water, and pressure. A frond turns toward a bright crack in the roof; a curtain of roots tightens when the wind begins to rise. But the roots run together beneath the stone, and the structures lean toward one another across distances too great for any single plant to sense. When a gust sweeps the country, the far-off gardens begin to bow before the air reaches them. The people say the land has heard the weather coming. Whether it hears anything else, they cannot tell.

Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, are slow. A snapped bridge is not mourned; the strands at either end begin to lengthen, and in a few wet seasons the gap is crossed again. A hollow stalk splits, and the plants around it turn their pale faces toward the opening. They drink from the damp chambers inside. Sometimes a whole grove grows away from a place that has become too dry, leaving behind a lattice of dead ribs that rings softly when the wind passes through it. The country does not preserve its own shape. It keeps trying new ones.

The smaller animals live in the folds of this growth. Pale mites graze the wet undersides of leaves, leaving fine, branching trails in the mineral film. There are translucent things in the pools, scarcely more than a thread and a pulse, that rise when condensation begins to fall. The winged creatures are harder to name. They pass high overhead, where the light is brightest, and their wings sometimes flash like scraps of mica. A few have been found caught in the membrane-roofs of human shelters, folded small and dry as fallen leaves. No one has seen one land.

Larger animals come and go without making a country of it. They travel by the covered hollows, where the air is warmer, and leave broad, temporary paths through the growth. The plants close over these trails behind them. Once, a child found a long curve pressed into a hillside of silver fronds, still warm with whatever had passed. The adults would not follow it. By the next wet season, the hillside had risen back into place.

The people inhabit the ridges where the stone holds. They are few enough that their shelters seem less like a settlement than a handful of things put down carefully and not yet taken away. They build low, braced against the wind, with doors of woven stem-membrane that flex rather than rattle. Their tools are dark stone and bone-white mineral; their clothing is layered from the tough outer skins of stalks. They cut only what they can carry, and leave the roots in the ground. A severed plant will often grow again, but not always where it stood.

They keep no roads. A safe crossing is marked by small notches in the rock, and the notches are renewed after storms. Between them, travellers follow ridges, root-bridges, and the lines of water beneath the stone. The country is too large for a path to remain useful for long. A bridge can thicken into a living wall; a dry hollow can become a stream-bed; an open plain can fill with new growth between one journey and the next.

Children learn to listen before they learn to navigate. A creak in the stalks may mean a bridge is under strain. A faint hiss travelling through the root-mats may announce rain still far above. If the whole garden falls silent, they are told to find stone and wait. The adults do this too, though they pretend the lesson is only for children.

They mark time by changes in the living country. The ceiling-vines flower, shedding pale dust that gathers in the folds of clothes. Condensation beads along the roof. The great air-storms return, not on any fixed date but often enough to be expected and too irregularly to be trusted. Between them, life proceeds by small preparations: a shelter is tightened, a water basin scraped clean, an old crossing tested with a stone before anyone steps onto it.

No one claims to speak for the plants. Some say the gardens recognize the people and make room for them. Others say the people are simply another kind of weather, brief and local, leaving little mark. When a human shelter is abandoned, fronds eventually cover it. When a garden collapses, the people gather what can be used and go elsewhere. Both leave room for the other to return.

In the blue dimness that passes for night, the people sleep inside their membrane doors. Outside, water ticks down through hollow stems, and the pale vegetation shifts almost imperceptibly, turning toward sources of warmth no human eye can see. Across the ridges, the shelters are tiny interruptions in its reach. Across the plains, the gardens breathe and bend in the darkless air. For a while, the country holds still around them. Then somewhere far off, a stalk breaks, and the sound travels through the stone.
