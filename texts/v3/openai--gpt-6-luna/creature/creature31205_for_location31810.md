At first, the plateau seems empty. The settlements are so low and so furred with growth that they can be mistaken for part of the stone; the inhabitants, when they move between them, seem no more than shadows slipping through fronds.

They are people, though the word does not quite describe the way they belong to the water. Their bodies are compact and deliberate, made for climbing along ledges and bracing against currents. Fine webbing joins their long fingers. Their feet are broad, with hard pads that grip slick rock. Along the sides of the neck, beneath folds of skin, are pale gill-fans that open and close when they rest. They can stay in the water indefinitely, though they sometimes gather in the warm chambers beneath the overhangs, where pockets of trapped air collect and the oldest voices are easier to hear.

Their skin is usually the color of dim stone: brown, slate, or a greenish pallor. It darkens where the light reaches, and bears faint, branching marks that deepen with age. They wear little that is sewn. Around the waist and shoulders they tie soft sheets of cultivated membrane, woven from plant fiber and weighted at the edges so they do not billow. The young decorate theirs with shells and chips of bright mineral. The old tend to wear only what keeps the cold off.

They speak in low notes, clicks, and pulses of breath. A conversation held across a narrow room is almost silent; a quarrel can be heard through several walls. They also talk with their hands, touching wrists or shoulders to make a word more exact. When they want to speak to someone far away, they tap on resonant stone. The answering knock may travel along a ridge for a long time before it arrives, altered by every crack and hollow it passes through.

Children learn the currents before they learn the paths. They are taught which fronds can be pushed aside, which must be cut, and which will close behind them if disturbed. They learn to recognize a new shelf of rock by its softness, and a dangerous seam by the fine shimmer it gives off. Their first journeys are short: from one chamber to the next, from the ledge where they were born to the place where the water falls upward in a warm veil. Later, they are trusted with the paths that vanish into the groves.

No one owns the land that has not yet hardened. A household may tend a bank of root-bound silt for generations, but if the ground shifts, the claim shifts with it. New ledges are approached cautiously. Someone will press a hand to the stone, return the next day, then the next. Only when it has stopped swelling do they begin to build. Even then, they build lightly. A wall can be moved. A bridge can be untied. A chamber can be abandoned to the roots that are already finding their way through it.

The settlements are not ruled from their towers. The towers are for watching water: its color, temperature, and slow changes in level. Those who keep watch leave small marks on the inner walls, one for each season’s rise or retreat. Many marks are too old to interpret. The inhabitants preserve them anyway. The marks say, at least, that someone was there to notice.

They do not hurry the plateau. They know it is growing, but no one agrees on what that means. Some say the land is climbing toward the silver ceiling. Others say the surface is lowering, and the deep water is merely making room. The oldest people insist that both are true, though not at the same pace. When new ground appears, they sing to it—not to welcome it, exactly, but to let it know it has been seen.

The town’s lamps burn with a subdued amber light inside small, protected cups. In the dim hours, every lamp is doubled by its reflection above, so that the people appear to live between two settlements: one built of stone and weed, the other wavering upside down overhead. They grow accustomed to seeing their homes reflected where no homes are.

Outside the settled ridges, life is quieter and less easily named. Tiny mouths open and close among the moss, filtering food from the passing water. Near the hot seams, transparent creatures pulse in pale clusters, their bodies brightening and dimming without any evident purpose. Children sometimes watch them for an entire tide and claim they are listening. The adults do not tell them they are wrong.

There are larger things below. Now and then a many-finned shadow crosses the deep channels, too distant to show more than a change in darkness. The people do not hunt it. They do not follow it. When it passes, the towns grow still, and afterward the stone carries a faint clicking sound for hours. The elders say the ridge is remembering the creature. The young say the creature is remembering the ridge.

And there are things that are neither animal nor settlement: root-mats that gather silt and slowly make new ground; mineral veils that shift over a season; plants that open pale fronds when warm currents rise. The inhabitants work among them, cutting corridors, tying bridges, repairing walls—but never quite separating their own labor from the growth around it. A fallen walkway becomes a trellis. A cracked roof becomes a garden bed. A chamber left empty grows soft with weed, then disappears beneath a green hush.

So the people remain in the hollows and on the shoulders of the plateau, building only where the stone will bear them and leaving the rest to become something else. They are not waiting for the land to be finished. Perhaps they know it never will be. Their homes, their paths, even the old names for places are temporary arrangements between water and stone. Each year the ground makes a little room for them; each year they make a little room for what is coming next.
