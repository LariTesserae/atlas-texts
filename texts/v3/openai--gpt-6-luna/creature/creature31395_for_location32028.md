The people who live there have learned to leave room for things that are still arriving.

They build narrow rather than broad, and upward whenever the rock permits it. A household may occupy three levels joined by an iron stair: sleeping above the warm pipes, cooking beside a wall that sweats, keeping its water jars in the coolest pocket under the floor. Neighbors know one another by the sounds of their homes—the tap of a ladle, the scrape of a shutter, the brief coughing start of an old pump. A person can be missed for half a day before anyone sees an empty doorway. But when someone is ill, water appears beside their bed without explanation, and a meal finds its way up the stairs.

They are careful with water, though not solemn about it. Everyone has a favorite basin, a trusted gutter, a place where the ceiling gives a reliable bead. Children are sent to check the cistern when the trickle begins, carrying little cups to test its taste. They learn the difference between a drip that can be caught and one that will stop before it reaches the floor. A good catch is shared. A bad one is still used for the plants.

The children grow up knowing the district’s shapes as other children know streets. They can cross a roof bridge without looking down, duck beneath a pipe by habit, and find their way home in darkness by the warmth of the walls. They draw chalk lines where the rock is pressing in, and sometimes return months later to discover that the wall has crossed them. The old people let them mark it again. There is no shame in being overtaken by stone; there is only the work of moving a shelf, cutting back a doorway, or finding a new route to the cistern.

Plants belong to certain households, but never entirely to them. A family may tend a stand of broad-leaved greens beside its window, while roots from the next room’s garden have worked their way through the mortar and into the same damp seam. People trim the leaves where they block a stair, prop them up where they have grown heavy, and speak to them in the easy, practical tones used for neighbors who cannot answer quickly. The leaves are warmed by the pipes. In the wet hours, when they release their herbal, dusty scent, the whole district seems to breathe out.

Below the streets, the engine keepers attend the oldest rooms. They move among flywheels and pumps with lamps held low, listening through their palms against the rails. Their work is done by small adjustments: a tightened bolt, a cleared valve, a wait to see whether the tremor has changed. They are not the district’s rulers, though every household depends on them. When a pump must be stopped, they tell everyone first. People bring their cups and pans to the cistern, and for a little while the city listens to itself without the engines.

There are other residents, less easily counted. White drain-creatures turn up in the wet channels, moving their many legs as delicately as fingers. No one kills them unless they enter a food jar. The blind moths are given a wider berth around the lamps; children sometimes leave a crumb beneath the glass, though nobody knows whether moths eat crumbs. And the green growths in the mortar are watched for changes in color, because they notice dampness before the walls do.

The district’s quietest inhabitants may be the ones glimpsed only in the water. When the cistern is full and the pumps have stopped, people sometimes see a stair descending where no stair has been built, or an open patch of ground shining between two familiar walls. They do not go looking for it. They wait for the surface to settle, then mark the place in chalk, more as a record than a warning. The next day there is usually only brick, stone, and the ordinary dark.

No one there expects the place to stay as it is. They expect a new bulge of rock, a pipe that needs rerouting, a patch of moss spreading over someone’s threshold. They expect to make room, and to be made room for. Their homes are crowded, their air close, their lamps hooded against the damp; still, they have arranged a life inside the hollow, not by mastering it but by noticing what it is doing. In the warmth beneath the young black stone, among the plants and the pumps and the careful little returns of water, they live as if the district is not finished becoming—and neither are they.
