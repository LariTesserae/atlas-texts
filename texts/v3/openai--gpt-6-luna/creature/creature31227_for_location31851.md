The people of the highland are easy to mistake for part of the place. At a distance they are small movements among the dark roofs: a figure crossing a terrace, someone bent over a channel, a door closing against the weather. Up close they are less like the stone than the rain has made them—warm, quick to notice a change in the air, and practiced at finding comfort in narrow, sheltered spaces.

They dress for damp rather than cold alone. Wool and felt, layered shirts, heavy boots repaired many times; hoods that shed water, cuffs darkened from work. Their clothes are mostly the colors already present in the highland: soot-gray, brown, dull green. A bright scarf or a red thread at a sleeve can seem vivid here, though no one wears it to be seen from far away. Faces are often wind-reddened. Hands are broad and nicked, with the ingrained darkness of stone dust and old mortar. People who spend their lives indoors have the same steady eyes as those who work outside; both learn to read the weather in the shine of a wall or the sound of water underfoot.

They know the settlement by its flows. They can tell which roofs will spill first in a hard rain, which stair grows slick, which cistern is low without looking inside. A child is taught where not to step before being taught the names of distant places. If a drain clogs, someone clears it. If a wall bulges, neighbors come with wedges and tools. The work is rarely spoken of as heroic. It is simply what keeps a house dry, a terrace upright, and a path passable through another season.

Homes are small and close-knit, often joined by courtyards, stairs, or shared walls. Inside, they are warmer than their thick stone fronts suggest. There are sleeping platforms, low tables, drying racks, shelves crowded with bowls and tools. A fire is kept carefully, not extravagantly; its smoke finds narrow ways out through the old roofwork. Water is stored in jars and basins, and rain is caught where it can be caught. A household may have little room, but it has corners known by touch: the step worn hollow, the sill that stays dry, the alcove where a lamp makes the stone glow amber.

People here do not hurry much, but neither are they idle. They carry, mend, sweep, prune, measure, and wait. Some tend the small plots tucked between walls, coaxing onions, herbs, or tough leafy greens from shallow soil. Others repair paving or guide runoff away from a weakened slope. The same hands may knead bread in the morning and set stone by afternoon. Much of their knowledge is local and exact: the way mist settles in one basin before rain, the pitch of a drip that means a cistern is nearly full, the patch of roof where moss should be left because it holds the soil in place.

They speak softly in the covered passages, partly from habit and partly because the walls carry sound farther than expected. Their silences are not awkward. A pause can mean that someone is listening to weather, thinking over a repair, or simply sharing a sheltered moment with another person. News travels in small exchanges at doorways and wells: a family arriving from the outer ridges, a stair closed for work, a late frost, a child who has begun to read. The broad world beyond the plateau is real to them, but it is not always urgent. Here, a neighbor’s leaking roof may matter more than a distant kingdom.

Children roam the passages in loose groups, knowing the dry routes and the hidden steps that rise between houses. They play with scraps of slate, seedpods, and strings, making games out of channels and courtyards. Their voices occasionally break the hush, then disappear into the stone again. As they grow older, they are given small tasks—carrying water, clearing leaves from a drain, watching a younger child on a steep stair. In time they learn the city’s ways not as a set of rules, but as a feeling for where the ground is firm and where it might give.

The dead remain close. Names are cut into lintels, marked on stones in quiet courtyards, or spoken when a familiar passage is repaired. Some families have lived in the same rooms for so long that the house contains the gestures of many generations: a notch in a doorframe, a patched wall, a basin set where an ancestor chose it. The past is not kept separate from ordinary life. It is underfoot, dampened by rain, and used every day.

There are few animals within the densest streets. A small, long-bodied creature may slip along an outer channel at dusk, and birds pass over the roofs more often than they settle among them. The people do not chase every trace of wildness away. Grass is allowed to root where it does not threaten a wall; moss is scraped back only where it holds water too long. A bird’s nest beneath an eave may be left undisturbed until the young have flown. The highland teaches a kind of carefulness: not kindness as display, but an understanding that life persists wherever there is a little shelter and moisture.

They are not solemn all the time. In a warm room, someone will laugh; at a gathering, food will be passed around and a tune may be sung, low enough that it seems to belong to the rafters. Festivals, where they exist, are modest affairs of lamps in courtyards, shared meals, and repaired thresholds. The celebration may end with people going home before the rain begins. Their pleasures are fitted to the place—warmth, company, a good roof, the clean smell that rises from wet stone.

Over generations, the inhabitants have become less like tenants of the plateau than one of its habits. They move through its folds, maintain its channels, and build where the stone allows. Their footsteps wear the stairs smooth; their roofs gather moss; their walls hold back soil that would otherwise slide. The city shelters them, and they keep the city from loosening into the hills.

From far away, the people are almost invisible. What can be seen is the shape they have made: terraces following the ridges, passages threading between roofs, a thin ribbon of water shining down a stair. Only when one enters the covered ways does the life of the place become clear—not loud or abundant, but steady. Doors open. Someone calls from an upper landing. Water taps into a basin. And behind the old stone, the highland’s inhabitants go on with the patient work of living there.
