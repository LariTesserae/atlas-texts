What lives here is not a crowd, but a scattering—life held so thinly across the stone that, at first, the chamber seems empty.

Along one sheltered seam, the pale film grows in branching threads. It clings where the water is warm but not too warm, where dissolved minerals arrive and the current does not scour it away. The film has no face, yet it answers its surroundings: thickening in a crease, paling where the water runs clear, withdrawing from a fresh trickle of heat. It lives by making a little more of itself from what the stone and water give.

In the coolest basin, minute translucent animals drift and settle. Their bodies are nearly all softness and water, with a dark fleck inside each one where the organs gather. They move by faint pulses, edging away from warmth, turning when the current changes. In the bright bands they become almost invisible. They do not flee the light; it means nothing to them. What matters is the slight difference between one part of the pool and the next: salt, temperature, the presence of something smaller to feed on.

And sometimes, from a crack in the warm rock, a larger softness uncoils.

It is no larger than a thumb joint, pale and without a shell. The water carries the shape of it more than the eye can: a narrowing, a slow flex, the delicate displacement of a film on the stone. It has no need to cross the chamber. Its world is the seam, the warm dark behind it, and the slick margins of the nearest pool. When a drop strikes too close, it gathers itself and slips back into the crack. There it waits until the water steadies.

None of them knows the light as light. They live by the chamber’s smaller messages: a current arriving after a pressure shift; a basin warming; a mineral edge breaking and releasing new traces into the water. The crystal may cast its bright bands across their bodies, silvering them for a moment, but they pass through that brightness as through any other change in the world.

Their lives are measured in narrow tolerances. A few degrees too warm, a rush of fresh water, a flake of stone landing in the wrong place—and a whole patch of the chamber may fall quiet. Elsewhere, where the conditions hold, the film spreads by a thread’s width, the tiny animals continue their slow crossings, and the soft-bodied resident returns to the edge of the pool.

The chamber’s inhabitants are easy to miss because they have not filled it. They occupy only the places that can keep them: a seam, a pool’s cool underside, the sheltered lip behind a mineral rim. Between those places lies bare rock, shining and immense. Their presence is a faint answer to it—not a conquest of the hollow, but a persistence within its changing terms.
