At first it seems that nothing lives there.

The houses have no doors opening and closing, the lanes no footsteps. No face looks down from the terraces. The quiet between the great breaths of air is so complete that the hollow might be mistaken for a place abandoned before it was ever finished.

Then the dark fronds stir.

They grow in seams between the basalt blocks, spill over retaining walls, and gather in the warm lee of the houses. Their colors are muted: green almost black, grey-green, the pale bone color of new growth. Some hold themselves in stiff fans. Others thread fine tendrils through the joints in the masonry. They do not drink or flower in any familiar way. They draw what they need from the mineral breath of the stone and the scarce, softened light slipping through the roof.

There is no single plant that rules the city. The growth is a multitude, yet it behaves like one presence. Where the walls are warm, it thickens. Where the vents blow too hot, it pales and retreats. It loosens grains of salt from the stone and finds purchase in the cracks that follow. Slowly, without haste or any visible hand, it alters the buildings it inhabits. Its roots trace the old channels; its leaves make delicate patterns on the walls. The city seems less built over than grown into.

The wind gives this presence its voice. Air threads through chimneys and roof slots, and the fronds answer with a dry, papery rattle. Sometimes the sound is almost speech, though no syllable repeats quite the same way twice. Salt patters down the lanes. A deep boom rolls through the rock, and every wall, leaf, and narrow metal sheet takes up the note for an instant. Then the hollow settles again, listening.

Perhaps the plants are not listening. Perhaps the city is not breathing. The wind passes through it; the heated stone expands and contracts; the mineral seams glow with their own faint color. Yet the whole place moves in a rhythm older than any house within it. It exhales through the vents, gathers its warmth in the terraces, and lets the dust travel where it must.

Nothing here hurries to become anything else. New salt crusts the vent mouths, then flakes away. The plants grow, stiffen, and are replaced by their own slow continuance. The walls round at the corners. The lanes keep their shape by yielding, a little, to heat and pressure. What lives here does not keep house against the hollow. It keeps the hollow inhabitable.

Above, the plain is bare salt, bright enough to erase distance. Below, the rooted growth and the city’s breathing passages share a small, dim world. There are no inhabitants to greet you. But as you stand in the lane, the air begins to move, the fronds tremble, and the buried city gives its low, patient answer.
