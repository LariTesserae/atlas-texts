At first, it seems that nothing lives there except the plants.

The ferns keep their small, folded beginnings tucked close to the damp wall. Their fronds open slowly, each one finding the same narrow reach of light. The waxy leaves hold beads of water without trembling beneath them. Moss makes a dark cushion wherever the concrete offers a crack, but does not cross the pale seams. Even the reeds seem to know the limits of their slots. Their roots run along the hidden pipes, taking the route already made for them.

Then, if you stay long enough, the room becomes busier.

A film of green has gathered in the basin’s shadow. It is not quite moss, not quite stain: a slick, fine life laid over the metal where water passes again and again. Microscopic things live in the black compost, breaking down the leaves that fall and returning them to the roots. The water carries them around its circuit. Nothing announces itself. The garden’s smallest inhabitants are the ones you would have to know to look for.

Sometimes a speck of an insect rests on the surface of the basin. It makes a tiny dimple in the silver, pauses, and slips away among the stems. Perhaps it came through the hatch while someone was working outside. Perhaps it was born in the damp, or arrived on a leaf. The room offers no way to tell, and no reason to decide. It disappears into the green, which closes around it without fuss.

There are no keepers, but the plants have learned to live as if the room were tending them. Water rises where it should. Fallen leaves soften and vanish into compost. The basin fills, spills its narrow measure, and drains away. Each root finds moisture; each surface sheds it. The machinery’s roar keeps every small sound from becoming a signal. The garden does not hear the world beyond it, or call to anything there.

Still, it is not lonely in the way an empty room is lonely. Its inhabitants are close to one another: leaf against leaf, root against channel, moss in the crack beside the old mineral seam. They share the water without needing to meet. Their lives are slow enough that a change might take a season to show itself, and small enough that it might pass for a stain, a new curl of green, a leaf gone soft at the edge.

If anything watches from the garden, it watches without eyes. The moss darkens where the drip falls most often. The reeds incline by a fraction toward the pale panel. The compost settles. The water keeps moving, and the plants keep using what it brings.

The garden’s oldest resident may be the quiet arrangement itself: concrete, metal, roots, moisture, and all the minute lives held between them. Not wild, not tame. Simply living here, in the spaces the waterworks left open.
