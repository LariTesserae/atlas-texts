The people who live there do not think of themselves as underground people. They have a word for the basin, and words for its chambers, shelves, wet places, and dangerous seams, but no word that makes the whole of it feel finished. To them it is simply where they live: a place whose shape must be learned again each morning.

They are quick to notice small changes. A person passing through a lane might pause to brush grit from a wall, not because the grit is troublesome but because of where it has collected. A jar set down on a shelf is checked for a new tremble. Children are taught to remember the sound of a familiar ceiling: the faint ticks and settling groans that mean nothing, and the different silence that may mean something. They are not solemn about this. They laugh, bargain, quarrel, and forget things like anyone else. But they have learned to keep one part of their attention on the stone.

Their homes are not treated as inheritances so much as useful arrangements. A family may stay in the same rooms for years, adding a platform or closing a draughty gap, then move across the basin when a floor sags or a better place opens. What they take with them is portable: cooking pots, woven screens, tools, the jars in which they keep seed and water. A doorframe might be left standing after the rest of a house has been dismantled. Someone will use it again.

They wear close-fitting clothes that can be mended often and do not catch on rough stone. Sleeves are rolled or tied back for work; scarves are used against dust, wrapped around a head in a low passage, or spread over a basket to keep grit from food. Their hands are nicked and strong. Some have pale marks on their shoulders where they have spent too long beneath the daylight cracks; others are darkened by lamps and kiln smoke. They look less alike than people from a place with a single trade or a settled edge. There are stonecutters, potters, water-keepers, builders, gatherers of the scarce green things—and people who do a little of each because the basin does not always offer the same work twice.

The children roam in groups, carrying messages and small loads, and learning which passages connect by getting lost in them. They know where the floor goes hollow, where water appears after rain, and which abandoned gaps are only narrow enough for a child. They make games of mapping the newest lanes, laying pebbles at turns and giving temporary names to places not yet claimed by adults. The names often stick. A market nook may still be called “the crooked tooth” long after its first sharp edge has been braced smooth.

People here do not ask only who owns a wall or a stair. They ask who will answer for it if the stone shifts. A building that narrows a passage, a cistern left uncovered, a roof made heavy without good support—these are not private mistakes for long. Neighbors help shore up a failing lintel, sometimes grumbling throughout. In return, the owner may help move a household’s belongings when the ground has made the decision for them. There is pride in making a place hold, but little shame in leaving it when it will not.

They have customs for the earth’s warnings, though not every custom is called a belief. When a fresh crack appears, people mark its ends with charcoal or flour and watch to see whether it grows. A cup of water is set on a questionable shelf. After hard rain, those living beneath a new seam sleep nearer the passage, where they can leave quickly. Some speak softly before a dangerous wall is opened; some tap it with a knuckle, waiting for the answer. Whether they think the stone listens is a question they would answer differently from one day to the next.

Their festivals are small and practical, at least in their beginnings. The first full cistern after a dry spell is shared. When a new route is made safe, food is carried through it and eaten at the far end, so that the passage begins as a way between people rather than a cut in the rock. On the longest days, those who can gather beneath the broadest shaft, turning their faces toward the plain daylight as if it were a visitor they have not quite got used to.

They are not trying to conquer the basin. They are trying to make a life that can shift when it does. Their courage is rarely grand. It is in carrying a child across a passage that has begun to crumble; in opening a shop again two chambers away; in planting a herb where the light falls, though the light may move when the roof breaks.

They have not made the place into a home once and for all. They keep making it one, with each brace fitted into a split, each jar placed beneath a leak, each new lane learned by heart.
