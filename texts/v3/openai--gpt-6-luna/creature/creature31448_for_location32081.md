The first thing to notice is that the plants are listening.

Not with ears, exactly. Their hollow columns tighten when the wind changes, and their scaled skins rasp against one another as they swell. The low fans lie flat through a gust, then lift again, leaf by narrow leaf. Their roots hold the loose ground in nets, but they do not anchor the hollow so much as read it: a tremor traveling through grit, a shift in pressure, the faint bitter breath that rises from a new crack. When the stone exhales, the plants take in what it gives them and keep it, concentrated in their thick inner tissues.

The grazers know which stalks to approach. They are pale as old bone, jointed in too many places, and they move with the abruptness of scraps caught in a draft. One stops; all stop. Their small heads tilt toward some shared instruction, then the whole group darts on. They scrape the brittle husks from the plants and lick the mineral films from the smooth plates. Their feet leave almost no lasting mark. A gust comes, and the ground changes its mind.

Above them, the membrane-winged hunters hang in the warm air. Their veined wings are nearly invisible until they turn, when the dim light catches them like thin copper. They do not flap often. They wait for movement below, or for a stalk to flower. Then they drop without a sound, folding their wings close around a chosen meal. The grazers scatter in all directions, though often they stop together again before the danger has passed.

The flowers are brief, and the hollow seems to know when one is coming. A stalk swells. Its scales loosen. Then pale bells open at its crown, each one thin-walled and hollow, trembling in the air. Their notes are not quite music. They are sharp little rings that carry from rim to rim, even through the dust-roar. The winged creatures gather first; the clicking creatures answer from the stone knobs; the grazers rush in beneath them, scrambling for fallen husks. For a short while, every resident of the hollow is moving toward the same place.

Then the bells collapse. By nightfall the stalk is empty, and the creatures have dispersed. The stalk remains, already drawing its scales shut around whatever it has kept.

The clicking sounds come from the knobs. The orange crust on their backs is not merely lichen: small, hard-bodied animals live beneath it, packed into seams in the stone. They click to one another across the floor, a language of distances and warnings, though no one listening from outside could tell which is which. When the wind is strong they fall silent, waiting for the grit to pass. When it drops, they answer one another again, and the hollow fills with small dry voices.

Something else lives under the floor. It is not often seen. It moves slowly, with a weight that makes the mineral plates lift and settle. Sometimes its passage raises a blister in the ash; sometimes it splits the ground and spills up a cloud of glittering grains. The smaller animals flee the first crack, then turn back. Whatever rises from below is food, or the promise of food, or at least a change in the ground worth investigating. The great buried thing passes on, and the surface closes behind it in a new shape.

There are no drinkers here, though thirst has not vanished so much as become irrelevant. The animals take salts from stone, bitter compounds from plants, and what they require from the charged air. Their lives are measured by pressure, heat, and the small avalanches beneath their feet. They have no need to remember a stream. They remember where a stalk will open, where a crack last breathed, and how long the hollow stays quiet after a storm of grit.

At the rim, where the black stone is smooth, something has left its mark. Three old posts bear spirals nearly rubbed away. A windbreak of stalks has sagged into the ash. Perhaps people once came to gather the seed-cases. Perhaps someone still does. The pale grazers avoid the ring of stones, but the clicking creatures shelter beneath it, and the plants have grown close around one side. The hollow has not kept the human signs intact. It has simply made room for them, as it makes room for every other thing that settles here.

Nothing in this place is still for long, though much of it is patient. Stalks wait through years of dust. The creatures wait beneath the stone. The grazers listen, all together, for a signal that may be no more than the ground changing its weight. And above them, in the copper dusk, the wide-winged hunters turn slowly in the heat, waiting for the next brief bloom to ring them down.
