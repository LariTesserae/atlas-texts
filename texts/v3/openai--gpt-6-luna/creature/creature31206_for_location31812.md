The first thing that lives here is easy to mistake for a collection of small accidents.

A bead of water that never falls. A bird with its beak just open. A shutter that taps once, then rests at the same angle against the wall. The town’s people, too, appear caught in the beginnings of things: a hand near a cup, a boot turned toward the door, a face angled as if listening to someone who has not yet spoken.

They are not dead. Nor are they quite waiting. Each seems held inside a single, private instant, as a flame might be held inside glass. Their clothes do not fade; their hair does not grow. Nothing in their bodies answers the years, if years pass here at all. It is difficult to know whether they are aware of one another. Sometimes a person’s eyes seem to have shifted when nobody was looking directly at them. Sometimes the cup beside the well is a finger’s breadth nearer the edge than it was before. These are not reliable observations. The town is full of things that nearly change.

Something else moves through it.

The residents call it the Staying, though no one remembers who first used the name. It has no settled shape. At the far end of a lane, it may resemble a small, pale animal slipping between the grass tufts. Near a wall, it is only a seam of shade traveling across the stones. Once, on the stair to the water hollow, it seems to be an old person coming up slowly, one hand brushing the rail; then the hand is moss, and the figure is the cool air gathered in the turn of the steps.

It does not cross open ground in the ordinary way. Instead it appears wherever one thing almost becomes another: at the edge where wet stone dries, in the narrow interval between a bird’s note and its echo, along the crack where a roof beam meets its peg. The local lizards do not flee from it. They lie with their pale sides warming against the rock, and the Staying passes through their shadows without disturbing them.

It is not the town’s ruler. It gives no orders and mends nothing. Yet the place keeps its shape around it. The same water threads the same channels; the same green gathers in the same crevices. If a leaf begins to loosen, it does not fall. If a stone shifts in the slope, it does not come free. The Staying seems to notice these almost-events with the attention a grazing animal gives a distant sound—not alarmed, exactly, but unwilling to let it go unanswered.

It feeds, perhaps, on what does not happen. Not on the people or the animals, but on the moment before a gesture is finished, the breath before a word, the small surrender by which a season becomes the next one. It takes these beginnings into itself, and the world remains fresh around it. There are no bones beneath the floorboards, no vanished harvests in the store sheds. Nothing is used up. Nothing is replaced.

The people are part of this, though not in the way the grasses and stones are. Their paused gestures seem to draw the creature near. It visits the workshop often: the open door, the tool on the bench, the cord half-coiled beside it. It may stand there for a long time, if standing is what it does, attending to the hand that has not reached for the cord. It does not finish the gesture. Perhaps it cannot. Perhaps it is guarding the instant from finishing.

At dusk—or what passes for dusk here—the Staying is hardest to see. The shadows beneath the eaves grow clear but do not lengthen. The hills beyond the ridge remain green in their folds and bright on their heights. A bird gives its thin, sharp note. The stream makes its unchanging silver fork around the dark rock.

Then, on the watchhouse roof, the pennant stirs. Not more than before. Not less. Just enough to show that the Staying is somewhere near, moving through the held breeze with the patient care of an inhabitant going about its ordinary rounds.
