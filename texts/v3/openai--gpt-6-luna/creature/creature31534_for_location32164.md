At first, the Fold seems empty. Its streets are too narrow for crowds, its windows too dark to show a face, and the machinery makes every sound of life seem mechanical. Then a shutter turns where no pressure change is due. A hand appears through a hatch and catches a drifting tool before it strikes a pipe. Somewhere behind the walls, someone is awake.

The residents are human, though the Fold has taught them to move as if they were part of its machinery. They pass through flooded corridors in close-fitting work suits, with small breathing rigs at their throats and weights at their hips. Their lamps are hooded, their gloves thick at the palms. In the dry rooms, they peel off the suits and hang them beside the doors, where the water can run from them into collection gutters. A person’s life is divided not so much between indoors and outdoors as between air and water.

They are compact people, not from any great change in their bodies but from the habits of a place where every passage is tight and every movement has consequences. They turn sideways without thinking. They know which rail will take their weight, which ladder will sway, which stretch of conduit carries heat. Their hands are broad and nicked from years of working on sharp stone and metal. Many keep their hair clipped close, so it will not catch on a valve or drift into a seal. They carry little that is not useful.

The Fold’s noise has made them attentive in a way that can look like quietness. Conversation is difficult in the streets. Voices blur or vanish beneath the pumps, so people speak in short phrases, or not at all. They knock on rails and pipe housings: a quick double tap to ask for passage, a long one to report a fault, a familiar irregular rhythm to say *I’m here*. Close friends can recognize one another by the way they touch a wall. Children learn the patterns early, along with the names of the dangerous currents and the places where a floor plate flexes.

They have another language, too, made of waiting. A person who pauses with one palm against a support is listening for the basalt’s deep note. When it comes, everyone knows what follows—not exactly what will happen, but how to prepare for it. Shutters turn. Bladders swell. The inhabitants leave certain passages clear and secure anything that might rattle loose. A child who has grown up in the Fold can feel the pressure change in the soles of their feet before the tone arrives. The adults call this a useful instinct, not a gift.

Their homes are as tightly fitted as the rest of the district. Some families live in sealed chambers, behind thick doors and layered windows. Others choose rooms built to flood, fixing their tables and cupboards to the walls and sleeping in harnessed bunks. No one pretends these arrangements are beautiful. They are orderly, and they work. A room may be small enough that its occupants can touch three walls without leaving the bed, but it has its own repaired hinge, its own warm pipe, its own particular dimness when the whole district’s light dips.

There are no pets. There is no place for them, and nothing for them to feed on. The children sometimes tend the few dark fronds in the protected seams, though the plants have little need of tending. They watch the fine green growth around the outflows and try to coax it into new shapes. Most of their living things are people. They know who is asleep by the unlit window, who has taken the long route back from the turbine court, who has been working too long by the way their taps slow.

The market occupies a narrow crossing where several conduits leave enough room for people to stop. Goods are clipped to mesh or sealed into clear pouches: tools, spare gaskets, preserved food, filter material, small personal objects passed from hand to hand. The market is not loud. Its bustle is a current of gloved gestures and brief exchanges, interrupted when a pump knocks hard enough to make everyone glance toward the same wall.

No one in the Fold believes the district is permanent. The basalt is new, the ocean is patient, and repairs outnumber memories of the original design. But the inhabitants do not live as though they are merely waiting to be driven out. They make their marks where marks will hold: a sequence of notches beside a hatch, a name etched into the inside of a locker, a line of bright thread wound around a rail to show a child how far they may wander alone.

They know the Fold by touch and vibration, by its warmed pipes and stubborn doors, by the pressure gathering beyond its membrane. They do not mistake the district for something safe. They trust it because they know how it fails, and because they have learned how to answer.

When the great note rolls through the basalt, the people of the Fold move with the city. In the cramped streets and flooded rooms, hands find rails, shutters turn, and the water begins to rise. For a little while, the whole district seems to breathe around them. Then the surge passes, the cables settle, and the inhabitants go back to work.
