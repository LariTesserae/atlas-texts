They are easiest to mistake for the settlement’s own shadows.

At first there is only a narrow print in frost, too sharply made for wind and too small for any person known to the surrounding mountains. Then, on another morning, a second set appears beside it. The tracks cross a terrace without following its lanes, pause at the dry cistern, and end at a wall where there is no opening.

The people who leave them are small and narrow-bodied, with long hands and feet made for finding purchase on broken stone. Their skin is the color of the shaded rock: pale where it is thin, darkening at the knuckles and along the spine. Their eyes are wide and dark, but they do not look about as we do. They attend to pressure, tremor, and the small changes in air that pass through cracks. When they move aboveground, they keep low, slipping between the fallen slabs and the walls as if they know every place the stone has settled.

Most of them live beneath the terraces, in chambers older than the rooms we can see. Their entrances are difficult to distinguish from seams in the rock. They need little space and less light. They drink from the seep and from dew gathered in shallow stone cups; in the coldest months they shelter together in the warmest hollows, close enough that their breathing makes a faint mist. They have no fire. They carry no tools that catch the light. Whatever they make is shaped from stone, fiber, and patient pressure.

They are not the makers of the settlement—or not, at least, the makers of all of it. Some lintels bear marks that their fingers still trace, though the meanings have thinned beyond recovery. They know which halls return a sound cleanly and which swallow it. They know where water will appear after rain, and which old steps should never be crossed when frost has lifted them. They treat the ruins less like a home they own than a body they have learned to live inside.

Their speech is quiet, and much of it is not speech at all. A fingertip against a wall can carry a message farther than a voice. In the roofless halls they answer one another with low, rounded notes that travel through stone. Sometimes the wind catches those notes and enlarges them until the whole settlement seems to hum. Sometimes a sound returns from an empty courtyard a little later, a little lower. The residents know the difference. They wait for the answer before they move.

One courtyard remains bare because it is where they gather when the weather turns. They sit around its edges and place their hands flat to the ground. No one knows what they listen for. The ferns leaning toward the blank wall may be responding to warmth from a passage behind it, or to the faint vibrations of many bodies gathered on the other side. The great stone at the end of the roofless passage is not a door to them. It is a boundary. None will touch it, though sometimes one stands before it for a long time with its head bowed.

They do not forage much aboveground. In warmer spells, they collect insects from the wet stones and take seeds from places where the grass grows thickest. They never strip a patch bare. They leave the old lanes open and step around the plants, so that the settlement’s few green things seem, from a distance, to have arranged themselves for them.

There are children among them, though their size is difficult to judge. The young learn the terraces by touch, walking a hand along the walls until they can find their way in darkness. They learn the old markings the same way: not to read them, exactly, but to feel where a curve deepens, where one line breaks into another. The eldest are difficult to distinguish from the oldest stones. They sit for hours in the shade, listening through the soles of their feet.

When weather arrives over the ridge, already spent, the residents know before it reaches the roofs. They gather below, leaving the courtyards empty. Then the wind moves through the abandoned halls and draws out its great hollow note. From beneath the terraces, the people answer it—softly, once, and not always in the same place.

By morning, the frost will show where they went. By noon, the prints will be gone. The settlement will look as it always has: quiet, open to the weather, waiting without expectation. Only the water in the cisterns will be disturbed, and, in one courtyard, the grass will lie pressed flat in the shape of several small sleeping bodies.
