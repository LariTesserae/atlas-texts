The people of the basin do not call themselves cave-dwellers. They have a word for those who live below the plain, but it means something closer to *those who stay while the ground moves*. It is not a proud name. It is a practical one.

They are a quiet people, though not a timid one. Their voices carry strangely across the chambers: a whisper may travel farther than a shout, then break against a bend and disappear. So they speak in low, measured phrases, and have learned to listen for the answers the stone gives back. They know the difference between water beginning to run and water merely gathering. They can hear a wall loosening, though the sound may be only a small click beneath the feet. Children are taught to stop talking when the floor trembles—not from fear, but so they can tell where the trembling is coming from.

Their houses are made to be altered. Nothing important is fixed so firmly that it cannot be moved: beds, cooking stones, shelves, the little lamps set in alcoves. A family may spend years in one room and still keep its belongings packed in easy-to-carry bundles. The oldest possessions are often not the finest, but the most useful: a copper hook that has survived three collapses, a bowl whose crack has been mended so many times that the repair is thicker than the bowl, a strip of cloth passed down because it catches condensation unusually well.

They have no word for morning that means the same thing everywhere. In one district it begins when a shaft fills with pale light; in another, when a water-clock has dripped through a bowl. People mark time by changes: the brightening of a high wall, the arrival of a cold trickle in a dry channel, the hour when the long-legged birds lift from their roosts. Their children grow up knowing that a day is not something the sky gives them. It is something they make together from signs.

They are not farmers, but neither do they simply forage. Some families tend the cisterns where pale crabs gather, keeping the stone clear and the water unclouded. Others watch the animal trails in the outer reaches and leave gates open or closed according to the herds’ habits. The residents know which mineral crusts are harmless, which pools conceal blind creatures, which shafts bring down enough drifting life to feed a crowded hollow. Their meals are plain and varied by subterranean standards; food is shared carefully, and waste is returned to the places where other life can use it. To them, a dead animal is not an ending but a provision passed onward.

The larger animals are treated with a respectful indifference. A herd moving through a street is allowed to pass. Doors are unbarred; children are called indoors; someone waits with a broom to sweep the dust from the threshold afterward. The animals do not belong to the people, and the people do not pretend otherwise. Still, a family may know an old herd by its tracks, or recognize a particular bird by the pale notch in one wing. In the brighter halls, children sometimes sit beneath the shafts and watch the creatures gather, their backs and shells appearing one by one as the light finds them. They learn early that the cave is not empty just because most of its life is hard to see.

Their crafts favor repair over ornament. They fit stones so that a shifted wall can be taken apart and set again. They weave screens that soften the shafts’ light and catch the damp. They make small bells from dull metal, not to call across the city but to hang near a newly cracked passage: if the floor shifts, the bell tells the household where to look. Their most skilled builders are not those who can raise the highest wall, but those who can tell which wall should be left low.

When a chamber changes, the people go to see what has happened. They do not gather in alarm, unless the water is rising. They bring ropes, wedges, baskets, and chalk. Someone marks the new edge of a fissure. Someone else notes where the dust has settled. If a passage has narrowed, a route is found around it; if a pool has emptied, its basin is inspected for what the water left behind. By evening, the change has entered conversation as a new fact of the city. The map is corrected, not mourned.

There are, of course, those who dislike this way of living. Young people sometimes leave for the plain above, where the earth seems steady and the sky is a single enormous room. Some come back. They return pale-eyed from the brightness, carrying stories of weather that arrives from every direction at once. Others remain above, and are spoken of without reproach. No one can say that the city keeps its people. It only offers them a place that has learned, slowly and without promise, how to make room.

At night—or what they call night—the screens come down over the shafts. The light becomes broad and milky, falling softly over the courts. People eat in doorways. Water ticks in the channels. Birds fold their wings high overhead; animals settle in the open halls. Beyond the last houses, the stone continues to shift in darkness.

The residents sleep lightly. They sleep with their tools nearby and their most precious things where they can reach them. Yet they are not waiting for the city to end. They expect it to change. They have built their lives around that expectation, until even a fallen wall can look, by morning, like a place where something might begin.
