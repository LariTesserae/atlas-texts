The people of the Bright Works are called keepers, though none of them keeps anything in the ordinary sense.

They live in small rooms fitted into the thickness of the plateau, where the walls are warm from machinery and the windows look onto other walls. Their clothes are pale and practical, usually marked at the cuffs with a stripe of color. The stripe may indicate a district, a task, or simply the direction the wearer was facing when they dressed. They speak softly, and not because the Works are quiet. Sound travels badly here: a sentence spoken across a room may arrive after its speaker has left it. So the keepers use brief words, gestures, and marks painted at junctions. They do not mark where a path goes. They mark which way the junction seemed to be facing that day.

Their work is mostly attentive. They inspect a seam, wait beside a drain, or stand at the end of a ramp until its slope has finished changing. If water climbs a channel, they watch where it goes. If it stops, they do not push it onward. They say the water is taking the shortest route, and they mean this as both an explanation and a warning. The keepers do not claim to know what the Works were made for. They do not even agree on whether they were made by anyone. Their oldest records are full of measurements that change between copies.

A keeper who has spent a long time on the plateau begins to move with its peculiar patience. They do not hurry across a court, even when it appears short. They avoid counting steps. They rest on the terraces where shadows point toward the sun, and seldom look too long into polished metal. In still water, a keeper can see their face clearly. In a metal wall they may see themselves a few seconds younger, or the room they will enter after this one, or no one at all. They treat these reflections as they treat the other small disagreements of the Works: with courtesy, and without asking them to explain themselves.

The herds are less cautious. They cross roofs and bridges in quiet, measured groups, their long legs moving over surfaces too clean to hold a hoofprint. They feed on lichen, salt, and the thin mineral films left by condensation. Their bodies are the color of old ivory in sunlight and of the surrounding architecture in shadow. At dawn they gather facing north. The keepers leave that hour undisturbed. No one knows whether the herds are waiting for something or remembering a place they have never been.

Black birds nest inside signal housings, where their calls become little ticks and clicks among the machinery. They descend in sudden crowds, blotting out a court, then lift again all at once. The keepers say the birds know when a district is about to shift. They say this because the birds often leave just before it does. They also say it because the birds sometimes leave for no reason at all, which is harder to work with.

The small quick creatures are never seen clearly. They slip through spaces that should not admit them, and leave dust arranged in delicate spirals. The keepers do not sweep those spirals away. On cool evenings, the winged dark gathers around lamps—not on the lamps, but in the lighted air where something ought to be. It knots and loosens there, like a thought that cannot decide whether to take shape.

There are other inhabitants, though the keepers do not call them alive. A drain may retain the warmth of a hand placed against it. A corridor may seem to draw breath through its vents. Sometimes a whole district shifts by a fraction, and every door in it opens toward the same empty court. The keepers respond by walking the edges, renewing the colored marks and waiting until the structures settle. They do not speak of the plateau as a creature. Still, they behave as if it can hear them.

At night, the keepers sleep in rooms that remain faintly lit by the surfaces beyond their windows. The birds tuck themselves into housings. The herds lie beneath long roofs, facing different directions at last. Somewhere below them, the water continues to find its way through channels that do not run downhill. The Works hold their bright, exact shapes above the clouds, and all its inhabitants—keepers, birds, herds, hidden things, and the structures themselves—continue their separate forms of attention.
