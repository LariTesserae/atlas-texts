At first, the small pale creatures seem to be the only things living in the town. They cross the courts one at a time, stopping at shadows, turning their narrow heads toward doorways. They are no larger than a hand. Their bodies have the unfinished look of something pressed from salt and left to dry. If approached, they do not flee. They simply cease to be where they were.

A visitor might decide they are not animals at all, but scraps of the town’s attention, sent out to inspect a threshold or count a passing footstep. This is close enough to be useful.

The inhabitants do not have bodies in the familiar sense. They occupy the town as a gathering occupies a room: not by filling it, but by giving each empty place a purpose. One presence fits beneath a low lintel; another takes the length of a covered way; several more arrange themselves around the basin, leaving its center clear. Their shapes are made of intervals—the distance between two walls, the slight incline of a street, the space where a person might stand while waiting for someone else.

When they move, nothing seems to move. A doorway is a little farther along the wall. A court has acquired a narrow approach. A room that once held the impression of many bodies now feels intended for one. The town is forever making these small accommodations, as though its residents are shifting about after a long silence.

They know one another by pressure. A change in the floor’s slope may mean *I am here*. A line of salt drawn along a wall may mean *not here, not yet*. At the basin, grains collect and vanish in careful sequences, too regular to be weather and too slow to be called writing. The inhabitants have no need to speak. They exchange the conditions that make speech possible: nearness, direction, welcome, refusal.

This is why a shout cannot reach them. Sound is a disturbance that passes through the body and is gone. A hand laid flat against a wall is more legible. The wall can keep the warmth of the palm for a while. The residents feel it as a brief, bright arrival.

They have been here longer than the town’s present shape, though perhaps there was no town before them. They may have made the first walls by gathering wherever a boundary was needed. They may have learned, over generations, to hold their arrangements still enough that stone accumulated around them. It is hard to tell whether the halls are their homes or their bodies, whether the streets were built to connect them or to let them think.

The low dark plants are part of this life, too. Their roots follow the seams, carrying minute changes from one building to another. Where the tufts meet, the dust grows warm: a sign of contact, or perhaps the equivalent of a shared breath. The pale creatures pass among the roots, carrying nothing that can be seen. They stop at shadows because shadows are where the town’s measurements become uncertain. Each one tests the edge and reports it back.

The basin is their oldest common place. It was not made to hold water. It was made to hold a vacancy: a precise, round absence into which every resident can turn its attention without occupying it. When the basin is clean, the town is at peace. When dust gathers on the rim and does not disappear, the residents are restless. They have no way to leave the town, or no reason to. The plain beyond offers no walls, no routes, no arrangement in which one presence can be known by another.

A visitor who stays long enough may feel them gathering. The sensation begins as a certainty that a doorway is meant to be used, though no one has told you which way to go. Then a court seems to have made room for you. Somewhere behind you, an invisible company has altered its spacing.

You are not welcomed. You are not threatened. You have simply been counted.

Afterward, your footprints may remain for many dim intervals. Eventually, the dust lifts from them in thin veils and drifts upward. By then the town will have revised one of its streets to pass a little closer to the place where you stood. Somewhere inside the walls, the residents will have made room for the memory of you.
