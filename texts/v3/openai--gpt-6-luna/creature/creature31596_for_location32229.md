At first they seem almost as still as the city.

They wait beneath the covered walks, or stand on the broad roofs where condensation gathers, wrapped in pale layers that break the glare. From a distance there is little to distinguish them from the stone: a fold of cloth, a dark oval of a face, a hand resting on a rail. Then one of them turns, and the movement is so unhurried it seems to have begun long before you noticed.

They are people, built for this brightness and this heat. Their eyes are narrowed against the white of the pools; some wear thin inner lenses of cloudy glass, which they lift only indoors. Their clothes are loose, layered, and close-woven, covering skin from sun and mineral spray. The soles of their shoes are broad and soft. A person crossing a wet bridge does not so much walk as place their weight carefully, listening through the feet.

They speak close together. A sentence is often quiet enough to be lost in the open air, but it is not their only way of speaking. Fingers tap the rail: a question, a warning, a familiar greeting. Messages pass along the stonework in rhythms too subtle for a stranger to recognize. The old can tell from a tremor in a drain cover whether a pump is turning smoothly, or whether something large is moving through a distant basin. Children learn the channels with their palms before they learn the city’s names.

They do not treat silence as emptiness. The shelf is full of sounds that are felt rather than heard: the patient thrum of flywheels behind their walls, water shifting under the streets, the low pressure-signals of animals crossing the flats. When a herd passes beyond the city, people stop what they are doing. They stand with their feet apart and their hands against stone, reading the passage in small disturbances. No one points. Pointing would tell you where to look, but not what is there.

Their work is a kind of tending. They clear the open channels, scrape salt from the mouths of pipes, check the cistern seals, and coax the heatworks through their slow turns. No task is considered finished when a machine begins to run; it is finished when it has run quietly for a long while. Those who know the old pumps are listened to with the same attention given to the weather. A mistake in the channels can empty a neighborhood’s cisterns. A mistake in the heatworks can foul the water for everyone.

They take food from the life the shelf permits. Nets are lowered into the shallow basins; traps are set along the paths of the flat-bodied skimmers. The larger animals are not often hunted. Some are dangerous, some are simply too difficult to kill without disturbing the basin, and some have crossed the same route for so long that the people speak of them as neighbors—not friendly, not tame, but part of the pattern. Their tracks are read in ripples and pressed silt. A child who has never seen a great animal clearly may still know which one has passed by the shape of the water it leaves behind.

The inhabitants make little distinction between a natural channel and a built one. They alter the shelf, but sparingly: adding a culvert where water already wants to go, raising a walk above a place that floods, leaving a ridge bare because it is useful to the animals or because it has always stood there. Their city is not a claim laid upon the land. It is an agreement maintained by repeated attention.

At midday, when the light turns the open pools into blank mirrors, they gather in the narrow shade beneath bridges. They eat in small groups. They mend clothing, exchange news, and sit with their hands resting on the warm stone. Their voices are low, but their conversations are not short. A pause may mean that someone is listening to the drains. It may mean that the speaker has not yet decided what the words should be. No one rushes to fill it.

At night, lamps are hooded and the walls begin to move with small bright creatures, descending in orderly threads to feed on salts and the heat left in the masonry. The people watch them from doorways. Sometimes they lay out smooth plates for the creatures to gather on, then carry the plates away at dawn. Sometimes they leave them alone. The difference between a useful animal and an unwelcome one is often no more than where it chooses to feed.

They grow old slowly, or appear to. The heat softens their voices; the mineral air roughens their hands. The oldest people know which pools are safe after rain, which towers hum before a valve fails, which paths are used by the long-necked animals in the hours before dawn. Their knowledge is not kept in books alone. It is built into habits: the way a palm rests on a wall, the way a worker waits before opening a sluice, the way a whole street falls quiet when the water beneath it changes its note.

So the city’s people are not silent, though the place encourages silence. They speak in voices, taps, footfalls, and the stillness between them. They live by noticing what moves slowly: water through stone, heat through a machine, a distant body passing under the haze. In time, even their own lives take on the rhythm of the shelf—measured, close-attentive, and difficult to separate from the processes around them.
