The first inhabitants you notice are the small black birds. They pick their way along the stone shelves, vanishing into fog and returning a moment later from a place they could not have reached so quickly. Their feathers shine as if wet even when they are dry. They do not build nests. They tuck themselves into warm seams in the rock, where the mineral hum is low and steady, and sleep with their heads beneath their wings. Sometimes one gives a single bright note. A moment later, the note comes again from somewhere under the moss.

The long-legged animals in the grass are quieter. They have narrow bodies, jointed legs, and eyes that catch the dim light like drops of amber. Their hooves sink into the cushion moss without leaving prints. They feed on pale grasses and on the tiny ringing leaves, but never seem to take enough to thin either one. When the fog thickens, they stand facing the same direction, ears lifted, listening to the deep thrum beneath the basin. Then they set off together, stepping carefully across the pools as if following a path only they can feel.

There is life in the water, too. Clear, many-jointed shapes turn beneath the surface, folding and unfolding like bits of light seen through a moving pane. They gather where the channels click and pulse. When they chatter, the pools answer in a metallic voice. Sometimes the water draws them down through a crack in the stone; sometimes they rise again in a pool on the other side of the basin, none the worse for the journey. They appear to have no need to catch anything. They sift the water, perhaps, or listen to it. Their bodies are nearly invisible, but the surface shifts around them in precise little rings.

The ringing plants are not plants in quite the same way as the ferns. Their glassy stalks lean toward footsteps, toward bird calls, toward the clank that sounds from deep in the mountain. A touch makes them chime, softly and separately. In a strong pulse of the hidden machinery, they all answer at once, each with a different note. The sound passes over the basin and down through the stone. Afterward, the moss seems briefly brighter.

Nothing here appears to be hunting. Nothing flees. The animals feed, sleep, wander, and gather, but all their lives fit neatly into the basin’s careful workings. A bird’s flight may stretch across an afternoon, its wings beating against the same low cloud. A droplet hangs from a leaf, falls, and hangs there again. Even the animals’ small departures seem less like leaving than like slipping from one part of the place to another.

The largest inhabitant has no visible body. It is the slow machinery beneath the cliffs: the bands of warmth traveling through stone, the fissure breathing at exact intervals, the pool that draws water down without growing shallow. Its pulse is felt in the feet before it is heard. The birds perch by it. The long-legged ones follow it. The clear shapes turn in time with it. Perhaps the basin is alive; perhaps it is doing something older and stranger than living. Either way, all its creatures seem to belong to its rhythm.

They do not keep the mountain running. They are part of the way it runs.
