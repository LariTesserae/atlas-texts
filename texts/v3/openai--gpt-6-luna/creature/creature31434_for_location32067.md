The people who live there are difficult to count. They do not gather in the avenues, and they rarely appear beneath the red lamps. Most of the district seems empty until a door opens and someone steps out carrying a cup, a coil of wire, a damp bundle of clothes. Then the door closes, and the street belongs to the machinery again.

They live in rooms tucked behind the blunt façades, often in spaces that were never meant to be rooms at all: old inspection bays, sealed waiting areas, cavities between one wall and another. Their windows, where they have them, look onto other walls. A household may consist of three people in a chamber large enough for a workshop, or one person in a room built for a crowd. They keep their belongings close and their paths memorized. Maps are unreliable here. The residents give directions by sound instead: follow the pipe that knocks twice, turn where the floor warms, wait for the gate to finish closing before crossing the square.

They have learned not to mistake a change for a warning. A corridor that grows warm may be making room for a shift in the rock. A door that unlatches by itself is often safer to go through than one that resists. When the central channel runs fast, people bring their washing down; when it thins, they leave the water alone. Children are taught to recognize the ordinary voices of the district—the pump’s low beat, the ventilation’s distant murmur, the little clicks of relays—before they are taught the names of streets.

There are few children, but the ones who grow up there can tell one another apart by their footsteps. They run along the broad, vacant streets and climb the stairs to platforms where no train comes, not because they expect one, but because the platforms are good places to listen. They press their palms against the walls to feel the slow pressure of the city passing overhead. They know which damp patches are merely damp and which have begun to move.

The older residents keep notebooks. Not maps, exactly: records of what the district did. A door opened on a Tuesday after forty years shut. The amber light lasted seven minutes. The water came warm from the western seam and tasted faintly of iron. Some entries are inherited from people who died before their writers were born. Others end mid-sentence, with a mark indicating that the route no longer exists.

The residents do not say the district is alive. They say it is working.

Still, there are customs that suggest otherwise. When the machinery falls silent, no one speaks until it starts again. When the amber light fills the concourse, people leave their rooms and stand in the open, even if they are busy, even if they have seen it a hundred times. At the far chamber, someone always clears the basin of grit so the little skyline can appear in the water. No one admits to doing it. The basin is simply clean again.

Other life is smaller and less certain. Pale things shelter in the drains, folding their legs beneath them when a light passes. Soft-bodied creatures follow the warm trickle through the channel and vanish beneath the buildings. In the courtyard trough, the translucent leaves open when the pumps change rhythm. The residents have tried to move them, but the leaves close and do not open again until returned to their place. Once in a while, a scrape travels inside a wall, too heavy to be water and too measured to be a settling stone. Nobody goes looking for it.

Perhaps the district’s oldest inhabitant is the system itself. It adjusts the heat, opens doors, alters the flow of water, and sometimes persuades the stone to give way. The people maintain what they can: clear a drain, replace a relay, mark a dangerous stair. The district answers by keeping their rooms warm, or by opening a passage when the old one has become unsafe. No one can say whether it understands them. No one is sure that understanding would make it behave any differently.

At the edge of the inhabited streets, where the paving gives way to rough stone, people sometimes go alone to the shallow basin. They watch the reflected lights tremble there. When a pump engages and the image breaks apart, they wait until it settles. Then they go home by whichever route the district has left open.
