Nothing on the plateau lives alone. Even the solitary things are only briefly out of reach of the others.

The great grazers are first to be seen: broad, low animals with shoulders like boulders and coats thick enough to hold a skin of frost. In a herd they move almost without gaps, each one keeping its flank against another’s. They crop the tough growth down to its roots, then drift onward, leaving dark paths through the vegetation that the wind soon fills with snow. Their young are born in the lee of warm stones, already silver-backed, already nosing toward the herd’s heat. When danger comes, the adults do not scatter. They turn together, a slow wall of horn and shaggy hide, and the whole basin seems to shift around them.

The small pale animals live underneath that moving wall. They thread the root mats and vanish into cracks, appearing in quick, nervous bursts to gather seeds, soft shoots, and the insects that hatch where meltwater lingers. At a distance, their movements make the hillside tremble. Their nests are warm, damp pockets packed with shed fur and fine grasses. Predators find them by listening: a long-legged ridge-runner can hear a colony fall silent beneath the ground.

The ridge-runners travel in pairs or strings, dark against the snow, their legs narrow and their feet broad as if made for the broken stone. They are patient hunters. They follow the herds for days, taking the sick, the young, or anything stranded when a shelf of ice gives way. After a kill, they leave the difficult scraps to the smaller scavengers, then climb toward the heights, where the wind combs their coats clean. Their calls are sharp and carrying; the plateau answers with echoes that seem to come from beneath the rock.

Above them, the gliders make their long circuits. Their wings are broad and translucent at the edges, dark-veined where the sun shines through them. They rarely beat them. Instead they rise on the warm breath of vents and slide along the cliffs, shoulder to shoulder, turning together when an updraft breaks. They feed on small animals, floating seeds, and whatever the wind lifts from a herd’s passage. At dusk they gather on the highest shelves, folded tight against the cold, their bodies flashing silver whenever they shift.

There is another population below the ice. It does not come up to breathe. In the deepest basins, long bodies move through the dark water under a clear, frozen lid. Their passage travels ahead of them as a line of dimples, a slow disturbance in the ice. They feed on blind things living among stones and roots, and perhaps on one another. In winter, when the water contracts and the ice thickens, they press into chambers warmed by seepage and wait. The plateau has no reliable calendar for them; it has pressure, warmth, and the taste of water moving through the stone.

Plants know those currents too. Pale-fuzzed cushions spread over exposed rock, catching mist and keeping it near their roots. Copper-leaved thickets gather in sheltered hollows, where their crowded stems hold warmth and moisture long after the sun has gone. Their roots enter fresh fractures and thicken there, binding loose scree until the next thaw, the next tremor, the next patient widening of the land. In summer they flower all at once—not brightly, but with a brief metallic sheen, as if the slopes had been burnished.

The animals follow the plants’ hidden geography. Herds favor the warm margins of mineral springs. Small creatures nest where roots hold the thinnest veil of soil. Gliders ride the same rising air that carries the vents’ white banners. Even the ridge-runners’ routes bend around the buried channels, where the stone is warm enough to soften underfoot. Tracks cross and recross until no single journey can be distinguished from the many that have passed before.

When the herd trails disappear under fresh snow, the plateau is not empty. Life continues beneath the white: roots drawing water through pores in the rock, eggs resting in warmth, bodies curled in dens, the slow traffic under ice. Wind covers what has happened, and the next thaw writes over it again.

The plateau is occupied from its highest air to its deepest water. Its residents do not tame its harshness. They use it, lean on one another within it, and become part of its long, unhurried work. To live there is to learn that the ground is never quite still—and that it is almost always alive.
