At first, the plateau seems to be inhabited by movement rather than animals: a scatter of clicks beneath the stones, a shifting of grit, a pale shape withdrawing into a crack before the eye can decide it was there.

The long-legged grazers are the largest creatures to show themselves regularly. They come out when the rock is damp, in loose herds with so much space between them that they seem less like a gathering than a series of solitary decisions. Their bodies are narrow and high above the ground, carried on jointed legs that place each foot carefully along the wet seams. Broad, dark pads spread under their weight. Fine grooves in the pads feel out the faintest differences in the stone: warmer cracks, fresh condensation, the tremor of water moving somewhere below. They feed on the black mats in sheltered pockets, cropping them with comb-like mouths, and scrape the bitter mineral film from wet basalt with the edges of their tongues.

Their hides are the color of ash under water. In mist, droplets cling to them; when they pass through a brief shimmer of mineral sparks, their flanks flash blue and are dark again. The young travel close to the adults, not from fear of being left behind but because the old animals know the routes. They know which basins will hold water until dawn, which ridges give warning of a discharge, and which deep clanks mean the ground is only settling into its ordinary rhythm. When the plateau’s pulse changes, the herd stops. Every head lowers. Then, as if a thought has passed from body to body, they turn toward the nearest fissure and walk away.

Smaller creatures live in the spaces the grazers leave. Some are no bigger than a clenched hand, with hard, overlapping plates that knock softly together as they hurry over grit. They collect shed hairs, broken leaves, eggs, and anything the wind has lodged against a stone. Others wait under the thorned shrubs, pale and nearly transparent, their many legs drawn close until moisture reaches the ground. Then they unfold and cross the open rock in quick, dry streams. In the deepest cracks, eyeless animals feel their way along the warm walls. They feed on the thin films that grow where vapor meets stone, and retreat farther down when the seams begin to sing.

The hunters are easiest to mistake for weather. Broad wings turn high above the ridges without a call, keeping to the slow upward currents that rise from warm rock. They do not dive at every moving thing. They wait for a herd to cross a narrow shelf, or for one small animal to break from its shelter during a dry interval. In the dark, they vanish altogether. Only a sudden hush among the clicking creatures tells that one has passed overhead.

Yet none of these animals seems to belong to the plateau as deeply as the little things in its seams. A sheen may spread over a wet crack, made by countless minute lives drawing minerals from the water. It retreats from heat, returns with condensation, and leaves behind a brittle skin that the grazers scrape away. In some hollows, pale fans open and close with the damp, though they are neither leaves nor quite anything that grows in the usual sense. The larger animals depend on them; the small ones depend on what the larger animals drop; and the hunters depend on the small ones. No creature appears to command the others. The place has arranged them by repetition.

They live by noticing what a passerby might not: a change in the warmth of the air, a tremor too low to hear, a new wetness along a familiar ridge. The plateau gives them no landmarks that last, so they carry their maps in their bodies. A grazer remembers the slant of a shelf through its feet. A hunter reads the lift of vapor against its wings. Deep in the cracks, the blind ones feel the great, slow turning of pressure through the stone.

When a seam discharges, life withdraws without panic. Feet tuck beneath ledges. Wings fold into narrow clefts. The tiny clicking stops. Warm vapor pours out, and for a while the plateau seems entirely empty. Then the shimmer fades; water beads along the rock; something tests the air from under a stone. One click answers another, far off. At last the grazers return, following the damp routes across the basalt, while below them the deep pressure gathers again. The animals do not understand the plateau’s workings. They have simply learned how to live inside its measure.
