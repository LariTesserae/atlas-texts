The first thing to learn is that nothing in the hollow calls alone.

A small animal springs from a mat of silver fronds and gives a bright, ringing note. Somewhere across the basin, a larger creature answers with a pulse so deep it seems to come up through the slate. The springing animal waits. The answer comes again, changed by the wind, and it vanishes into the green.

The small ones are called chimes, though they have no metal in them. They are narrow-bodied, many-legged creatures, quick as thrown sparks, with dark glassy eyes and pale plates along their backs. Their feet grip stone; their bodies tremble when the wind shifts. Each carries a voice suited to a different distance: a sharp click for the nearest fronds, a thin ringing call for the open flats, a rattling trill for danger. They do not sing to fill the air. They sing to find out what the air has done.

The grazers are slower. They travel in ones and twos along routes worn almost invisible through the growth, their long limbs folded close beneath them when they rest. Their mouths are small and blunt. They feed not by stripping leaves but by scraping the mineral dust from the fronds and drawing out what the plants have caught from the wind. When a grazer presses its face to bare ground, it is tasting the seams beneath the stone. It may remain there for a long time, still except for the fine quiver of its nostrils. Then it rises and walks on, leaving the ground apparently untouched.

Above them move the gliders. They have no wings that can be seen, only long bodies and trailing filaments that open and close like a hand in sleep. They ride the basin’s broken currents, rising when the air falls and falling when it rises. Sometimes one hangs over a bare flat, almost motionless, while the wind streams around it. Their calls are low and wavering, too soft to travel far except when the tower’s turning rings catch them. Then the sound crosses the hollow all at once, as if the creature had spoken from everywhere.

The largest inhabitants live near the ledges. They crouch on stone shelves, folded into themselves, their backs ridged with dull plates that collect dust. They rarely move unless the air changes. When one gives its booming answer, the call can be felt in the ribs before the ear finds it. The smaller creatures scatter—not in panic, but in practiced widening arcs, each finding a patch of growth where the sound is less strong. Afterward the big animal settles again, and grit sifts slowly from its back.

None of them seem to own territory in the way creatures elsewhere might. They keep their distances, but the distances are alive with exchange: a chime’s warning passes from frond to frond; a grazer turns before the gust reaches it; a glider shifts course toward a sound not yet audible to anything below. Their paths cross without meeting. Their calls overlap without becoming a single chorus. The hollow has no center of attention, only a thousand small negotiations between bodies and air.

The plants take part. Their hollow columns shiver when certain notes pass through them, and their fronds turn their pale undersides toward gusts that carry a useful charge. Copper-threaded roots tighten in the mineral cracks. Where animals have fed repeatedly, the growth is not bitten down but polished, its surfaces smooth with use. Dust gathers in the hollows beneath the plants, then lifts again when the wind folds through.

The old instruments are part of this life, though perhaps none of the animals understands them as instruments. Chimes gather beneath the turning tower, where its rings break the wind into delicate currents. Gliders drift past the black pylons, altering their course just before the air around them folds. The large ledge-dwellers answer those distortions with deep pulses. Now and then a loose stone rises from the ground and hangs in the air. Several small animals pause to watch it. One gives a questioning click. The stone falls; the hollow takes the sound away.

At night, the creatures do not quiet. Their voices change scale. The bright calls grow sparse, while the deep notes travel farther through the cold. The gliders vanish against the sky, and the grazers crouch in the lee of the green islands. Dust continues to move. The plants rasp against one another. Beneath it all, the stone roars steadily, carrying whatever the basin has to say into its own depths.

The inhabitants are not restless, though nothing in them is still. They have learned the hollow’s brief silences, its sudden reversals, its pockets of shelter and its dangerous open places. They do not flee the wind. They listen for what it is becoming, then lean, spring, feed, answer, or wait.

From the rim, the basin may seem almost empty: scattered plants, bare stone, a few old structures. But each empty reach is crossed by a route, each gust carries a message, and each quiet patch lasts only as long as the hollow allows. Its life is spread thinly, like a net drawn tight across the air. The mountain holds the net. The wind keeps it trembling.
