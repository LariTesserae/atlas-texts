The people who live in the Sedgeworks are difficult to see from the road. Their windows are screened with leaves; their doors open onto lanes that dip below the level of the surrounding yards. A passerby might hear someone moving inside a house and see only the ivy tremble.

There are not many of them, but enough to keep the presses running and the water moving. Most have learned several kinds of work. The person tending a roller in the morning may spend the afternoon clearing a channel, mending a hedge, or checking the numbered gates after rain. Nobody speaks of the district as reclaimed or preserved. It is simply kept—though what that means changes from week to week.

They know the water by its small signs. A dark line on a wall means one channel is backing up. A patch of moss gone silver means the flow beneath it has shifted. The older residents can tell which pump is straining by the note it makes through the floorboards. They teach this knowledge without much ceremony, pointing with a wrench, a twig, or the toe of a boot. Children learn where the planks are loose before they learn the old street names.

At home, people make room for the damp. Boots stand on slatted racks. Coats hang in warm passages near the pipes. Window ledges are crowded with jars, folded cloth, seed trays, and screws saved because the right screw may not be easy to find again. The houses are close enough that a neighbor’s kettle or cough can become part of the day’s general sound. In the evenings, lamps shine through the trained stems around the windows, turning the houses into dim green lanterns.

The workers at the central hall do not hurry the machinery. They listen to it. A press that knocks twice where it should knock once is stopped; a belt that begins to slap too sharply is eased back into place. Fibre arrives from the beds and sorting yards in tied bundles, wet at the edges, smelling of leaf and river mud. The finished boards leave on a small cart, stacked under canvas. They are sent elsewhere for insulation, packaging, and uses the residents rarely hear about. The Sedgeworks makes its things quietly, as if it would rather not draw attention to the fact.

Some of the residents have lived here all their lives. Others came for a season of work and stayed, or returned after leaving. They are not cut off from the city beyond the embankment, but they move between it and the Sedgeworks with the slight reluctance of people stepping from a familiar room into a bright corridor. Out there, they say, everything is sealed too tightly. Here, a broken thing can still be useful.

They have names for the places that no longer appear on maps: the bent stair, the cold grate, the room behind Number Nine where the floor always lifts in winter. They know which courtyards belong to which households, but the boundaries are porous. A pruning knife travels. So does a pot of soup, or a length of pipe. When a roof gives way, neighbors arrive with timber and soil, and the next spring something grows over the repair.

The district has its ordinary disagreements. Someone leaves a sluice open too long. Someone else cuts a hedge back too far. The press hall runs late, and the dull thud carries through the houses after bedtime. Still, arguments rarely last past the next shared task. There is always a blocked drain, an unstable step, a bundle of fibre to move. The work does not make everyone kind, but it keeps them in one another’s reach.

And then there are the things no one quite counts as residents. Fish under the duckweed. Snails on the warm stone. Sparrows in the chimney braces, returning year after year to nests patched with stolen fibres. Roots passing through the old drains. The people make space for these where they can, though not always out of tenderness. A channel clogged with roots must be cleared; a bird’s nest in the wrong brace must sometimes be moved. The district is generous, but its generosity has limits and maintenance schedules.

If the Sedgeworks has a life of its own, it is not separate from theirs. It lives in the turning belts, the watched gates, the repaired plankways; in the residents’ habits of listening before they act. It lives in the slow green work that continues after the lamps go out, drawing water through brick and root, holding the paths in place a little longer.

At night, from the road above, the houses disappear beneath the canopy. Only the high factory windows remain visible, faintly lit. Below them, people sleep while the pumps murmur and the water slips through its channels. By morning, the place will have changed almost imperceptibly: a fallen leaf will have settled in a grate, a root will have found a seam, a familiar sound will be missing. Someone will notice. Someone will go and see what can be done.
