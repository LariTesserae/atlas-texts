The people who live there are difficult to distinguish from the work of keeping the place livable. They are always carrying something out of the water’s way: a stool, a basket of meal, a sleeping child, a coil of rope. A person pauses at a doorway not because they are uncertain where to go, but because the trickle crossing the threshold has changed its course since morning.

They are compact, thick-clothed people, with hands made deft by stone, reed, and cold. Their sleeves are tied close at the wrist; their boots are soft-soled and patterned underneath with little ridges for wet ledges. Most wear a belt strung with small useful things: a wedge, a length of cord, a shallow spoon, a smooth weight that can be dropped into a current to learn its pull. Their hair is usually cut short or bound under felt caps. The damp makes loose hair troublesome, and the people dislike having anything obscure the small signs by which they read the basin.

They do not walk quickly. They walk attentively. A child learns early not to trust a dry-looking stone, or a familiar stair, or a doorway that was open yesterday. Before they can carry water, they can tell which drains are breathing cold air and which have gone quiet. A feather caught against a wall may send a whole household moving its stores to higher shelves. Adults notice without seeming to look: the tremble in a shallow pool, the shifting shine on a roof, the way a bird stands with one leg lifted. They are not solemn about this knowledge. It belongs to them as easily as the names of relatives, and is used as casually.

Their speech is low and brief, though not because they have little to say. Sound does not travel well, and it is easier to call someone by tapping a beam or knocking twice on the stone beside a passage. They have many words for kinds of water: water that is going somewhere, water that has lost its way, water that will return through a different opening. They speak of a channel being hungry or a stair being asleep. Visitors sometimes mistake these phrases for superstition. The people do not bother correcting them.

A household is not quite the rooms it occupies. It is also the routes between those rooms, the ledges where a neighbor may leave a bundle, the warm hollow under a shared oven, the plank that can be pulled free when a wall begins to leak. Privacy exists, but it is made of curtains and habit rather than distance. People hear one another stirring at night. They know who has moved their bed higher, who is mending a roof, who has not yet come home by the pattern of light beneath a door.

The young are raised by many hands. They learn to swim before they learn to climb, though the pools are so shallow that swimming often means crawling through clear water on their palms. They play at finding the hidden exits of familiar channels and invent new ones with cups and scraps of slate. Their elders let them be wrong in small ways. A flooded game court, a soggy bundle of reeds, a startled fish in a sleeping room: these are useful lessons. A child who has once watched a toy boat vanish beneath a doorstep and reappear in the next house will never again assume that what is lost has gone far.

The animals are not pets, exactly. They are neighbors with poor manners and their own reasons for being there. Birds nest above kitchens and drop shells into the cooking pots. The thick-furred creatures under the houses are fed the tough ends of roots, partly from kindness and partly because a well-fed animal is less likely to gnaw at a support. The pale fish are caught, but only when they gather in the right pools. No one will dam a gutter to trap them; the gutter has other obligations. On certain nights, people set out shallow dishes for the bright, transparent things in the cracks. Whether this is an offering, a habit, or a way of keeping them out of the bedding is not agreed upon.

They have few possessions that cannot be moved quickly. Their best tools are kept within reach; their stored food is divided among several high niches; important objects are tied down even when there is no visible current. A family may lose a room and suffer less than a visitor expects. What grieves them is the loss of a route: a stair that can no longer be used, a channel whose sound has changed, a passage that has become unfamiliar. They mourn such things quietly, then begin to make another way through.

There are elders who remember the basin as it was before certain bridges, or before the water began passing through the upper terraces. Their accounts disagree. Each version is held with affection, like a story about a relative who may have been taller than anyone can prove. The town keeps no single map. It has instead many small maps: notches cut into doorframes, colored cords, remembered turns, a child’s palm laid flat against a wall while an aunt explains where the current used to emerge.

When the pools show houses that do not yet exist, people gather to look. They bring no instruments and make no attempt to keep the image. Someone may point out a roofline, or remark that the reflected steps seem poorly built. Then a fish breaks the surface, the picture comes apart, and everyone goes back to work. The basin has offered something; it has not promised anything.

At night, the people sleep with their clothes folded close and their bundles fastened shut. A hand may rest against the floor, feeling for a change in vibration. They wake when the water asks them to—not in alarm, usually, but with the resigned familiarity of those who have shared a house with a restless guest for generations. Before dawn, a door is unbarred, a lamp is carried to the nearest crossing, and someone steps out to discover which way home goes now.
