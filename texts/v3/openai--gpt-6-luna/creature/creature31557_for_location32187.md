At first, the hollow seems uninhabited. There is only water, stone, and the measured violence of the works above. Then, in the milky water at the basin’s edge, a pale film gathers where no dust has fallen. It is thin as breath on glass, almost invisible except where it catches the light.

It lives in the seams.

The film is not quite a plant, though the dark-green mats in the creases belong to it—or perhaps merely share its shelter. It is a colony spread through the wet stone: fine, living threads drawing warmth and dissolved minerals from the water, laying down a chalky skin wherever the seep slows. Its oldest growth has hardened into the pale crust on the rock. Its newest growth is soft and faintly opalescent, trembling when a drop lands nearby.

Nothing about it happens quickly enough to watch. The colony advances by the width of a fingernail, then less; it narrows a crack here, cups a little water there. When the pump strikes, the basin shivers through the soles of the building. The film stirs—not in any visible wave, but in the way a surface seems to gather itself. After the concussion passes, its threads settle again, holding fast.

It takes what the hollow gives: warmth from below, water from the seams, metal and stone dissolved into a slow, bitter nourishment. It has no need to hunt. The rusted bracket leaks into its reach. The cut pipe offers beads of hotter water. Even the square of perforated metal, sinking by degrees into the wet floor, is becoming part of the place it knows how to use.

The colony has no face, but it keeps a boundary. It does not cross the hottest bare stone, nor climb toward the narrow opening where the air dries. It thickens instead along the sheltered channels, where water lingers and the ceiling’s patient drops replenish it. The wiry shoots lean into the light above it; below them, the living film holds to the dark.

If it has any sense of the machinery, it knows it only as pressure: a recurring blow transmitted through the ground, a certainty as regular as warmth. It cannot know the factory, or the hands that fitted the pipe, or the reason the foundation split. It knows the pump as weather. It knows each drop as an arrival.

The hollow is small enough that the colony may already occupy most of its usable world. Yet it is still making that world: sealing a seam, whitening a ledge, turning water into a shallow basin and a basin into a place where something can remain. The works continue above. Beneath them, almost too slowly to be called movement, the living crust keeps gathering.
