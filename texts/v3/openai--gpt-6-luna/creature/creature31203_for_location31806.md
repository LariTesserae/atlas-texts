Nothing there is quite a passenger. The plateau’s inhabitants do not merely live among its workings; they are caught up in them, passing water, spores, seeds, and signals from one living tract to another.

The smallest are almost indistinguishable from the wetness. They gather beneath the cushion-plants and along the black rims of pools: pale, many-legged things that feed on the fine growth coating the stone. They retreat at the first change in pressure, leaving the surface briefly clean and shining. The white grazers come after them. These are slight, quiet animals, often mistaken for folds in the fog until they move. They feed in loose bands, cropping the dark mats without stripping them bare. Their hooves sink into the saturated ground and lift without a sound. Where they pass, the plants grow back in narrow, brighter lanes.

The grazers are not always looking where they go. Their ears turn toward the ground, catching the deep pulses traveling through root and stone. When a slope begins its slow pumping, the bands drift away from it. When the thrum falls, they return. They seem to know the plateau’s rhythms not as separate events but as changes in a single, immense body.

At the pools stand the long-necked birds. Their legs are narrow and dark, their bodies the gray-white of old bone. They can remain motionless for hours with their heads tucked beneath their wings, awake the whole time. Now and then one lifts its head and gives a small, hollow call. Others answer from somewhere in the fog—not always from the direction they seem to come from. The birds drink from pools that reflect the wrong sky, and sometimes probe their beaks into the air above the water, where a reflection hangs without its source. No one knows whether they are feeding, listening, or testing the distance.

Beneath the plant mats, larger things travel slowly. Their passage raises long, soft humps that cross whole fields and vanish into ravines. On rare occasions a section of mat lifts and settles again, revealing a slick, plated back, dark as the stone beneath it. The creatures disturb the roots as they go, loosening old bands and opening channels. Water follows those passages uphill or down, according to whatever pressure the plants are holding. The burrowers leave no permanent tunnels. The ground closes after them, as though it has only briefly remembered being open.

The largest animals are difficult to see. The plateau reveals them first by sound: a hollow bark from a ravine, a clicking chorus from a slope where nothing appears to move, a low boom that travels through the stone and arrives in the body before it reaches the ear. Sometimes, far off, a shape crosses a shelf of rock—too tall to be a grazer, too slow to be a bird—and is gone behind a low rise that ought not to conceal it. These great callers may be solitary, or may only seem so because the plateau rearranges their distances. Their voices carry through roots and water as well as through air. When one calls, the stalks answer with a tremor.

The stalk-fields are alive in a different way. They do not hunt or flee, but they respond. Their jointed stems bow toward changes in pressure; their roots tighten when water is scarce; their seed cases open in a sequence that can travel across a hillside like a thought moving through a body. Spores lift from them and drift sideways against the wind. Sometimes, in the damp gray dusk, faint lights travel along their seams. The animals gather at the edges of these fields, but do not enter when the glow is strongest.

No single creature commands the others. Still, the plateau has patterns that look almost like decisions. Grazers leave a basin before the rain begins to rise from it. Birds assemble along a ridge just before a distant slope starts pulsing. The burrowing things turn away from a patch of ground whose roots have begun to draw water too quickly. Whether they sense one another, the plants, or the changing shape of the weather is hard to tell. Perhaps there is no distinction between those things here.

If there is a mind on the plateau, it has no face and no fixed center. It might be in the stalks pumping water, in the burrowers shifting the ground, in the birds calling across distances that will not stay put. It might be the slow exchange between them: root tightening, hoof passing, beak lifting, spores traveling against the wind. The plateau does not seem to think in words or plans. It responds, remembers, and begins again.

Its inhabitants are not tame, but neither are they afraid of the land. They cross it by senses that fit its strangeness: by vibration, pressure, reflected light, and the sound of water moving where no stream can be seen. They do not make paths so much as briefly reveal the ones already there. They feed, call, burrow, and gather, each at its own scale, while the old stone keeps wearing smooth beneath them.

From far away, the plateau looks empty. Up close, every quiet surface is being used.
