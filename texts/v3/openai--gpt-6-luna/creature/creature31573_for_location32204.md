At first, it seems the little fish are what live here. They gather in the pools by the thousands, pale flanks flashing whenever a lamp trembles across the water. They turn as one body around the mineral knobs, then scatter at a vibration too deep for anything aboveground to hear. In the shallows, blind shrimp sift the grit. Crabs pick their way over soft deposits, feeling ahead with their claws. Eels keep to the channels. You may see only a crease in the water, a dark line slipping between stones; long after it has passed, the ripples continue.

But the basin is fuller than its animals. Every wet surface wears a skin of life: translucent films, thread-fine growths, small mats gathered around a warm leak. They cling where the water brings what they need, and vanish where it does not. In the silt, nearly invisible shoots test their way upward. They do not make a forest. They make tiny holds—greenish places where other small lives can feed, shelter, or wait.

Above them, bats hang in the fissures, folded into the darkness. They occupy the dry heights and return from their flights with the smell of the plain still caught in their fur. Below their roosts, shells and droppings soften the stone into a pale crust. The bats are among the few inhabitants that travel regularly between this buried country and the world overhead. They carry scraps of that world down with them: seeds, insects, the occasional leaf caught in a crease of wing.

Then there are the things that do not move, or move too slowly to be noticed: the mineral skins spreading over mud; the little pillars gathering beneath leaks; the soft floors that rise grain by grain around the pools. They are not alive, but they are not merely scenery. They alter the routes available to everything else. A new ridge divides a channel. A deposit closes a gap where shrimp once passed. A fallen stone makes a quiet pocket where fish can shelter from the current. The inhabitants live among changes they cannot see being made.

The old machines have their own place in this company. A pump turns, pauses, and turns again. Its low, uneven thud travels through water and rock, a sound the fish have learned to follow. They gather in the channels warmed by its work. Crabs find food in the softened mineral growth around its casing. The pump does not know that it shelters them, any more than the water knows it is feeding the pump’s slow corrosion. Its pipes have become passages; its warm housings, reefs. A machine meant to manage the basin has been fitted into the lives it once only interrupted.

Nothing here is tame, though much of it has adjusted to disturbance. The animals know where the floor gives way and where the water is deep enough to hide them. They know the shelter of broken machinery, the warm channels, the ledges above a sudden rise in the pools. They are quick to leave a place when the stone shifts, and quicker still to return when the silt settles. The roaring water is not noise to them. It is a map: a thousand overlapping signs of falling, flowing, feeding, and danger.

There may be no single creature one could point to and call the basin’s keeper. Its life is scattered across scales too different to gather into one body. A fish turns in the dark. A film spreads across wet stone. A pump completes another slow stroke. Water lays down a thin new floor while, somewhere beyond sight, a roof seam opens and begins again.

Together, they inhabit the basin not as owners, but as continuities. Each makes use of what the others leave behind. Each is altered by the water’s patient rearranging. The basin does not watch them. It holds them, feeds them, confines them—and keeps, without hurry, becoming the place they know.
