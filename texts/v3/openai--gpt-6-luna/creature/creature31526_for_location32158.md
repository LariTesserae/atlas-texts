At first they look like people who have made a habit of waiting: listening before they step, testing a plank with one foot, pausing in the middle of a sentence because water has begun to run somewhere it did not run yesterday.

They are not patient by nature. They are patient because impatience wastes things here. A loose board can be lifted and saved; a wall cannot be hurried into standing. Children learn early which puddles are harmless, which ones conceal a sudden drop, and which must be left untouched until the rain has had its say. They can tell the depth of a channel by the sound a pebble makes when it lands. They know the difference between a roof dripping and a roof beginning to fail.

Most of their work is done in company. One person holds a wall upright while another packs mud into its seam; neighbors arrive with poles when a bank begins to sag. Nobody asks who owns the repair. Ownership is a frail thing on ground that can move between supper and morning. The storehouse is raised on stone feet, and its contents are counted carefully, but its steps are swept by everyone. The town’s few dry places are treated almost as public tools.

They are a close people, though not always a gentle one. In the lanes, bodies and tempers meet at narrow angles. Arguments happen quietly, with the speakers leaning close so as not to waste a voice on the damp air. A quarrel may last three days, until one of the quarrellers needs the other to hold a ladder. Then the ladder is held. Apologies, when they come, are often made with a repaired hinge or a basket of kindling left under an eave.

Their children play at building towns. They make little walls from broken brick and reed stalks, then pour water over them and watch to see where it goes. The game has rules, though the rules change with each child’s invention: one child may be the rain, another the bank, another the person who insists the channel must run straight. The smallest children like best to sit with their ears against the paving stones. They say they can hear the water thinking.

The adults tell them not to say that where strangers can hear.

There are few strangers. Those who come by boat stay near the raised storehouse and keep their feet on the stepping boards. They bring metal, salt, and news from firmer places. The town gives them water whose clarity has been judged by several people, and asks after the level of the distant channels. Visitors sometimes mistake the residents’ attention for fear. It is not quite fear. Fear expects a danger to arrive. Here, danger is already underfoot, and most of the time it can be worked around.

People are buried on the highest ridge, where the soil is gravelly and the reeds cannot reach. No marker is trusted to remain upright. Instead, the names of the dead are kept in the mouths of their families, repeated while work is done: over a net being mended, over a pot being scraped clean, over a channel being cleared after rain. The names become part of instruction. This board was set by Nara. That drain was widened by Tovan. Your grandmother knew not to seal the eastern gap.

They all know about the gap.

The stream that appears beneath the eastern wall has no name that anyone agrees on. It comes in a thin run, sometimes after rain, sometimes in a week of clear skies. It crosses the lane and vanishes under the storehouse. The town has tried to follow it with rods and cups, but the water is gone by the time they reach the other side. So they leave the opening clear. A child who once pushed a rag into it found the rag returned the following morning, neatly folded and placed on the storehouse step. The adults said it had washed back. The child said nothing, and never blocked the gap again.

At night, the town becomes more attentive. Doors are latched, but not so tightly that they cannot be opened from outside. Lamps are hooded against the mist. Someone always wakes when the gutters begin to tick. On certain nights a hollow note travels under the houses, rising through the floorboards and fading into the walls. Those who hear it do not call out. They wait to learn whether it is followed by a change in the water.

Perhaps nothing lives beneath them. Perhaps the sound is only air caught in a channel, or the settling of young ground. The people have explanations enough for ordinary things, and they do not waste good certainty on the rest. Still, each morning, someone walks to the eastern gap and looks in.

They are not trying to master the place. They have learned that the place is not waiting to be mastered. They keep it inhabited by noticing: the bank’s fresh cut, the gutter’s altered pitch, the plank that has lifted a finger’s breadth. They mend what can be mended, move what must be moved, and leave room for whatever has not yet decided where to go.

When the water withdraws, they do not cheer. They set out their traps again. When it returns, they lift the floorboards. And beneath the town, in the dark between gravel and silt, something goes on moving—not quickly, not blindly, but with the slow, exact purpose of a resident who has been there longer than anyone can remember.
