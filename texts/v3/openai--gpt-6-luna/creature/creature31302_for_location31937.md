The first things noticed are the small ones, because they are already there before the eye has learned to see the dark.

Crickets stand among the gravel, so pale they seem made from the stone’s dust. Their antennae test the air in slow arcs. At a footstep they do not leap all at once; a tremor passes through them, and the ledge appears to loosen and settle. Beetles shelter in the narrow undersides of fallen slabs, where dampness lasts longest. They emerge singly to feed on scraps too small to name, then vanish beneath the rock again. Nothing here is wasted for long, but much of it is left alone.

The bats occupy the high roofs in dense, uneven colonies. Up close, each is a small folded body, hooked tight to the stone. Their wings, when opened, are almost translucent in the weak light from above. At dusk—which is not a true dusk, but a change in the air and the stirring of one colony after another—they pour from the chambers into passages no wider than their spread wings. They return with the night’s insects clinging to their mouths and fur. Beneath their roosts, the stone is darkened by generations of droppings, and there the hollow grows unexpectedly fertile: threads of fungus, mites, pale larvae, beetles that never travel far from the rich ground. A few steps away, the rock is bare again.

The larger grazers follow those narrow patches of abundance. They are low, broad-backed animals, with blunt muzzles and soft, clouded eyes that do not seem to focus on anything. Their lips move carefully over fungal fans and the thin growths along wet seams. They travel in loose groups, though not in any obvious formation. When one stops to feed, another may pass behind it without alarm. Their coats are the color of old limestone, except where mud and mineral water have stained them. In stillness they resemble heaps of fallen stone. Only the slow working of the jaw gives them away.

The long-limbed scavengers are more difficult to watch. They use the rubble slopes as paths, placing their narrow feet with such care that loose gravel barely shifts. Their faces are thin and furred, their noses constantly moving over the ground. They take what has died, but also the eggs and larvae that can be found beneath a slab, or a cricket caught in a shallow pool. They seldom challenge the grazers. A grazer that turns its head is usually enough to send them on along the wall, where they continue their patient searching. Their calls are small, rasping notes, easily mistaken for stone rubbing against stone.

In the pools, life has a different pace. Blind fish hold in the deepest water, nearly colorless, with mouths that open and close as if they were breathing the whole basin. They gather where a thread of current brings food, but disperse when the water goes still. Salamanders wait in the gravel at the margins. Their bodies are narrow and pale, their gills feathering the water behind their heads. A disturbance brings them briefly to life: a dart, a ripple, then the same suspended patience as before. They eat what crosses close enough. The fish do not seem to notice them until they are touched.

Nothing in the hollow is fully separate. A bat’s flight feeds the ground beneath its roost. The beetles and mites draw other hunters. The grazers browse where the small lives gather, and the scavengers move along the places where the larger animals sleep. Water makes its own small neighborhoods: a wet seam draws fungus, fungus draws grazers, and a pool gathers fish, salamanders, and the insects that fall into it. Between these places lie long reaches of bare floor, crossed by animals going from one necessity to another.

Their sounds give the hollow its scale. Crickets tick in the rubble; wings rasp against the roof; bats make thin calls that pass around bends and return from farther chambers. The grazers breathe audibly when they feed. A scavenger’s claws click once, then are lost in the greater noise. In the quietest galleries, there is always something nearby—a movement under a slab, a faint scrape, the soft stir of air through a colony.

They are numerous without seeming crowded, and familiar with one another without seeming tame. They have learned the same routes and the same limits. When the water recedes, they draw closer to what remains. When it returns, they spread out again. They do not remake the hollow. They occupy it, carefully and by degrees: a gathering beneath one roof, a trail across one slope, a few pale bodies feeding at the edge of a pool. Between those close-lived places, the stone keeps its great emptiness.
