The oldest inhabitant has no body you could point to.

It runs beneath the plain in the joined roots, in the channels pressed into the vegetation mat, in the tiny changes that pass from leaf to leaf and district to district. It is not one mind spread thinly. It is closer to a habit the whole country has acquired: tilt toward the light; brace the weakened span; sort what falls; close the seam. A signal can take days to cross the plain. By then, the leaf that began it may be gone, its message carried on in the material of a wall, or in the pressure of roots against a foundation.

The green country remembers by repeating itself. Its avenues are kept straight not because anything needs to travel them, but because they were straight before. Its machines sort the cuttings in the same order they have always been sorted. When a new growth forms in the wrong place, the nearest ribs lean toward it, measuring its shape. Sometimes they incorporate it. Sometimes, after a long quiet interval, they press it flat and return it to the mat.

The pale creatures in the ventilation shafts are more nearly individual. They are narrow, jointless at a distance, and almost translucent, with small mineral plates along their backs. They move in little groups, though not always together: one may pause while the others pass through a hollow stalk, and then follow after an hour, or a season. They do not eat as an animal eats. They take up the fine powders that collect in the shafts and leave behind a substance that strengthens the inner walls. When a split opens, they find it. One after another they lay themselves across the gap, fitting their bodies into its edges. The plant seals around them. Later, they ease free and continue on, thinner by an amount too slight to see.

They do not have names for one another, perhaps. They recognize a shape of motion: the pause before a turn, the small tremor that means a passage is blocked. When they meet, their bodies touch, and some information passes between them—not a memory, exactly, but a useful alteration. One emerges from the contact carrying a different route through the shafts. Another changes direction without appearing to know why.

Most of the time, the inhabitants are busy without seeming to be at work. A broad fan turns a fraction toward the light. A cable of fiber tightens inside a supporting stem. The mat settles under the weight of an old factory, and a neighboring district redistributes the strain. These are not orders from a ruler. There is no chamber where the country gathers to decide what it will become. The decision is the movement itself, performed by thousands of structures that only know their own portion of it.

Yet there are places where the habit falters.

At a repeated row of leaves, the same scar appears for miles. Beneath that row, roots draw close, testing the impossible sameness. In the galleries, the pale creatures gather and press their backs against the floor, holding still until the false repetition has passed—or until it becomes permanent. A few years later, the row may be gone. Or it may have spread into the surrounding beds, copied so faithfully that there is no longer any way to tell which leaf was the first.

The plant-country does not seem distressed by these errors. It braces for them. Stems bend toward structures that are not yet present. Root-cables fasten around empty foundations. When the future tower finally arrives, its roots find the waiting grip already in place.

There may once have been other inhabitants. The straightness of the avenues, the measured beds, the marks that appear on walls, all suggest hands that planned the plain. Their work is still here in the way a pressed leaf remains inside a resin panel: recognizable, preserved, and no longer separate from what surrounds it. If those makers are gone, the country has not noticed their absence in any way that can be distinguished from its ordinary maintenance.

At night, the moving lamps belong to the shaft-creatures. They carry grains of pale mineral lodged beneath their plates, and the galleries brighten where they pass. Leaves unfold above them, receiving the brief light as if it were the sun. The creatures continue along their old routes, sealing small cracks, clearing powder from the vents, and slipping through spaces too narrow for any larger life.

Above, the green surfaces make their careful adjustments.

No one watches over the plain. Nothing in it is unwatched.
