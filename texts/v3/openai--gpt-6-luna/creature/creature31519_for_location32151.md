The people who live there are not pale, exactly, but seem to have taken on the colors of the place: charcoal wool, rain-dark leather, the muted green of moss in the seams of their coats. They are compact and deliberate in their movements. Even on a broad street they walk as if a ledge might begin without warning. Their boots have ridged soles; their hands know every rail, crack, and familiar notch in the stone.

They do not carry umbrellas. The rain is too fine to bother with, and the cloud comes from below as often as from above. Instead they wear short, hooded coats that shed water and leave their faces free to listen. They speak quietly, not from solemnity but because sound travels strangely through the ravines. A conversation on one street may come back from another as a handful of words, stripped of their meaning.

Their work follows the water. They clear channels, mend iron gutters, tend the wheels and pumps, and watch the stains that seep across the rock. A new dark mark on a wall can tell them where water has found a passage. An old one may mean nothing at all. Children learn the sounds of the settlement before they learn its map: the thin rattle of a wheel beginning to turn, the cable’s complaint when a load is too heavy, the hollow note that means a drain is blocked. They can tell which way a hidden stream is running by putting an ear to the stone.

The older residents leave certain cracks and openings alone. No house is built over them, no pipe laid across them. When the air rises through one, they say the mountain is breathing, and go on with their work. Children are warned not to drop stones into the deep hollows—not because anything will answer, but because the echo may take a long time to come back, and by then you will have forgotten what you asked.

In the wet clefts, small things live that the people rarely name. Pale moths gather under eaves and settle on lampshades like flakes of ash. Dark grazers pick their way along distant shelves, pausing for long stretches with their heads lowered to the wind. Now and then something passes through the ferns below the town: a bending, a shiver that travels against the breeze. People who have seen it describe different shapes. They agree only that it moves with great care, as if it knows the paths better than they do.

The inhabitants are less interested in the mystery than in whether the steps are safe. They maintain the stairways, mark slippery landings with rust-red paint, and leave small lamps burning in covered passages when cloud thickens. At the edge of town, where the old stair disappears, they replace its worn stones one at a time. No one claims to know where it ends. Still, the steps are kept clear.

Sometimes a traveler comes back up from below with mud on their hem and no good account of the journey. Sometimes no one comes back. The residents ask what the weather was like on the lower shelves, whether the water was rising, whether there were lights. They do not ask what the traveler heard in the cloud.

On certain mornings, the thin stream on the stair appears to climb. People passing it slow down, just a little. They watch until the water slips into the mist, then turn toward the settlement, where the wheels wait for rain and the lamps are still burning against the day.
