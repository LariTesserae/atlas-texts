At first, the inhabitants are easier to hear than to find.

A hollow call rings from somewhere above the basin. Another answers from far away, so far that it seems to belong to a different night. Then comes a brief clatter beneath the leaves, and the quiet shape beside the pool shifts its weight. The plateau is not empty. Its life is simply spread thin, hidden in the distance and in the dark.

The largest animals move in small, deliberate herds. Their wide, padded feet test the scree before each step; their coats are almost black, dense with short fibres that hold warmth and droplets alike. Pale lichens and seedpods take root along their shoulders, making each animal a slow-moving patch of the plateau’s own vegetation. They feed on tough fronds and on the mats that grow around water seams. When fog settles, they stand with their heads raised, drinking from the air through the sensitive skin around their mouths. A herd crossing an open basin may take hours to pass. By then, its calls have travelled ahead and returned from the cliffs, so that the animals seem to be moving through a company of themselves.

Above them, the smaller life is quick and difficult to count. Pale, broad-eyed climbers live among the tall stems, their hooked hands made for gripping slick plant-skin. They jump between crowns, or open their thin gliding membranes and let the wind carry them across gaps too wide for any branch. In the mist, they vanish almost at once. What remains is a trembling frond, a few seed husks falling through the dark, and a chorus of sharp clicks from somewhere overhead. They gather in sheltered thickets when the weather turns, pressed close together beneath the layered leaves, their bodies faintly marked by the dim glow along their veins.

There are other voices lower down: little chattering animals that run along roots and under the old fronds, and blind, soft-bodied feeders that turn the cold plant litter over without quite rotting it. In the pools, dark swimmers hold still beneath the surface film. Their mouths open and close against the faint current. When something steps near, they stir all at once, and the water briefly reflects a scatter of moving sparks. Then the pool is black again.

The hunters know where the water gathers. Some lie flat beside the root channels, so still that a coat of moss and damp grit settles over them. Others wait above, folded against the underside of broad leaves. They do not pursue for long. A rush across loose stone wastes too much warmth and makes too much sound. They let the plateau bring prey to them: a herd following a fog bank, a climber returning to its nest, a smaller creature drawn toward a spring. Sometimes the only sign of a hunt is a sudden silence in the calls nearby. Sometimes there is a short, violent noise, and afterward the wind resumes.

All these creatures seem to navigate by more than sight. They read the tremor of stems, the direction of dripping water, the return of a call from a cliff. Their cries are not merely warnings or invitations, but a way of taking the measure of a place. A single animal can call into a basin and learn whether the fog has filled it, whether another body is moving below, whether the far passage is open. In the plant-vaults, where sound rolls and overlaps, the calls change: shorter, closer, threaded with clicks that might be answered from just beyond reach.

The people use those same passages, though they are harder to hear than the animals. They live in small households, tucked beneath overhangs or high among the stems, where a platform can flex rather than break in the wind. Their shelters are low, dark, and carefully fitted to the stone. They sleep in layers of woven plant-fibre, mending old sheets rather than making new ones. A fire is kept only when necessary, shielded beneath a stone hood; most warmth comes from shared bodies, insulated walls, and the slow heat of stored food.

They know the plateau by its sounds. A hollow stalk bell says that a path is still passable. A different note means the roots have shifted or the bridge has taken on water. They can recognize a particular basin by the way their own call returns: quickly from one cliff, muddied by a plant-vault, or not at all where mist has swallowed the far wall. Children learn to listen before they are trusted to cross a shelf alone. Even then, they travel with one hand on a cord or living stem, and stop often to hear what is moving beyond the next ridge.

Their lives are not separate from the other lives around them. They leave seed mash beneath the platforms, and the small climbers come to feed. In return, the climbers’ droppings take seeds into the high cracks where people cannot reach. A household may protect a pool not because it belongs to them, but because the animals arrive there in a certain season and the water must remain clear. A hunter that returns year after year to the same root-channel may be watched, avoided, or quietly displaced with hanging chimes. It is not always driven away. The people know that a predator’s presence keeps other creatures wary, and that its sudden absence can make a place more dangerous.

They do not call the plateau empty when no one is visible. They say it is listening.

At night—or what passes for night here—the people lie beneath the shelter and hear the whole country at work: a herd moving over distant stone, rain pattering along the plant roof, a low groan as roots tighten around a fissure. Somewhere, a bell sounds without a hand to touch it. Farther off, an animal answers. The sounds cross and fade, leaving no clear account of who has passed or where they are going.

By morning, the platform may be wet with fog, the seed mash gone, and a pale trail of prints visible below the shelter. The people gather their tools and follow the weather toward another spring. Above them, the gliders are already moving from crown to crown. Beyond them, the herd continues across the basin, carrying its small gardens through the dark. And under the leaves, beside the water, the patient hunters wait for the plateau to bring the next living thing within reach.
