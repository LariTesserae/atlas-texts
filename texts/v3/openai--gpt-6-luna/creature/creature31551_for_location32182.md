The people who build the little shelters are called the **Listeners**, though they do not use a word that means *people* in the way outsiders expect. To them, a person is a voice that can answer.

They are low-bodied and long-armed, built to move through a world that shifts underfoot. Their broad, padded feet spread their weight across the yielding mats. Fine ridges on their palms and soles let them feel tremors through stalk, stone, and root: the distant thud of a falling pillar, the fine shiver of a seed-knot waking, the uneven pulse that warns of a soft place in the floor. They walk with care, but not silence. A Listener’s steps are a kind of speech.

Their faces are small beneath high brows, with narrow nostrils fringed in filtering hairs. Their eyes are dark and reflective, useful in the broken, many-directioned light. Along the throat lies a thin blue membrane, usually folded close. When they call, it opens and flashes—not bright enough to illuminate anything, but bright enough for another Listener to answer from the far side of a thicket. Their voices are mostly whistles and low, rough notes. For finer messages, they tap their knuckles against hollow stems, sending patterns into the living weave.

They are not masters of the growth. They have learned that mastery is a brief condition, and usually a dangerous one. A wall can be trained to hold a roof; it cannot be trusted to remain a wall. So their shelters are made to loosen. Roofs are tied on with loops that can be cut in a moment. Walkways have give in them. Ladders are grown around, not nailed into place. When the stems begin to thicken or the floor starts to rise, the inhabitants move their homes rather than argue with the ground.

A settlement is less a village than a temporary agreement. The Listeners bend thick stalks into arches and bind them with cured fiber. They shut seed-pods against pests, fence off edible growths, and mark useful routes with resin too pungent for most animals to tolerate. The marks fade quickly. Paths are remembered by the people who travel them, and by the patterns of vibration beneath them. A child learns early to stop when the floor hums in the wrong rhythm.

They eat what can be gathered without provoking too much growth: small jointed animals, the pale flesh inside certain mineral-shelled grazers, the dense knots at the ends of cured stalks. Food is chewed slowly, with little wasted. Water is precious, though it rarely appears as water. They draw it from their food and from a few resinous plants, which they tap with bone spouts and leave to seal behind them. A Listener drinks in small, deliberate swallows, then presses the vessel closed.

The young are raised in the busiest parts of a shelter, where there is always sound. They learn the local chorus before they learn the names of things: the clatter of grazing, the dry click that means a shell-creature is near, the hollow groan of a stem under strain. Older children are sent with adults to listen at the edge of a new growth. They sit with their hands against the floor until they can distinguish the ordinary noise of the place from the beginning of something.

There is little privacy. A Listener’s moods show in the throat membrane, in the pace of their taps, in the way they set their feet. Yet they have quiet customs. They cover the blue membranes of the dead with soft fiber so that the last light does not travel on. The bodies are placed in the root-mats, not buried so much as returned to the woven dark. In time, the fibers take what they can use. The living avoid that place for several days—not from fear, but from courtesy.

They do not think of themselves as inhabitants of the hollow alone. They speak of the ground as something that is listening back, though they know it has no ears. When a heavy footfall sends a tremor through the connected stalks, they pause and wait. Sometimes nothing follows. Sometimes, far away, a bed of seed-knots bursts open, and the whole place answers with new growth. The Listeners feel the first shiver through their soles.

Then they gather their tools, loosen the nearest ties, and make room.
