The first things to notice are the little ones.

They are pale as the inside of a shell, narrow-bodied, and quick enough to seem like a flicker in the corner of the eye. They live in the wet seams and under the mats of hanging fronds, where they pick at the soft growth and the fine mineral dust gathered in it. Their feet leave pinprick tracks around the hollows. When the blue-green veins brighten, the little things stop feeding and press themselves flat to the stone. They know the light is a message, though perhaps not what it says.

The long-legged grazers are easier to see. They stand on shelves that look too narrow to hold them, toes splayed, bodies held very still. Their coats take the color of wet rock; their eyes are dark and wide-set, made for noticing the smallest change in cloud or shadow. They feed on the pale jointed plants, but not at random. A herd will leave one cleft untouched for years, then return to it all at once, as if answering a season no other creature can read.

They do not flee from the broad-winged hunters. They fold themselves down and wait. The hunters glide the ravines with wings stiff as sails, turning on the slow currents that rise from the cracks. Their undersides are almost colorless. From above, they resemble scraps of cloud caught below the peaks. They take the small ones when they can, but they are not always hunting. Often they circle a lit seam, waiting for the warmth or vapor that comes before a plume. When the rock exhales, they ride it upward and vanish into the low ceiling.

The plants are not passive, either. The dark fronds open to mist and close against frost. Their dangling roots comb the air for water, and their slick surfaces carry a faint charge from leaf to leaf. In the dark, sparks pass through them: brief, greenish points that leap along the edge of an overhang and disappear into the stone. The pale reedlike growths answer more slowly, bending toward a vein that has begun to glow.

And beneath all of them—under the shelves, down through the towers and into the hidden depths—something lives that has no single body.

It is not a creature in the ordinary sense. It has no head, no mouth, no place where it can be found whole. Its nerves may be the blue-green seams in the rock; its skin, the broad plates of the plateau; its slow breath, the vapor that escapes a cliff after pressure has gathered below. The plants conduct its faint charges. The animals feel its tremors through their feet. Perhaps it feels them in return.

It does not speak. When a gust crosses the shelf and the mineral veins flare in sequence, the living hollows open their fronds. When the light fades, the grazers descend, the small pale things return to the cracks, and the hunters settle on high ledges with their wings furled. This may be instinct, or it may be an answer passing through a body too large for any of its inhabitants to comprehend.

The high country is not empty, and it is not quite tame. Its residents have learned the patience of stone. They drink when the mist comes, shelter when the wind turns, and keep still when the deep tremor travels beneath them. For generations beyond counting, they have lived among the ranks of towers and shining seams, in the shelter of a vast, dark process.

Whether that process is only the earth at work, or whether the earth has become something more, none of them can tell.

They live as though it matters.
