What lives there is not one thing, though at first it might be mistaken for one.

It begins with the leaves: broad, dark, and overlapping around the hollows where water lingers. Their waxy surfaces hold the little that falls from the pipes. Beneath them, pale roots thread through grit and into the new cracks, following dampness without hurry. They do not seek to cover the chamber. They keep to the wet seams and warm fissures, leaving the bare stone bare.

But the roots have found the machinery, too. One has slipped into the split lip of the pump casing. Another follows a pipe down into the wall, where the heat rises in slow pulses. At the joints, condensation gathers; beneath them, the leaves wait. Each drop darkens the soil a little, and the roots make use of what remains.

Over years, the plants have learned the shape of the chamber. They bend away from the hottest places, then lean back when the heat eases. They close over hollows that keep water and grow sparse where the salt shines. Their pale stalks climb the pipes, though the tips dry and curl. Perhaps the stalks are reaching for something. Perhaps they are only following the warmth.

The machinery has not joined them, exactly. Its dead lamps still hold their glass eyes open. The pump casing gathers a skin of moisture. The gauge trembles when the pipes change temperature, and, in time, a root has grown close enough to feel each faint shiver. Nothing turns; still, the old apparatus alters the plants’ small world, making one wall warmer, one crack wetter, one patch of darkness faintly green.

Together they give the impression of a single resident: something spread thinly through stone, water, leaf, and rust. Not awake in any human sense, perhaps. Not waiting for a visitor. It answers only to what the chamber offers—heat, seepage, pressure, the occasional fresh fracture—and answers at the pace of roots.

If it has a shape, it is the chamber’s slowest shape: a dark fan of leaves around a pool, a fine white thread disappearing into a seam, a bead of water swelling under a pipe joint. It is there in the pause after the drop falls, in the almost imperceptible leaning of a stem toward warmth.

Then nothing moves for a long while.

And the chamber keeps changing.
