At first, there seems to be no one in particular. The life is too well distributed to gather into a crowd: pale flecks on stone, glassy backs moving through the low meadows, fins stirring beneath the curtains. The shelf appears to be inhabited by its own breathing.

Then the flecks move together.

They are small, flat animals, scarcely thicker than the rock they cling to. Each is the color of the dim light above, with a fringe of fine feelers that combs the stone for the dust ground loose by the seed-pods. In a current, they let go and glide, almost invisible. When danger passes, a whole ledge can empty in a ripple, the animals slipping into cracks so narrow they seem to vanish into the rock. They return when the water is quiet. The stone resumes its faint, living shimmer.

Out in the meadows, the glass-bodied grazers travel in loose files. Their insides show through their clear skins: slow contractions, branching vessels, a little cloud of food moving through each transparent body. They feed without stripping the plants, taking only what the fronds continually shed. The files cross one another and part without alarm. There is no leader, no destination apparent, only routes worn by repetition through a landscape that has held still around them for ages.

Under the forests hang the larger animals. They have broad, soft fins and bodies shaped to hold against the plant-made currents. Some remain beneath a single canopy for years, rocking gently in its flow, their mouths open to sieve the drifting life. Others move between forests in long, silent passages. Their fins brush the fronds, and the fronds bend around them without breaking. When one of the great stalks pulses, these animals tilt their bodies to the answering rhythm. They know the machinery by touch.

The animals that hunt them are seldom seen whole. A forest edge darkens. A line of grazers folds suddenly inward. The hanging feeders turn their pale undersides to the current and disappear among the stalks. Far off, something long and dark passes between two provinces of growth, so slowly that for a moment it is mistaken for a change in the water. Then the bass roar closes behind it, and the forest is forest again.

But there are larger inhabitants than these, though they are easy to mistake for part of the machinery.

They cling high on the trunks, where the currents divide. Their bodies are broad and flattened, with branching limbs held against the stalk like a second arrangement of fronds. Along their flanks lie rows of sensitive pits. They do not feed on the plants, nor quite shelter beneath them. They listen to them. Every thrum passing through a stalk travels across their bodies; every change in the pressure of the water touches their skin before it touches the leaves.

They may remain in one place for most of their lives. A young one, once grown, leaves the parent’s trunk and drifts until it finds a vacant station in the forest. There it grips the living wood and begins its long attendance. The old ones are scarred with pale lines where fronds have rubbed them. Some have grown around the stalk’s contours so completely that the two seem joined. When one dies, the currents carry it down into the woven litter, where its bones become another hard, quiet part of the floor.

They speak in clicks, but not often. A short series of knocks can mean that a seed case has opened nearby; a longer, low vibration can travel through a whole stand and be felt rather than heard. The young learn the local pattern as they settle into it. They learn which pulses belong to the great bladders filling and emptying, which mark the strain of a frond, which are made by a passing animal. They learn the difference between the usual and the dangerous, though danger may not come for many years.

No one among them knows the entire shelf. A forest has its own voice, and the open tracts between forests are broad silences filled with scattered signals. But messages pass along the stalks, through the rock, and from one listening body to another. What they share is not a story of how the world began or where it is going. It is an unbroken account of what is happening now: the current holding; the leaves bending; the grazers crossing the western meadow; the deep water beyond the rim, cold and immeasurable.

Their young receive no inheritance of possessions. They receive a place in the chord.

So the living pattern persists—not because nothing is born or lost, but because every vacancy has always been answered. A body drifts down, another settles into its station. A frond fails, another takes the current. Among the forests, the listeners remain, feeling the great living works turn beneath their limbs and sounding, now and then, a small note into the vast and unchanging roar.
