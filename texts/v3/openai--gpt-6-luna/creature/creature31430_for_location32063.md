The people of the high country are not shaped like pioneers. They are shaped like people who expect to stay.

They live in cities that are small only from a distance. Inside, the terraces turn inward against the cold, with shared kitchens, warm courtyards, workshops, schools, and long windows looking out over the pale land. Most residents know the weather by its effects before they see it: a change in the pressure of the doors, a fine tremor through the floor, the taste of water caught in the air. They dress in layers of soft, close-fitting fabric, with outer shells made to shed rain and dust. Their boots have broad, flexible soles. On the rare days when the stone is warm beneath the feet, children run outside without them.

The ground is always present in their thoughts, though not always in their conversation. A wall may be old; its footing is not. A street can keep its name after being moved a little to the east. Residents learn to distinguish the ordinary adjustments of their homes from the deeper, slower strain that means a district must be inspected. The city’s instruments make most of these decisions for them, but people still pause when they feel a vibration pass through a railing or hear the distant groan of the shelves. It is not fear, exactly. More like attending when someone else has entered the room.

They have a quiet affection for maintenance. Children learn early how to read a water gauge, how to tell lichen from mineral staining, why one marsh channel must be left open even when it seems to be doing nothing. At school they study the land as something unfinished: its rock, its weather, the lives that have taken hold in it, the limits of what a foundation can ask. Later, many will work with the survey crews, tending the transit lines, caring for the marshes, or revising the city’s plans as the plateau shifts under them. Others stay indoors and make music, cook, teach, repair small machines, argue over ordinary things. The high country does not make every life solemn. It makes solemnity one of the available tools.

Their gatherings are not usually loud. A city can be full of people and still keep its hush: conversation absorbed by insulated rooms, transit murmuring below, rain ticking against the outer skins of the buildings. Yet in the common terraces, voices overlap warmly. People meet there after work to eat food grown under glass or in the sheltered warm rooms; to trade stories about a fracture found, a new pool formed, a herd seen beyond the west causeway. News of the land travels with the same ease as news of one another. A newly opened seep may matter to the whole city. So may a baby’s first word.

The young are not kept away from the open country, but they are taught how to enter it. They go out in groups with guides, crossing the causeways and then leaving them where the ground is safe. They learn the tracks of grazers and scavengers, the places where crust is thin, the difference between a harmless warm seam and one that is still shifting. The first time a child sees a herd moving across the plateau, the animals may be only dark lines against the brightness. Then the lines separate into bodies, long-legged and alert, and the child understands how much distance can be alive.

The animals make their own arrangements with the cities. Grazers pass at a distance, sometimes in lines so extended that the first have vanished into weather before the last emerge from it. The hunters ride the fronts above them, broad wings set still against the wind. In the marshes, small creatures stir under reeds and black water. People notice these lives in fragments: a flicker beneath a boardwalk, tracks at the edge of a pool, a pale scavenger standing very still in the dawn. They do not always see an animal clearly. They do not need to. The country’s life is most evident in the way the empty ground is never quite empty.

The residents preserve corridors for that life, and keep the margins of their cities open. This is partly practical. The land needs room to move, the animals need room to pass, and no one trusts a settlement that has confused closeness with safety. But the open intervals have become beautiful to them, too. People will take a long walk to watch rain cross an unbuilt basin. They will stand on a terrace after dark, looking toward the faint moving lights of survey instruments, and feel no need to put anything in the distance.

They are not untouched by the plateau’s remaking. Sometimes an old neighborhood is dismantled, its materials carried to a more stable edge. Sometimes the city must close a causeway for months while the ground beneath it settles. People mourn these changes even when they agree with them. A home can be rebuilt; a place in memory does not always move as easily. Still, the residents have learned to make continuity out of things other than permanence: a route walked with a friend, a recipe handed down, the names given to pools that migrate, the familiar voice of a neighbor calling from across a courtyard.

In clear weather, they seem almost delicate against the land: figures crossing pale stone, windows lit above a shallow marsh, a string of travelers moving along a causeway. In bad weather, they draw close to one another. They wait indoors, listen to the rain, and let the instruments speak for the buried rock. Then, when the cloud passes, they go out again—not to claim the ground, but to find out what it has become.
