What lives there is not a crowd but a continuous, slow-growing presence.

The fronds occupy the cracks first. Their narrow blades stream with the current, bending around stone rather than resisting it. Where the rock offers shelter, moss gathers into dark pads, and pale root-fans spread through the sediment, holding fast to grains that would otherwise be carried away. In hollows where seepage warms the water by a fraction, stalks rise in close ranks. Their folded leaves open and close as the current changes—not as a response to light, but to the slow exchange of minerals and dissolved chemicals passing over them.

Nothing here grows quickly enough to claim a ledge in a season. A stand thickens across centuries. A pale fan reaches over a patch of bare sediment, then another; its fine threads bind the grains together until the plant and the slope are difficult to tell apart. When a frond breaks, it is combed into a hollow and settles there. It softens, darkens, and joins the sediment, while new growth takes hold above it. The oldest slopes have become layered archives of growth and decay, their surfaces smooth with age and furred with living tissue.

At the mineral chimneys, life gathers in subtle gradients. The seep is warm; the surrounding water is cold. Dissolved substances meet and change in the narrow space between. A close-knit film spreads over the blunted stone, almost invisible until it gathers into a faint sheen. The taller plants root into that film, or into the mineral crusts it helps form. Whatever nourishment passes among them is exchanged quietly, through water and contact, with no need for pursuit or capture.

Their spores are the nearest thing the plateau has to weather. Released in pale clouds, they rise until the currents catch them beneath an overhang. There they hang for a while, like dust in a dark room, drifting apart as the water folds and turns. Most are carried into the open basin. Some settle in a crack or upon a ledge, where they may take hold if the stone, seepage, and current happen to suit them. The plateau does not seem to mourn the ones lost. It has always produced more.

There are no eyes watching from among the leaves, no mouths opening in the sediment. No creature passes through the thickets. The plants do not hide from the grinding ice or turn toward the distant pallor overhead. Their lives are measured instead by pressure, chemistry, and the slow rearrangement of stone. A current combs a stand one way; later it combs it another. A ridge groans. A shelf is coated in new growth. Change arrives without announcement, and the living answer by continuing.

The small stone constructions remain among them, not quite abandoned by the landscape. Roots have threaded their openings; dark growth softens the joints between their stones. Mineral-furred cord still clings to a peg here and there, but nothing tends it. The plants have not entered the shelters with purpose. They simply grow where conditions allow, and the old walls happen to provide ledges, cracks, and pockets where spores can settle.

So the plateau’s inhabitants do not occupy it so much as become it. They blur the boundaries between shelf and thicket, old growth and new sediment, living surface and stone. They make no calls to answer the roar in the channels. They have no need to announce themselves. In the dark, through pressure and seepage and the patient work of roots, they keep making more of what they are.
