The first inhabitants you notice are the ones mistaken for chips of ceramic.

They lie in the wet seams, pale and folded, their backs smooth as river stones. When the rain has passed and the Reach is quiet, they uncurl. Too many fine legs carry them over the mortar, pausing at each joint as if reading it. They test the softness of fresh clay with their mouthparts, then vanish into cracks too narrow to admit a finger. Sometimes they emerge carrying grains of ash, which they deposit in a line beside the water. The line may be gone by morning. Or it may have become the lip of a tiny new channel.

They are called the **setters**, by the few who have seen enough to name them. No one can tell whether they make the Reach, or whether the Reach makes them. They do not build in the manner of creatures with plans. They thicken a weak wall. They fill a hollow that holds water too long. They press a loose plate back into place with their clustered bodies, and by the time they have gone, the plate sits differently than before. Their work is so small it seems accidental—until you return and find the passage changed.

The larger inhabitants are never seen whole. They live in the covered drains, where their passage travels ahead of them as a tremor under the tiles. A few quick knocks, a pause, then more knocks farther off. They move slabs, perhaps, or drag something through the dark. Once, in a low culvert, a witness saw a pale, jointed limb withdraw around a bend. It was broad as a shovel and ended in three narrow fingers, each tipped with clay. The witness later found those fingerprints on a new wall.

The Reach has other occupants, too, though it is hard to say whether they are inhabitants or merely places where the inhabitants have gathered. In some recesses the darkness seems thickened, packed into the corner like damp material. It loosens when approached. In some walls, a sound resembling a murmur passes from one side to the other, never forming words. The setters grow still when it happens. Then, after a little while, they begin moving again.

Nothing suggests that the inhabitants need rooms. They do not furnish the courts, keep stores, or leave recognizable nests. Instead, they occupy the seams: between one layer and the next, beneath the lip of a cistern, in the cool thickness behind a blocked doorway. A space may seem empty for years and then, after a rain, reveal dozens of tiny backs shining in its cracks. There are places where the walls click all night, as if something inside them were shifting its weight.

The builders—if builders they are—have no evident face for the work they do. They do not seem to be making a city, a fortress, or even a home. They make room for water, then narrow it. They raise a ledge and bury it under another course. They open a passage that leads nowhere and seal a door that must once have led somewhere. Their labor continues with the patient uncertainty of a hand feeling its way through darkness.

Perhaps the Reach is their body, built outward in layers. Perhaps they are only the living matter in its joints, the small movements by which a vast thing repairs and alters itself. If you keep still long enough, you may hear them beneath your feet: a soft, busy ticking, interrupted by the heavier knocks in the drains. Then a drop of water falls through the dark, and somewhere close by, though you cannot see it, a wall gives a quiet settling groan.
