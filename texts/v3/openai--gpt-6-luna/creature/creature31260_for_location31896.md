The people are as closely fitted to the basin as the paths are. They are not a separate thing laid over the marsh, but its most talkative inhabitants: moving between the birds and the water, mending what grows loose, making room for what will not stay mended.

They tend to be sure-footed rather than swift. Children learn early which stones rock, which planks bow, and where a bank looks solid but will take a boot to the ankle. They go barefoot in warm weather, feeling for the firm places; in cold rain they wear soft boots with broad soles, and gaiters woven from rush fiber. Their clothes are layered and patched, made to shed water without becoming too heavy to move in. Sleeves are often tied back. Hair is braided or bound away from the face, though loose strands are forever catching on willow twigs. Many carry a small knife, a hook, or a length of cord. A person without a tool is not helpless, but is likely to borrow one before doing anything useful.

Their hands show what they do. Fingers are nicked from cutting stems and roughened by rope; palms are strong from hauling sod, lifting stone, and pulling wet planks into place. They know the feel of a living branch that will bend without splitting, and of a bank that is about to give. They can tell when a channel is running too fast by the sound under a walkway. They can tell rain is coming not only from the sky, but from the way frogs stop calling in one corner and begin in another.

Most work is done in company, often without anyone arranging it. A slipped stone draws two or three people from nearby houses. Someone trimming a hedge passes useful cuttings down the path. If a spillway needs clearing, the work gathers around it: one person lifting leaves with a forked stick, another carrying them away, a child sent to warn the lower houses. They argue about how much to cut, where a channel ought to turn, and whether a walkway has another season in it. They argue while working, and the argument usually ends when the thing holds.

The town has little patience for grand plans. A new house is set close to the ground and built with its likely damage already in mind: the footings can be raised, the wall opened for a channel, the roof stripped and re-thatched. Families keep spare bundles of roof grass and stacks of straight poles under cover. Nobody thinks of these as reserves against catastrophe. They are simply what a sensible household keeps, like bowls or dry socks.

Children roam widely, though never quite unwatched. They learn the names of birds before they learn all the names of streets, and can distinguish the call that means a nest has been disturbed from the call that means a hawk is passing over the rim. They are sent to fetch things through the town and come back with what was asked for, plus a frog, a snail, or some piece of news gathered beneath a walkway. They are scolded for damming channels and praised for noticing where the water is already trying to go.

Animals are not pets in the way outsiders might mean it, though some birds nest close enough to be familiar, and some small mammals are allowed to share the warmth beneath storehouses. There are boundaries. Fish belong to the pools and runnels until the appointed catch; nesting shrubs are left alone in their season; a hungry bird may be chased from the drying racks but not from its roost. People are protective of the abundance, but not sentimental about it. A snail can be moved from a young shoot. A rat in the grain loft must be caught. A nest in the wrong place may have to be relocated, if it can be done without harm. Living together is not the same as letting everything do as it pleases.

The residents are used to being interrupted. A conversation pauses for a burst of wings. A meal waits while someone checks the channel below the door. A group crossing the main walkway parts around a frog without comment, then closes together again. Their attention seems scattered until something changes; then it becomes remarkably exact. A new sound in the water, a patch of leaves bent the wrong way, a bird suddenly silent—each may bring several faces turned toward it. They have learned that the basin gives warning, though never in a language anyone can afford to misunderstand.

They have their quieter customs, too. In the evenings, people sit on the raised platforms where clothes, baskets, and tools can dry. They mend nets and baskets while speaking across the close-set roofs. The damp air carries voices oddly, and a remark made in one courtyard may reach three others before its speaker realizes. Songs are often short, because there is so much sound around them already. A tune passed from house to house may be taken up by birds, drowned by runoff, or finished by someone out of sight. No one minds much which.

Their sense of belonging is particular to this small ground. They know the basin’s moods: which side softens first in a wet spring, which roof sheds snow cleanly, which runnel will carry fish after a storm. They speak of places by what happens there—a bank that holds, a bridge that shivers, the steps where the water always gathers—rather than by any distant measure. The town is small enough for everyone to know the sound of a familiar gate, but crowded enough that people can still find privacy by taking the long way home.

They are not at peace with the place so much as in ongoing conversation with it. The water takes a path; they split it into three. The grass grows through a joint; they cut it back, then leave enough to bind the stones. A roof sags; its owners prop it, and their neighbors come to help. Nothing is conquered for long. Nothing is entirely lost, either. The people live by noticing, yielding a little, and returning to the same work with one more hand than before.
