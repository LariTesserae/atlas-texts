The people of the Sluice Garden are good at noticing what has changed by a finger’s width.

They notice when a channel begins to run cloudy, when a belt makes a new sound, when the willow roots have raised a paving stone enough to catch a wheel. They are not solemn about this knowledge. It is simply useful, like knowing which stair is slippery or which neighbor keeps a spare kettle. In the workshops, they mend motors and sort metal; at home, they bind bean stems to lengths of salvaged pipe and set bowls beneath the eaves to catch clean rain. Their clothes carry the town’s weather in small signs: damp cuffs, green smudges on the knees, iron-dark fingerprints that never quite wash out.

No one person tends the sluice. It is looked after by whoever passes it at the right moment. A worker clears leaves from its teeth on the way to the engine house; a child fishes a fallen stick from the channel with a hooked length of wire; an old woman checks the level before she opens her garden gate. The sluice is not grand enough to inspire ceremony, but everything in the basin takes some account of it. People pause to listen when its water changes pitch.

They have made habits with the animals, though they would not call them agreements. A fox may cross the edge of the yard at dusk without being chased, provided it leaves the hens alone. Rats are tolerated near the warm pipes and driven from the flour store. The swallows are welcome under the factory eaves, where they consume insects and leave their nests undisturbed. Children know which pools contain frogs and which are only runoff, and can distinguish the engine’s ordinary clatter from the knock that means someone should fetch a mechanic.

They are not all alike, and the town does not turn them into a single, patient family. Some would pave every weed-filled yard if they could afford it. Some resent the work of clearing channels and insist that the roots are doing more good than harm. A few leave for higher, drier places and come back with stories of broad streets where rain has nowhere to go. The ones who stay tend to speak of the basin as if it were both a workplace and a relative: not kind, exactly, but familiar enough that you learn its moods.

At night the people withdraw behind patched glass, and the Garden does not grow quiet. The machinery clicks as it cools; water threads through the grates; wings stir in the roof gaps. From time to time a light appears in a workshop window, and someone steps out to check a sound, a flow, a small trouble that may not have been trouble at all. Then the door closes, and the basin keeps listening to itself.
