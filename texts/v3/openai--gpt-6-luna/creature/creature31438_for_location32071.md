At first, the plateau seems to be inhabited by the herds.

They cross the basins in loose, quiet bands, their long legs carrying them from one seam of grass to the next. Each animal knows the hidden water by scent, or by some small pressure in the ground that the rest of the world cannot feel. At dusk they gather where the stone sweats. They drink from hollows too shallow to hold a reflection, then stand for a while with their heads raised, listening to the dark.

The little creatures in the cracks are harder to count. They have bright eyes and narrow, folded bodies, and they can flatten themselves against a ledge until they seem no more alive than a shadow. They emerge after rain to gather seeds and pale insects, then vanish at the first change in the wind. In the morning, their tracks make delicate maps across the dust: loops around old pillars, straight lines to seep-holes, sudden stops where the ground has opened beneath them.

The great birds feed on what the plateau gives up. They circle in the heat, sometimes so high they appear fixed to the sky, and come down only when a herd has left something behind. Their roosts are hidden among the upper ridges. Before settling, they fold their wings and hang for a moment in the air, as if the last lift of the day has not yet let them go.

But there are other inhabitants, less easily mistaken for animals.

They live where the old works meet the stone. At dawn, when beads of water shine beneath the ledges, they come out of narrow openings in the ridges—slow, low beings with jointed limbs and bodies the color of weathered cream rock. Their backs are ridged like the edges of broken shells. They do not hurry, and they rarely travel far from the dark towers or the shallow channels cut into the ground. From a distance, one might take them for a fallen stone being turned over by the wind.

They are listeners. They press their forelimbs to the ground and feel the small movements beneath it: water shifting through the rock, the far-off weight of a herd, the long, low trembling that sometimes travels from ridge to ridge. When that deeper vibration comes, they stop whatever they are doing. The herds stop too. Even the birds seem to draw their circling wider. For a little while, the whole plateau attends.

The listeners do not appear to command the old works. They do not enter the towers, and no one has seen them open a door. Yet they visit the straight cuts and the dark stones with the regularity of creatures tending a garden. One will crouch beside a channel and lay its long fingers in the dust. Another will stand beneath a tower’s shadow, turning its head as the shadow divides in two. Afterward, a basin may hold water a little longer, or a scatter of loose stones may rise and settle again in a different arrangement.

Perhaps they maintain the works. Perhaps the works are maintaining them.

They keep no visible fires and build no walls. Their shelters are hollows in the lee of ridges, lined with dry grass and flakes of dark stone. They sleep through the harshest part of the day, waking when the evening damp begins to gather. Then they move between the towers in small groups, never touching, but keeping one another in sight.

The ring of pale pillars draws them most often. After rain, when the water inside it lies smooth and unbroken, the listeners gather along its edge. They watch the other sky moving across the pillars’ surfaces. Whether they recognize that landscape as somewhere else, or as something that has not happened yet, is not known. They do not gesture toward it. They only remain until the water thins and the reflection is gone.

At night, the plateau belongs to smaller lives: insects tapping against stone, grasses stirring, water ticking far below the surface. The listeners lie still among the ridges. The herds bed down nearby, close enough to share the faint warmth released by the old stones. Above them, the scavengers fold their wings.

Nothing living here seems to mistake silence for emptiness. Each creature leaves room for the next sound, the next brief rain, the next tremor traveling underfoot. And when the plateau gives its low, glassy note, all of them pause—until the note passes on into the farther ridges and the land is quiet again.
