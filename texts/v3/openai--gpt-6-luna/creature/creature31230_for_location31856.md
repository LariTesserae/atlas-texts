At first, the room’s inhabitants seem to be no more than the motions the eye invents in a bright, empty place.

Then the pale mites resolve: little commas of life, crossing the grit in routes that bend around dampness, light, and one another. They carry nothing anyone can see. Still, they pause at certain grains of sediment and touch them with their heads, as if checking that the world remains where they left it. Their scattered lines are not quite trails. They are more like decisions made visible for a moment.

In the seams, smaller creatures move beneath the stone. Their many legs pass through the mineral as through clear water, though no wetness follows them. They gather where a crack narrows, press their bodies together, then disperse along different veins. Perhaps they are feeding. Perhaps they are teaching the rock how to split. When someone looks straight at them, their legs lose count; when attention slips, each one is whole again, intent on its passage.

The round animal beneath the ceiling is harder to dismiss. It hangs without a foot, mouth, or face, a soft pale weight with the patience of a drop that has not yet fallen. Every so often it draws inward. The room brightens—not sharply, but with the clean, attentive glow of a mind waking. Then it relaxes, and the wet stone returns to its ordinary brightness. The animal seems to have no need to move. Its contraction is movement enough.

Near the water, the winged specks keep a little weather of their own. They hover in a space where the air does not stir. Their wings are nearly invisible, but sometimes a thin glimmer travels along them, like light passing through a crack in ice. One settles on the rim of a pool. It is a small insect, delicately jointed, its wings folded. Blink, and the insect is gone. What remains is not an empty patch of stone, exactly, but the certainty that something had meant to land there. A moment later the certainty lifts and flutters away.

The three colorless threads and the tuft of translucent fronds belong to the room in a quieter way. They take nothing obvious from the soil. They do not lean toward the light. Their reflections make slow, liquid gestures after the plants themselves have stopped moving, as if a second, softer growth lives in the water and is still catching up. Now and then a mite follows one of these reflected gestures, reaching the edge of a pool and turning back without touching it.

And then there is the dark, slender animal that passes through the floor.

It is almost never seen directly. A slight displacement travels beneath the raw stone; a seam goes briefly black; the grit seems to remember a shape passing under it. The mites react to its reflection, scattering before the animal arrives. Their flight leaves a narrow cleared path, which remains for a little while after both animal and reflection are gone. Nothing pursues anything. The dark creature does not appear to hunt. It moves with the solemnity of a thought crossing from one part of the room to another.

The inhabitants have begun to leave signs. A half-ring in damp grit, repeatedly made and repeatedly softened. A little stone balanced upright in a cleft too narrow to admit a hand. Shallow marks that travel purposefully across the floor and end at a wall. The mites pass through the marks but do not erase them. The seam-creatures gather beneath the upright stone. Once, the round animal contracts as the little stone trembles, and for a breath the whole room seems to be listening.

No one can say whether these are homes, signals, games, or accidents with a longer memory than usual. The animals have not yet made a distinction between doing something and leaving evidence that something was done.

They are not afraid of visitors. They are disturbed, perhaps, by the force of being noticed. Under a direct gaze they thin to suggestion. At the edge of attention they thicken into bodies, each busy with its small and particular errand: following moisture, holding a place open, testing a crack, waiting for a reflection to arrive.

Sometimes, from beyond the rock, comes a breathlike sound. The mites stop. The hidden things in the seams go still. The winged specks hang without wings. For one short interval, even the fronds’ delayed reflections are motionless.

Then a drop touches stone, or rises back into its crack, and the lives of the room resume.

They are not yet settled into a world. They are helping settle it. With every crossing, pause, contraction, and vanishing, they make the little cavity more habitable—not larger, but more completely itself. The room holds them as a cupped hand holds water; they hold the room in return, keeping its bright, unfinished shape from dissolving into all the other shapes it might have been.
