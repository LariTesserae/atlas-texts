The Sill is inhabited by the seam-kin.

That is what they are called by the few who have stayed long enough to notice them, though it is not clear whether the name describes a people or a way the place behaves. No one has seen a seam-kin standing in the open. They are encountered as small departures from expectation: a bead of water that turns toward a footstep; a dry patch of air that travels slowly across a wet cliff; the pale dart beneath the ledge, returning each day to a different crack.

If they have bodies, they are made for narrow places. In the warm seams, something fine and pale sometimes presses outward, no larger than a hand. It may be a folded creature, or a growth of mineral membrane, with delicate divisions that open and close like gills. It does not emerge so much as become briefly distinguishable from the stone. When the rock cools, it withdraws. When the spring changes course, it appears again farther along, already waiting.

The seam-kin move water. They do not carry it; they persuade it. A trickle pauses at a fork, then takes the path that had been dry for years. A pool draws itself into a bead and hangs in the air, not falling until a little white shape has passed beneath it. Steam gathers under the black arch and returns as warm rain. These are their gestures—slow, exact, and easy to mistake for weather.

The old constructions belong to them, or else the seam-kin belong to the constructions. The arch’s pinprick holes are sometimes dark, sometimes bright with a light no stronger than a wet stone’s shine. The post in the pool holds its distance from the water because something beneath it maintains the gap. At the metal frame, the seam-kin keep a small piece of air dry. They seem to use it as a passage, though nothing visibly passes through. A snail may enter the rectangle and fail to come out on the same side. A person looking away for only a moment may find the cliff much nearer.

They have no need to conceal themselves from visitors. They simply do not arrange the world for a visitor’s understanding. If you speak, the place does not answer. If you stand quietly, the low vibration may stop; then, after a while, one of the springs will begin to run uphill. This is not a greeting, exactly. It is closer to the Sill making room for the fact of you.

The smaller life belongs to them without being owned. The snails graze along the warm seams, leaving bright tracks that the seam-kin do not erase. The red flowers open where vapor gathers. The silver grass bends when there is no wind because some fine pressure has passed through it. The white dart beneath the ledge may be an animal in its own right. It may also be one of the seam-kin’s briefest bodies: a thought made mobile enough to cross bare stone.

Nothing hunts there. The seam-kin have no obvious mouths, and no sign of a hunger that would make sense to an animal. What they want—or what they continue doing—is to keep the Sill from becoming still. They open springs, close them, and let them return elsewhere. They encourage green over bare rock, then permit it to withdraw. They allow the cliffs to shed their fresh shards. Even the old devices are not kept pristine. Their work is not preservation, but adjustment: a constant, patient tending of a world that has not finished deciding what it is.

At the rim, when the cloud parts, they gather—not as figures, but as changes in the air. The blankness below seems to deepen. The dark folds far beneath the shelf slow their passage. For a moment the Sill’s warmth can be felt not only underfoot, but extending downward, as if the seam-kin are listening into the hidden country.

Then the cloud closes. Water resumes its thin, ordinary trickle. The white thing slips under the ledge.

And the mountain, still young, continues cooling around its inhabitants.
