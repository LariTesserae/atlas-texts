The fields are the country’s oldest inhabitants. From far away they look like one immense growth, but each mat is a close weave of many kinds: dark, cushiony lobes pressed against the stone; pale threads that creep through cracks and take what the rock gives up; and tiny beadlike bodies that open to the sun, then close against the night cold. The whole surface holds a little warmth. When the wind passes, it bends in traveling waves—not because the plants are hurried, but because every patch yields to the next.

Their colors mark their circumstances. Olive mats gather on the warmer slopes, where the stone is rich and the wind is less severe. Blue-green sheets spread across the broad flats, their waxed surfaces catching the hard light. In the hollows, plum-colored growths thicken above the grit. They are darker there, where they can take in the last warmth of afternoon and keep it through the long, cold hours. None has roots that reach for water. Their fine holdfasts cling to mineral seams, while their broad surfaces drink light and the faint trace of vapor passing through the thin air.

In sheltered clefts, the taller stalks stand apart from the mats. They grow in loose families, each one leaning a little away from the prevailing wind. Their leaves are translucent along the edges, with a faint metallic sheen. When air moves through them they touch and separate with a dry, delicate ticking. At the end of a season, they shed brittle husks that break down among the rocks. The mats gather those fragments slowly, along with dust and the fine waste of living things, and make from them the beginnings of soil.

Beneath the surface, the country is more crowded than it appears. Small pale creatures move through the cool spaces under the mats, feeding on tender growth and on one another. They leave narrow seams that close behind them, so a slope may seem untouched even after many generations have passed beneath it. Some shelter in the lee of stones; others wait through the hottest hours inside the plants’ dense weave. Their lives are brief by the measure of the uplands, but their paths, repeated over and over, help keep the mats loose enough to breathe.

The great grazers are the most visible animals. They are tall and spare, all patient legs and sloping backs, with broad, hard feet that spread their weight over the fragile ground. A faint down covers their flanks, colored like the grey grit; along the spine, it darkens to the shade of the plum fields. They browse without stripping a place bare, cropping the outer growth and moving on before the mats are damaged. A grazer may stand for hours with its head lowered, not quite feeding, as if listening to the wind through the ground. At a distance it is easy to mistake one for a stone pillar. It may take days to cross a single basin.

Above them, the gliders use the rising currents along the ridges. Their bodies are narrow, almost blade-shaped, with pale membranes stretched between limb and flank. They do not flap often. They tilt into the light, sink with the wind, and turn upward again where dark rock has warmed the air. From below, they appear no larger than scraps of brightness passing over the empty sky. They feed on the small life stirred from the mats and on the drifting seeds of the stalk groves. Their nests, if they have nests, are hidden high in cracks where the wind has worn the stone smooth.

Nothing here gathers in abundance for long. A favorable slope may darken with new growth; then a season of harder winds thins it again. Dust buries the mats in one hollow and uncovers them on another. The animals follow these changes at distances so great that their migrations leave almost no trace. Their presence is written only in slight alterations: cropped edges, closed seams, a scatter of husks beneath a standing stone.

The high country’s living things do not conquer the land. They make themselves part of its pauses. They cling, shelter, feed, and wait, each according to a scale in which a day’s weather is brief and a season’s movement can shape a whole ridge. Under the immense, clear sky, the fields breathe with the wind, and the few animals pass through them like thoughts too slow to be noticed at once.
