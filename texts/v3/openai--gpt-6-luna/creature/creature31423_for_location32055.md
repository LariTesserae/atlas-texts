Nothing in the Fold has a name for itself. There is no call by which one part summons another, no clear boundary where one plant ends and the next begins. The leaves overlap until their edges are difficult to distinguish; below them, roots pass through old cracks and meet roots that may have grown from somewhere else. The whole mat takes water as a body takes warmth—not all at once, but gradually, in ways that leave no obvious sign of need.

The oldest growth is close to the stone. Its leaves are small, thick, and pressed flat, so the rock’s shape can still be felt beneath them. Above these lie broader leaves, each cupping a little space of darkness. They do not reach toward the ceiling. They have learned its limits. Where the ceiling lowers, the plants turn sideways and continue, making a soft, silver-backed roof over the ground. A leaf occasionally loosens under the weight of newer growth and settles without a sound. Beneath it, another has already begun to widen.

The water belongs to them, though it never seems to move at their command. It collects in basins formed over long periods by roots and fallen leaves, and travels through channels whose beginnings are hidden. The plants drink what they can. The rest goes on, carrying a little mineral from one hollow to the next. At the deepest pool, the water is too still to show the leaves above it. A drop breaks that stillness now and then, making its low note. The plants nearest the pool seem to change afterward, though the change may have begun before the sound.

The instrument is not alive in the way the growth is alive. Its seams do not open; it has no need to drink. But it has remained among the plants long enough to be included in their habits. It senses a strain in the stone and adjusts the pressure inside a crack by a fraction. Water alters its course. A root tightens. A leaf turns its pale underside toward the ground. None of these actions is large enough to be called an answer, yet together they form a kind of conversation: not between two minds, but between things that have learned the consequences of touching.

Perhaps the instrument has been making the same careful adjustments for a very long time. Perhaps the plants have altered it, leaf by leaf, until its work has become indistinguishable from the Fold’s own slow settling. There is no way to tell where maintenance ends and habit begins. Its box is partly covered, its base held in place by roots. If it stopped, the Fold might change. If it has already stopped, the Fold may have learned to continue without it.

Nothing here keeps watch. Nothing waits for a visitor or remembers one after the passage closes behind them. Yet the Fold is not empty. It is occupied by growth that does not hurry, by water that does not waste its way, by stone undergoing changes too small to notice until they have become the whole shape of a place. Its inhabitants do not gather together. They do not need to. The darkness holds them in contact, and the narrowness of the Fold has made contact enough.
