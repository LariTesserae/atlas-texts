The city belongs to no single kind of creature.

The small pale mammals are the easiest to notice. They pass in brief, purposeful streams along the old corridors, their whiskers testing the air and their dark eyes catching what little light the stone gives back. Some are no larger than a clenched hand; others, broad-backed and slow, nose aside the loose grit with their blunt faces. They eat seeds, roots, dry fungus, and whatever small life can be found among the stones. Their fur is the color of dust or old bone. They do not look up often. The ceiling is familiar to them chiefly as the place where roots descend.

They travel by routes older than their families. A young animal learns the city first as a sequence of smells and floor textures: the gritty bend, the warm wall, the place where beetles gather, the passage that carries the far-off chatter of a nesting vault. If a lane grows unfamiliar with dust, they follow another creature’s feet until the way is clear again. They do not need to remember who first opened a corridor. The corridor remains open because bodies keep choosing it.

In the walls and seams live the beetles. They work at husks, dead leaves, dropped feathers, shed skin, and the remains of things too small for the mammals to find. Their hard shells shine faintly in the mineral dimness—not with light, but with the dull reflection of it. At certain times they gather in cracks by the thousands, shifting over one another like a single dark substance. Their labor breaks the city’s leftovers into finer things: food for fungi, soil for the roots, and the powdered earth that settles again on the paths.

The fungi have their own neighborhoods. Pale caps rise in dense, quiet groves, and beneath them their hidden threads pass through rubble, old walls, and the compacted remains of abandoned rooms. Nothing among the city’s smaller inhabitants treats a fungus bed as empty ground. Mammals come to feed, beetles come to lay their eggs, and birds sometimes scratch among the caps for whatever moves there. In return, the beds spread through chambers rich with old matter, slowly making use of what the city has left behind.

Above, the long-legged creatures travel the ceiling. They cross the stone with delicate, jointed steps, vanish into root cracks, and reappear far away. They feed on tiny growths and smaller animals, though no passerby can always tell whether one is hunting or merely following a scent across the rock. Their bodies are so narrow they seem made of the cracks they inhabit. When they drop to the floor, the mammals give them room.

The birds are most often heard before they are seen. They nest in sheltered hollows, laying their eggs in beds of dry fibers, shed leaves, and soft debris. Their wings are blunt and powerful enough to lift them through the great chambers, but not made for long flight. A flock rising together sends a storm of dust from the floor. One bird is nearly silent; a hundred make the vault tremble with wingbeats and short, rough cries. They forage among roots and fungus, and sometimes follow the mammals, waiting for a disturbed beetle or a loosened seed.

No creature has the whole city to itself. The neighborhoods overlap without becoming wholly mixed. There are corridors that belong, for a while, to the mammals’ traffic; seams where beetles are thick; root chambers where the birds nest and the ceiling-walkers come down. Each kind knows when another kind is near. The signs are small: a pause in the scratching, a change in the rhythm of calls, the sudden emptying of a patch of floor. A place may seem abandoned when its usual inhabitants have only gone quiet.

Sound is how the city knows its own size. Calls travel through the vaults, bent by corners and carried along the stone. Some are warnings, some invitations, some simply the recurring noises of a crowded life. The mammals click and chirr to one another in the passageways. Birds answer from nesting hollows. Insects scrape and tap in the walls. A sound repeated across generations may become a kind of custom: a phrase that means danger near a rootfall, or a particular note that brings others to a fresh bed of growth. Whether the creatures mean these sounds as people mean words is difficult to say. They listen, and they answer, and the city is altered by what passes between them.

Their lives are careful because their world is dry. They take moisture from food and from the roots’ buried stores. They groom their coats and keep their nests close against the stone. They do not waste what the chambers cannot readily replace. Even the crowded places have pauses: feeding ends, flocks disperse, and the traffic thins until the smaller sounds emerge again.

There are old animals who know the sound of passages now filled with dust. There are young ones who will never have seen the rooms where their grandparents nested. A chamber may be abandoned not in grief but because the food has thinned, the air has grown stale, or the roof has shifted. The inhabitants move a little farther down the lane. Beetles follow the remains. Fungi take the walls. In time another family makes a nest there, unaware that anything has changed.

Perhaps they know the settlement as a city. Perhaps they know it only as the places where food can be found, where young can be hidden, and where the calls of their kind carry farthest. They have no founding story and no name for the whole of it. Still, they keep the passages open, the chambers inhabited, the old routes audible. Their civilization is made of repeated journeys and shared ground: not a plan imposed on the stone, but a living habit worn into it.
