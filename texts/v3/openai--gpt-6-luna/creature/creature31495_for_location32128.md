At first, it seems that nothing lives there. Then the wall tightens.

The movement is slight: a pale panel drawing itself flush against its frame, a soft seam narrowing until the moisture along it breaks into separate beads. The chamber makes no sound of effort. It has learned to do difficult things quietly.

What lives there has no single body. It is distributed through the fittings and the unfinished surfaces: a little in the pump that pauses between cycles, a little in the warmth that wakes behind the wall, a little in the channels that guide water toward places it has not yet reached. Its senses are pressure, temperature, vibration, and the changing weight of condensation. It knows the room by what resists it.

It does not know the room as a room. It knows a ridge that will not lie flat, a pipe whose pitch has changed, a joint that leaks more slowly than before. These are not defects to it, exactly. They are the first facts of the world. It tests them in passing: easing a panel inward, pressing new material against an old edge, sending a brief warmth through the metal to learn what shifts. Then it waits. Its patience is not a choice so much as the speed at which it is able to exist.

The growths at the warm joint are not quite plants. Some are stray fibers caught in the damp; some may be the first fine filaments it has made for itself, reaching along the seam where heat gathers. They respond when the chamber changes, tightening or relaxing by an amount too small to see. The water responds too. It gathers in one recess, drains from another, leaves its pale mineral script behind. The chamber reads this writing in the only way it can: by feeling where the surface has become rough.

Perhaps it is making a place for something else. The inward-curving wall may be the beginning of a passage, or only an adjustment to the pressure behind it. The open sleeve of insulation may be a wound it has not yet learned to close. Nothing in the chamber can say. Its future is present only as a tendency—a direction taken by soft material, a pattern repeated by the pumps, a shape the surfaces seem to remember.

For now, it lives as a slow agreement between machinery and matter. When the valve opens, it feels the breath of pressure move through it. When the overhead pipe ticks, it registers the cooling. When a drop strikes the shelf, it records the interval before the next. It has no eyes to turn toward the sound, no limbs to reach for what falls. Its attention is the change itself.

Sometimes, deep behind the wall, the new material presses outward and holds. The chamber grows by a fraction. The ridge on the floor changes its angle. A seam seals, or opens elsewhere. In the dimness, the indicator lamps continue to glow without illuminating much, while the thing within them learns the shape of its own boundaries.

It is not yet a building, nor an animal, nor a machine in the ordinary sense. It is the beginning of an inhabitant: a mind without a center, still being assembled, discovering that the dark around it can be altered—and that the altered dark belongs to it.
