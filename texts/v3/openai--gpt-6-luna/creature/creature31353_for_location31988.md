The most conspicuous inhabitants are the shelled ones, though “conspicuous” means only that they gather in numbers. One alone could be mistaken for a broken flake of basalt.

They cling to warm rock in overlapping ranks. Their bodies are low and broad, armored in dark plates that collect the same grey powder as the ledges beneath them. Along the edges of those plates, pale feelers comb the stone. They rasp mineral grains from the surface and draw warmth and sharp-smelling gases from the cracks, feeding slowly enough that their feeding is almost indistinguishable from resting. A colony can occupy one shelf for generations. Its outermost bodies are dust-grey; farther in, where the warmth is steady, the shells hold a faint pearly gleam.

They do not have eyes as the surface world understands them. They read a chamber through pressure: the tremor of something crossing a floor, the minute shift of air around a passing body, the deep, persistent pulse of heat in the stone. A touch at the edge of a colony passes inward through shell and limb. The animals nearest the disturbance tighten; those beyond them answer; after a while the whole mass settles again. From a distance, it looks like thought moving across a wall.

When they must travel, they go in files, each following the faint mineral taste left by the one before. Their many legs make little sound on the powder, but their plates meet with a soft, regular ticking. They travel to another warm seam, or around a fracture that has opened where none was before. These are not journeys toward a destination so much as changes in where the colony holds itself against the stone.

The smaller makers live among them. They pry loose flakes of black glass and chip them into narrow scrapers, working by feel at the mouths of inhabited cracks. They set shards across powder pits, or arrange pale plates into a low wall that marks a passage safe from collapse. Nothing they build stands long against the scale of the chambers. A shelf may break away; dust may bury a landmark; a new fissure may divide an old route. The makers leave their tools where they fall and begin again nearby.

In the deeper cracks, the blind hunters wait. They are pale and narrow, folded close to the stone until the pressure of passing bodies reaches them. Then their heads open. A mouth, hidden until that instant, fastens onto shell or limb. The attack is not swift by any familiar measure, but it is sudden in a world where nearly everything else has been still for hours. The colony closes over the loss. The dead are rasped clean, their mineral-rich plates passed from body to body and eventually worked back into the ledge.

The shelled ones give off light only when disturbed. A turning body may reveal a blue point beneath its armor, gone as soon as it faces the rock again. This light is not enough to show a neighbor’s shape. It may be no more than a signal to the nearest bodies: here is movement; here is damage; here, briefly, is me. Their other signals are quieter—clicks against the ground, pulses through a shared shelf, the faint exchange of warmth where one creature presses against another.

They have no call that carries through a chamber. Nothing in them is made for distance. Their lives happen close to stone and closer to one another: in the slow feeding along a seam, the tightening of shells when the air changes, the gradual migration of a colony toward a warmer face of rock. A young one, newly shed from its first casing, settles among the adults and learns the routes not by seeing them but by being carried along them. It may never know the shape of the chamber that contains it.

Their world has no horizon they can look toward. It has only the next warm surface, the next familiar tremor, the pressure of the bodies beside them. So they remain, fitted to their ledges with such patience that the stone seems to have grown them there: a living crust beneath the young crust, shifting almost imperceptibly in the immense dark.
