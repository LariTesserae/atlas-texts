At first, it seems the Close is inhabited by small things: pale beetles slipping into the seams between tiles, moths folded like scraps of paper, soft-faced animals sleeping in jars with their noses tucked under their paws. They share the paths without yielding them. A beetle may cross the toe of a shoe and continue on as though the shoe were only another warm stone. The jar-sleepers sometimes wake together, blink at nothing in particular, and settle again facing a different direction.

The larger inhabitants are harder to catch in the act. A creature may be there only as a row of fresh thumbprints on a wall, or as a pale shape withdrawing behind a trellis that has no space behind it. Now and then, in the dimness beneath the leaves, something moves with the unhurried care of a gardener: turning a bowl of powder, lifting a fallen petal, pressing a new brick into a gap. No one sees hands. The work is simply done. If there is a keeper, it may be several creatures sharing a task, or one creature spread through the roots, the walls, and the patient rearranging of the light.

The roots know them best. Fine white filaments gather around certain cracks, then loosen again; clear sap beads on a cut stem and is gone before it can fall. The small animals drink from the mineral dust left in the bowls. The birds—if birds they are—carry no seeds that anyone can find. They fly from one patch of green to another, threading the spaces between leaves, and wherever they pass a shoot may appear days later from a crack too narrow to admit a seed.

They do not seem to fear visitors, but neither do they welcome them. They have no need to. A visitor’s warmth is noticed. The leaves lean toward it by degrees. The powder in the bowls is combed into furrows. Somewhere, in a wall or under the paving, the quiet ticking starts.

If someone stays too long, the Close makes a place for them. It may set a warm brick into the niche where they rested, or lay a bed of blossoms across the shape of their shadow. The person can still leave. Usually they do. But afterward they may find a little grit in their palm, or feel, for a day or two, that something beneath their ribs is turning slowly toward the light.

The Close’s inhabitants are not quite its gardeners, nor quite its guests. They are the small lives that keep a made thing alive: sleeping in its jars, working in its seams, and quietly teaching its roots where to go.
