At first, it seems that the shelf belongs to the water.

Water has the oldest routes. It knows which seams stay open beneath the ice, which hollows hold a little warmth, and where a drop can fall without being caught by moss. It moves through the hand-cut channel as readily as through the cracks it made itself, and the basin is never quite empty. Even in the coldest weather, something ticks and travels under the stone.

Then the birds make their claim. They live in the cliff’s small dark rooms, tucked where the rock folds inward. They are not pretty at close range so much as purposeful: blunt little bodies, strong feet, wings that beat as if the air were heavier here. They know the shelf by its drafts. One can vanish around a corner of stone and emerge far above it, while another sits on the shelter roof and scolds the empty sky. They gather grass and feathers from the ledges, and their nests show at the cliff’s mouth like untidy pockets. When the wind is about to turn, they go quiet—not all at once, but in a passing hush that a person might mistake for peace.

The fox keeps farther out. Its shape is pale against wet rock, long-legged and careful, its head held low when the cloud comes thick. It has several routes, all of them narrow: a ledge above the drop, a notch behind the basin, a crack in the cliff that leads to some den no one has seen. It does not come close to the shelter, though sometimes its tracks pass the entrance in the night. The person who lives there leaves no food for it. The fox would not thank them for the offer, and the person understands the difference between living alongside an animal and making it dependent.

The human is hard to notice at first, partly because the shelter is built so low against the wall, and partly because the shelf teaches anyone who stays to move without flourish. They are called the keeper, though perhaps only by people who have not met them. They have no grand title and no uniform: just a hood of dark wool, a weather-stiffened coat, and hands made pale and cracked by cold water. Their face is usually turned toward whatever needs attention—a loose stone in the channel, a new skin of ice in the basin, a patch of moss lifted by frost. They look up often, but not to admire the view. They are reading the cloud.

They know which stones can be stepped on in rain and which only look solid. They know the sound of a bird dropping from the cliff, the sound of the wind coming up through the chasm, and the long pause that sometimes follows a far-off fall of rock. If they speak, it is usually to mark something: “Water’s high,” or “Not that ledge today.” A visitor might take this for shyness. It is more like thrift. Words, too, should be used where they can do some good.

Their work is small enough to be missed and constant enough to shape the place. They clear the channel when grit gathers in it. They fit a fallen stone back where it belongs. They keep the shelter’s entrance clear, repair the roof with slabs gathered nearby, and leave the safest crossings as they found them—or safer, if they can manage it. There is no hoard of supplies, only what can be carried and what the mountain allows them to keep dry. In the shelter, the warmth is brief and close. It smells of damp wool, smoke, stone dust, and the faint green odor of plants brought in on a sleeve.

In clear weather the keeper may stand at the rim, but never quite at the edge. The peaks appear across the cloud, white and remote, and the birds fly between them and the shelf without seeming to notice the distance. The keeper watches until the cloud closes, then turns back to the basin, the roof, the stones underfoot. The mountain gives no answer to being looked at.

At evening, all the lives on the shelf draw inward. Birds tuck themselves into the cliff. The fox leaves the rim to its darkness. Water goes on moving in the channels, and the keeper shuts the shelter door against the weather. For a while, there is the sound of breathing, rain on stone, and the hidden waterfall descending into depths no one here can see. The place does not feel deserted. It feels occupied in several careful ways, with room left between each life for the wind to pass through.
