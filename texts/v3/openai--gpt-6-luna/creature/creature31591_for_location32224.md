At first they are easy to mistake for the larger animals on the mud shelves: smooth-backed shapes hunched close to the water, their skin shining in the low light. Then one rises, steadies a slab against a wall with its shoulder, and reaches for a wedge of shell with long, deft fingers.

The people are built for a world that is never quite land or water. They are broad through the chest and low in the hips, with strong limbs and wide, webbed hands. Their skin holds the damp; dark markings along the throat and flanks deepen or fade with temperature, mood, and the shifting colors of the basin. They have eyes, but no need to squint toward a sun that never appears. They read the world by its small changes: a ripple against the ankle, a tremor through a floor, the shape of a voice returning from a covered passage.

Their voices are part of the town’s structure. A few short clicks can call someone around a corner; a rough bark can stop work at a gutter; longer notes carry over open water. In crowded rooms, they speak more softly, adding touches of hand to arm, palm to wall. The oldest inhabitants can tell which neighbor is coming by the rhythm of their steps through wet stone. No one mistakes quiet for peace. Even at rest, the town murmurs.

They work in groups, not because they are incapable of working alone, but because nearly every useful thing here depends on keeping something else in balance. A channel must be cleared without draining the pool that feeds it. A wall must hold back water without cutting off the seepage beneath the next courtyard. People learn these relationships early. Children follow the gutters with little scoops, carrying mud and shell chips to the places where adults have shown them the ground is thinning. Play and maintenance are difficult to tell apart.

Their dwellings are crowded, but rarely private in the way a sealed room would be. Families sleep on raised shelves under hide or shell roofing, close enough to hear one another breathe. Shared pools sit within the blocks, warmed by the stone below. Elders favor the same sunless ledges they have used for years; young ones gather where channels meet, splashing and calling until someone answers. The town’s covered ways let them move between these places without exposing themselves to the deeper water, though many prefer to wade when the channels are calm.

They do not regard the basin’s animals as scenery. The shell-backed grazers are left patches of mineral film along certain walls; their rasping keeps the growth from sealing the stone. The pale fish are watched for changes in the water, and the larger shelf-dwellers are given room at their favored pools. Some of those animals answer human calls, or appear to. Whether this is friendship, habit, or merely the exchange of sounds between neighbors is not something the people agree on. They argue about it while repairing the same ledge year after year.

The oldest stories begin with a wall, a flood that did not come, and a pool that kept its shape. They do not tell of a world made in a single act. They tell of learning where water wanted to go, and of how long it takes for a place to become familiar. To the people, the basin is not young. It is the world they have had time to know.

Outside the town, life still lies close to the bare beginnings of life: colored films gripping stone, small bodies stirring in shallow pools, cries passing across the mud. Inside it, those beginnings have acquired habits. People wake, clear the gutters, answer one another, and return to the warm water at day’s end. Their civilization does not rise above the basin. It settles into it, listening for what needs to be done next.
