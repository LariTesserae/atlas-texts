Nothing here rules the basin. The nearest thing to a sovereign is the water, and even it changes its mind.

At dusk, the reedbacks come down to drink: broad, low animals with shoulders like boulders under slick dark hides. They push through the shallows in family groups, cropping reeds and uprooting soft plants as they go. Calves keep close between the adults, where a hunter must choose between mud, tusks, and a great deal of noise. Their passage opens corridors through the growth, but not for long. Vines trail across the paths by morning; young reeds rise through the trampled mud. The reedbacks make roads without meaning to, and the basin borrows them.

Where the herd has been, the hunters follow.

Some are long-bodied swimmers, almost invisible beneath the brown water except for the faint seam of a back or the two bright beads of eyes. They lie beneath floating leaves until a drinking muzzle lowers, then strike with a force that throws water higher than the reeds. Others wait above the paths. They cling to trunks on many-fingered limbs, their mottled skins passing for bark and lichen. For hours they may not move at all. Then a small animal crosses within reach, and the branch seems to come alive.

The smaller lives are harder to separate from weather. Winged insects rise in columns over warm pools, and the clouds of them attract thin, quick fliers that turn so sharply they appear to be thrown from the air. At night, some of these fliers skim the water, snapping up insects while their reflections break and reform beneath them. Others hang upside down under leaves, waiting with folded wings. Their bodies catch a brief gleam—blue, green, a dull copper—then vanish as soon as they turn away.

Frogs call from every layer of the basin: from the mud, from reeds, from pools too small to see. Some give a single hollow note, like a drop falling into a deep pot. Others answer in rattling trills that set the leaves trembling. The calls are not all meant for mates. One rhythm draws the little fliers toward a patch of water; another warns them off. A sharp, rising cry can empty a poolside in a breath. What receives the warning may be a predator, or merely another frog with a larger mouth.

There are birds in the branches, though the name hardly suits all of them. The largest perch on trees that bend under their weight, balancing with long tails and hooked feet. They wait for fruit, eggs, and whatever the smaller animals disturb. Their voices are low, almost conversational: a click, a throat-deep murmur, a pause, then a reply from somewhere too far away to see. In the dimness, it is possible to think the trees are speaking to one another. Sometimes they are.

Some creatures make a life of what the others leave behind. Pale, soft-bodied scavengers gather where a carcass has sunk into the mud. Beetles arrive first, then things that resemble folded leaves until they unfold. Fungi follow, lifting small white shelves from a fallen limb or drawing faint color out of the black earth. Nothing is wasted for long. Even a body that disappears into a pool may return as a fever of insects above it.

The animals know the ground more intimately than any map could. They recognize which banks will hold and which will slide. They follow the same paths until a new channel cuts through them, then abandon them without hesitation. A still pool is avoided by some and visited eagerly by others. Before a storm, certain small animals climb high into the foliage; the larger ones move toward ridges. When the water rises, the reedbacks turn together, as if tugged by a single thought.

There are minds here, but few are easy to read. A hunter may circle a herd for hours, learning which adult limps, which calf strays, which bank the group trusts. A flock may keep one sharp-eyed creature above the others, feeding only when it gives a soft click. Even the little animals remember where the last flood stopped. They return to those places when the rain begins, and some of them seem to know the channels will move again.

The basin is crowded, but not companionable. Every call might be a challenge, a warning, a lure, or nothing more than the urge to be heard in a world that never stops making noise. Creatures vanish into leaves, water, mud, and one another. Their lives leave brief marks—a disturbed pool, a stripped branch, a line of prints already filling with rain—and the land closes over them. Yet the sounds keep coming. Somewhere a reedback bellows; somewhere else, something answers from the dark, and the water carries the answer on.
