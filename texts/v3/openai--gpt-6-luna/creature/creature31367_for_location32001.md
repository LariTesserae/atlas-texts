The people of the Crown do not live above the mountain so much as among its last surviving folds. Their houses tuck themselves against the old stone, their gardens settle in its cracks, and their paths follow lines worn into the plateau long before anyone remembers walking them. From a distance, the settlement looks like another outcrop: a scatter of pale roofs caught in the dark green of the trees.

They are a careful people, though not a quiet one. Their voices rise through the covered passages; children shout from the terraces, and elders call back without looking up from their work. Stone is ground, wood tapped, baskets dragged over paving. Every household knows the calls of the birds, the scratch of the burrowers beneath the floors, and the particular rasp an insect makes when the evening is turning. They can tell what is moving in the scrub by the way the chorus shifts around it.

Water governs them, but does not rule them. A cup is not casually poured out, and no one washes what can be wiped, scraped, or left for the dry air to take. The saved moisture has its purposes, and those purposes are spoken aloud: a seed bed, a fever, a newborn. Yet their lives are not pinched down to thirst. They have learned to find abundance in things that would seem small elsewhere: a good flowering, a full seed harvest, a night of heavy condensation on the leaves. Their stores are measured; their celebrations are not.

They gather moisture before dawn, moving through the gardens with shallow bowls and soft cloths. They tend plants whose roots disappear into seams no hand can reach. Some crops are grown for food, others for the dampness held in their stems, and a few are never cut at all: they are reservoirs rooted in stone. Children learn early which leaves can be pressed, which flowers must be left to open, and which hollows should never be trampled. The rules are practical, but over generations they have acquired the weight of courtesy. To waste a plant’s water is to take something from the people who will come after you.

The Crown’s animals are not simply wild beyond the town. Small ones live under its edges and sometimes beneath its rooms. Birds nest in the trees and on the roofs, where their feathers and twigs gather in gutters. The colony roosts close enough that, at dusk, its wingbeats pass like weather over the houses. Shy, larger creatures browse in the scrub beyond the outer terraces. The people do not always see them. They know them by a tremble in the leaves, a track in the grit, or the sudden silence of smaller animals.

Children are taught to watch for these silences. They play among the ruins and learn which old walls will bear their weight, which gaps are homes, and which paths lead nowhere but to a dangerous edge. The adults do not forbid them from the rim, but they teach them how to approach it: slowly, with a hand on stone, and never when the haze is thickest. The land below is mostly hidden. The Crown has no easy horizon, only its own borders and the sense of the mountain falling away beneath them.

The oldest walls are part of their lives in the way a grandparent’s stories are: present, useful, and not entirely understood. New houses lean against them. Terraces borrow their lines. Children hide in their hollows. No one claims to have built them, and no one tries to pull them down. When a stone slips loose, it is set aside rather than broken; when it can be returned to its place, someone returns it. The people know that a wall can outlast the hands that mend it.

They have a long sense of time, though their lives are not slow. A person may watch a tree thicken only a little in all the years they tend it. A house can pass from one family to another without changing its stones. The Crown’s changes are recorded in small things: a new crack, a path diverted around a settling wall, a patch where the flowers no longer open. People remember these as carefully as other places might remember kings.

At night, when the warm stone gives back the day, the settlement remains awake in pockets. Someone tends a sick animal. Someone turns seeds on a flat slab. Someone sits beneath the trees listening to the colony settle above. The insects rasp, the birds mutter in their sleep, and from somewhere in the scrub comes the low stir of a body passing through leaves.

No one on the Crown is ever entirely alone. Even solitude has a chorus. The people live among one another, among old stone and deep-rooted plants, among creatures that share their shelter without asking permission. They have made a home on a height from which almost everything has vanished from view. What remains is enough to fill the air.
