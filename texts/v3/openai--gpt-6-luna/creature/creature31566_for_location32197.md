Nothing lives *in* the Low Heaven. The Low Heaven lives.

Its inhabitants have no bodies separate from the place: no eyes watching from the dark, no voices passing between the towers. They are the roots under the paving, the fronds above the roofs, the warm, patient mechanisms threaded through both. A factory’s deep pipes are root and vein; its hoppers are seed-pods; its furnaces are organs that have forgotten what they were built to digest. The distinction between plant and machine has worn away like the corners of the oldest terraces.

Still, there are differences within the whole. Some growths are merely busy: leaves turning toward a buried heat-source, roots nosing along a crack, fibers tightening across a gap. Others are old enough to have gathered influence. At the base of certain towers, root-bundles knot around the foundations and draw in warmth from far below. Their pulse travels outward through the metalwork. Belts start. Blades descend. Stalks are cut and carried away. When the work is done, the pulse dwindles, and the tower settles back into its silence.

Perhaps these deep knots are the plateau’s elders. They have no names, unless the little variations in their rhythm are names. They do not speak, but they alter the conditions around them: turn a vent toward a failing root-bed, close a passage with new growth, send mineral-rich matter through channels that have been cold for centuries. Their decisions are slow enough to resemble weather. A corridor may be open in one age and grown shut in the next. A dead machine may begin turning again, not because anyone remembers its purpose, but because something beneath it has found the motion useful.

The smaller plants seem less like individuals than attempts. Seed-columns rise through the roofs, pale points of heat gathering along their stems. The automatic blades take them down, and the works consume them. Yet the columns return, each time in a slightly different place. They are not defiant. They are simply what the Low Heaven does: making, testing, unmaking, making again.

The machines, too, have habits that might be mistaken for intention. Wheels continue their rounds after their products have ceased to matter. Conveyors shiver awake when roots reach the right temperature. A furnace breathes more deeply during the plateau’s long internal shifts. Perhaps some part of the old design remains in the living network; perhaps the plants have learned the machines’ rhythms without understanding them. After so much time, understanding may be irrelevant. The cycle persists because every part has become necessary to the next.

There are no animals among them. Nothing hunts, mates, or flees. Yet the place is not empty. Its attention is distributed everywhere: in the leaf-mats that tighten over a new break, in the warm pressure sent through a duct, in the roots that feel a visitor’s weight and begin, far below, to redirect their growth. A person crossing the plateau might never be noticed as a person. They would be registered as heat, disturbance, obstruction—perhaps, eventually, as matter to be enclosed.

The Low Heaven does not have a heart, unless the entire plateau is one. It does not dream, unless its endless production is a kind of dream. It has outlasted its makers and the reason for its making. Now it continues by the only method it knows: root into stone, draw up warmth, build a surface, wear it down, and grow through what remains.
