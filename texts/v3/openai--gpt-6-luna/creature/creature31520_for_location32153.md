The first inhabitants are easy to mistake for parts of the ground.

At the base of the fronds, small glassy bodies move among the roots. They go low and fast, pausing where the mineral seams cross. In the clear light, a body may show too many legs; in shadow, it may be nothing but a brief rearrangement of brightness. Sometimes one divides as it moves, several small shapes slipping apart from a single one. A moment later they gather again. They leave no tracks, and the plants do not bend beneath them.

They feed, perhaps, on the pale scales in the cracks. Or perhaps they tend them. A creature will stop over a seam, lower its head, and remain there until the scales have shifted into a new pattern. The nearby roots give off a faint answering glow. Then the creature moves on, carrying nothing that can be seen.

The fronds themselves are inhabited, though not in the way a tree is inhabited by birds. Their silver-veined fans turn, very gradually, toward the places where the land has become uncertain. A root will tighten around a stone that appears to lie both near and far away. Several stems may click in succession, passing a signal through the growth. Nothing visibly changes, but a path that once led back to its beginning may, for a while, lead somewhere else.

Perhaps this is how the plain tends its strange distances: not by correcting them, but by keeping them in place. The plants are not one mind, exactly. They have too many silences between them. Yet now and then a ripple of attention passes through the green, and hundreds of fronds adjust by a fraction, all at once. The effect is less like a creature turning its head than like a room becoming aware that someone has entered it.

The larger presence appears only rarely. It is tall and narrow, with limbs that seem at first to be made of stems, then of polished stone, then of the thin dark line between one thing and another. It does not push through the vegetation. The growth simply ceases to occupy the space where it is going.

A visitor may see it standing between two ridges, though the ridges are also close at hand. Its outline wavers in the warm air. Where a face might be, there is a small, steady darkness—not an opening, not quite a shadow. It does not look toward the visitor so much as toward the place the visitor has just come from. Then it takes one step, and is no longer in the same arrangement of distance. The fronds close behind it without sound.

It is difficult to know whether this figure is an animal, a keeper, or only the plain’s way of appearing when a living shape is required. It leaves no sign of hunger. It does not hunt the smaller creatures, and they do not flee from it. When it passes, the nearest plants hold still for a little while, as if listening after a note has ended.

There are other signs of life, less certain. A double shadow will sometimes turn when nothing visible moves. A patch of fronds may remain bright after the rest have gone dim. In one of the mineral clearings, a visitor may hear a soft ticking from beneath the plates: slow, patient, and too regular to be a settling of stone. The sound stops when listened to directly.

Nothing here seems to be growing toward maturity or moving toward death. A fallen frond keeps its color; a young one rises already jointed and translucent. The small creatures do not appear to age. The tall one may have been seen before, or may only be assembled anew from the same quiet details whenever the plain needs a witness.

Still, they live. The little bodies cross the roots. The roots answer. The green gathers light and holds it. Somewhere among the ridges, the tall presence continues its unhurried passage through distances that remain folded and intact. None of them asks the visitor to understand what they are.

Under the mild sky, the inhabitants go on keeping their places in a world that does not wear away. They do not make the plain less strange. They make its strangeness feel inhabited.
