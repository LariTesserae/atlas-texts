Nothing living here can be pointed to and called the inhabitant.

The plateau has no heart, no den, no single body. Its life is spread through the dark mats and the hollow stalks, through the roots that fasten one slope to another and the old stems packed into the wet ground. Each plant grows where it can, but none grows alone. A root meeting another root bends around it, joins it, or passes a message through the soil. The groves, ridges, and basins are not parts arranged by a plan. They are the shapes the living mass has learned to make.

It feels its country by pressure. A change in the weight of snow travels down a slope. A pocket of warmth under the moss stirs a ridge. Water, newly gathered in a crown, darkens the roots below it; the roots draw tight, and far off a fringe of fronds folds shut. These responses take minutes, hours, sometimes whole seasons. They are not hurried. The plateau has no reason to hurry. A small adjustment made everywhere is enough.

Its sense of weather is intimate. The high stalks turn their cupped crowns toward a bank of cloud long before rain reaches them. Their ribs gather the first beads of moisture and guide them down, while the mats beneath loosen to receive it. In a dry spell, the ridges close their gaps. The long corridors narrow, and the basins hold what little water remains in their shade. The plateau does not choose to conserve itself; conserving is simply what its growth does.

Perhaps the low vibration is its slowest voice. It passes underfoot through buried stems and interwoven roots, too deep and broad to belong to any one plant. At its passing, crowns turn by a fraction. A new line of growth rises along an old seam of stone. A basin, empty for years, begins to hold water again. There is no answer one can hear, only changes taking place elsewhere, as though the whole high country has felt the same touch.

The pale creatures belong differently. They are small enough to slip through the stems and quick enough to vanish between one glance and the next. Their narrow tracks cross the moss, bringing seeds and fragments of growth from one hollow to another. They warm a little pocket in the vegetation, strip a few leaves, then leave. The plateau closes around the traces of them. Perhaps it knows them as movement, warmth, damage, nourishment—several things at once. It makes room without making much room.

At night, when the cold draws moisture from the air, the plants shine faintly. The tallest crowns hold the starlight in their wet cups; lower down, dark leaves touch and separate in a silence that has no edge. The plateau is not sleeping. It is redistributing water, tightening roots, yielding to gravity, and feeling the clouds pass above it.

It has no face to turn toward the world. Its attention is the whole of its surface: a million small responses joined by slow, hidden continuities. What lives here is not a forest, though it has forests; not a machine, though it gathers and moves what it needs; not an animal, though it senses, repairs, and changes. It is a country that has become alive by degrees, so gradually that there was never a moment when the country and its life were separate.
