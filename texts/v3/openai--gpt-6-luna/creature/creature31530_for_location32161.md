The inhabitants are easiest to see when they are not moving.

A pale bead clings beneath a basalt flake, six fine legs folded against its body. It could be a mite, if a mite had been made of wet glass and carried a red line of iron through its middle. The pinhole of daylight shines through it. For an instant, the bead contains the whole bright sky.

Then the water shifts.

Its legs unfold, and it becomes a thread. The thread draws itself through a crack, lengthening without seeming to grow. Somewhere inside the stone it divides, or passes through a place where distance has folded over. On the other side of the crack, two small creatures emerge: one carrying the red line, the other nearly clear. They touch their feelers together, taste the warmth between them, and move on.

There are many such lives, though none keeps a single shape long enough to be counted. Some graze the mineral films from the walls with combing mouthparts. Some drift through the pools with their limbs tucked close, steering by faint changes in heat. Beneath the flakes, the larger ones lie folded in patient heaps, each body seeming to contain several bodies arranged back to back. When the pools rise, they loosen and climb. When the water drains, they stream down the stone, slipping into newly opened basins. Their packed movements make a shimmer, like one creature considering where to go.

They do not seem to recognize individuals as we would. They recognize warmth, salt, the taste of a familiar stain, the pressure of a body passing nearby. A neighbor is perhaps a pattern that returns: six legs at the edge of a pool; a ribbon crossing a ledge; a faint tremor in the film of water. They meet, exchange something too small to see, and separate. Their young may be shed, divided, or simply left behind when a body changes its mind about being one body. Nothing in the place is quite born once.

The cycles are their calendar. At the first swelling of a seep, the smallest grazers climb to the highest ribs, where the rising water cannot drown them. The long swimmers coil in the shallows. Those under the flakes press themselves into the stone’s narrowest shadows. Then comes the roof’s brief cooling, and the fine rain begins to gather above them. Drops swell overhead like inverted pools. The creatures wait beneath them, feeling each one through the rock. When a drop falls, they scatter—not in fear, but with the quick, practiced ease of things that have always lived under a changing sky.

They are not drawn to the daylight, exactly. They turn toward it when the water carries its warmth, and away when the bright pinhole becomes merely bright. Sometimes it appears in three places at once. The creatures pass beneath each image without hesitation. Perhaps they know that all three lights are one. Perhaps, to them, the question has no meaning.

Their oldest are the ones that have changed the most: clear bodies clouded with mineral dust, limbs worn down and grown again, red seams crossed by yellow stains. They rest in the deepest warm pools, where the stone is softest and the boundaries are least reliable. A fissure opens beside one, and the creature enters. Far off, across what seems a ravine, another pale body comes out. For a while the two hold still, tasting the same water from opposite sides of the world.

Then the ceiling cools. A drop gathers. The creatures move on.

Nothing calls to them, and they make no answer. Their abundance is a quiet occupation of every wet surface, every seam, every place where heat gathers beneath stone. When the bead slips inward and the pinhole goes dark, they do not pause. They feel the water tremble around them, and continue combing the walls for the world’s next small offering.
