The place is inhabited by a multitude that has never needed a name.

From the daylight slit they look like dust disturbed by a passing breath. Up close, each has its own blunt little certainty: feelers testing the stone, jointed legs finding purchase, a pale shell powdered with the colors of the place. They travel pressed close together, not quite touching and never quite alone. Where one body passes, another follows; where a gap opens, it is filled.

They know the chamber by feel. The shallow basin is a feeding ground. The arch holds the cooler air. The old gear is a crossroads, its central hole worn smooth by generations passing through. In the pipe’s dry throat, the smallest ones shelter from the broader, rasping grazers. They move through the human iron as through another kind of stone, without curiosity about its making.

The blind grazers work the mineral skin from the rock, rasping patiently at what little nourishment it offers. The narrow hunters follow, unfolding from cracks with a suddenness that sends the crowd scattering. For a moment, the chamber’s steady clatter breaks into sharp taps and scrapes. Then the gaps close. The little scavengers take what remains, slipping into spaces too small for any other mouth. The dead do not lie long enough to become separate from the living.

They have no leader and no single voice. Their signals pass as clicks: a pulse repeated in the roof, a tremor carried through the floor, a ripple running outward from the faint tick of cooling iron. One answering wave follows another until the whole pocket seems to speak through its feet. Perhaps none of them knows the chamber’s full shape. Together, they know every hollow in it.

At the entrance, where gray light reaches the ground, the traffic thins. A few larger bodies pause there, feelers lifted toward the weather. Farther in, the animals crowd so densely that the stone disappears. Their backs make ridges and banks; their passage wears corridors into the dust. They have turned the little chamber into a country, though no one body could see more than a corner of it.

The chamber’s cold, dry quiet is not quiet to them. It is full of footsteps, warning clicks, feeding scrapes, and the faint shifting of air. They live on what the stone gives slowly, and on one another, wasting almost nothing. The rust-red bands, the white salt, the iron fragments, the powdered basin: these are not scenery around their lives. They are the ground beneath them, the shelter over them, the food between them, the old boundaries they have spent an age learning.

The place does not so much contain them as take their shape. And when the iron ticks, the multitude answers—not as one creature, exactly, but as something that has lasted so long together that the difference is difficult to find.
