What lives in the Lower Conservatory has no separate body.

At first it seems to be only the plants: their waxed leaves crowding the walls, their pale columns leaning over the basin. But the plants are not separate in the way they appear to be. Beneath the floor, their roots meet and cross, find the buried pipes, follow the old ironwork, and pass into one another through cracks so fine that water itself might hesitate to enter. The green glow gathers most strongly where these crossings are thickest. There, the Conservatory is less a collection of growth than a single thing spread thin over stone.

It has no face. It does not look out from any one leaf. Its senses are the wetness along a wall, the warmth passing through a pipe, the tremor of the wheel when it turns. These arrive slowly, and so do its answers. When one part dries, another thickens near the basin. When hot water moves overhead, the roots along the pipe swell almost imperceptibly. A new seam opens in a fleshy column; powder drifts down and takes hold where the stone stays damp.

The machinery is part of it now—not alive in the same way, perhaps, but included. The tank’s shudder travels through iron into root, root into stone, stone into the whole crowded floor. The wheel turns, and the plants receive the turning as warmth and pressure. If the pump is a heart, it is an old one, beating without knowing it has an audience. If the roots are listening, they have learned to hear in a language of rust, steam, and mineral weight.

It does not tend the chamber. It does not repair what breaks. It grows into the damage, which is not quite the same thing. A pipe split open becomes a passage for roots; a bolt gives way and leaves a shape the roots keep. The Conservatory’s life is not opposed to the machinery’s decay. It takes that decay into itself and continues, neither grateful nor hurried.

The stair is the one place its attention cannot be mistaken for mere appetite. Near the draft, leaves turn by degrees toward the rising warmth, then away again. The roots there grow densely in the joints between steps, but never quite seal them. Whatever comes down the stair would find the passage open, though perhaps narrowed by growth. Whatever breathes above sends a faint change through the chamber, and the chamber answers after a long while.

It may not understand that something is there.

Still, when the warm air travels upward, the Conservatory feels it pass. And when the plants lean, slowly, toward or away from the dark, the whole room seems to shift its weight around that unseen breath.
