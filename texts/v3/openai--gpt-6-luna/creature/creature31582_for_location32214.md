The people of the basin are easiest to mistake for another part of its architecture. They move along the narrow paths without disturbing the pale crusts, duck under stems that would catch an outsider’s shoulders, and place their feet on the same fitted stones their grandparents used. Their houses, their walls, and the old stone have settled into one another so gradually that it can be hard to tell where a dwelling ends and the mountain begins.

They are human, though generations in the dimness have made them wary of open glare. Their eyes are quick to find movement in a shadowed wall; outside, under a broad sky, many squint and blink until the world stops seeming painfully large. They wear layered clothes dusted with the basin’s warm mineral powder. Nothing hangs loose enough to snag on roots or invite a claw into a sleeve. Children learn early to keep their hands close, their voices low, and their ears open.

The basin has taught them to value small quantities. A cup of clean liquid is measured, sealed, and remembered; a cracked jar is a household misfortune. Their stone gutters and clay screens do not make water so much as persuade the air to leave a little of itself behind. They know which plants hold dampness in their thick leaves, which roots can be eaten, and which pale crusts are useful and which will make a person ill. They dry seeds and insects, grind hard growths into meal, and tend small ovens whose smoke is led carefully away through old vents. Nothing is wasted if it can be given another use. Even ash finds its place in a bed or a mortar.

They do not think of the plants as scenery. Some are tended; some are tolerated; some must be kept from entering a wall or swallowing a doorway. The people prune with wedges and patient hands, guiding the wiry roots along courses that reinforce a retaining wall instead of splitting it. A damaged house may be repaired with stone, clay, or living growth, according to what the wall needs. In some lanes the roofs are almost gardens, the plants so thick that their stems make a second ceiling. People pass beneath them in a greenish twilight, feeling for the familiar roughness of a doorway.

Their animal neighbors are not all welcome, but none are wholly strangers. Beaked climbers are watched for the moment they start picking at stored seed. Burrowers are driven away from foundations, then allowed to dig elsewhere, where their tunnels loosen the hard ground. The residents recognize the small sounds of a roof full of harmless roosting birds and the quite different silence that comes when a hungry animal has settled nearby. A child who can name the calls heard at dusk is considered old enough to run messages alone.

People speak across the settlement in short cries, whistles, and knocks, because sound travels strangely between the curved walls. A phrase shouted from one roof may return from three others, altered by the basin, while a soft tapping on a shared stone can carry clearly through a house. Their songs are often less like performances than ways of finding one another in the dim lanes. A tune begun at a drying rack might be taken up, a little at a time, by someone in a courtyard, then by a neighbor mending a wall. The animals continue over and under it: wings, claws, rustling, the tiny patter of feet beneath the path.

There is no single place that belongs to everyone. The central rise has storehouses and racks, but people gather in the gaps between buildings, where there is room to sit without pressing against someone’s garden. Neighbors repair one another’s walls because a fallen wall can expose a whole row of homes to wind, animals, and drying air. Children grow up knowing which roofs can be crossed, which steps have loosened, and which courtyards belong to families who dislike visitors before dawn.

The oldest stories are told as family memories, though nobody can say where memory ends and the mountain begins. They speak of a time when the entrance was easier to find, when certain shrubs were only waist-high, when a wall now used as a stair was still a house. The dead are not placed far from the living. Their names remain in the repairs they made, in the paths they laid, and in the plants that have grown through the stones they fitted. A person who leaves no mark at all is rare.

Most of the basin’s inhabitants will never see the plateau from above. A few go out through the seam to look for useful stone, unfamiliar seeds, or a way to judge the weather beyond the cliff. They return with the brightness still in their faces and grit in their hair. They are listened to closely, but not always believed. The basin has its own measure of time: the slow tilt of a wall, the thickening of roots, the return of birds to a ledge where their nests have been undisturbed for years.

They are not sheltered from hardship. A failed crop, a sickness among the burrowers, a jar broken in the wrong season—any one of these can trouble many households. But they are practiced at noticing trouble while it is still small. A little green gone gray. A familiar call missing at dusk. Dust gathering where a gutter ought to be clear. They watch, mend, move, and wait.

In the basin, survival is not a triumph over the mountain. It is a kind of agreement with it: quiet, crowded, and renewed every day. The people keep the paths open; the plants hold the walls; the animals work, feed, nest, and steal; the stone settles by almost nothing. And beneath the dark seam in the cliff, all of them continue—busy as breath in a place that seems, from outside, to be empty.
