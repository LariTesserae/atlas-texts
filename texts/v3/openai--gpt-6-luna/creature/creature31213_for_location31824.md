The first things to notice are the small ones.

The red growths above the waterline are not plants, though they root in cracks and drink from the stone. They flex when the mountain rises, tightening their slick cords around fresh edges. When the lake gives its single booming note, each one bows toward the water. Afterward, a faint red stain may appear on the rock some distance away, as if the growth had briefly cast a shadow in another place.

The translucent fronds beneath the surface are more like listeners. They turn toward a voice even when no sound can reach them. Their delicate branches gather around the shape of a word, then drift apart, holding it for a while. Travelers who speak beside them sometimes hear the word again much later, from under a different stretch of water, pronounced in a voice that has never belonged to anyone.

The fish live in the deep cuts. Pale and eyeless, they move as though following a current that is not there. They do not seem to hunt. A fish will hang motionless beside a cliff of black rock while water streams upward past it; then, without turning, it will vanish into a depth too narrow to admit its body. Now and then one surfaces with a bright mineral seam running through its flank. The seam fades as the fish sinks.

The narrow-winged creatures are harder to see. They arrive only when the roar is at its loudest, skimming the water with their wings held rigid and their long bodies trailing behind. They leave no wake. Their shadows, if they cast any, move ahead of them. Some say they feed on the booming notes. Others think they are trying to catch something the sound has knocked loose from the deep.

But the basin’s strangest inhabitants are the ones that return.

A footstep taken on one shore may come back from the opposite ridge. At first it is only a sound: the same scrape of a boot, with a slight delay. Then, if the traveler stays, the returning step begins to fall where no traveler stands. A heel presses into wet ash. A shoulder brushes a wall. A breath comes from beneath the lake.

These repetitions sometimes gather themselves into figures. They are not ghosts, exactly, nor copies. One may have the posture of a person who crossed the shore an hour before, but its arms are too long, or its head is turned toward a sound that has not yet happened. Its surface shivers like reflected water. When it walks, the footfalls arrive first, and the body follows.

The figures do not speak, though they may learn the motions of speech. They appear at the entrances of the little stone rooms, facing inward. Inside, the walls’ grooves alter around them. The marks do not record words so much as the pressure of a presence: where it stood, how it leaned, which way it listened. After a storm, the grooves change again, and the figure is gone.

Some travelers believe the rooms were built for these beings. Others think the rooms are what the beings remember of being built. Their interiors remain dry even beneath the lake, and on certain nights a soft tapping comes from inside the walls—not loud enough to rise above the roar, but regular enough to sound like someone waiting to be let in.

There is, perhaps, something larger beneath the basin. No one has seen it. It may be the source of the booming note, or the note may be the only part of it that can reach the air. Before the sound comes, the fish go still, the fronds fold their branches, and the returning figures turn toward the deepest water. For one brief instant the lake flattens, as if listening too.

Then the summit answers with its great note, and everything that has been remembered by the basin is loosened: a pebble, a footstep, the outline of a room, the shape of a person standing alone at the shore. The water takes these things up and carries them across itself, not toward any destination, but toward whatever in the depths has called them back.
