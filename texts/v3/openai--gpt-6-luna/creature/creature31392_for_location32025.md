At first, you might think the Crown Sill is empty except for its machinery.

Then, beside the pumping house, a curtain stirs where there is no wind. A kettle is warming on the little iron stove. Someone has hung a pair of patched trousers over the cable that pins down the roof. The trousers are too small to belong to a worker grown old in the usual way, and too large for any child who could comfortably cross the open ground.

The person who lives there is called Sella. Or that is what she says when asked, though no one has asked in a long time. She sleeps in the pumping house on a narrow cot, with one hand often resting on the wall. The pump’s knock comes through the boards and into her palm. She can tell by its rhythm when the pressure is wrong, when the spring is running hot, or when a pipe has begun to clog beneath the grass. She makes repairs with wire, old bolts, and pieces of metal whose original purposes have been forgotten. She keeps a notebook of these things, though the pages swell with damp and the ink runs.

Sella is not much of a gardener. She never planted the ferns or the red flowers, and she pulls no weeds. But she moves stones when they pinch a root, and lays scraps of cloth over seedlings during the coldest nights. If asked why, she shrugs. The plants have made their own arrangements around her: grasses part at the pumping-house door; moss grows thick on the step where she sits to mend her boots. Nothing grows over the narrow strip of ground she walks each morning, though she never cuts it back.

There are other inhabitants, though less easy to name. In the shallow pools live tiny, pale things with jointed legs, almost transparent except where their bodies hold a faint blue sheen. They shelter beneath mineral crust and vanish whenever a shadow passes over them. Sella says they were there before her. She does not say how she knows.

The things in the pools seem to travel through the Sill by means of its wetness. A few appear in the spring channel; one was once found inside a length of pipe that had been capped at both ends. They eat the reddish film that gathers on the iron, and perhaps something else. When the pump pauses, they gather in the basins and hold still, their feelers raised, as if listening for the next knock.

Sometimes Sella finds a wet print on the floor of the pumping house, although the door has been closed and the ground outside is dry. Sometimes the water in her kettle ripples before the generator changes pitch. She has stopped marking such events in her notebook. It is not that she thinks them ordinary. It is that they have become part of the work.

And the work, on the Sill, has no clear beginning or end. Sella keeps the pump going; the spring keeps rising. The plants press into the fractures; the stone opens new ones. The little creatures travel through the damp, and the damp carries their traces across the ground. Now and then, at the rim, Sella hears the waterfall below before she can see it. She pauses, listening. In those moments, the Sill seems to be listening back.

If you ask who lives there, Sella will say she does.

If you stay long enough, you begin to suspect she is only the one who answers.
