At first, it seems that no one lives in the city.

Then a droplet hangs beneath a bridge, trembling without falling. A tower shifts by the width of a hair. Far across the chamber, a sealed doorway grows deeper—not opening, exactly, but making room.

These are the city’s inhabitants, though they have no proper bodies. They live in the intervals between things: in the slant of a street, the pressure beneath a stair, the space a wall leaves for a door. The mountain’s warmth and the city’s hidden fields give them shape. One may be a cool patch crossing a terrace; another, a pause in the metallic ticking. They are easiest to sense in the moments after a building changes, when the air seems to be considering its new arrangement.

They call themselves nothing. If they have names, they are made of distances.

They travel by altering the measures of the city. A lane lowers gently under one of them; a bridge takes longer to cross. They can pass through a blank façade as easily as through open air, but they prefer the narrow streets, where every angle gives them something to feel. When several gather, the city becomes unusually still. Droplets stop falling. The far walls sharpen, as if the chamber has drawn a careful breath.

They do not speak, but they answer. A whisper carried across the terraces may be returned as a small change in gravity: a bead rolling uphill, a loose stone settling where it ought not to fit. Water is important to them. They collect it in hidden cisterns, not to drink, but to keep the city’s memory cool. In the warm phases they move through the walls, guiding the seepage along channels finer than a hair. In the cold, they wait beneath the suspended droplets, listening to the mountain.

They are neither builders nor caretakers, though they do both. They adjust a stair by one degree when the mountain shifts. They deepen a doorway when the stone behind it is nearly ready. Their work is patient, and not always sensible by human standards. A whole block may be turned slightly toward a fracture that has not yet appeared.

There are fewer of them near the empty square. Or perhaps they are harder to distinguish there, where the city’s geometry thins and the mountain is still rough. They gather at the sealed buildings and attend to the dark plates. Nothing opens. Nothing refuses. Beyond them, where the new stone glows at its broken edges, the inhabitants leave a space unmeasured.

Sometimes, during a warm tide, the square’s shadows move in the wrong direction. The city has made room for someone.

The mountain has not finished deciding who.
