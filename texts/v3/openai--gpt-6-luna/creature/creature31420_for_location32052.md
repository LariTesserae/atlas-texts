The first inhabitants a traveler notices are usually not the ones they see. Something is moving in the stalks: a succession of hollow knocks traveling from stem to stem, then a shiver high in the leaves. The small creatures of the upland have learned to live inside its noise. They tap, rasp, pulse their throats, and send calls through the hollow plants; each species has its own pitch and rhythm. A warning can cross a basin before the animal that gave it has moved at all.

Bright, many-eyed climbers cling to the stalks in clusters, their toes spread wide over the smooth green skin. When the sun is high, they flatten themselves against the shaded side and become nearly invisible. At dusk they gather on the crowns and call in overlapping bursts, throats swelling like little lanterns. Far above them, the soft-bodied pollinators drift from one spray of leaves to another. They are heavy enough to bow the stalks, yet cross the gaps with slow, buoyant strokes of their broad wings. Pollen dusts their backs in colored bands. A whole forest may change its flowers’ timing to meet their passage.

At ground level, the mat of roots is full of quick lives: pale runners that vanish into leaf-folds, burrowers that leave only small fans of fresh soil, and patient animals that wait in the tunnels beneath the woven surface. Some hunt by scent. Others feel the weight of a foot through the root-web, and know whether it belongs to prey, predator, or the slow passage of a herd. In the clearings, where cover is scarce, the hunters lie as still as scraps of sun-bleached stalk. A traveler may see one and take it for a fallen bundle of leaves. It is often the prey that notices first.

The great grazers arrive in bands that seem to have no beginning or end. They are long-legged, deep-bodied animals with broad, cushioned feet that spread their weight across the living ground. Their heads swing low through the pale growth, cropping it close; their upper lips are tough and nimble, able to strip leaves from a stalk without tearing the whole plant down. The older animals carry seed in the cracks of their hides and pollen along their flanks. Their paths recur over generations, pressed into shallow arcs that the plants gradually learn to accommodate.

The herds are not silent, though their voices are easy to mistake for weather. They communicate through low calls and through the ground: a forefoot stamping once, a whole band answering with a measured rumble. Calves walk in the sheltered middle, learning the routes by following the changing taste of the plants and the remembered shape of the land. When the herd reaches a ridge, scavenger flocks lift behind it in a dark, wheeling cloud. Small animals follow the slow advance, feeding on what the grazers disturb. For a time, the clearing is crowded with life; then the band moves on, and the pale ground lies open again.

The predators depend on that openness and on its opposite. In the stalk-fields they use the root-tunnels, where a body can move unseen beneath the surface. In the clearings they use stillness. Their backs resemble dry litter; their eyes, when open, are narrow slits under ridged brows. They do not chase far. They wait for a young animal to lag, or for the edge of the herd to loosen near a water hollow. The hunt may last an hour without a sound anyone can identify as a hunt. Then the clearing breaks into running shapes, and the stalks ring with alarm.

Water makes its own gatherings. When dew has filled the folded leaves and stem-cups, tiny animals drink from the edges, holding their bodies sideways to keep from sliding in. Larger ones come later, leaving prints dark enough to read after they have gone. The grazers know which root-hollows will still hold water at noon. So do the predators. A wet hollow is a meeting place and a danger, and the paths toward one are worn not only by feet but by generations of caution.

People live lightly among these multitudes. Their shelters stand on the ridges, above the dampest ground, where the wind keeps insects from gathering too thickly. Some are little more than platforms of split stalks, roofed with overlapping leaves; others have a narrow watchtower from which a person can look over the tops of the nearer growth. They cut the hard outer rind of the stalks for tools, and take water from the joints, but leave the root-banks standing. A shelter is built to be abandoned. The upland will cover it soon enough.

The people learn to listen before they learn to travel. A stalk can carry a sound from far away, but it can also alter it: a call may come back lower, or from the wrong direction, or with the long hollow note that means the wind has entered a mature stand. Experienced travelers distinguish the ordinary booming of leaves from the foot-thunder of a herd. They also know that silence has a shape. If the insects stop rasping in one patch, they give it a wide berth.

They do not own the paths. They borrow them from the herds, then clear them briefly through the growth, and lose them again when the stalks lean together. They set no lasting boundary around a spring. At most, they leave a marker—a pale strip of rind tied high on a stem, visible from a ridge if the light is right. Other people understand what it means: water here, recently; approach quietly; take what you need.

By evening, the creatures become easier to hear and harder to judge. Calls separate across the dark. Something heavy brushes the upper leaves. A herd, still distant, sends a tremor through the ground. In the shelters, people sit close beneath their leaf roofs and listen to the plateau arranging itself around them. They are only one small kind of life in a country full of lives. The stems speak, the root-mat carries footsteps, and somewhere beyond sight the great grazers keep to their old road.
