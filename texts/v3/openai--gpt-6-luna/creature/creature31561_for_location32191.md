The people who live there have learned to recognize the city by touch.

They know which stones tilt when the water rises, which stair treads stay slick even in a dry spell, and where a wall gives off a faint warmth because the sun has found it through a crack above. They walk with their hands free and their attention divided: listening to the channels, watching the pools, feeling for the day’s weather in the dampness of a doorframe. Newcomers look up for the sky. Residents look first at the water.

They are not a silent people, exactly, but they speak without trying to fill the cavern. A word passed across a room is enough; a shout is saved for danger, or for calling someone home through the terraces. Children learn the shapes of the place before they learn all its names. They are sent to fetch water along familiar routes and told not merely where to go, but what to notice: a stream that has changed color, a new crack above the eastern steps, the smell of earth before a rise in the pools. When they are small, they fear the dark parts. Later, they come to understand that darkness has its own inhabitants and its own uses.

Most homes have a high room and a low room. The low one is for work, washing, storage, and the things that can be moved quickly. The high one is for sleeping when the rain comes hard. Mats roll up; baskets hang from pegs; wooden stools are light enough for a child to carry upstairs. Nothing precious is kept where the water can claim it without warning. The people are fond of beautiful things, but their beauty tends to be useful: a carved lintel, a bright woven shoulder-cloth, a bowl glazed dark as a pool. They mend what can be mended and let the damp leave its marks. A house that looks untouched is either new or uninhabited.

Their days turn around light. The sun does not rise in the cavern so much as arrive in pieces, at different hours on different walls. People know when a certain terrace will brighten enough for seedlings, when the western pool will hold a reflection, when the narrow strip of daylight reaches the steps where old neighbors sit to sort leaves. They carry their work toward the light when they can. Where they cannot, they learn to work by memory: fingers separating roots, a knife finding the grain of wet timber, a familiar path crossed without seeing its far end.

They grow food wherever the stone permits it. Gardens spill from roof edges and fill the spaces between houses; edible leaves thrive beside moss, and small fruits redden in the brighter pockets. The best soil is passed from one bed to another in baskets, mixed with rotted leaves and the dark sediment left by the pools. People know which plants tolerate shade and which must be coaxed toward an opening in the roof. They also know that a garden is never entirely theirs. Insects arrive first, birds after them, then the occasional hungry animal following the birds. A harvest is counted with some of it already given away.

The creatures of the cavern are not treated as ornaments or omens. They are neighbors, though not always convenient ones. A fish in a channel is a sign that the water is still running clean. A newt under a step is left alone; people learn to place their feet elsewhere. Bats are given the high cracks, and families whose roofs lie beneath a roost keep lids on their pots. Birds may be encouraged into certain trees and driven from others, depending on what is ripening. The fox is watched carefully, but not hunted without need. It belongs to the outer lanes and the long twilight between the cavern and the forest above.

There are old people who remember when the city’s upper terraces were bare stone, when the roof-falls had not yet grown green and every new channel had to be watched for sudden collapse. Their memories are kept not in books alone, but in the names of places: the Stair That Went, the Pool Before the Flood, the Wall Where the First Fern Took Root. Children hear these names and imagine events older than they are. They are not entirely wrong. The cavern changes slowly enough to seem permanent, then all at once enough to make permanence foolish.

When the rain gathers above, the city becomes attentive. Word moves from terrace to terrace. Mats are lifted; baskets are carried up; drains are cleared of fallen leaves. There is no bell to summon people, only the sound of water strengthening and the sight of neighbors beginning their preparations. No one argues with the rising pools. They have seen the water take a lane and leave another untouched, climb a stair and withdraw from a doorway without breaking it. They make room for what cannot be governed.

At night, or beneath a heavy sky, the people draw close together. They cook in shared spaces where several households can make use of one fire, and sit in the dimness with their damp clothes steaming around them. Someone tells a story; someone else corrects the ending. A child falls asleep against a wall warm from the day’s thin sunlight. Above them, drops continue to gather and fall. The city does not go quiet so much as settle into its smaller sounds: a spoon against a pot, water under the paving, a bat’s wing brushing the air.

They are a practical people, but not a hard one. They leave gaps in walls for the animals that pass through. They plant flowers where no food will grow. They give directions by sunlight, share steps and channels, and remember who lives in the houses that must be emptied first when the pools rise. Their lives are full of inconvenience, damp, and careful work. Still, they do not speak of the cavern as a place they are enduring. It is home: unfinished, generous in patches, dangerous in familiar ways.

And when the light falls through the roof in pale columns, they pause—not all at once, and not for long. A hand still turns a leaf; someone still carries water. But faces lift as the brightness reaches the roofs, and for a little while the whole city seems to be growing toward it.
