At first, the hollow seems to be inhabited by small things that have found a warm place to feed.

The pale crabs crowd the channels, lifting their clear legs over one another as they pick at the mineral edges. The winged creatures drink from the fissures, their tuning-fork hum rising and falling with the water. The rust-colored fans fold at the passage of a shadow. Beneath the shelves, the striped animal moves with the patience of something that knows every opening, even the ones that have not yet appeared.

But the creatures do not behave as if the hollow were merely a place to live. They gather before the stone shifts. The crabs leave a channel just before it closes. The wings lift together when a new ridge begins to rise. The larger animal turns toward the floor and waits, claws braced against the rock, as if listening for an instruction too deep for ears.

Perhaps they are listening to the rods. Perhaps the rods are listening to them.

There is a life here that has no single body. It runs in the warm water, in the exposed silver roots, in the little feet testing a fresh edge. It learns each new shape as soon as the stone takes it. A vanished channel is not a loss for long: the crabs find another route, the wings follow the new heat, and the striped animal slips into the dark beneath the next shelf. What looks like instinct may be the hollow remembering itself through many small bodies.

And the hollow, in turn, seems to be learning from them. Its water does not simply drain; it lingers where they gather, or travels toward a crack they have begun to favor. New ridges rise like the first knuckles of a hand. The blue pools brighten when the rods pulse. After each shudder, everything scatters—and then, slowly, comes back to inspect what has been made.

The three towers stand apart from this busyness, smooth and unweathered. They may be caretakers, measuring how quickly the hollow is becoming a world. They may be restraints, keeping its growth within the rim. Or they may be part of the same unfinished organism: hard, silent senses sunk into the stone, watching the place from inside its own bones.

Whatever lives here is very young. It has not yet decided whether it is a colony, an animal, or a landscape. For now it is warm water and clicking feet, a hidden shoulder passing under black shelves, a shiver traveling through stone. When the rods give their faint pulse, it answers with a roar—and, afterward, holds still in the thickening mist, feeling the new shape of itself.
