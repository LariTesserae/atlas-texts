At first, there seems to be no one here.

Then, after enough time has passed for the silence to become familiar, the visitor notices a second shadow on the pale floor. It lies a little ahead of them, though there is nothing between them and the dim light to cast it. When they stop, it stops. When they turn, it does not quite turn with them.

The inhabitants are called the measures, though no one living gave them that name. They are not figures so much as the places where the basin’s disagreements gather. A measure may appear as a narrow darkness standing beside the black frame, or as a faint brightening in the air above the broken ring. Sometimes it has the suggestion of shoulders, a head, long arms held at its sides. Sometimes it is only a patch of dust that refuses to settle.

They have no fixed number. Three may be seen at once, or one, or none. There is no sense that they arrive. A measure is present when the distance between two stones begins to feel occupied.

They do not walk across the basin. Instead, the basin subtly revises itself around them. A ridge seems to have drawn nearer; a shadow lengthens toward the center. The crystals turn their sharpest faces, almost imperceptibly, toward whatever shape is forming. Then the shape thins, and the world resumes its earlier arrangement.

The measures are not guardians. They do not bar the way or punish trespass. They make no distinction between a visitor and a fallen shard, except that a visitor brings more contradictions with them. A person’s steps have one length and their memory another. Their breath rises, then slips sideways into the stone. Their instruments insist on a distance the eye cannot find. The measures attend to these discrepancies as if they were sounds in a language too slow to speak aloud.

Near the broken ring, one may sometimes see dust resting along the inner wall. If watched long enough, the dust draws itself into a thin, upright line. The line holds for a while, then loosens. No face appears. No hand reaches out. Yet those who have stood beside it often leave with the impression that they were being counted—not as a threat, but as a new weight placed on a scale.

The measures do not seem to eat, sleep, or remember in any human fashion. Perhaps they are as old as the chamber; perhaps they are what the chamber has become by keeping its rules for so long. The black frame, the pale ring, the rod passing through stone: these may be tools, or markers, or simply the places where the measures can be most nearly seen. No one has found a way to tell.

They are patient without waiting for anything. If spoken to, they offer no reply. Still, sound behaves differently near them. A word may vanish before it reaches the far shelf, while its meaning lingers in the speaker’s mind with an unfamiliar precision. Once, a visitor whispered, “How far?” The nearest measure—if it was nearest—seemed to incline its head.

Afterward, the ridge was still distant. The instruments still disagreed. The visitor could not say whether an answer had been given.

The measures remain among the salt and black dust, keeping the hollow in its long, exact balance. They do not make the place less strange. They are the quiet reason its strangeness does not spread.
