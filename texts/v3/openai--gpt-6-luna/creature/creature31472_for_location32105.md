What lives there does not have a shape that can be separated from the place.

At first it seems to be only the pale vegetation: stalks in the cracks, soft mats on the shelves, the long hanging fronds trembling to a rhythm no ear can pick out. But follow one root and it vanishes into stone; follow the seam where the stone flakes away and, far below, another root emerges. The plants are not rooted in the mountain so much as threaded through it. Their distant stems may belong to the same living body, though no one has found where that body begins or ends.

It has no face. Its attention is slow enough to resemble geology. A visitor stands still on a ledge, and over the hours the nearby fronds incline by a fraction. The stems do not reach or recoil. They simply arrange themselves around the presence, as if making room for a new pressure in the world. Beneath them, the rock gives a nearly imperceptible shudder. The sound continues unchanged.

Perhaps the sound is what lives here.

It fills every hollow and presses through every ridge, a vast, even vibration rising from the unfinished ground. The plants answer it in tiny motions: a quiver in one tassel, a bend in a jointed stalk, a faint ripple traveling through a mat of silver-veined growth. Their response is too exact to be chance and too slow to be conversation. They may be listening. They may be transmitting. They may be the only parts of the place capable of hearing the world being made.

The stone, too, seems alive, though not in any familiar sense. It shifts without falling, remembers shapes it has not yet taken, and sometimes presents the same mountain with two different histories. Whatever stirs beneath it has no need to burrow. It is already everywhere the land is going to be. The cliffs are its thoughts, perhaps; the gullies, unfinished sentences. The suspended stones are moments it has not decided how to complete.

The white grit inside the roofless shelter has not been touched. The presence—if there is one—does not enter. It may know the structure is there. It may have grown around it, accommodating the black stones as it accommodates every other irregularity. The ring of grit could be a boundary, or an offering, or merely a shape that the world has not yet learned to alter.

Nothing hunts here. Nothing flees. The plants do not seem to feed on one another, and there are no small lives hidden in the mats. There is only this patient abundance: growth passing through stone, stone lifting beneath a sound too large to break, and a landscape that may be less a home than a body still discovering what it can become.
