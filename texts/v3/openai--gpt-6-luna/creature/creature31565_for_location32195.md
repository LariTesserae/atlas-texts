Nothing with a face lives here. Nothing needs one.

The inhabitants are the growths: reeds, fronds, roots, fungal fans, and the mats that drift over the drowned ground. They crowd together without competing in any familiar way. A reed forest may lean aside for a passing current, then knit itself shut behind it. Leaves open toward a light that has no direction. Roots pass through stone and mud as if looking for something, though they never stop when they find it.

They are not plants in the ordinary sense. They attend to pressure, vibration, and the slow rearrangement of the land. When the turbine knocks beneath the water, the reeds answer with a clatter. When a bank splits, pale fronds turn toward the fresh edge. Their movements are too gradual to look like decisions until you have watched them for a long time. Then you begin to suspect that the whole thicket has noticed you—and that noticing is not the same as seeing.

The machines belong to the population too, though no one knows whether they were built, inherited, or grown into their present shapes. A pump works without fuel; a gear turns with no shaft to drive. Vines swell through the station’s pipes, and the station hums back through their roots. Iron takes on a skin of mineral bloom. Where water passes through a broken mechanism, it sometimes holds its shape in the air, a hard column trembling like a stalk. The machinery does not serve the growth. The growth does not overrun the machinery. They persist together, joined by a purpose neither could explain.

There is no single body to find. The inhabitants are spread through the flooded country and gathered nowhere in particular: in the clatter of reeds, the stubborn hum beneath the water, the bright beads of rain hanging among the leaves. Their attention travels by current. A shift at the center can make the farthest roots tighten; a hook swinging on the crane may set a mat of greenery folding miles away. Perhaps the place is communicating. Perhaps it is only moving. Here, the difference is difficult to keep.

Sometimes the land itself seems to join them. Fresh stone rises, clean-edged and black, and the forest takes hold before the water has settled around it. Silt gathers into ridges. Walls assemble from drifting pieces, fit imperfectly together, then sink or rise as the channels change. The inhabitants do not build so much as continue: plant matter around iron, iron against stone, stone lifting through water. Every new surface becomes another place for something to grow.

They do not hunt, nest, or flee. They leave no tracks that can be trusted. Yet the mud fills with impressions—great hollows, tiny marks climbing tree trunks—and the leaves turn toward sounds that have no source. Perhaps these are traces of the inhabitants moving through a world too large to contain them. Perhaps they are only the shape of the water after it passes.

At the center, where the slabs have newly broken through, their scattered motions draw together. The forest bows and straightens. The fountains rise silently between the stones. Beneath them, the machine begins its deep metallic cough, and the whole flooded region shifts a few inches east.

Nothing comes forward to meet you.

The reeds turn their leaves toward the pallid brightness. The water gathers its force. And the place, immense and crowded, goes on becoming.
