The first thing to notice is that nothing in the hollow is hiding.

The blind, many-legged things show pale joints through their shell-thin backs as they thread the plant mats. They move along the same routes, over and under the same leaves, feeling their way through the salts with feathery mouths. When a larger shadow crosses them, they do not scatter. Their bodies tighten; their little feet continue to test the ground. They are not fearless. They simply have nowhere else to go.

The ceiling-clingers hang head-down in the mineral cracks, each one pale as the inside of a shell. Their hooked feet grip stone too sharply broken for anything so delicate. Their mouths open and shut without sound, while the trilling rises from somewhere deeper in the hollow. A few have turned toward the entrance. Their faces are smooth, except for the narrow dark slits where eyes might be. They appear to be listening with their whole bodies.

Among the fronds, the grazers sit almost indistinguishably from the plants. Their broad, fringed mouths rest against the leaves. Fine cilia comb the lacquer-dark surfaces, taking something from them—or perhaps giving something back. They are squat and heavy, yet their folds and layered hides make them look like heaps of shed growth. Only the steady movement of their jaws betrays them. Their eyes, when visible, are small and milky. They do not reflect the dim light. They seem instead to have kept some other light, one the hollow no longer offers.

The larger animals belong to the open ground. They lie where the salt gives way to black stone, folded so closely into themselves that a passerby might mistake them for dried leaves or discarded skins. Each has a long, jointed limb tucked against its body. One has the shape of a hand. Another, near the low wall, has the blunt end of a digging tool. When an intruder approaches, a seam opens in the hide and an eye appears: wet-looking, lidless, and black. It fixes on the newcomer without shining. The animal does not rise. Its attention is enough.

There is a pecking order here, though no one has ever seen it established. The small creatures move freely through the dark plants but rarely cross the bare salt. The grazers occupy the shelter of the fronds. The folded animals keep to the open patches, where they can be seen from every part of the hollow. None hunts. None flees. The larger eyes watch the smaller lives as if watching a fire that will never go out.

The plants do not feed on water. Their roots press into the pale seams, drinking the mineral breath that passes through the stone. Near those seams, the leaves grow thickest and darkest. Their undersides are strangely pale, almost luminous; the dimness gathers there and clings. Some fronds lie flat over the salt, while others rise in rigid fans, jointed stems locked in graceful, woody angles. Their leaves do not turn toward the light. They turn toward the warm rock. If touched, they hold their shape beneath the fingers. If broken, they give up their peppery smell of heated stone and bruised green, but no sap, no bead of moisture, nothing that could be called a wound.

The calls belong to all of them, though it is difficult to say which creature makes which sound. The small ones trill and rasp; the ceiling-clingers click; somewhere beneath the floor, a low hum enters the noise and never quite leaves it. The sound does not rise into a song or fall into silence. It is a single, layered note, crowded with tiny variations, as though every living thing in the hollow were holding its own part of the same breath.

Among the plants, where the calls can be separated, each voice has a pitch of its own. Some are high and dry as a fine saw drawn through wood. Some are low enough to feel in the teeth. But each voice repeats itself exactly. Nothing answers. Nothing calls back. They do not need to: the sound fills every gap before it can become a gap.

For all their alertness, the inhabitants do not seem to expect anything. They have the attentiveness of animals listening for a change in the weather, but no weather comes. Their jaws keep working. Their leaves quiver at the tips. A many-legged creature tests the same grain of salt with its feet, withdraws, and tests it again. The hollow is full of activity without consequence. A visitor can feel the animals notice them; can even feel the larger eye following them across the bare ground. Yet the attention does not become pursuit, or fear, or welcome. It remains attention, perfectly present and perfectly unchanged.

Perhaps the three stone flakes were placed by one of the folded animals. Perhaps the small wall was made by a hand like the one tucked beneath that bundle of hide. The chipped cup could have held salt, or a seed, or some liquid that no longer exists here. The neat fan of flakes has the composure of an offering, but there is no sign of whom it was meant to reach. No paw has disturbed it. No dust has dulled its edges.

The inhabitants pass around these objects as they pass around the bare salt: without touching them, without avoiding them in any visible way. The wall and the cup have no more age than the creatures do. They belong to the same unspent instant. Whatever made them is absent, but absence, here, cannot deepen into loss.

From the entrance, the noise presses against the chest. Inside the groves it becomes intimate: the small clicks of hidden mouths, the dry rasp of fronds against one another, the soft working of the grazers’ jaws. Sometimes, looking up, one sees the scattered points overhead. They are too close together to be stars. One has the unmistakable shape of an open animal eye.

The creatures do not look up.

They keep their places among the plants and mineral cracks, alert to every sound, every warmth, every presence. They are not asleep, and they are not waiting to wake. They live here as a flame lives in a fixed image of fire: bright in its own way, wholly alive to the eye, and never consuming what it has become.
