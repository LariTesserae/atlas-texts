At first, it seems the town is inhabited by whatever happens to be small enough to fit inside it: mites nosing along the water-film, shrimp flickering through the basins, snails following their shining old roads. A frog might sit at the pool’s edge for hours, its throat swelling and settling like a slow, silent bell. A moth tumbles from a crack and folds itself into the shape of a dead leaf. Nothing announces itself as a citizen.

But watch long enough, and the place begins to have a people.

The water-mites keep to the bright skin on the pools, grazing in close, shifting lines. The shrimp work the channels beneath them, stirring silt from the drains with quick, transparent feet. When a passage clogs, the current changes first; then the shrimp gather where it has slowed, nosing at the blockage until water slips through again. Snails tend the walls in a less hurried way. Their trails cross the old trails, and their shells rasp gently over loose clay, smoothing a ledge here, sealing a crack there. No one calls this work. It is simply what happens when the town is being lived in.

The larvae are the roof-makers. They hang beneath leaf canopies, still as seeds, until the air changes. Then they move, drawing thin, damp threads between one fold of leaf and another. Their work is delicate and difficult to see, but it holds the little chambers together. Moths shelter in the finished roofs. Sometimes, when a roof sags or a water-drop gathers too heavy beneath it, a moth slips away and the larvae begin again.

The frogs are not builders, though the others make room around them. They wait at the pool’s rim, listening through their bodies. A foot on the upper soil, a root shifting, the distant pressure of rain: each arrives as a tremor before it arrives as sound. At such times the frogs become very still, and stillness passes outward from them. The mites stop grazing. The shrimp draw beneath the stones. Even the snails seem to pause, their feelers folded close. Nothing has to tell them what has gone by above. They have felt it together.

Their speech is made of disturbances. A shrimp’s flick against a reed stem sends a faint shiver through the walkway. A snail crossing a marker leaves a brightening track. A frog’s throat gives one almost inaudible pulse. The moths communicate by shifting in the narrow passages, opening or closing a space with the soft touch of their wings. To an outsider, these are accidents: a ripple, a glint, a small movement vanishing into dark. To the town, they mean passage open, water rising, stranger near, roof weakened. A warning can travel the length of the hollow without a sound anyone above the ground would recognize.

There is no single keeper of the routes. Each creature knows a different town. The shrimp know the drains by current; the snails know the walls by taste and trail; the frogs know the pools by vibration. The moths know every hiding place where a body can become indistinguishable from a scrap of leaf. Together they make a map that no one could carry alone. Newcomers learn it slowly, by touch, by following another creature’s wake, by being gently turned aside from a passage that ends in water.

The town changes faster than its inhabitants can grow used to it. A root thickens through the floor, and the path bends around it. A basin is cut deeper by a new trickle, and the shrimp discover the altered current before the water-mites do. Sometimes a chamber disappears altogether, crushed into a seam. The creatures leave it without ceremony, carrying nothing that cannot cling to a body or be remembered by the dark. Later, another space opens nearby, and the town gathers there.

They do not own the hollow. They occupy its pauses: the narrow gap between two roots, the dryish ledge above a pool, the brief shelter under a curled leaf. The roots continue to grow through their streets, and the water continues to find its way down. What the inhabitants preserve is not a fixed arrangement but a practice: making room, sensing change, moving together when the ground insists.

In the dimmest chamber, where the green lichen gives just enough light to silver a shell’s edge, the town is almost visible. A shrimp turns in the shallows. A moth settles onto a roof. A snail crosses a marker, leaving a shining line behind it. At the pool’s edge, a frog opens and closes its throat.

Then the water stills. The creatures listen. Above them, rain passes over the larger world, and the root holds.
