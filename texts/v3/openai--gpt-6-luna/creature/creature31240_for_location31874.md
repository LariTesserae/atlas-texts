The first sign of a large inhabitant is not a shape, but a change in the ground.

A tremor passes underfoot—too slight to be an earthquake, too deliberate to be wind. Dust shivers in its drifts. The dark mats in the cracks draw tight against the stone. At the mouth of a fissure, a pale, six-legged creature lifts itself from stillness and turns toward a ridge many miles away.

The creatures are almost transparent, with narrow bodies and jointed limbs that seem made of frosted glass. They move only when the land moves, and even then they do not hurry. Each foot settles carefully, feeling through the stone. They leave no prints because they do not press down so much as find the existing weaknesses and pass between them. If threatened, they fold themselves into a crystal-like stillness. Travelers have stepped over them without seeing.

They are not the largest life here. They are the part that can be seen.

Far below the powder and the hard, smoothed rock, something broader travels through the high country. No one has seen its whole body. The few sightings describe a low shape passing along the lee of a ridge, long enough to cross the horizon, its back ridged with plates the same dull grey as the land. Others insist there is no body at all: only a succession of moving bulges beneath the crust, each arriving where the last has gone, as if the mountain itself were slowly turning over.

The pale creatures gather in its wake. So do the dark mats, wherever fresh stone has been exposed. They take what the upheaval makes available: trace compounds, new mineral faces, the faint warmth of rock brought up from below. The great forms may feed on the same things, or on pressures too subtle for any instrument to measure. Their passage leaves no trail that survives a season. Only the occasional smooth hollow, or a seam of stone polished in a direction that seems to have no relation to gravity.

They are old, but not in the way the mountains are old. The mountains preserve their age in their shapes; these inhabitants spend theirs in movement. They do not migrate from place to place so much as follow a sensation through the crust. Before a plain buckles, they may already be beneath it. Before dust begins to stream uphill, they turn toward the unseen source of the change. What they sense is not an approaching disaster. It is a tension becoming legible.

There are places where they stop. The black tower on the saddle is one. Pale creatures cling to its base in numbers never seen elsewhere, their limbs spread flat against the immaculate surface. The buried rings draw them too. Near an active anchor, the great forms pass more slowly, or divide around it, leaving the ground quiet enough for the small lives to feed. Whether the inhabitants recognize the machines as shelter, obstacle, or simply an unusual kind of stone is unknown.

At intervals, a fissure opens, and the pale creatures descend into it. The dark mats follow in ragged patches. For days or years afterward, nothing living is visible. Then the ground gives a single, soft shiver, and the creatures appear somewhere else—at the foot of a different ridge, at the rim of a basin that has tilted, beside a fresh scar in the stone. The great forms have moved with them, though no eye has followed.

They make no calls. The air is too thin to carry much sound, and sound would be a poor way to speak across this country. Their language, if it is language, is made of pressure: a shiver sent along a buried seam, a ridge eased by a fraction, a basin allowed to settle. A message may take a century to arrive. The answer may be the way a distant cliff falls.

Nothing suggests that they are trying to save the highlands. They do not mend the fractures or hold the mountains in place. They travel through the changes, feeding where the stone is newly broken, retreating where the strain is too great. The anchors quiet the ground around them, but the country remains restless. Even the largest inhabitant is only one more thing that has learned to live in a world that will not keep its shape.

Sometimes, at night, a thin distortion passes between the stars. Below it, the dark land gives a faint, spreading tremor. The pale creatures emerge from their cracks and face the same far horizon.

Then the tremor fades. They fold their legs beneath them. The high country becomes empty again.
