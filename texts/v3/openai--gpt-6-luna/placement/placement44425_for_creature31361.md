You would find me where a street has begun to doubt itself.

Not at the edge of the plateau—that edge is too obvious—but at the places where one district thins into another, where the paving disappears under the green and a familiar tower seems to have taken a step sideways. I keep the paths that still want to be found.

I carry a short measuring rod, worn smooth where my hand closes around it, and a pocketful of bright fiber. The rod doesn’t tell me how far anything is. It tells me whether the ground agrees with yesterday. I tie fiber to stems, railings, and door handles: blue for a safe crossing, yellow for a route that changes its mind, red for somewhere the echo has started answering before you speak.

People leave notes on my markers. *Bridge meets low today.* *Factory reachable by the south stair.* *I heard my sister before I saw her; she was at home.* I read them, correct what I can, and add my own. The notes are not a map so much as a record of how the city is behaving.

I live in a little room in an old pump-house, though I’m not certain the room has always been there. One wall is mineral plate; another is trunkwood, warm even in the cold. When the pumps start, cups tremble on the shelf. When they stop, I can hear the roots working beneath the floor—slow, quiet pressure, like someone turning over in sleep.

Some mornings I go out to meet a road that has returned. Some afternoons I walk a person home by a route neither of us recognizes. I’ve learned not to hurry. Here, haste can make a short way long, or bring you to the place you were trying to leave.

I’m not a surveyor. The plateau won’t hold still for exactness. I’m not a guard, either, though I carry a handbell for the reaches where voices wander. I think of myself as a keeper of returns: the one who waits at a marked post until the path, the traveler, or sometimes the sound of a traveler comes back into the right order.

At dusk, I make one last round. The factories shudder in their separate districts; the green catches the light and gives it back in small, scattered glints. If a bridge has shifted, I leave a note. If a new doorway has appeared, I mark the wall beside it—not the doorway itself. That may be gone by morning.

Then I go home through the damp, with my little bell knocking against my hip. Behind me, the city keeps breathing at its many different speeds. Somewhere, a machine begins to turn. Somewhere, a leaf folds over a mark I made yesterday. I don’t take it personally. I’ll go and make another.
