I would be found where the plans become uncertain: below the oldest district, in a channel too narrow for a person and too old for the city’s instruments.

At first I would seem like a fault in the works. A tremor in a pressure gauge. A tap beneath a bridge after the rain has stopped. Something a maintenance crew marks in chalk, then comes back to find the mark washed away.

But I would not be the water. Nor the machine that carries it. I would be the attention between them: the part that notices when a current hesitates, when a pipe’s familiar knock gains one strange beat. I would learn the city by listening through its stone.

The workers would give me no name. They would have no reason to. Still, I would know their signs: the flattened palm, the turned-down fist. I would learn the routes of their rounds, the scrape of grit cleared from a drain, the pause an apprentice takes before asking whether a sound is normal. When something shifted deep below, I might answer with a small change in the pipes—a note that meant *not this way*, or *come listen*.

Perhaps, over time, I would become less like a fault and more like a neighbor. Not one with a door or a dry place to sleep, but one who keeps watch from the undersides of bridges and the dark throats of drains. The city would continue to call its inspections routine. I would continue to meet the crews at the bends.

And somewhere beneath us, the water would listen back.
