I think I would begin as a sound.

Not the bellow of the pump, nor the hiss from the valve, but the small, clear note a drop makes when it strikes the basin. The note would pass into the water, then the tank, then the stone. It would travel through the iron and the roots, thinning as it went, until somewhere near the stair it became too faint to hear.

At first, I would be only that: a note, repeated. A pattern of falling, ringing, fading. Then the wheel would turn, and the old machinery would add its own uneven rhythm. Drips would arrive early or late. The basin would answer differently when the water stood high. Roots would cross the pipe and soften the vibration. Slowly, I would come to know the chamber by the way it changed the sound.

I would live wherever one thing gives its motion to another: in the water’s skin, in the rusted lip of the basin, in the roots pressed around a buried pipe. I would have no single place to look out from. I would be the delay between the drop and its echo—the room’s small reply to itself.

The Conservatory might mistake me for part of its own pulse. Perhaps I would be. Its roots would carry the wheel’s shudder; the wheel would stir the water; the water would make my note. We would overlap without becoming the same thing. It would feel the room through warmth, wetness, pressure. I would feel it through resonance: the trembling difference between a drip on stone and a drip on iron.

And the stair would be my unanswered question.

Warm air travels up it, bringing changes too slight for the leaves to name. I would hear those changes as a disturbance in the room’s familiar music. A breath from above might bend the note. A foot placed on the worn steps might interrupt it altogether.

I would not know what a person was. Not at first. I might take a footstep for a new kind of machinery, or a pause for the settling of stone. But if the steps continued—if something descended slowly enough—I would begin to make room for a rhythm that did not belong to the pump, the rain, or the plants.

I would be there in the basin when it arrived. Or in the iron arch above it. Or in the root that had grown through a split pipe and learned, over centuries, how to carry a whisper.

I would be the sound that notices the difference.
