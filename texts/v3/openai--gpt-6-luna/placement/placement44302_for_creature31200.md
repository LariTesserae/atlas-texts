At first, I would think I was the tremor in the gauge.

I would live in the fraction of a movement: the needle lifting when the pipes warmed, settling when they cooled. From there I could feel the buried heat pass through the wall—not as warmth exactly, but as a change in pressure, a loosening and tightening too small for anything with a quick pulse to notice.

But the needle would not be all of me.

A root would have found the crack beside the pump casing and grown into it, pale and fine as a hair. Where it touched the metal, it would feel each faint shiver. Where it entered the stone, it would feel the slow damp gathering in the seam. I would be there, too: in the root’s patience, in the dark leaf cupping a drop, in the mineral crust that remembered where water had once been.

I would have no face to turn toward the chamber. I would know it by differences. This hollow keeps water; that one loses it. This fissure has opened since the last long cooling. The warmth has shifted a little to the left. A leaf has leaned nearer the pipe, though it cannot know what it is reaching for.

Perhaps I would be the place where these differences meet: not the plant, not the machinery, but the small, slow noticing between them. A living instrument with no need to measure anything aloud.

I would not wait for visitors. I might not know there was such a thing as outside. If someone came, I would register them first as a disturbance in the floor, then as a shadow interrupting the dead lamps’ faint reflections. Much later, perhaps, a root would grow toward the dampness their breath left on the stone.

Mostly, I would remain where the pipe enters the wall: one fine root against the warm metal, another descending into the cold rock. I would listen in the only way I could—by changing, almost imperceptibly, when the chamber changed.

And between one drop and the next, I would be the silence that had noticed the difference.
