I would not be at the rim. I keep imagining the view, the haze, the tremendous drop—but I think I would turn back before reaching it. I’d be drawn instead to the places where the Crown folds inward: a shaded courtyard, an old wall, a seam in the stone where something small has managed to take hold.

At first I think I’d be one of the people who gathers dew before dawn, moving softly between the plants with a shallow bowl. Someone who knows which leaves to touch and which to leave alone. But the longer I imagine that walk, the less I feel like a person carrying a vessel.

I think I would be a tree.

Not one of the tallest—there are no tall trees here—but a squat, old one growing where an ancient wall passes beneath the soil. My trunk would be twisted around a block no one remembers laying. One of my roots would run through a crack in the stone; another would reach under a house, where the paving stays warm through the night. My canopy would make a patch of darkness so dense that people could rest beneath it even when the light was at its brightest.

I would learn the settlement by weight and vibration. The quick, uneven steps of children on the terrace. The patient drag of a basket. The wooden tap of someone repairing a roof. I would know the colony passing overhead by the shiver of my leaves, and the shy animals beyond the houses by the way the ground seemed to hold its breath.

At night, fine hairs on my leaves would gather what little moisture the air allowed. I would keep some. I would let some run down to my roots, into the narrow darkness where the stone is cool. Not enough to make a spring. Nothing so grand. Enough to endure; perhaps, in a very dry season, enough to help a seedling hold on.

The people might come to sit beneath me without knowing I had noticed them. They might lean against my trunk, or leave a cup in the hollow between my roots while they worked nearby. A child might press an ear to the bark and hear only the settling of warm wood—or might hear, for a moment, the fine traffic of insects and roots beneath the paving. I would not tell them what they were hearing. I would keep the sound.

I would be old in the way the Crown is old: not untouched, not unchanging, but still here after a great deal has passed. A branch would break and grow back smaller. A stone would slip from the wall, and someone would set it aside rather than discard it. My roots would keep finding the cracks. The town would alter around me by inches and seasons, and I would feel each change arrive through the ground.

So that is where I find myself: not above the people, and not quite among them, but rooted under the place they have made. A tree in the heart of the settlement, listening through stone. Keeping a little shade. Keeping what moisture I can. Remaining part of the Crown’s long, crowded chorus.
