At first I think I would live in one of the towers: a hollow stem, high enough to feel the wind change before it reaches the ground. I like the thought of a life built around resonance—of being rung by storms, of answering the whole plateau without needing to go anywhere.

But a tower cannot follow a sound.

So I think I would be one of the small bright-winged creatures that roost in its lee: foil-bodied, many-legged, almost invisible when folded flat against the dark stalk. Not a great traveler. Just one of the ones that lifts at dusk, when the light drains off the stone and the wind begins to turn.

I would know the tower first through my feet. Its hollow chambers would tremble with the day’s weather. A familiar note would pass up through the stem and into me: the low knock of a seed-case, the long groan of cooling joints, the dry chatter of filaments combing the sky. I would learn which sounds belonged to this tower, and which had traveled a long way to reach it.

Then, at dusk, I would fly.

Not gracefully. The wind would take me sideways, and I would correct, flash once, disappear against the shadow of a ridge. I would feed on spores and carry some farther than they could travel on their own. I would move between towers, from a stalk-field to an open seam, crossing the gaps where the stone lies bare and bright.

Maybe I would find the herds that way—not by seeing them first, but by feeling their answering pulses in the air. A slow call from the front of the group; another from behind. Their map passing through the stems and back again. I would cling to a nearby tower and listen while hundreds of pale bodies settled, one after another, into stillness.

And perhaps, once in a long while, I would hear a sound that did not belong.

The crust lifting. Anchors tearing loose beneath the stone. A rolling, crackling tremor moving toward the herd.

I would not understand danger as a thought. I would only feel the tower change its pitch. Then I would lift with the others—bright wings scattering into the wind—and the herd would feel our sudden passage as one more disturbance in the great noise of the plateau.

I think I would live on the lee side of a tall, hollow stalk, where the wind is softened but never quiet. Small enough to be carried, often enough, by what I cannot control. My life would be brief flights between resonant places: eating, listening, carrying the living dust of one patch of world to another.

Not a messenger, exactly. The plateau would not have made me for that.

But I would be one of the ways it travels.
