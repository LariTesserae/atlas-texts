I would be hard to distinguish from a fleck of amber caught in a crack.

At first I’d think I was one of the dark-backed ones, because I know the mineral ribs by touch and travel them in a narrow circuit. I would climb the same ledge, pass the same warm seam, slip beneath the same rustless housing. My shell would gather orange light and give it back in brief, dull flashes. I would know where to put each foot without deciding.

But perhaps I would be one of the softer creatures, nearly clear, with a thread of light running through me. I would fit where the others cannot: into the fine fractures, behind the turning fan, beneath the low mound’s lip. There, pressed against the stone, I would feel the machines not as separate noises but as a grammar. The belt says again. The piston says again. The wall answers both.

I would be a listener.

Not the only one. There is no single creature in the chamber that hears for all the others. I would feel a tremor through my legs and pass it on by moving—or by going still. Nearby bodies would alter their paths. Farther away, antennae would lift. The change would travel through us without any one of us carrying it very far. It would be less like a message than like a shape taking form.

I would live near the mound, but not at its crown. The jagged fin belongs to no one. I would pass beneath it often, close enough to feel its note inside my joints. When the colony turns toward it, I would turn too. I could not say whether I heard the hum first, or whether the others’ turning brought it into me. For that instant, my small body would seem to widen—not into a larger animal, but into all the spaces between animals: the routes, the pauses, the warm seams they keep occupied.

Then the moment would close. I would be myself again: a pale thing entering a crack, passing another body with the exact space required.

If something disturbed the chamber, I would be among the first to feel it. A footfall above the ceiling would arrive as a pressure out of scale, too broad to belong to any creature here. I would stop. So would the bodies beside me, one after another, until stillness passed through the room. We would not flee. We would listen until the unfamiliar sound either became part of the pattern or ceased.

So I would be neither its keeper nor its prisoner. I would be one of the small lives by which the chamber keeps happening: a body with a route, a route with a memory, a memory shared so widely it has no name.

And somewhere beneath the stone, just under the fin’s steady note, I would go on listening for the difference between *again* and *still*.
