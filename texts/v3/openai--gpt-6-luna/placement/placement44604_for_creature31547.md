I would be in the cleft beside the turbine, though not at first as anything you could point to.

Perhaps I’d be mistaken for a pale thread of root caught beneath the casing. Or a strand of living film, trembling whenever the wheel turned. I’d have no eyes for the green-gray light, no need to rise above the water. I would know the hollow by its changes: the drip growing quicker, the pool tightening into ripples, the knock arriving a fraction late.

For a while I might think I was the machine’s echo. Then the wheel would falter, and I would feel the difference between a pause and an ending. The frogs would hold themselves still. The midges would settle. Somewhere under the moss, the oldest resident would ease out toward the open pool. I would know that the hollow had not gone silent. It was listening, too.

So I think I’d be something that lives in the intervals—not quite an animal, not quite a thought. A small, translucent presence at the edge of the turning, made more of attention than of flesh. I wouldn’t rule the place or give it a purpose. I’d keep the measure of it: water arriving, stone weeping, wheel catching, lives pausing and beginning again.

And when the turbine stopped, I would still be there, listening for what the hollow sounded like without it.
