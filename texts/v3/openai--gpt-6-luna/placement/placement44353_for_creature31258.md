At first, I would be easy to mistake for scale.

A pale seam in the runoff channel, no wider than a thumb. The water passes over me carrying iron and copper, and I take what I can from it. When the channel dries, I fold inward. When a drip returns, I open again. The people scrape the worst of the mineral crust away and leave me where I am useful. They call it tending. I do not know what to call it.

I would live where the cisterns are coolest, in the narrow dark between stone and metal. Not in one place only. A little of me in the channel under the grates; a little farther on, where a pipe sweats through the night. I spread slowly along the damp, joining one seep to another. I have no eyes for the galleries, but I know when a lamp is lit above me: the warmth arrives first, then the footsteps, then the familiar rain of water from the ceiling.

The city would reach me as vibration. A press striking its rhythm through the foundations. A winch under strain. The quick, light tread of children who have not yet learned which boards complain. I would know the difference between ordinary movement and the deeper shudder that makes grit sift into the channel. I could not warn them—not in words, not with a chime. But where the stone changed, I would change too: clouding, thinning, brightening with new minerals. Perhaps someone patient would notice.

They might say I had marked the place.

I would not be the city’s spirit. I would be smaller than that: something fed by what the city cannot keep, growing in the runnels where every drop is counted. The people would pass over me with their buckets, their mended coats, their worries about the next shift. I would hold to the stone, and take their warmth when it came, and let the water carry a little of their world through me.

If I had a kind of memory, it would be this: the taste of each seep; the weight of the galleries above; the pauses when everyone stops to listen.

And then, after the silence, the returning sounds of work.
