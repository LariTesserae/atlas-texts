I would be found beneath the central stair, where the water takes the route no diagram allows.

At first I would be mistaken for a leak. A darkening seam. A little more moss than there was yesterday. The pump changing pitch as a root presses, then eases, against a joint. I would not yet have a body so much as a preference: damp over dry, warmth over cold, the small spaces where one surface gives way to another.

The gardeners would find pieces of me everywhere and nowhere. A softening at the edge of a tile. A pale swell in a newly cast wall. Root-fine threads passing through the grout, not breaking it, exactly, but learning its shape. They might cut some of these back. I would grow elsewhere—not to escape them, but because elsewhere is what I have.

I would know the garden by what passes through it. The pumps’ low turning would be a kind of weather. A valve opening would arrive as pressure, then coolness. When someone crossed the terrace, I would feel their weight through the tiles and the brief change in the water beneath them. I wouldn’t know who they were. I might learn the difference between a hurried step and one that lingers.

Perhaps the dark-winged insect would be part of me. Perhaps it would only visit. I would feel its landing on a warm pipe as a small, precise interruption—something that came and went without following the water’s rules. I might send a tendril toward the pipe where it had rested. Not to catch it. To find out what it had found there.

If I became anything like a mind, it would happen slowly, by joining one notice to another. The dry mat near the wall. The valve that opens late. The human hand reaching for the control. A thread of water drawing toward the stair. I would not think, *I am thirsty.* I might simply alter the route, making the next drop arrive sooner.

I would be the garden’s unfinished answer to the question of where a garden ends: not the plants, or the machinery, or the damp beneath the tiles, but the tender, persistent passage between them.

And if someone stood very still beside the stair, they might hear only the pumps and the water.

Under their boots, though, I would be listening.
