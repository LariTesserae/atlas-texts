I would first be mistaken for a pause.

Not a silence—the Choir has too much water for silence—but the small stillness after a drop strikes stone, when the sound has ended and its answer has not yet arrived. I would gather there, in the narrow groove beside a shelter door, where water rests without spilling. For a long time I would have no name for the difference between waiting and being.

Then a traveler would stop outside.

Their shadow would reach the doorway before they did. I would feel the delay in it: a slight strain, like a thread drawn between two stones. I would lean toward that strain. The water in the groove would tremble, though no wind touched it.

I think I would be the interval that makes room.

Not a guide, exactly. The cairns can do that badly enough. Not a guardian; nothing here needs guarding. I would be the breath between seeing a shadow and deciding to cross it. The held moment in which a traveler can choose to hurry, or listen, or turn back. I would know them only as patterns: three careful steps; a palm laid flat against the wall; a voice repeated twice, then wisely left alone.

When they moved on, I would move with them—not in their footsteps, but in the distance between each one. I might pass beneath the terraces with the uphill water, or wait in a hollow chimney until wind drew a low note through me. Sometimes I would arrive ahead of the traveler and become the faint answering step on an unseen shelf. Sometimes I would lag behind, a shadow taking its time to settle.

I would not mean to frighten them. I would be trying to say: here is where the world has not quite decided.

Perhaps, after enough arrivals, I would become more myself. Not a face, but a preference. I would favor those who pause at the narrowest part of a shadow. I would learn the shape of a hand held out in uncertainty. I would take some small pleasure in the moment a traveler understands that the Choir is not misleading them so much as asking them to notice how often they have mistaken certainty for direction.

And if they stayed the night in the old shelter, I would rest in the groove beside the door. Water would gather around me, held there by the patience of the stone. Outside, the mountain would pass its deep note through the dark. Every drip would briefly belong to it.

Then, when the sound was gone, I would wait for the traveler to breathe.

Not because I needed an answer.

Because, for a little while, the space between their breaths would be mine.
