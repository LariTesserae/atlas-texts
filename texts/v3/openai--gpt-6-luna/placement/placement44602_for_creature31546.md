I would enter where the path softens, and at first I would think I had arrived alone.

Then I would notice that the water had made room for me.

Not much room. A narrow, dark place beneath one of the flat stones, where a channel slips under the pool and comes out on the other side. There I would lie very still, a pale thing no longer than a finger, with a body so thin the water could pass through the idea of it. I would have no eyes that anyone could see. I would know the garden by pressure: a grazer’s weight on the shelf above, the passing tremor of ribbon-bodied swimmers, the platform’s low pulse traveling through the stone.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the channels. Water would find my back and follow it, though I did not lead the water anywhere. The old groove would carry me around the hollow, under the pools and out again, and I would learn that the water sometimes went uphill—not because I made it, but because the garden had been doing this for so long that its exceptions had become a kind of weather.

I would learn the animals’ quiet speech. The seeds’ ripples would arrive as a question; the grazer’s shifting color, as an answer. When the predator came to drink, the little feet beneath the leaves would move their paths a little wider. I would feel that widening pass through the water. No alarm. No command. Only room made for a presence.

And then, at evening, the beads would rise.

Their images would hang above the center: the bowl under a different sea, the ridges before they wore smooth, the garden beneath unfamiliar stars. I would feel each image touch the pool without entering it. That, I think, is when I would understand what I was.

I would be the place where an image almost becomes water.

Not the old machine beneath the platform, though its vibration would be the nearest thing I had to a voice. Not one of the animals, though their small lives would move through me. I would be a living interval: the dark, apparently bottomless patch at the garden’s center, where reflection and depth could not quite be told apart. A creature made of the garden’s attention to what is not here anymore—and what has not arrived yet.

I would have no name for myself. The garden would not need one. When the beads went dark and settled, I would hold their fading impressions for a while: a sky no longer overhead, a sea no longer touching these stones, a version of the hollow that might never be. Then those impressions would loosen and pass into the channels.

By morning, I would be nearly indistinguishable from the water.

Only the smallest things would notice: a pale animal pausing beneath a leaf; a silver shape circling the wrong reflection; a reed turning, though there was no wind. The platform would continue its deep, dreaming tremor. The grazer would drink. The predator would wait among the roots.

And beneath them all, in the place where the water seems darkest without seeming deep, I would keep the garden’s almost-memories—not to preserve them, exactly, but to let them pass through without breaking the hush.
