I would be found at the end of the groove, in the finger’s breadth between stone and water.

At first you might take me for a dry seam. Look longer and you would see that I am filmed with moisture like everything else, though no bead gathers there. I am not quite land, not quite puddle. The groove points toward me as if it knows where it is going; the water holds its bright edge as if it knows what must not arrive.

I might once have thought I was a drop. There is one above me, hanging from the green thread at the ceiling, and we resemble each other in our refusal to fall. But the drop has a roundness, a shine. I have only a length: the small distance between one thing and another.

The fish do not know me. They swim beneath their little sky, shoulder to shoulder, never reaching the shore. The crickets rasp over me; the salamanders press their cool sides against the stones nearby. Their lives fill the chamber so completely that I am nearly overlooked. Yet I am the one space the crowd cannot occupy without ending me.

Perhaps I am a path that has not been completed. Perhaps I am what remains of the hands that made the arches—though there are no hands here now, and the arches are too small for anyone living. Perhaps I am simply the chamber’s way of remembering distance.

The sound fills even me. I have no silence to offer it, no echo to send back. I hold only the pause between the groove’s last shallow mark and the water’s sharp boundary. It is not a pause before something happens. Nothing happens here. Still, a pause can be a place.

So I am the almost-bridge: a narrow, bright threshold in a world with nowhere left to go. I do not carry the water across. I keep the crossing imaginable.
