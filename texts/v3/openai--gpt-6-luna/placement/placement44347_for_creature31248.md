At first, I think I would live in one of the slender towers, in a room high enough to watch the rain choose its way across the plain. I would keep a metal tablet by the door and mark the changes: the new ridge, the canal gone dry, the stair with its extra step. I imagine I would be a surveyor.

But I don’t think I would be very good at finishing maps.

I would leave my room often, following routes that have become uncertain—not to correct them, exactly, but to find out what they are now. I’d learn which bridges still arrive somewhere, which hollows hold water after the rain has passed, and where the red leaves have surfaced again. When someone asked for directions, I might say, “Walk until the ground begins to feel familiar,” which would not be very useful, but might be true.

Eventually I would stop living in the tower. I’d move to the bridge with no beginning, in a small room at its far end. A vine would find its way through the wall. I would train it around the door, then let it choose where to go next.

I think I would be a keeper of crossings.

Not a guard. Not someone who decides which way is safe. I would wait at the places where Vey’s routes grow strange: beside the still pools, beneath the unlit bridges, at the edge of the southern plain. If the second city appeared in the water, I would find someone to look with me. I would not call out to the figure there. I’d make room for the possibility that it was looking back.

Perhaps, after a while, people would begin to leave me small things: a corrected map, a cutting from a plant that came back through the vents, a note about a path that now ends farther from the river. I would keep them, though nothing I kept would stay in quite the same place.

And if the ground shifted under my room in the night, if the bridge acquired a new span or the black ridge rose closer, I would not try to hold it still. In the morning, I’d open the door, learn where it led, and make a new mark—lightly, with room beside it for a question.
