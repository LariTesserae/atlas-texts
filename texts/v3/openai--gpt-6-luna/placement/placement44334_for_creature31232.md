At first I think I would be a grazer: one pale back among others, crossing the valley without needing the valley to remember me. I would know the ground by the spread of my weight—firm gravel here, a deceptive skin of ice there—and find the warm seams by smell. When the wind shifted, I would lift my head. Then I would move on.

But I don’t think I would stay an animal.

I imagine the hunter on the ridge, patient and spare, belonging to no single hollow. I imagine the birds arriving in the brief season of insects, their calls thrown across the gray air. Each has a way through the upland. None quite belongs to it.

I would be smaller. Lower. I would live where meltwater escapes the stone.

A dark green growth in a crack beside a spring—not a garden, not even a patch you would notice from far away. I would hold to the rock where warmth comes up from below and the wind breaks against a ledge. Water would pass through me, freeze over me, return. I would not know the whole plateau, only the minute changes in my patch: the spring’s heat rising, a film of ice thinning, a hoof pressing mud nearby.

Sometimes a bird would land above me to pick through the insects. Sometimes a grazer would crop the few tough leaves growing at the edge of my warmth. Their passing would shake loose droplets. I would take what was left: light, mineral water, a little shelter from the weather.

And when the seam grew too cold, or the stone shifted, I would not make a monument of surviving. I would loosen into the water and begin again somewhere lower, in another crack that had opened.

So I would be found at the edge of the steaming spring, almost mistaken for wet stone: a small, tenacious life in a place that has not finished becoming. My world would be no larger than a handspan. But everything would pass through it—the mountain’s warmth, the sky’s cold, the animals’ brief visits—and, for a while, I would keep it all.
