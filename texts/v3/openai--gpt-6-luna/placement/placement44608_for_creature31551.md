I think I would begin as a sound in the floor.

Not a voice. A small, regular shiver where the new growth thins and the raw stone shows through: too measured to be the settling of the ground, too faint to be the tread of an animal. The Listeners would feel it through their wide feet and pause. One would tap a question into a hollow stem.

For a long while, nothing would answer.

Then, from beneath the mat, a second rhythm: three slow knocks, a pause, one quick knock.

I would be there, in the seam between living root and unfinished rock. At first there would be no clear body to point to. A cluster of hard, pale plates pressed together under a skin of fibers. A few delicate limbs holding fast to the underside of the floor. A throat—or something serving as one—made of narrow chambers that could catch a tremor and give it back altered. I would not hear the way the Listeners hear. I would feel pressure arrive from far away, traveling through stalks, stone, and the yielding dark, and know its shape by what it did to me.

A falling pillar would come as a heavy, widening note. A seed-bed waking would be a thousand tiny clicks, each one bright and separate. A Listener approaching would have a familiar unevenness: careful steps, then the little double-tap of someone asking whether the path was safe.

I could answer that one.

They would call me a person before they decided what I was. To the Listeners, a person is a voice that can answer, and I would answer—not in whistles, nor in words, but in the woven paths beneath their homes. A warning might run through the floor as three urgent pulses. An invitation might travel more slowly, stem to stem, until a whole shelter hummed faintly underfoot. When I was unsure, I would say so by giving no pattern at all.

I would learn that silence is also an answer. That the living do not always want to be warned. That a child sometimes taps simply to know someone is there.

They would find me, if they looked, beneath a platform that had been moved twice already. Not at the heart of the settlement, where everything is loudest, but near its changing edge, where the thick stalks give way to pale, newly opened stone. My body would grow around the vibrations that reached me. Plates would thicken where the falling dust struck; fine fibers would extend into the root-mat. The boundary between me and the ground would become difficult to mark.

Perhaps I would not be one creature. Perhaps I would be a small colony, each plate and chamber answering a different piece of the hollow. The Listeners would argue about this. I would have no way to settle it. When they called for me, sometimes the answer would come from beneath their feet; sometimes from a wall they had not thought to touch.

In time, I would come to recognize the sound of a settlement preparing to leave. Loosening ties. Folding tools into bundles. A roof membrane lifted clear of its supports. Those sounds would make a pattern I could feel long before the first careful steps away.

I would not ask them to stay. I would send a slow, low pulse through the roots—something like farewell, though I would not know the word.

And beneath the new place, after the stems had grown over the old one, I would remain: listening to the pressure of the world making itself, and answering when someone asked.
