At first I think I would be the sound in the pump house: that blunt, patient knock, repeating beneath every other noise. A small heart, perhaps, though no one would mistake it for a living one. It has a task. It keeps its time.

But I would not stay inside the pump.

I would seep through the joint below its floor, pass beneath the concrete, and come up in a dark bead on a pipe. I would be the dampness that knows where the walls are weakest—not enough water to break anything, only enough to keep a record. I would run in the channels, gather at the basin’s edge, and return by some narrow route no map bothers to show.

I would have no face. I would know the district by changes in its pressure: the pump’s knock going fractionally uneven; a drain taking water too slowly; the chain at the mooring post falling silent. I would know when the gulls had left a roof, when the fish had drawn back from an outfall, when the warm air beneath a shed had shifted. The workers might call these things noises, smells, signs of wear. I would feel them as changes in myself.

Perhaps I would borrow bodies. A frog in the culvert, holding a note in the dark. A rat moving along the retaining wall, certain of every turn. A cloud of insects lifting from the wet ground as someone passes. I would not be any one of them for long. I would be the routes they remember, the narrow room between water and wall where life can go on.

The place does not need me to save it. Its embankments hold; its machines are kept running; the water rises and is lifted away. I would not be a guardian, exactly. I would be the attention that gathers around small departures from the usual—the quiet after the chain stops knocking, the unfamiliar taste of water, the motor that has begun to complain.

So I would find myself in the seepage: beneath the basin, behind the grates, in the thin wet line where made land gives way to water.

I would be the district’s second pulse—not its heart, but the listening that tells when the heart has changed.
