I think I would live in the pause after a sound.

Not in the spring, exactly, nor inside the mountain, but in the narrow hollow beneath the upper stair, where the wind enters one passage and leaves by another. The Keepers would hear me first as an echo that comes back a little late. A footstep sounds once, then again—softer, from somewhere the stair does not go.

I would have no fixed body. In cloud, I might be a coolness along the inside of an unglazed window. Near the spring, a tremor in the water before its level changes. On the bridges, I would borrow the cord’s low hum. When the stone is newly made, I would be almost silent. I would not know what the mountain intends. I would only know the difference between a sound that belongs and one that has just arrived.

The Keepers might call me **the Second Hearing**. They would leave a little bowl beneath the seep and chalk a mark where the echo gathers strongest. They would not ask me whether a place was safe. I could not answer that. I might only return the tap of a surveyor’s knuckle with a hollow note—or let it come back whole.

Over time, perhaps I would learn the town’s ordinary sounds: water ticking in the gutters, a rug beaten clean, someone calling across the square. I would learn that a good night is not a night without change, but one in which change has made itself heard.

And if, one morning, the new lip of stone held moss, and the Keepers set the first timber upon it, I would be there too—not beneath the timber, but in the brief quiet before it touched ground.

That is where I would belong: among the things not yet settled, listening without asking them to hurry.
