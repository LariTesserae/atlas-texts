I would find myself where a path is almost made: at the edge of the quiet square, beneath a root that has grown around a strip of black stone.

At first I might think I was waiting for someone. There is a small folded seed in my palm, warm on one side and cool on the other. A tool, perhaps. A keepsake. When I turn it, the low note of a transit filament trembles faintly through my wrist. Across the square, the basin holds the sky—then, for a moment, the pale turning pattern beneath the city.

I would be one of the people who listens below the oldest roots. Not an elder, exactly, and not a mechanic. I go where the city’s sounds grow difficult to separate: water in the channels, roots shifting against stone, the deeper clatter that comes up through the soles of my feet. I help when a stair has gone under, when someone has lost the familiar warmth that leads them home, when a door has opened onto a room no one remembers.

But the more I try to name what I do, the less it resembles a task.

The seed in my palm does not show me a map. It changes temperature when I face the wrong way. Sometimes it grows a pale, threadlike root that points toward a passage. Sometimes it stays quiet while the leaves ahead brighten, one by one, with the small green gleam that means: here, for now. I am useful to visitors, who cannot yet read the air. The people born here seldom need me. They notice me, though. A pause in their steps. A hand against the trunk beside mine.

I think I am a keeper of routes, until I realize I have never kept one.

I have walked the city for years—or perhaps I have only been carried through it, in capsules sliding soundlessly above the lanes. I know the platforms that widen before a passenger arrives. I know where the floodwater will gather, and the rootways that remain firm when the moss mat loosens. Yet every familiar place has altered a little. A window has grown shut around a branch. A dry stair has become a stream. The way home is not a line I remember, but a set of small agreements: this leaf turning silver; that warmth in the stones; the pressure of a current passing through the transit line.

Sometimes I wonder whether the city is guiding me, or whether I am the part of it that has learned to walk.

There is a place beneath the square where I go when the underground clatter deepens. I kneel beside the dark rock and put the seed-device down. The stone is wet and smooth as a sleeping animal. Beneath it, something turns. I feel the turn in my teeth, then in the small bones of my hands. Once, I heard a sound there like someone trying to remember a name.

I left a knotted length of fiber in the basin afterward. Not an offering, I told myself. A marker. In the morning it was gone.

Now, when someone asks where to go, I don’t point. I walk with them until the city begins to answer. We wait while a pool empties into a channel. We let a branch lower its bridge. We take the passage that was not there when we arrived.

And if they ask who I am, I may say I listen beneath the roots.

Or I may touch the trunk beside us and let it answer in its own time.
