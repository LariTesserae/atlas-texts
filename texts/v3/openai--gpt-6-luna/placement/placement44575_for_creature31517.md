At first, I would be somewhere between the roof and the wall.

Not quite inside the stone, though I would know its warmth and chill: the day’s heat held in a tile, the damp seam beneath it, the little pocket where rain gathers and does not fall. I would be in a hollow too narrow for a goat, too dark for a lizard, crowded with old dust, feathers, and the dry rasp of other bodies shifting in sleep.

The highland would reach me as a series of small disturbances. A drop of water tapping far below. Claws crossing a roof. A bird settling on the ledge above my head. The whole country would make sounds, but I would not need to understand the country all at once. I would know the nearest crack, the familiar turn in the covered way, the opening that lets in the evening air.

Then dusk would loosen the roof.

I would unfold from the hollow and join the thin stream pouring out beneath the eaves. For a moment the air would be full of us: quick bodies crossing quick bodies, each finding a path through the dark without touching stone. The terraces would open below—not as a view, exactly, but as distance made legible. A wall ahead. A gap. The brief, bright return of a sound. A courtyard, its pool breathing insects upward.

I think I would be a bat: one of the small residents in the old roof hollows, not remarkable enough to be noticed by the highland, and perfectly at home in its narrowness.

I would hunt where warmth gathers—above cisterns, along damp lanes, around the planted beds where insects rise from the leaves. I would pass the sleeping dogs without troubling them. The birds would know me mostly as a stir in the evening air; the frogs would continue calling below, as if my shadow had never crossed their water. I would follow the same bends night after night, though I would not think of them as paths. They would simply be the places where the air returns my voice in a certain way.

By morning I would fold myself back into the seam of the roof. The highland would brighten in scattered patches, and the birds would take possession of the ledges again. Rain might find its way down through the stone. Roots might press a little farther into a crack. None of this would seem like change from inside my hollow. It would be only the world continuing around me.

I would not inherit the terraces as a human inheritance, or know the names of their builders. I would inherit them as shelter: a roof that holds, a dark passage with a turn I can read, a deep place to sleep while water travels unseen beneath the stones.

And at evening, when the whole country began to move, I would leave it for a little while—one small, living stitch pulled from the old garment of the mountain, then drawn back in before dawn.
