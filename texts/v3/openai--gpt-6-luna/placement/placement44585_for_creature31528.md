I would be easiest to find where the stone sweats.

Not at the vent itself, where the breath rises white and hot, but just beyond it: in a crack under a shelf of black rock, where warmth meets cold and water gathers without quite becoming a stream. There, pale filaments would lace the stone. They would hold beads of condensation, catch drifting seeds, and thread themselves through the roots of the copper thickets. At first glance I might look like frost that had forgotten to melt.

I would not have a face. I would have a listening surface.

A herd passing above would reach me as pressure: hundreds of feet, each impact distinct for an instant, then joined into one long rolling weight. I would feel the small animals moving in the roots, their quick pauses and sudden scurries. When a ridge-runner came near, the ground would tell me before its shadow did. I could not warn anyone in words. But I might hold the water a little tighter; the root mats might stiffen; the small ones might go still.

The warm seep would enter me slowly and leave changed. I would take a little mineral, keep a little heat, offer a little moisture to the roots. In summer, when the copper leaves shivered with droplets, some of that water would have passed through my threads. In winter, when the surface froze hard enough to ring beneath the wind, I would remain below it, working at the scale of pores.

For a while, I would think of myself as one small pale patch under one ledge.

Then the thaw would come. Roots would lengthen into a new fracture. Water would find another path. A glider’s shadow would pass over a neighboring hollow where filaments like mine held mist against stone. The same vibrations would arrive there, delayed and altered. I would begin to understand that my edges were not where I ended.

I would be a colony, perhaps. Or a living seam. Something between plant and weather, between root and rock: not the plateau’s mind, but one of the ways it keeps touch with itself.

You would find me under the crowded growth, in the narrow places no animal can see and the ice cannot quite seal. I would be there when the herds return, when the basin fills by imperceptible increments, when another stone loosens from the ridge.

And I would know the plateau not as a view, but as a series of pressures passing through me: water seeking a path, roots widening a crack, bodies gathering warmth, the slow pulse below the ice.

I would be small enough to miss.

I would be spread far enough not to be alone.
