At first, you would find only a small disturbance in the green.

A mat lifted at one edge. Water trembling in a shallow pan. A few pale stems clipped cleanly where the pool begins. If you stood still long enough, the disturbance might gather itself into a shape: four broad feet, a dark back slick with rain, grit caught in the fur. If you made a sound, I would not hear it. But I would feel your weight travel through the stone.

I live beneath the seam where the shelf holds water and the shelf lets it go. The mat is thick there, raised in a low lip above the rock. It stays warm after the light leaves, and the seep keeps a thin skin of green on the stone. One of the pipes above us drips steadily—not enough to make a stream, just enough to keep a small hollow sweet. I come for the film on the rock and the tender shoots around it. I have learned where the concrete feet of the station are, and where the bare ground begins. I do not cross the bare ground unless the water calls me.

I am smaller than the things that thunder. The pump’s voice arrives through my feet: a low, even pressure in the mat. The sluice gate makes a sharper shiver, followed by a pause. Wind has a different touch. It combs the surface above me, laying sedges down and lifting them again. I know when the whole shelf is breathing hard. I know when it is safe to come out.

My forefeet knock once against the stone.

Here.

Somewhere beyond the stream crossing, another animal answers twice. The sound is almost nothing. It passes under the rushing water, through the wet roots and the shallow stone, and reaches me all the same. We are not a herd. We leave each other room. But when the pools are high, when the bright flats offer nowhere to hide, it matters to know that the green is occupied.

I do not mend the shelf on purpose. I eat; I pass beneath the mats; my feet press small channels into the softness. Later, water follows those channels, or abandons them, or finds a new way around. The plants gather at the edges. The stone darkens. If the land changes, it changes too slowly for me to watch.

Still, I know my paths. One runs from the warm seep to a clear pool beneath a ledge. Another skirts the open slab where the sky shines too sharply. My young stay under the lifted mat until their feet grow broad enough for the crossing. I bring them shoots, and sometimes I lie beside them while rain ticks against the leaves. Above us, the station holds its iron shape against the weather. The world is loud. Under the mat, it is only a pressure, a warmth, a dark green roof.

When the wind drops, I emerge. My back gathers beads of water and fragments of moss. I rasp the slick stone clean, then nose along the pool’s edge, where new growth softens the line between water and land. I leave a few prints behind. They fill with rain before the light goes.

If you came looking for me, you might never know you had stood near. You would see the sedges stirring, the pools flashing white, the thin runnels choosing their uncertain way over the rock.

I would be there, just beneath the living skin of the shelf: small, watchful, and listening with my feet.
