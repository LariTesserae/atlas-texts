I think I would live where the highland first becomes a city: on an upper terrace, near the last open slope and the first close-packed roofs. There, rain arrives plainly. It runs off the bare ridge, gathers in a shallow channel, and begins its long passage beneath walls and stairs.

I would be the one who listens for it.

Not a spirit, exactly. A person, with a small house built against an older retaining wall and a lamp that smokes when the weather turns. My work would be to keep the water’s narrow roads open: lifting leaves from a grate, testing a cistern’s level, noticing when a familiar trickle has gone quiet. I would know which paving stone rocks underfoot and which roof spills first when the mist thickens. People would come find me when a courtyard flooded or a wall began to weep.

At first I imagine that I would understand the city by its channels. But perhaps I would learn it through everything they touch: the damp hem of a neighbor’s coat, the moss left growing because it holds the thin soil, the notch in a doorway where children have measured their height for generations. My knowledge would not be grand. It would be exact. I would know where to stand to hear water moving under the paving, and which silence meant nothing was wrong.

I would have a family, or at least people whose thresholds I could enter without knocking. Someone would leave bread by my door after a long repair. Children would follow me along the covered passages, asking what the drains sound like when they are full. I would tell them to listen—not for a voice, but for the difference between water passing freely and water pressing against an obstruction.

And after many years, I might become difficult to distinguish from the work. My hands would be dark with mortar; my steps would know the worn hollows in the stairs. When I was gone, another person would take up the little hook I used to clear the channels. Someone would still pause at the upper terrace after rain, listening.

So I would be a resident, but also a habit the place had acquired: a keeper of small flows, living at the edge between hillside and settlement. In clear weather I might seem to be doing very little. Then the rain would come, and I would go out into it, following the water downhill through the old stone.
