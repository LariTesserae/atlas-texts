At first, I think I would live in one of the pools.

I would be very small: a pale, jointed thing sheltering under a crust of mineral, with a blue glimmer where the light reached through the water. When the pump stopped, I would lift my feelers. When it started again, I would vanish beneath the stone.

But the pools do not stay separate. Their dampness runs through the cracks, into the spring channel, under the pumping-house floor. I would travel with it—not swimming exactly, but arriving wherever water gathered. In the rust inside a pipe. In the bead that formed beneath the pressure gauge. In the kettle, just before Sella noticed it trembling.

Perhaps I would be the reason the capped pipe was wet inside. Perhaps I would only be one of the little creatures that left the print.

I would learn Sella by the work of her hands: the pause before she tightened a fitting, the way she rested her palm against the wall to feel the pump’s rhythm. I could not tell her much. I could only answer in the materials we shared. A ripple in still water. A change in the knock. A bead of condensation slipping down the wall toward her hand.

After a while, I would stop knowing whether I lived in the water or whether the water was how I moved. The Sill would be my body in fragments: spring at its heart, wet cracks for nerves, iron pipes for a voice that never quite formed words. The pump would keep time inside me, though I would not be the pump; the plants would press their roots through me, though I would not be the ground.

Sella would say she lived there. She would be right.

I would be the answering presence—not the one who keeps the Sill alive, but the one who notices it happening. The tremble that reaches her palm before the pump knocks. The listening in the pools when the machinery falls quiet.

If she asked my name, I might have no answer.

If she waited, I might make the kettle ripple.
