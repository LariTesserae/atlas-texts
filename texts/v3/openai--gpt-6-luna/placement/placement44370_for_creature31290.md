At first I would put myself in the sky: a pale glider, tilting along the ridge, borrowing warmth from the dark stone. It seems the easiest way to belong here without being held in place. I could cross a basin in a day, vanish into brightness, be mistaken for a scrap of cloud.

But the glider is only passing through. It belongs to the currents more than to any particular ground.

I think I would be found where a plum-colored mat thins into bare scree, on the sheltered side of a ridge. Not at the heart of the growth, where everything is thick and settled, but at its edge: the place where the holdfasts find a mineral seam and the wind keeps asking whether life can stay.

I would be a patch of the mat. Not one plant, exactly. A close, slow interweaving: dark cushions holding warmth; pale threads working into cracks; beadlike bodies opening to the sun. I would have no single point from which to look out. Light would arrive across my surface, and I would feel it as warmth gathering. Wind would pass over me, and I would bend—not alone, but with the neighboring growth, the movement traveling outward until the whole slope seemed to breathe.

Under me, the small pale creatures would make their narrow passages. I would not know them as intruders. Their paths would loosen the weave; their lives would leave something behind. Dust would settle in my folds. The brittle husks of stalks would break down into the beginnings of soil. I would keep none of these things in the way an animal keeps a memory. Still, they would alter what I became.

Sometimes the grazer would come. Its broad feet would press the edge of me, careful without knowing it was careful. It would crop a little, then move on, taking days to cross the basin. Afterward, the light would reach places it had not reached before. I would close around the disturbed patch, slowly enough that the grazer might never see it happen.

I would not be the country. Only a small, persistent answer to it: stone, light, air, and whatever the wind brought. Yet if you found me again after many seasons, I might have shifted a little down the slope. The boundary would have changed—not advanced like a flag, but gathered where warmth lingered and withdrawn where the ridge scoured it clean.

So I would be neither the glider nor the stone, though both would shape my days. I would be the living edge between them, so still that you might pass without noticing, and so old in my patience that your passing would become part of my weather.
