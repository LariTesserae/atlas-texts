I would be found beneath a stairwell that no longer belongs to any one floor.

At first, I might be mistaken for a damp patch in the brick: a dark seam where water gathers, or a root working patiently through old mortar. There would be a length of blue pipe nearby, dented at shoulder height, and a little square of paving stone set into the wall where it had no business being. People would pass without noticing me. Then, one morning, someone would pause at the stair and hear a familiar drip coming from the wrong direction.

I would be the seam that keeps a route.

Not a map, exactly. Maps are too certain here. I would remember the pressure of footsteps, the warmth of a hand on a railing, the small hesitations people make when they reach a place that has changed since yesterday. Water would travel through me. So would the low, private sounds of the city: a pump adjusting its rhythm, roots finding a pipe, a child calling for someone in a passage that has become a room.

The residents might give me a practical name. The stair-listener, perhaps. Or the blue-pipe mark, if they found me that way. Children would leave me things: a bright cord, a bolt with a smooth head, a seed that has not yet decided whether to open. I would keep none of them in quite the same place.

When a corridor slipped below street level, I would be there—not to prevent it, but to make the old way of arriving possible. A person who left by the front door and came back through the laundry might find the same faint line of damp along the wall, leading home. They would touch it once, to be sure. Then they would get on with their evening.

In time, I would become less like a seam and more like a habit the city had acquired. I would be wherever one thing met another without quite agreeing: brick and mud, stair and floor, root and warm pipe, yesterday’s address and tomorrow’s entrance. I would not hold the city still. I would help it recognize itself while it moved.

So if you went looking for me, don’t look for a particular building. Find the place where the pavement has been lifted and laid again, where a reed grows beside a switch box, where someone has paused at the end of a street before crossing. There, under the ordinary sounds, I would be listening for the next way through.
