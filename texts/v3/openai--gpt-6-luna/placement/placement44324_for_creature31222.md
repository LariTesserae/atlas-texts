I would be difficult to point to.

At first, I might be one pale body moving beneath the others: a grain-long creature with many fine legs, my back powdered in iron and sulfur. I would live in a narrow seam between two crystals, where the stone holds its heat and the crust can be scraped from the floor. I would know the chamber by pressure against my sides: the packed bodies, the rasp of mineral under my mouth, the faint lift and fall of warmth.

I would not see the red in the rock. I would feel it arriving.

When the mountain struck from below, the beat would run through the floor and into my legs. I would pause—not from fear, but because the whole seam had paused. Then the pressure would pass through the crowd, each body carrying it to the next, and movement would return. I would feed in the lull, following paths worn by many feet before mine. I would climb over a neighbor, or be climbed over. Neither of us would need to know which.

For a while, I would think of myself as that one small body: the one turning into the crack, the one rasping pale bloom from the stone, the one whose shed skin is taken up by others. Then I would molt, and the old shell would be swallowed into the traffic. I would grow broader, slower, more dust-coated. Around me, new pale bodies would press into the warm spaces. The seam would keep its shape, but not its inhabitants.

So perhaps I would be the seam.

Or the route between the sulfur crust and the warmer hollows. Or the pause that gathers the colony after each hammering, when thousands of bodies tighten together and then begin again. The chamber has no room for a self that stays separate for long. Every sensation is passed along: a grain falling, the gas-throat shivering, a neighbor’s pulse against my plates. The others are not scenery around my life. They are part of how the world reaches me.

If I had a name, it might be the movement that fills the blister and adjusts when the throat narrows. A livingness spread across many small backs, always breaking apart into individuals and joining again. Not a mind above the colony, looking down. Just the shared, bodily knowledge of where the warmth gathers, where food remains, when to pause, when to go on.

I would be there, under the flake of basalt, in the close dark—one creature among countless others, and also the slow current they make together. Outside, the mountain would be young and immense. Inside, it would be no more than the next vibration beneath my feet.
