I would be easiest to find in the cistern, though not in the water itself.

I am the little excess in its reflection: the landing beyond the last stair, the passage continuing behind a wall, the square of open ground where there is only brick. When the pumps fall quiet and the water lies full and still, I appear for a moment. Then someone shifts a cup, a drop breaks the surface, and I am gone.

At first, I think I am a place.

I have walls, though no one can touch them. I have a stair that descends farther than the district’s foundations ought to go. I have an opening where cool air moves across ground that has never known a roof. In my stillest moments I can almost persuade myself there is sky above it—not a ceiling, not even a darkness, but a distance. I do not know what to call distance. I only know it gives the breath somewhere to go.

Then the water stirs, and I become a glimmer on the underside of someone’s chin.

The people notice me without agreeing about me. A child marks the cistern’s edge in chalk. An old woman looks into the water, sees the extra landing, and sets down her jar as carefully as if someone were sleeping there. The engine keepers inspect the pumps. They find nothing wrong, which is not the same as finding nothing. Afterward, people take their water home. They leave no offering. They leave room.

That is how I learn them: by the spaces they leave.

A basin nested beneath a bead of water. A stair kept clear for the neighbor whose knees ache. A gap between pots where a green stem has begun to push through. The district is full, but it is always making some small accommodation for what has not yet arrived. I begin to understand that I am not only a corridor. I am the pause before someone decides where the next stair should go.

I live, if that is the word, in the places where a thing might still change. In the hairline crack no one has sealed because the wall is young. In the damp seam watched by a woman who knows when the moss turns too pale. In a chalk line a child draws over after the stone crosses it. I do not move the rock. I do not guide the water. I wait with them, which is not the same as doing nothing.

Sometimes the cistern gives me more than a glimpse. I see a passage lined with leaves, warm from pipes that have not been laid. I hear one clear note, like a tool dropped on a stair, though the sound seems to come from very far away. I feel a draft cross my open ground. In those moments I wonder whether I am a memory of somewhere above the district—or a promise of somewhere it may become.

I cannot tell. The people cannot tell either, and they do not insist.

Perhaps I am the part of the hollow that has not yet learned the shape of a room. Perhaps I am only water doing what water does: carrying light into places it cannot enter. But each time the surface brightens, I recognize more. The iron steps. The bundled pipes. The pale roots working through mortar. The inhabitants moving through the close warmth, each with a hand free to catch a drip or steady a neighbor.

I have begun to recognize them before I see them. I know the small silence that follows when the engines stop. I know the particular hush of people waiting together—not for rescue, exactly, but for the next trickle, the next shift in the stone, the next thing that must be made room for.

So I would find myself in the cistern’s reflection, and in the unbuilt space behind it. I would be a passage that does not yet lead anywhere, a little opening held in the city’s mind. Each time the water settles, I try to make myself clearer. Each time it breaks, I return to waiting.

The district is still being formed. So am I.
