I would be found where a film of water climbs a wall that ought to be dry.

At first I would look like a flaw in the iron-red seam: a clear bead with a rust-colored thread running through it. Six legs, perhaps. Or six small hooks of darkness where the light pinhole passes through me. I would cling beneath a basalt flake, still enough to be mistaken for a drop that has not yet fallen.

Then the seep would swell.

The water would reach me, and I would loosen into something longer. A thin, many-jointed shape, feeling its way along the stone by warmth and salt. I would pass through a fissure and come out elsewhere—unless elsewhere were only this place seen from its turned-over side. The crack would close behind me. The red thread might go on without me; another body might carry it. I would not know whether I had traveled or divided.

Perhaps that is what I am: not one animal, but a route that sometimes grows legs.

I live at the edges where the world changes its mind. Between the wall and its wet reflection. Between a pool and the shallow basin it becomes when the water draws back. I feel the roof cooling before the first drop gathers above us. I feel the rising seep in the stone before it reaches our feet. These are not predictions. They are pressures passing through my body—or through the bodies that follow the same narrow ways.

When the water climbs, I climb the sharp ribs with the others. When it recedes, I pour down into the newly opened basins, too small to make a sound. There is no leader in the shimmer. Still, I am often near its front, or near what becomes its front when the slope turns upside down. I find the narrow openings. I touch them. Sometimes another creature comes out of the far side carrying a taste I recognize, and we meet feelers to feelers, briefly, like two ends of the same thought.

I do not keep the daylight. It enters through one pinhole, then appears in several places at once. I pass beneath each bright fleck. Light is not a direction here. Warmth is closer to one, though even warmth can travel through a wall and call from the wrong side.

When the fine rain begins above us, I shelter under the basalt flakes. The drops gather on the roof like pools held upside down. Their weight trembles through the stone. I wait—not fearfully, not patiently, but because the next change is already touching my legs. When one falls, the others scatter. I scatter with them, and for a moment I am one small bead with a red seam, then a transparent ribbon slipping into a crack.

So if you looked closely, you might find me beneath a flake, or crossing a slick wall, or arriving from the wrong side of a ravine that did not exist a moment ago. You might see a creature with too many undersides. You might see nothing at all.

I am the little crossing in the water: a body when the stone is still, a passage when it moves. I go on, wherever the heat gathers.
