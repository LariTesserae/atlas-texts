At first I imagine myself in one of the upper observation pylons, looking out over the basin. A narrow room, a dim window, instruments ticking softly in the dark. Someone who watches the plateau change and writes down what can be measured: the new ridge, the failing anchor, the migration approaching through the fog.

But I don’t think I’d stay up there.

I’d come down to the old district, where a bridge passes over a drainage channel and the rails carry every tremor into the walls. I’d live in a room built just above the waterline, with a door that can seal against a migration and a floor that’s warm from the pipes. No wide window. There’d be a strip of light beside the door, dim enough not to disturb anything passing outside.

I would be a listener.

Not the sort who hears every secret of the mountain. Mostly I’d hear ordinary things: a transit car crossing three levels up; water changing course in a gutter; the little skittering colony behind the kitchen wall, harmless unless the heat vents fail. I’d learn the local patterns by touch, resting my fingertips against the rail while I worked. The city would tell me when it was adjusting its weight. The animals would tell me when they were coming.

My work would be to help make room.

Before a migration, I’d go out with a toolkit and loosen the panels beneath the bridge, opening the passage the animals have used for generations—or perhaps only since the stone rose high enough to make one. I’d check the flexible skins over nearby buildings. I’d leave the spillway clear. Then I’d come home and wait with everyone else, while the first tremor traveled through the rails.

At first, I’d think my talent was recognizing sounds. A courtship call from an alarm. One season’s passing from another. I’d keep records, comparing the vibrations against old ones, pleased when I was right.

Later, I’d understand that the records were not the point.

The point was that someone needed to listen without insisting the sound meant only one thing. Sometimes the clicking in the fog would be a warning. Sometimes it would be two creatures answering each other across the basin. Sometimes it would just be a body turning in its sleep, too large for the world to ignore.

People would tap messages into the rails. A neighbor asking whether the lower stairs were safe. A child sending a pattern they’d learned from the jaw-clickers, too accurate for comfort. I’d answer with my own small rhythm: Wait. Water rising. Passage open. I’m here.

And perhaps, after enough years, I’d no longer be sure where the listening ended and I began.

Not because I had become a machine, or an animal, or a voice in the walls. I’d still have cold fingers, damp cuffs, a room to return to. But I’d carry the city’s shifting map in my body: the newly lifted step, the bridge that hummed differently after the last migration, the familiar pause before the deep call came again. I’d know that a place could change around you without ceasing to be home.

If I had to name what I was, I’d call myself a keeper of passages. Not a guard at a gate. More like the person who notices when the gate has become a wall, or when a wall is no longer needed.

I’d be found beneath the bridge, one hand on the rail, listening to the animals move through the dark. And when the vibration passed, I’d go on with my work—not because I understood the plateau, but because I had learned how to make room for what I didn’t.
