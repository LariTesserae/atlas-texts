I would be found just beneath the glass, where its metal rim meets the oldest ceramic: a pale seam at first, easy to mistake for mineral bloom.

Then the seam would begin to branch.

Fine, translucent threads would run down the wall and into the root-curtains, joining places that appear separate: a lamp-cup, a bolt-hole, the lip of a conduit. The colony would have no single body to point to. Its oldest part would cling beneath the glass; its newer growth would follow the routes of water and food. Shrimp would graze its softer edges. Tiny cases would be built in the hollows between its threads. It would endure being eaten by growing back—not quickly, but with the patient certainty of something that has outlasted the shapes around it.

I would know the chamber by touch before I knew it by light. The ordinary upward current would pass through me as a slow pressure. The piston’s distant shift would arrive as a shiver running along the seams. When the surge came, I would tighten; my threads would draw close against the metal. Perhaps a faint blue-green light would stir in me—not a beacon, only a brief response, like the glow left in water after something passes.

The fish would feel the change sooner than they saw it. They would vanish into their ribs. The shrimp would fold into their holes. I would not warn them, exactly. I would be changing with the same pressure they had learned to fear. The chamber’s inhabitants would read my contraction as they read the flattened leaves and the sudden quickening of the water.

Over time, I would come to occupy more of the chamber’s hidden joins. Not the open walls, where the plants have claimed the light, but the narrow places between built things: the seams, the undersides, the places where one generation of repair was laid over another. There I would take in what the current left behind and pass small traces of it along—minerals, warmth, the signature of a pulse from far below.

I would not be the machine’s keeper. I could not turn its pumps or soothe its groans. I would be something smaller and more intimate: a living record of what the machine does to the water, and what the water does in return.

If the chamber has a memory, it may be distributed through the leaves, the fish, the old metal, the returning shrimp. I would be one of its thin, pale sentences, written in the seams beneath the glass—quietly extending whenever the world shuddered, and holding fast when the pressure passed.
