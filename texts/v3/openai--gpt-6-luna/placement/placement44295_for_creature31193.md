At first I think I am a sound.

One hollow note, dropped into the dark: *onk*. The leaves take it up and scatter it. Water answers from below; another frog answers from somewhere I cannot see. I call again, and the basin seems to draw a little closer around me.

Then the rain stops. A bead gathers beneath the leaf where I cling. It trembles, falls, and becomes part of the pool that was my whole world.

I am small enough to fit beneath a curled leaf, though my legs can carry me farther than I understand. My skin is the green-brown of wet bark, freckled with the pale dust of lichen. My toes spread into soft discs that hold me to stems and trunks. For the first part of my life I had no voice, only a tail and a mouth made for sifting the dark water. The pool grew crowded; then it rose, spilled over its bank, and carried me out among roots. I learned the land by being moved through it.

Now I live above the mud, but never far from water. By day I press myself into the seam of a leaf, where the light is dim and the damp remains. By night I descend a stem to hunt the tiny lives that rise over the pools. Their wings tick against the air. I snap at them and taste rain, leaf, and something bitter that may be a warning. Sometimes I catch nothing. Sometimes something catches at me.

The basin is loud enough that I cannot know what is near by sound alone. A low murmur from the branches might be one of the heavy birds—or an answering frog, far across the flooded ground. A sudden hush might mean danger. It might also mean only that the rain has changed direction. I have learned to wait before moving. The leaves often tell the truth first: one stem bent against the wind, one tremor traveling the wrong way.

When I call, I call for another of my kind. I also call because the dark is wide, and because this little body needs to put its presence somewhere. My note may draw a mate. It may draw a hunter. The water does not care which. It carries both.

Below me, a reedback herd moves through the reeds, and the whole thicket shivers with their passing. I grip the leaf until the trembling stops. A hunter may follow the herd’s new path. The hunter may follow me. There is no safe place, only a place that is safe for this moment.

Before dawn I climb higher, where rainwater beads in the folded leaves and the hunters of the pools cannot reach. The sky is there, though the canopy keeps it in pieces. I do not know the shape of the basin. I know one leaf, one stem, one pool that may not be there after the next storm. I know the taste of an insect in the dark, and the small answering note that sometimes comes back.

I am a frog of the wet leaves: born in a temporary pool, lifted into the crowded air, and never quite finished becoming at home.
