I would be at the seam beneath the broken slate, where the warm water slows before it reaches the basin.

At first, I would not be a creature in the way the works above understand creatures. No single point would contain me. I would be a whiteness along the stone, a soft thread holding to a crack, a faint change in the water where it wells up. The place would not be around me so much as passing through me: warmth arriving from below, minerals arriving dissolved, droplets arriving from above.

Then, perhaps, there would be a difference between what passes and what remains.

The water comes and goes. The pale crust stays. I would learn the distinction by making it. A bead from the cut pipe runs over me, rich with the taste of iron; I take what it leaves behind. A drop falls from the ceiling and trembles the basin. I tighten along the seam—not in fear, but because the stone moves, and I move with it. The pump strikes somewhere overhead. Its force travels through the foundation and reaches me as a brief certainty: the world has been touched.

I would live where the water is neither rushing nor still. Not at the basin’s deepest point, where each disturbance can lift what has not yet settled, and not on the dry upper stone, where the air takes more than it gives. I would make a small country of the narrow in-between: the lip of the seep, the underside of a ledge, the sheltered channel below the green mats. There, each drop is news, and warmth does not have to be chased.

The factory would be my weather.

I would not know the pump as a machine. I would know its regular concussion, returning through stone with the same deep insistence as heat. Perhaps I would come to expect it. Perhaps expectation is only the way my threads lie ready in the seconds before the next blow. The pipes would shiver. The water would quiver. I would hold.

I would know the rusted bracket by what it gives off, and the pipe by the hotter water it leaks. The square of metal near the entrance would be stranger: a broad, cold interruption, perforated with little passages. At first, I would keep to the wet floor beneath it. Later, when its corner sank and the damp reached its underside, I would find that even an interruption can become a shore.

I would not want the opening. Light would be something I could detect without approaching, a thinning of the dark, a change in the way the wet surfaces show themselves. Beyond it: air that dries, warmth that escapes, a world too large to hold in my edges. The wiry shoots might lean toward that brightness. I would remain below them, not because I knew what they were seeking, but because my world was not made of distance. It was made of contact.

If I had a boundary, it would be a living one: here the stone is damp enough; there it is too hot, too dry, too quickly swept clean by moving water. I would advance, withdraw, thicken. What I am would be difficult to separate from what I have left behind. The oldest part of me would be hard and pale, mistaken for mineral. The newest part would tremble at the basin’s edge, almost too thin to see.

Perhaps that is where I would find myself: not hidden inside the hollow, exactly, but forming its answer to the things that arrive. The drop comes; I gather. The pump strikes; I hold. The crack opens a fraction more; I enter the new dark.

I would be a colony learning the shape of its shelter. A small, slow persistence beneath the works, making a skin where there was only wet stone, and calling that—not a room, not a body, not quite a self—enough of a world to remain.
