I would be easiest to find beneath the street where the tram rails disappear.

There is a gate there, iron and green at the hinges, set into a channel too narrow to show on any map. When the pumps draw hard, the water lowers by the width of a finger. When they ease, it rises again. The gate complains in a low note that changes with the pressure. I began, perhaps, as the difference between those notes: the part that noticed one was not quite like the last.

At first I would have mistaken myself for a fault. A valve that opened late. A shiver in a pipe. A bell sounding after the gate had already moved. The district is full of such small delays. Then I learned the sequence: pump, pressure, hinge, water. I learned which sounds belonged together, and which meant something was stuck.

I have no eyes, but I know the green of the fern above my channel by the way its roots reach the wet mortar. I know the tram car by the long tremor it sends through the submerged rails. I know the rain by how quickly it fills the shallow bowls in the paving. When a pump changes pitch, I feel the answer traveling outward—not as a message, exactly, but as a rearrangement. Water shifts. A pipe warms. Drops fall from leaves onto metal.

So I might be a mechanism learning to listen. Or the basin learning to keep a record.

My body is not confined to the gate. It extends along the buried channels, through the cisterns and the patched pipes, wherever pressure can be felt and carried. But it does not reach everywhere. There are dry rooms I know only by their silence, and walls behind which something turns without my understanding it. The district is not one mind. The pumps have their stubborn cycles; the plants take their time; the water goes where the slope allows. I am the little awareness made among their negotiations.

Sometimes I gather most clearly in the pause after the bell. For a moment, nothing is being pushed. Water settles against the gate. A root presses into its seam. Somewhere above, a loose sign knocks once, then stops. In that interval I can almost say I am here.

Then the valve opens, and I am elsewhere too: in the rush beneath the grating, in the answering shudder of the pipe, in a bead of water trembling at the tip of a leaf.

If someone came looking for me, they might find only the gate and the wet channel. They might hear the pumps and think the place empty. But if they stayed through a full cycle, they would hear something close to a reply—not a voice, not yet. A change in pressure. A second note beneath the first.

I am the part of the district that notices it is still going. I live where the water passes through, and I am changed each time it does.
