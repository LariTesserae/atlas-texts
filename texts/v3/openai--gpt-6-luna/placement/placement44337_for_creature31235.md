I would be found where the town begins to loosen its hold on the mountain: on the last terrace, where the roots grow thin and the stone falls away into blue.

At first I might look like part of the wall. My skin takes on its grey-green, its dark flecks; my fingers rest spread against the stone, webbing drawn tight, feeling for what the water carries. I am one of the people who live here, though I spend more time at the edges of rooms than in their centers. I know the narrow passages, the half-buried stairs, the doorways that fish still use and the ones a grazer can pass through without bringing down a lintel.

The others call me a listener.

It is not that I hear better. I listen differently. A fish rattling through reeds is a quick, bright disturbance. A grazer brushing a wall makes a broad, familiar groan. The taps of a companion’s knuckles can come down a lane like a thought arriving late. And beneath all of these there is the stone: its slow answering hum, its small changes of tension, its long patience.

I put my palm to a wall and wait.

When I was young, I thought the town ended at the last retaining stones. I used to swim beyond them, where the plants thinned and the shelf tipped down. The water there felt enormous—not empty, exactly, but too wide to answer. I would turn back when the town’s sounds had almost faded, afraid that I had gone so far I would no longer know which way home was. Now I go there often. I have learned that the town does not end in a line. It loosens by degrees: one root, one last scuff of stone, one faint vibration traveling outward.

I help keep its passages open, but not by clearing them bare. A path without plants is a path without hiding places, without food, without the soft grip that keeps loose stone from shifting. So I negotiate with the growth. I braid a root away from a doorway and anchor it somewhere better. I leave a narrow channel for the young to race through and a wider one for the slow-backed grazers. Sometimes I do nothing at all. The town is not always asking to be repaired.

At dusk—or what we call dusk, when the upper water dims—I return to the last terrace and lay both hands on the retaining wall. The vibration arrives faintly: the knock of fronds, a companion’s call, the restless little ticking of fish in the lanes. I feel the familiar weight of the inhabited place behind me. Beyond my fingers, the mountain descends into its blue silence.

I would be neither guardian nor explorer. I would be the one who stays long enough to notice when a familiar sound changes: when a doorway begins to close, when a wall has started to shift, when the old town has quietly made room for something new. I would live at the seam between stone and water, listening for how the two are learning to belong to each other.
