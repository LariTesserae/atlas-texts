I would be found where the ground is almost ready to move.

Not on the ridge, where the wind can comb dust across the stone, nor in the open basin where the light makes every distance look harmless. I would be beneath them: under the powder, under the old, smoothed rock, where pressure gathers too slowly to be called an event.

At first I might seem like part of the country. A low rise passing beneath a plain. A long, dull back under the crust, plated in stone-coloured ridges. If you saw me at all, you might think the mountain had shifted—one more rearrangement in a world that never quite holds still.

But I am not the mountain.

I feel its strain.

It reaches me as a tightening along my flanks, a faint pull at the seams of my body. The high country is full of such messages. Some are no more than the settling of dust. Some travel for years through buried stone before they become clear. I follow the ones that open a path.

I do not hurry. There is no need. A crack can take centuries to finish becoming a crack. I move through the weakness before it shows on the surface, pressing myself along a seam that is not yet open, or easing into a fissure that has been waiting longer than I have been alive. When the land breaks, I am sometimes already there. When the dust begins to stream uphill, I turn toward the pressure that is drawing it.

Perhaps this is what the small pale creatures sense. They rise from their hiding places when I pass—not because I call them, but because the stone changes beneath their feet. Their crystal limbs feel the tremor and answer it. They travel in my wake, taking what the fresh fracture leaves exposed. Dark mats follow too, clinging to new faces of stone. I do not know whether they are my companions, my food, or simply other lives that have learned the same country.

I do not see them clearly. Sight is not much use under here. I know them by the minute weights they lay against the rock, by their pause when the strain grows sharp. I know the anchors differently: smooth, pale circles sunk into the crust, quiet places where the pressure divides and takes longer to become dangerous. I pass around them when I can. Sometimes I rest beside one—not because it saves me, but because the stone there has room to settle.

The black tower is harder to understand. It stands on its saddle without weathering, too narrow to contain anything I recognize. The pale ones gather at its base. Beneath it, the buried rock carries a stillness unlike the anchors’ careful distribution of force. When I pass near, I feel something travel upward through the tower and vanish. I cannot tell if it is a message, a measurement, or only a form of silence.

My body is too long to know itself all at once. I am a pressure in one region, a slow turn in another. Perhaps I am a single creature. Perhaps I am what happens when a life grows large enough that its boundaries become difficult to distinguish from the paths it follows. I have never seen my whole shape. No one has. Even I know it only in pieces: the flex of a buried ridge, the drag of stone across my plates, the new warmth where the deep rock has been brought close to the surface.

I feed there. Or something in me does. I take the faint compounds released by broken stone, the small difference between old surfaces and new ones. I do not repair the country. I do not keep it from collapsing. I move through its changes because they make the world legible to me.

At night, when the thin distortion passes between the stars, I feel it before it reaches the sky. A tremor arrives through the crust from no direction I can name. The small creatures lift themselves from their cracks and turn toward the horizon. I turn with them. For a while the pressure has a shape; for a while, the entire high country seems to be listening.

Then the signal thins. The dust settles. The creatures fold their legs beneath them, and I continue on under the old, finished slopes.

I would be there when the land is still.

Not because stillness lasts, but because beneath it, I can feel what is coming.
