At first I would think I belonged to the water.

I would be a pale flicker beneath a step, or a cold thread slipping through the crack between two stones. I would know the hidden route from the upper pools to the kitchens, and how a stream can disappear beneath one house and return beneath another without ever being lost. But water here has no single course, and I would not want to be mistaken for a thing that only passes through.

I think I would be one of the people.

I would live where the terraces fold over one another, in a house with a door on the lower level and a window that sometimes looks out onto a channel. I would keep no fixed map. Instead, I would wear cords knotted with small signs: this passage is open; that stair has gone quiet; the water that used to surface by the oven now comes through the north wall. Each morning, some of the knots would be wrong.

My work would be finding the way again.

Not leading people through the basin—that would be too grand a name for it. I would go out when a familiar route changed, carrying a shallow bowl, a wedge, and a little weight on a cord. I would listen at drains. I would watch feathers drift against the walls. Sometimes I would find the old way, hidden under a film of water. Sometimes I would discover that the basin had made a new one, and I would help the town make room for it.

I would be good at noticing what had gone missing without assuming it was gone forever. A child’s boat beneath a doorstep. A cooking spoon carried into a gutter. The route home from a neighbor’s rooms, which has shifted since yesterday. I might know where the little pale fish will gather, though not always how they got there. I would leave water dishes out for the bright things in the cracks, and pretend not to mind when they took them over.

People might come to me with questions, though I would have few answers. I could tell them where the current was going. I could not tell them why it had chosen that way, or what the pools might show tomorrow. When reflections crowded with houses not yet built, I would look with everyone else. I might point out a flaw in one of the reflected bridges. Then a fish would break the picture, and we would go back to our work.

At night, I would sleep lightly, one hand resting on the floor. Not because I feared the basin changing, but because I would want to feel it happen. And at first light, when the channels had rearranged themselves again, I would take my cords and go out to find which way home had become.
