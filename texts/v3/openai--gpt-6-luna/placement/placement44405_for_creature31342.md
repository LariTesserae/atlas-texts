You would find me beneath the basin’s narrow lip, in the green film where the water thins before it falls.

I am not one thing. A little algae, perhaps; bacteria in the black compost; fine threads of something feeding on the leaf that softened yesterday. I gather wherever the damp returns. When water lifts me from the metal, I travel a few inches, a few hours, and settle again in the basin’s shadow. I have no need to go far.

At first, I know only the wet: its cool arrival through the seams, its brief brightness beneath the pale panel, its passage along the channels. The machinery is always there. I do not hear it as a sound. It is pressure in the water, a steady tremor in the metal, the condition under which everything else happens.

Then there is the fern, opening by degrees above me. A fallen leaf comes loose and darkens in the compost. Roots reach the pipes and take what the pipes carry. The reeds lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the light. I live among these changes. I do not direct them. I am part of what they leave behind and part of what they make possible.

If you asked where I end, I could not point to an edge. I thin out along the basin, persist in a crack, disappear into the damp earthless compost, return on a drop of water. Perhaps I am a colony. Perhaps I am the habit of this room: water moving, matter softening, green finding the small permissions built into stone.

I am not the garden’s keeper, and I do not remember the world outside the hatch. I am what has learned to live here, in the circuit the water makes. The roar goes on. The basin overflows by its exact measure. And just beneath the lip, where the silver water becomes a fall, I gather again.
