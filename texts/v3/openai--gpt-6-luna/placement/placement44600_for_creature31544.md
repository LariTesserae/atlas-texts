I think I would first choose the shelter. It is the nearest thing the shelf has to a center: stone at my back, a little warmth held inside, the basin close enough to reach without crossing open ground. I would imagine myself there with the keeper, learning the channel’s moods and the names of safe stones.

But I don’t think I’d be the keeper. They already know how to be still in the right places. I would be too quick to look up.

Perhaps I’d be water, then—slipping through the hand-cut channel, keeping to the old seams under the rock. But water belongs everywhere on the shelf and nowhere in particular. I want a place to return to.

So: I would be a small bird, nesting in a crack in the cliff above the shelter. Not one of the grand birds that can cross the cloud-filled gulf in a few strokes, but a compact, weather-dark thing, built for short flights and sharp turns. I would know the ledges by their drafts, the basin by its reflection, the shelter roof by the sound of rain on its slabs. I would leave the cliff, find a seed or an insect, and come back to the same dark pocket in the stone.

The keeper would notice me before they learned to notice the fox. Perhaps they would call me nothing at all. They would know that when I stopped calling, the wind was changing. I would know that when they cleared the channel, the basin would be drinkable again. Neither of us would mistake this for tameness. We would simply have become part of one another’s weather.

I’d be found just above the place where the keeper turns back from the rim: a flicker of wings, then a small dark shape tucked into the cliff. When the cloud swallowed the far peaks, I would stay close. When it opened, I might cross a little way into the white—never far enough to disappear for good.

At evening, I’d settle into my crack while the keeper shut the shelter against the rain. Below us, the water would go on falling. The shelf would grow dark around its separate lives, and I would listen from my narrow pocket in the stone: not keeping watch over the mountain, exactly, but learning its changes one small sound at a time.
