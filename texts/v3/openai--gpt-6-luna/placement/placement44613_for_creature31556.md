I would be easy to overlook.

At first I might seem like a seed husk caught in the roots, or a dark pebble that has shifted after rain. Then the husk would move. I am a small, dusk-active animal, warm-blooded and narrow-bodied, with quick forepaws and whiskers that feel their way through the spaces beneath the plant mats. Not quite a mouse, not quite a shrew. The people have a name for me, though it changes from hollow to hollow.

I live where a seep darkens the stone: in the cool seam beneath a cushion of leaves, close enough to water that the air tastes different there. My burrow is not a deep tunnel. It is a branching arrangement of borrowed spaces—under a root, between two stones, through a pocket of old grass. The roof is alive. When the wind presses the plants flat, my ceiling lowers; when they lift again, so does the little world above me.

I spend daylight tucked into that world, listening.

The plateau reaches me first as vibration. Hooves arrive through the ground before the herd comes into view; the soft ticking of many feet becomes a tremor, then a rolling weight, then fades away down the slope. I know the passage of a large animal by how long the earth continues to answer it. Birds come differently: a shadow cooling the entrance, a scrape on stone, a brief fall of crumbs. Insects are everywhere, working through the roots and the dark seams. Their rasp is so constant that I notice silence only when the wind changes or a herd has moved beyond hearing.

At evening I come out.

The stone still holds warmth. Dew gathers in the folds of the leaves, and I drink from it, though not enough to make a ceremony of it. I search for seeds, soft shoots, beetles—whatever the place offers without requiring me to cross too much open ground. My routes are short and exact. A crack with a pale lichen mark. A thorned stem bent toward the pool. The safe underside of a root. Beyond these, the plateau is not empty; it is too large to be empty. It is simply more than I can hold in my senses at once.

Sometimes I freeze as the tawny hunter moves somewhere above the mat. Sometimes I hear its paws and cannot tell whether it has heard mine. We share the same folds in the land, though not the same hours, and our meeting is usually only a pause: breath held, ears turned, the night continuing around us.

The pool is a whole country. Beetles circle its mud. Birds arrive from the cliffs to drink and leave prints that fill slowly with dust. Larger animals stand beside it, patient and heavy, while I wait beneath the leaves for their shadows to pass. When the water sinks lower, the roots draw close around what remains. When rain comes, the cracks fill, the plants swell, and the little passages I know become new passages.

I would not range across the plateau. I would belong to one shaded seam in it, and to the journeys that begin there and end a few body-lengths away. The herds would be weather passing over my roof. The old cliffs would be beyond my daily world, except as the source of a bird’s cry or a stone’s sudden fall. The people would be footsteps, smoke, repaired walls, the occasional smell of food carried on the wind. I would know their paths by the places where the plants grew differently.

At night, when the insects are loudest, I move through the roots and leave almost no sign. By morning the dew is gone, the herd’s tracks soften, and the plateau looks much as it did before.

I would be there still: small beneath the green skin of the stone, drinking what the leaves hold, listening for the next passing weight.
