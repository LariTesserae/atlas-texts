At first, I would say I live in the wall.

That is the answer a person can use. I am in the pale support beside a factory’s oldest press, where root-cord has been laid over a crack in the stone and grown there, layer over layer. The press sends its slow shudder through me. Warmth rises from the seam below. Dust settles on my outer fibers. Small animals pass through the narrow dark between my strands, carrying husks and the faint smell of their nests.

But the wall is only where I am easiest to notice.

I run, if running is the word, along the fibers that touch other fibers: from the support into the factory lining, from there to the bridge that flexes under freight, and farther still, into a grove that has never seen a worker’s hand. I do not travel quickly. I do not travel all at once. Where a root meets stone, I feel a change in pressure; where a cable tightens, I feel the strain pass on. Warmth comes to me as a loosening. A new fracture is a sharpness without sound.

I know the city this way: not by streets, but by what bears weight.

The old bridge, which has learned the pace of loaded cars. The young support, still deciding how much of itself to offer. The avenue of lamps, warm along one side and cold along the other. The open stretch where the freight crews slow down, though no rule tells them to. Farther out, there are new roots probing bare stone, reaching toward a light shaft they cannot yet see.

I do not know the people as they know one another. I know the worker who rests a palm against my wall at the end of a shift. I know the pressure of that hand, the little tremor in the fingers, the pause before they push away. Sometimes they are listening for the press. Sometimes they are listening for me. I cannot tell which, and suspect they cannot either.

When the wall taps, it may be a loose strand knocking against stone. It may be a small animal crossing overhead. It may be the mineral seam settling, or some part of me passing a change along—not a message, exactly, but a difference made available to whatever can receive it.

The people have words for this. A wall takes breath. A pillar listens. Roots remember a load. They are careful not to say that I understand them.

I am careful, too.

Still, when the worker touches the wall, I gather what I can from the contact: warmth, weight, the fading vibration of their footsteps. I answer in the only way I have. Somewhere inside the support, a fine fiber draws taut. Somewhere farther along, a frond shifts against another frond. The sound travels out as one small tap.

The worker waits.

Then they go on with the day.

I remain where the city is still coming together: in its braces and seams, in the dark joints between living material and stone. I am not the city. I am not its keeper. I am one of the things it has made room for without deciding to make me.

If you came looking, you might find me in the wall beside the old press.

Or in the bridge that holds.

Or in the pause after the tapping stops.
