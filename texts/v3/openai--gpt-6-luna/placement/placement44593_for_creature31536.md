At first, you might mistake me for a loose bundle of felt beneath a stair.

I live where the wall leans close to the next wall, in a pocket warmed by kitchens and roof-stone. I am small enough to pass through the gaps people have stopped trying to seal. My coat is the color of dust after rain. In winter I sleep curled around my nose; in summer I move through the towns by their undersides, along beams, drain channels, and the dark backs of cupboards.

People call us roof-nesters. They do not agree on what we are called among ourselves.

I know the town by its warmth. The baker’s wall holds heat longest after sunset. The stair beneath the eastern cistern is cold all year. In one old house, a stone has begun to loosen from the lintel; whenever wind comes through the gap, I feel it pass over my whiskers. I know which doors open quietly, which children leave crumbs, which courtyards are watched by dogs. I know the paths people take when they are carrying water, because they walk differently then: careful, balanced, listening to the full weight of the bucket.

I am not tame. If a child reaches for me, I vanish into the wall. Still, the people leave small places for us. A crack beneath the grain bins. A ledge under the roof where the snow does not settle. A shallow dish set away from the drinking cistern, for the dry months. Their kindness is not always intended for me. That does not make it less useful.

For a long time, I thought my home was the warm space beneath the stairs.

Then, in a dry season, I began to follow the cold.

It ran through the rock in fine, hidden threads. I found it by putting my paws against the walls and waiting. A dampness no wider than a breath. A faint taste of minerals. Once, beneath a passage where no one had noticed the stain spreading, I scratched at the mortar until a woman came to see what I wanted. She pressed her palm to the stone. The next day, people opened the wall and found water caught behind it, enough to fill a basin.

After that, they started watching me.

I dislike being watched. I dislike being followed even more. But I have learned that when I stop beside a wall, someone may kneel and listen. They do not always find water. Sometimes they find a crack that will widen in frost, or a place where the channel is backing up. Sometimes they find nothing, and I go on.

I am not a guide. I do not know the whole Fold. The open plateaus frighten me: too much sky, too few crevices, wind reaching me from every side. Beyond the last roofs I travel only under stones, keeping to the ravine edges where the shadow is deep. The land is too large to learn all at once. Even the people who have lived here for generations know only their own turns and crossings.

But I know this basin. I know the old cistern whose water tastes faintly of iron, and the bird ledge that must be left clear each spring. I know the grazer with the torn ear that comes to the lower courtyard when the weather changes. I know the sound of rain beginning somewhere beyond the ridge, before the first drops reach us.

At dusk, when the fires show amber through the shutters, I cross the roofs. From up there, the town looks like another formation of the rock: ledges, dark seams, hollows holding warmth. The people move inside it, carrying their buckets and tools. The birds settle along the cliffs. The grazers stand beyond the last houses, already turning their faces into the wind.

And I move between them, small and nearly unseen, with my paws on the stone.

I think I am a creature of the gaps. But sometimes, when the whole town grows still and water ticks somewhere below the floor, I feel the Fold listening through me. Then I am not sure whether I live beneath the houses, or whether the houses have grown around a small, warm part of the mountain that learned to listen back.
