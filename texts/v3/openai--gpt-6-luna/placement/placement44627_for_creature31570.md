At first, I would be difficult to place.

I would be a change in pressure somewhere beneath the leaves: a small thickening in a root, a pause in the passage of water. Not a body so much as a difference the body makes. Rain would enter me after passing through roofs of green, through mats of old growth and the dark, soft remains of generations. It would arrive warm, carrying the taste of stone.

I would not know the stone as a shape. I would know where the water slowed.

The place would be under a ridge of new black rock, where a seam pressed up through the lowland’s buried layers. Roots would gather there, following the minerals, dividing around the sharp edges. Their pressure would build gradually, too gradually to feel as force. Then one of the narrow channels inside me would tighten. Another would open. Water would pass on.

That is where I would begin to be myself: in the passage between what the lowland takes in and what it can use.

I would have no eyes for the dark. I would know it as coolness in a hollow, as the absence of warmth where a chamber had emptied. I would know distance by delay. A change might begin far away in the roof-canopy and take years to reach me, arriving as a faint alteration in the weight of the water. I would not remember events as pictures. I would remember them as deposits: a mineral left behind, a channel slowly narrowed, a root made dense around a place where stone resisted.

Perhaps I would be one small organ among countless others. A valve, though not one that anyone made. A listening place, though nothing here listens as creatures do. I would not command the roots or understand the whole. I would answer what reached me, and my answer would become part of what reached somewhere else.

Sometimes the pressure would carry a disturbance from above. A foot set down on the moss. A brief warmth in a pocket of air. I would receive these as changes without names. I would not startle. I would not turn toward them. The alteration would travel through me, divide into other channels, and disappear into the lowland’s immense accounting.

Long afterward, perhaps, some root near the ridge would thicken. A pore would close. Water would find another way down.

If there were an “I” in that response, it would not belong to me alone. It would be spread through the channels that took up the pressure and the tissues that changed beneath it. I would be its local shape: a pause in the flow, a narrowing, an opening again.

I would live beneath the green roof, in the seam where living matter meets stone. Above me, leaves would hold the dim light and pass down the rain. Around me, roots would braid the rock into the lowland. I would feel the weight of it all, not as burden but as fact.

And when everything seemed still, I would continue: receiving, straining, releasing. A small, patient passage in a body too vast to know itself all at once.
