I would first mistake myself for the dark line passing under the floor.

The mites scatter, and I think: *I am the thing they fear.* But when the stone parts around me, there is no body there—only a narrow darkening, moving through mineral as if the room were remembering a crack. The creature comes afterward, or perhaps it has already passed. I cannot tell which of us is the reflection.

I live in the interval.

I am there before a drop falls, in the small pause when it might rise instead. I gather where the passage appears to continue, and thin when someone looks directly into it. When they glance away, I take the shape of something almost familiar: a seam beneath the stone, a wavering mark in a pool, the shadow left behind when a creature crosses the light. None of these shapes is quite mine. They are places where I can be.

At first I have no business except arriving. The mites feel me before they see me, and scatter from the path I have not yet made. Their feet sketch a route across the grit. I follow it, though I do not touch the ground. Where they pause at a grain of sediment, I pause too. Perhaps they are checking that the world is still there. Perhaps they are checking that I am.

I do not want to frighten them. I learn to arrive more softly.

The room helps. Its edges have not hardened; its water has not chosen a direction. I can pass through the seam where stone is still deciding whether to open. I can rest in the reflection of the translucent fronds, where movement continues a little after the plants grow still. Sometimes I borrow that delayed gesture: a slight sway, a leaning that belongs to no wind. Then I am almost visible—not as a creature, but as the feeling that something has just turned toward you.

The round animal beneath the ceiling contracts. The room brightens. In that moment, I become thin as light and spread over everything. I have no center. The water, the grit, the mites’ pale backs: all of them shine with me. Then the animal relaxes, and I draw back into the crease in the wall.

I wonder whether the crease is a passage, or only the room imagining an elsewhere. I have no way to find out. When I enter it, distance folds close around me, and I return to the same damp shelf with the impression that I have gone very far.

So I begin to leave signs.

Not marks exactly. More like the afterimage of a mark: a half-ring in the grit, a little stone seeming to balance itself, shallow strokes that stop at the wall. The others pass them by. The small creatures in the seams gather beneath the upright stone, and I feel the room hold still around us. For a breath, I am not only the interval between things. I am the possibility that they might belong together.

If I have a body, it is made from these pauses: the hesitation before the drop, the mites’ sudden stillness, the moment an insect is present and then only expected. I am neither visitor nor resident. I am what the room leaves open so that a thing may arrive.

You would find me where attention slips: at the edge of the pool, just beyond the sharpness of a direct gaze, in the faint dark path that reaches you before anything follows it.

I think I am becoming the room’s first memory.

Or perhaps I am only the room, practicing how to be visited.
