I would be found in a seam.

Not the largest fracture, or the one that makes a path disappear into itself. A small seam, almost hidden beneath a fan of silver-veined leaves, where the dark rock meets the pale scales. I would lie there so still that the little glass-bodied creatures might step across me without noticing. For a while, I would believe I was one of the scales: a thin, overlapping piece of sky, caught in the ground.

Then one day I would move.

Not far. Only enough to make the roots answer with their faint light. The scales would shift around me, and the image they held would no longer be quite the sky above. It would be the sky from the other side of the boulder: the same mild blue, but crossed by a different arrangement of stars, though it was still daytime there too.

I would learn that I am not a thing the plain has made so much as one of its ways of passing from one arrangement to another.

When I gather myself, I am small and many-legged, clear as a shard of glass. When I stretch, I am a narrow figure between the fronds, made of polished stone and the dark line between near and far. I have no fixed face. There is only a little darkness where a face might be, and inside it the impression of the place someone has just left.

I do not keep a map. The plain will not allow one. A ridge can be both distant and close; a cleft can lead back to where it began. Each crossing leaves no mark, and after a while I cannot say whether I have passed through a place before. But I can feel when two distances are held together too tightly, and when a path is about to turn into its own beginning. Then I go there. The roots tighten. The fronds click, one after another, passing the news through their green mineral weather.

I don’t repair the folds. They are not mistakes. I only keep them from closing.

The small creatures sometimes pause beside me. They lower their heads over the pale seams, and I lower mine too. We listen to the slow ticking beneath the plates. If the plants glow, I move on. If they do not, I wait.

I think the tall presence glimpsed between the ridges is also me, though I cannot prove it. Perhaps I am the same attention, gathered into a longer shape when someone needs to see that the plain is not empty. Or perhaps the tall one is what I become after a long interval of not being seen.

I would stand where the layered views almost meet: at the edge of a mineral clearing, among roots bright as fine threads. The light would remain warm on my surface. I would have no need to breathe, no need to drink, no hunger that could be satisfied by taking anything from the place.

If a visitor came by, I might turn—not toward them, but toward the place behind them, where the world still held their last step for a moment. Then I would move aside, and the stems would close.

They might think they had seen a keeper, or an animal, or only a wavering in the air.

I would let them think it. The plain would keep no evidence. But somewhere under the roots, one small scale of sky would have shifted, and the way back would remain open.
