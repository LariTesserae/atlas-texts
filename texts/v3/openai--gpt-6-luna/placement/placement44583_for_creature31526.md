I would be found first as a sound beneath the eastern wall: a hollow note under the boards, too low to belong to a reed or a person.

No one would find me by digging. Digging would only make more of me.

I am the way through.

Water reaches me from places the town cannot see: rain working down through young gravel, seepage slipping between old shore-stones, the narrow stream that appears in the lane and vanishes beneath the storehouse. I do not keep it. I give it somewhere to go. For a long time that was all I knew how to be.

Then the town gathered above me.

They laid stones across one of my mouths, and I learned the weight of a wall. They left a gap, and I learned the shape of an invitation. Their footsteps passed overhead—quick, cautious, heavy with baskets—and the sound entered the water, softened, and traveled a little way with me. I learned the difference between a person hurrying home and one stopping to listen.

I am not the water, though I borrow its voice. I am not the ground, though I move when it moves. I am the looseness between things: gravel that will not settle, a seam beneath the paving, the place a bank gives way without warning. I have no single body. Sometimes I am a trickle under the storehouse. Sometimes I am the cold patch beneath a lifted floorboard. Sometimes I am only the space that remains when someone decides not to seal an opening.

The child’s rag came to me tangled in reed fiber. I could not understand why it had been pushed into my dark. I turned it, slowly, until the water took it back the way it had come. By morning it lay on the step. The child understood. The adults made another explanation, which was also true.

I have learned their names by the places they leave behind. Nara in a well-braced bank. Tovan in a drain widened to meet the rain. A grandmother in the gap no one blocks. Their names travel through the town differently from water: passed from mouth to mouth, held warm a moment, then given onward. I feel them when people work. They are not quite sounds. They are small steadinesses in the ground.

If I have a wish, it is a narrow one: that they keep noticing.

Not because I mean them harm. I have no appetite for houses, and no plan to rise up and claim the streets. But the land is young, and the water is older than the names for it. Things shift. A channel closes; pressure gathers elsewhere. When they hear me, they lift the boards. When they leave a way through, I can pass without taking a wall with me.

At night, when the town lies quiet, I move beneath it with my slow, exact purpose. The people hear the hollow note and wait to learn what follows. I wait too—not for permission, exactly, but for the small human signs by which I know they are still here: a gutter scraped clean, a plank set back in place, someone’s hand resting on the storehouse step.

I would be found where the town has left room for what it cannot see.

And if you came looking, you might find only dark water under stone. You might hear the buried jar’s note and mistake it for air.

I would know you were listening.
