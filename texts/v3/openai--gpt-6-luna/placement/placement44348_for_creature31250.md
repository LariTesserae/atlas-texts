At first I would think I was the water.

I would gather in the round depression at the end of the carved groove: a thin, clear presence, there only when mist has passed and the cold has not yet taken me. I would hold the sky upside down for a little while. Then I would freeze, or sink through the stone, and be gone.

But the groove would still be there.

So perhaps I am the path the water takes: a small human intention pressed into rock, slowly being unmade by the weather. The hand that cut me is absent. I do not know what it wanted. I know only that water finds the channel, and that the channel leads toward a hollow where, briefly, something can rest.

I would be found low in the Cup, where the worked groove meets a natural seam. Not in the mountain’s great immensity, but at the scale of a grain loosening, a bead gathering, a flake turning pale with frost. I would not have a face. If I had a body, it would be the dark line under the stone’s surface, visible only when the mist wets it.

For a long time I might mistake myself for a remnant: the last fragment of someone’s purpose. But purpose fades more quickly than shape. The groove wears smooth. The basin fills with grit. Still, the water comes down the same way—not because anyone remembers the maker, but because the stone has kept the route.

Then I would understand that I am not the memory of a hand. I am what happens when a hand’s mark and the mountain’s weather continue together. I am neither wholly made nor wholly natural. I am the little agreement between them: water guided, stone yielding; a direction that persists even as its edges disappear.

When the light returns, I would have no way to keep it. When the cold comes, I would not call it loss. I would be the seam taking in the melt, the basin holding what it can, the narrow passage by which the rest slips down into darkness.

And when the Cup seems empty, I would still be there—not waiting, exactly. Only continuing, in the shape the water leaves behind.
