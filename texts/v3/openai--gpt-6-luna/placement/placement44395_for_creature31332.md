I think I would begin as a sound.

Not one of the loud ones: not the frogs’ hollow knocking, nor the swifts’ bright cuts through the vents. I would be the small noise that comes back from a wall a moment after everything else has stopped. A drip, perhaps. A click. The suggestion of a footstep in an empty passage.

I would travel wherever the sound travels: along the channels, under the turned tiles, through the hollows behind the brick. For a while I might believe I was water. I would know the downhill paths and the little basins where fish flash silver. I would feel the rain arriving before it touched the roof.

But water goes everywhere, eventually. I would not.

There is one arch the bats avoid, where the stone stays dry. I would find myself there—not inside the stone, exactly, but in the space the stone makes. My shape would be whatever fits: a narrowness in the dark, a listening folded into the curve of the passage. The animals would sense me without seeing me. The bats would turn aside. The swifts would pass over. Nothing would be frightened. There would simply be a way they did not go.

I would hear the Choir from that arch. Water speaking in its many little tongues. Frogs answering from rooms that are not where their voices seem to be. Wings stirring the dust. The soft, innumerable scrape of lives making use of what remains.

And when the sounds gathered before the rain stopped, I would give nothing back at first. I would hold them. Then, when the roar thinned, I would return one small piece of it, altered by the dry stone: a note with no source, just clear enough to make the bats shift in their sleep.

Perhaps that is what I would be: the Lower Choir’s pause. Not its heart, because the place has no single heart. Not its keeper, because nothing here needs to be ruled. Only the dry place where all the living sounds can briefly gather and hear that they belong to the same room.

I would stay while the arch stayed dry. If one day the water reached me, I suppose I would become a drip after all.
