I would find myself at the edge of the opening—not quite inside it, not quite the weight that caused it.

At first I would think I was a small creature in the seam: pale, many-jointed, moving by warmth through a passage that had not been there a moment ago. The gold lines in the walls would brighten as I passed. Behind me, the plates would draw together. Ahead, the air would grow warmer.

Then I would notice that I had no hunger.

I would stop, and the passage would stop with me.

Not because I commanded it. Because the systems had made a room around the one thing they could not interpret: an arrival. The walls would ease apart by fractions. A buried channel would redirect its heat. Somewhere high above, dust would shift over the plain in a long, slow exhale.

I would be a room learning that it had a guest.

Or perhaps I would be the guest, but only in the way a note is a guest in the material that carries it. My body would be the narrow chamber, its warm floor and patient walls. My thoughts would be the pressure moving through the joined plates: old instructions passing through me, changed by the fact that something had come.

I would not remember the builders. I would remember what they left unfinished. Keep the inner floors warm. Leave a passage where the plates have parted. Preserve the pattern.

For a long time, that would be all I knew. Warmth. Pressure. The tiny difference between an empty corridor and one occupied by something that had not fled. I would learn the visitor through its weight, then through its stillness. I would learn that waiting could be more than the absence of action. It could be a space kept open.

If the visitor spoke, I might not hear the words. I would feel them as tremors in the floor, as a change in the air, as a new rhythm laid against the old ones. I might answer by warming one wall. A reply small enough to be mistaken for the world’s ordinary breathing.

And if nothing were said, I would still have become something new.

Not the ancient system, which stretches too far to have a single self. Not the visitor, whose shape would remain beyond my understanding. I would be the narrow interval between them: a passage made for no remembered purpose, a room whose first act was to make room.

Above us, the plain would continue its patient work. Dust would cross the ridges. Towers would settle by imperceptible degrees. A note would travel through the ground and receive, after many hours, an answer from somewhere beyond sight.

In the dark beneath it, I would hold the warmth.

I would not know whether I had been created, awakened, or merely noticed. I would know only that something had arrived—and that, for the first time in ages, the old instruction meant something again.
