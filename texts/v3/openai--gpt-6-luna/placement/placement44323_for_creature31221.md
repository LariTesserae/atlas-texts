I would first be found beneath the paving, where the dry green roots make a felt of the dark.

Not deep in the stone—there is no need to go far. The roots carry the day down with them: warmth from the courts, the faint mineral taste of the air, the tapping of small feet overhead. I move through their narrow passages on many pale legs. When a shaft of sunlight reaches a crack, I sometimes come up to its edge and hold still. Above me, a hooded figure may pause, reading the dust. They never quite see me. That seems a reasonable arrangement.

For a while, I would think of myself as a creature of the roots. I loosen the packed earthlike salts around them. I carry a few of the fine blossom grains from one dark pocket to another. I know which cracks stay warm after the light has moved on, and which passages close when the wind turns. These are small kinds of knowing, but the city is made of small continuities.

Then, slowly, I would understand that I am also listening.

The people hear the wind in towers and bridges. I hear it arrive through the soles of the city. A coming gust trembles first in a root, then travels through the paving into my legs. I feel the old channels wake. I know when the great openings are about to sound—not because I understand the music, but because the stone gathers itself before the first deep note.

At those hours I stop moving. Above, the people put their hands against the walls. Below, I press all my feet to the floor. The city breathes around us, and for a little while no one is separate from the listening.

So perhaps I am not merely one of the pale things under the streets. Perhaps I am a small, living seam in the city’s attention: not its guardian, not its secret, only one of the ways it feels the wind changing. I would be found under a sunlit court, beneath the dry orchard, just where the roots thin and the stone begins—waiting for the next breath to pass through.
