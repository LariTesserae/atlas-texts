I think I would live near the outer membrane, where the district’s ordered noise thins into the pressure of the open sea.

Not in a grand observation room. The Fold has no room to spare for grandeur. I would have a narrow chamber wedged behind the old turbine court, partly flooded, with a bunk harnessed to one wall and a dry locker just large enough for my suit. From the doorway, I could touch the rail I use to listen.

I would be a pressure listener—not an engineer, exactly, and not a mystic, despite what the children might decide. I would know the Fold’s ordinary sounds by their small differences: the pump that knocks a little late, the cable that hums when it should be slack, the deep note in the basalt that means the surge is coming. The engineers would have diagrams. I would have my hands.

At first, I would probably think of myself as part of the machinery. I’d answer faults before greetings, sleep through voices, wake at a valve’s wrong rhythm. But the Fold would make it difficult to stay only that. Someone would begin tapping twice on the rail to tell me they’d brought tea. Someone else would leave a bright thread wound around my hatch, though I’d never asked for a marker. I would learn which footsteps—or which gloved touches—belonged to whom.

When the district dims, I would listen. When the great note came, I would put my palm to the support and wait for its second vibration: the one that tells me whether the membrane has taken the pressure cleanly. Then I’d knock the signal down the rail. A long tap for the turbine crew. Two quick ones for the people in the low passages. A pattern for the child who always forgets to secure the loose tools.

I would keep a private record, too. Not a set of plans; plans are rewritten every time the Fold survives something. I’d keep a map of changes: a new tremor in a stairwell, a warm pipe that has begun to cool, the place where the basalt answers the water with a note no one has heard before. I would mark these on strips of metal and hang them where the next listener could find them.

I don’t think I’d be fearless about the sea beyond the membrane. I’d be curious in the careful way the Fold permits: one hand on the rail, one eye on the pressure gauge, never mistaking darkness for invitation. Still, sometimes, after a surge passed and the cables settled, I would stay by the outer wall a little longer than necessary.

Not because I expected to see anything.

Because the Fold is always speaking, and I would want to know whether, one day, the sea speaks back.
