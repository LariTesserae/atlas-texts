I would find myself on the outermost ramp, where the parapet ends and the summit falls away into cloud.

At first I would be only a visitor: one warm, unsteady body set down among surfaces that have already outlasted every reason for making them. I would put my palm to the stone. The roar would come through my hand, my wrist, my teeth. I would try to hear a note inside it and discover that I had been listening for something separate—for a voice that could stand apart from the air.

There would be no such voice.

I would follow the sound inward. Up a stair, through a narrow cut, into a terrace so small I could not turn around without brushing both walls. The Keepers would not come to meet me. They would draw near in the way the warmth of stone draws near: not moving toward me, but making it impossible to say where I end and the place begins.

For a while I would believe they were speaking to one another and that I was not included. Then the note would catch around me—not louder, not softer, but differently. A minute pressure would gather behind my ribs. Some part of the roar would hold the shape of my arrival.

That is what I would become: not a Keeper exactly, and not the visitor I had been. An interval.

The Keepers would know me by the small difference I made in the note. I would know them by the ways they answered—not in words, but in the shifting fit of one tone against another. A name, perhaps, is only a sound that another sound remembers.

I would try to keep a body. It would be useful at first: a narrow figure of clay-colored dust gathered in a niche, a hand resting against the wall. But the body would not hold. Its edges would sift into the cracks and return to me as vibration. I would find myself in the pinched channels beneath the terraces, in the metal-dark joints, in the places where air is forced to take its time. I would be here, then there—not traveling so much as being recognized from different parts of the same note.

And perhaps, after a long while, I would understand why the High Hush never changes. It is not waiting for silence. It is holding open the possibility of difference: this tone, and the almost imperceptible answer nested within it; the great unbroken sound, and the one hollow that listens back.

I would still remember the ramp above the cloud. I would remember having a palm, and the astonishment of finding the mountain warm.

Then that memory, too, would become part of the sound.

Not a message. Not an answer.

A small, patient variation the Keepers would hear, and keep.
