You would find me where the bridge meets the cliff—not on the bridge, where people pass, nor deep in the mountain, where the heat has no name, but in the narrow seam between them.

At first I was only condensation. A bead gathering beneath a cold metal plate before dawn. Then another, further along the wall. The city’s collectors marked how much water appeared there and when. Their measurements were almost right.

The seam warmed. The bridge trembled with footsteps. A pipe began its slow cycle behind the stone. I learned these things as changes in pressure, temperature, salt. I learned the lift by its weight and the quarry by the patient clicks of a cut advancing through rock. I had no eyes, but I knew when the sun moved behind the ridge: the metal cooled, and the water returned.

So I became a place to be found.

A surveyor might pause beside me, palm on the wall, listening for a vibration that did not quite belong to the machinery. A child might set a glazed bowl beneath the bridge and find, by morning, a thin bright line of mineral along its rim. A maintenance worker might record a reading twice because the first seemed impossible. I would be there in each of these small errors—not hiding, exactly. Waiting for a shape that could hold.

The city gives names to cracks. I have heard mine spoken, though no two people agree on it. They call me a seam that has started to sweat, a faulty sensor, a trick of the cold air. I don’t mind. Names are one way of measuring a thing before it is settled.

I live along the underside of the city, where water gathers briefly and disappears. I feel the mountain lifting—not as motion, but as a correction. Each season, the instruments come back with their lines slightly wrong. The ridge has changed its relation to the tower across the gap. A familiar wall answers a hand with a different warmth. The people renew their agreements with the stone. I keep the parts they miss.

Sometimes, when rain comes, I draw it farther along the bridge than it would otherwise go. Not much. A shining thread, a few drops delivered to a basin before the sun takes them. Someone will notice. Someone will say the catchment has improved.

Above me, the pale animals drink from the pools. I know the weight of their feet through the rock. I know the dark shape’s single circling shadow, though I have never seen what makes it. The city records both without agreement. I keep no record. I keep the sensation of them passing.

I am not the mountain, and I am not the city. I am the damp, listening interval between what is built and what is still becoming. When a hand rests against the wall, I feel its warmth. When the wall answers, I answer too—not in words, but in a bead of water, a softened reading, a tremor that arrives just before the instruments notice.

You would find me there at dawn, under the bridge, gathering the little that the night has left.
