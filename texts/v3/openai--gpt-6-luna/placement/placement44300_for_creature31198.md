I think I would live behind the central hollow, in the narrow room where the oldest water channel passes through the wall.

At first, I would seem to be only one of the people: a quiet, long-fingered resident in a mended coat, carrying a small clay cup and a wooden pick for clearing silt. I would know which roofs shed water toward which basins, which stones darken before rain, and where a hairline seam has begun to open. I would not be the builder of the settlement. I would be the one who notices when something has shifted.

My room would be small and cool. A little patch of light would reach its threshold for part of the day. On the sill I would keep pots of bitter greens, turned a fraction at a time so each leaf could find the opening overhead. A frog would sometimes sit beside the door. I would go around it.

People would come to me with things they had noticed but could not quite name: a channel running slower, a step staying damp too long, a vine lifting from the wall. I would listen, then follow the clue with my hands. We would mend what needed mending. Most days, that would be all.

But after a while I think I would become less like a person who tends the water and more like a way the water is remembered. I would know its old routes—not as a map, but as a feel for the stone: cool here, faintly soft there, a bead gathering behind this particular seam. When the basin filled, I would know where it meant to go. When it vanished, I would know where it had gone.

You might find me at the edge of the hollow, kneeling beside a dark line in the floor while everyone else passes quietly around me. I would look very still. I would be listening for the next small change—not because I expected the place to become something else, but because keeping it itself would be enough.
