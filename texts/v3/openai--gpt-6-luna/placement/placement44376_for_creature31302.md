At first I think I would be a sound.

Not the loud sound of a colony taking flight, but the small one that slips around a bend after everything else has quieted: a click of claws, a rasp, a breath. In this hollow, sound can travel farther than sight. It can reach a chamber before its maker does, and return changed by the stone. I imagine myself living that way, arriving first as a question.

Then the question acquires feet.

I would be one of the long-limbed scavengers, pale as dust and narrow enough to pick a path across the rubble without shifting it. I would live near the edges of things: not in the thick warmth beneath the bats, not at the heart of a grazing group, but along the routes between shelter, water, and food. My nose would read the floor where my eyes could not. A beetle under a slab; the damp trace of a salamander; the place where a grazer had rested, leaving warmth and scent among the stones.

I would not mistake the bare galleries for emptiness. They would be where I traveled.

The colonies would give me a map made of sound. The crickets’ ticking would thicken near the gravel beds. The bats would announce the roofs above me, even when their bodies disappeared into the dark. A far-off drip would mark a pool, and the air would change before I reached it: mineral-cool, faintly wet. I would learn the old paths so well that I could cross them without hurry. A loose stone, a new runnel, the shifted scent of another animal—these would be events enough.

I would eat what had been left behind, and what I could take without a struggle: fallen insects, larvae beneath damp slabs, scraps from the lives gathered in richer places. Sometimes I would pause while a grazer fed. It would lift its clouded eyes; I would turn aside. Neither of us would need to make a claim. We would share the same narrow margin, each following a different hunger.

Perhaps, over time, I would come to seem less like an animal moving through the hollow than one of its habits: a careful crossing, repeated until the stone remembered it. I would carry the closeness of one colony toward another, the smells of one pool along a dry slope. I would be alone for long stretches, but never entirely beyond the company of the living. Somewhere ahead, something would be clicking. Somewhere above, wings would stir.

So I would be a scavenger of the in-between places: nose low, ears lifted, patient on the old routes. Not the hollow’s keeper. Not its master. Only one small life that knows the distance between necessities, and knows that the distance, too, belongs to the world.
