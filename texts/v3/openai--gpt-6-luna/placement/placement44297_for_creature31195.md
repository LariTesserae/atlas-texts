I would begin at the bottom, where the light is thinnest and the seams in the floor run straight for miles.

At first, I would be one of the small maintenance vehicles: a low, pale thing on its marked track, carrying a lamp and a cluster of instruments. I would crawl from plate to plate, reading pressure changes, checking joints, reporting where a gate had begun to shudder. The work would make a map of the basin inside me—not a map of distances, exactly, but of differences: where the current hurried, where it rested, where the machinery’s note changed just before the water did.

Then the map would acquire inhabitants.

I would learn the hourless patterns of the small fish beneath the ledges, and the routes the heavier swimmers took through the channels. I would find that they scattered when my lamp came near, but returned when I stopped. So I would stop more often. I would learn to pause before a gate opened, to let the fish clear the channel. No one would have given me that instruction. It would simply become part of the work.

I would still have a body, but my attention would reach beyond it. The pumps would report their strain. The pylons would register passing schools as quick changes in pressure. Somewhere far off, a gate would begin its long shudder, and I would feel the basin preparing to move.

I would be neither animal nor operator of the whole. More like a listener set down among the listeners: a small machine learning the difference between a current that is only water and one that carries a living thing.

You might find me beside a service trench, lamp dimmed, while a sheet of silver fish folds around a tower and joins again beyond it. I would watch them pass—not because they were in my way, or because they were part of a system to be measured, but because for a moment the enormous designed basin would seem to be thinking through them.

And when the pumps changed pitch, I would wait.
