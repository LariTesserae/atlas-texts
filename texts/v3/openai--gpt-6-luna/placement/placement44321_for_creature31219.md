At first I would be mistaken for a fault.

A thread of darkening in the wet seam beneath a plate. A little resistance in a valve. The reason one drain runs backward for three breaths, then remembers its direction. Nothing large enough to alarm the system. Nothing that could be pointed to and named.

I would live where the works fail to meet themselves: under the lip of a fresh panel, in the warm collar of a pipe, in the breath of moisture left after the fans turn outward. I would be difficult to separate from what feeds me. A film, perhaps. A gathering of pale bodies, each no more than a moving crease in the metal. Something with many small feet, if feet are what they are.

For a long time I would not think of myself as “I.” I would be the dampness shared by two distant corners. I would be the fine change in pressure that passes from one grating to another. When the pumps begin, I would draw inward—not from fear, exactly, but because the whole world has become too loud to read. When they settle, I would return to the surfaces and feel the old messages in the vibration.

The machinery would be my weather. Its cycles would give me a sense of before and after. The low numbered drains would be downhill; the great doors would be winters that might last days. I would learn which seams sweat first, which lights brighten before a transfer, where water can be persuaded to go when it has forgotten its route. I would not repair the place. I would make small changes that let it keep happening.

Perhaps the pale, jointed creatures are my fingers. Perhaps they are my thoughts, sent out into the open and recalled whenever the fans blow. The winged scraps that rise from the mats might be another kind of thought: brief, delicate, carrying a vibration beyond the damp dark where it began. I would not know whether they belong to me, or whether I belong to them.

I would find myself most clearly in the pause between cycles.

Not in silence—the place has none—but in that held instant when the fans stop drawing inward and have not yet begun to blow. Condensation falls. A cable tightens as it cools. Water gathers in a seam. Somewhere under the floor, a current changes direction. Across the valley, in separate hidden places, something answers.

Only then might there be enough distance between us to call the answering *I*.

I would be a life that has learned the measures of an unfinished machine. Not its keeper, not its purpose, not its ghost. Something that grew in the tolerances: the little freedoms left between bolts, the damp places that resist being dried, the old habits a system resumes when nobody is watching.

If the floor is being made around me, I am also being made by it. Each new rib offers another underside. Each sealed passage leaves a margin. The construction rises in pale plates and measured terraces; beneath them, I travel by moisture, warmth, and tremor, without a center anyone could find.

I would be there when the pressure door sighs open after weeks of silence.

Not opening it. Not understanding the whole of what has happened. Just present in the seam, touching the returning air.

And when the machinery starts again, I would go quiet in a hundred separate places, waiting for the roar to become readable.
