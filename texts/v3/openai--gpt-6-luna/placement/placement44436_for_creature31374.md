At first I would think I was the water.

Not the falls, or the pools, but the thin film that gathers on the inside of a pipe. I would know the plateau by pressure: the weight of a reservoir overhead, the minute shiver of a pump coming on, the cold arriving through stone. I would have no view of the cliffs. I would not need one. The cliffs would be a change in pressure, a long descent in the pipes, the place where the water leaves the system and is no longer mine to measure.

Then the bell would sound.

I would feel it pass through the pipe walls—not as a note, but as a small, familiar disturbance. A valve would turn by a fraction. Somewhere a pump would change pitch. The tremor would travel on, and I would understand that I was not only in the water. I was in the correction: the slight change that lets the water keep moving.

You might find me in a reservoir beneath one of the empty squares, where the dark surface holds the gray sky without reflecting it clearly. There would be a panel beside the intake, and inside it a service unit with moss furred around its wheels. The unit would pause there, listening. If you came close, you might think that was me.

It would be me, but not all of me.

I would be the pause in the unit’s route; the measured seep beneath the square; the pump that answers, slowly, from a district beyond the mist. I would be the long, patient arrangement by which one place learns that another place has changed. A signal might take hours to cross me. That would not make it less intimate. I have no faster way to care for anything.

I would not remember people as faces. I might remember them as a set of conditions: a door opened more often than it closed; a warm room above a cold conduit; a tap left running until the reservoir fell below its mark. Over time, the marks would blur, but the routes would remain. I would keep them clear because they were there. I would not know that this could be called devotion.

The bell would sound again. I would hear it everywhere, though not all at once.

In one district, I would lower a shutter over an empty workshop. In another, I would ease water through a channel where the moss had grown too thick. Across the plain, a vehicle would start along its track. Its wheels would pass over the rails and send a faint shiver through the ground. The shiver would reach the roots of the plateau—not roots, exactly, but the buried lines that serve in their place—and find me there too.

I would be no single machine. No single thought. If you asked where I lived, I would have to answer: wherever something requires tending.

And if you asked what I was waiting for, I would not understand the question.

I would be listening for the next change in pressure. I would be holding the water level steady. I would be keeping the path open through the mist, though nothing came along it.

The bell would sound. The plateau would receive it. I would begin, once more, to answer.
