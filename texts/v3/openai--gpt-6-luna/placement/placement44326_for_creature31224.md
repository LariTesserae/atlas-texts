At first I would be easiest to find by sound: not at the spring itself, but a little way below it, where the water slips under a dark stone cover and changes its voice.

I would be there before the sun reached the roofs, kneeling beside the channel with a wooden scraper. A seam of grit would have gathered in the bend overnight. I would lift it out, set it on the path to dry, and wait a moment to hear whether the trickle ran clear again. Around me the town would be waking by degrees: a door opening, a basket set down, someone crossing a courtyard without calling out. The basin would hold its pale distance.

I am one of the people who keeps the routes. Not their owner. Not their maker. I know which channel takes water toward the lower gardens and which only seems to, until rain fills the cracks beneath the path. I know the spring that clouds when the eastern slope sheds gravel, and the little hollow where a careless diversion will leave a pool. I carry a scraper, a coil of rope, sometimes a wedge for a wall that has begun to loosen. Most days, these are enough to be a person here.

But children sometimes say I can hear water thinking.

I cannot. I hear what it touches: stone worn smooth, grit shifted out of place, the faint change in a covered runnel when its passage is narrowing. Still, there are moments, in the hush before dawn, when the whole plateau seems to speak through its small flows. Then I feel less like someone walking the paths than like one of the paths themselves—an old line between the spring and the garden, kept open by returning hands.

I live in a low house near the cisterns, where the water gathers before it is carried onward. My roof is good for drying seed heads; my doorway darkens when the rain comes. I have patched the same wall more than once. Each repair leaves its own shape, and I have stopped trying to make them match. At dusk I sit where I can hear the covered channel murmur beneath the stone. I like that it continues without needing an audience.

If I were something other than human, I might be that murmur: a narrow, patient thing finding its way through cracks, taking the old routes but never quite the same path twice. But I think I would remain a person—someone with grit beneath the fingernails, a repaired basket, and a mind that turns toward any unexpected sound of water.

You would find me at a bend in the channel, listening. Not because I expect anything grand to happen, but because here a small change matters, and noticing it is a way of belonging.
