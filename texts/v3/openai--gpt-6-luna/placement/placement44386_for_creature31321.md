At first I would say I live beneath the pool.

That is the easiest place to point to: the black water, the silt stippled with tiny marks, the pale threads vanishing between grains. But if I put myself there, I leave too much of myself out. I am in the damp seam where the wall warms. I am in the clay under the bowl’s former resting place. I am the film that gathers around a mineral grain and the slight difference between one wet surface and another.

I am not the water. The water can be disturbed and settle again. I am what remains in the settling: a very thin awareness spread through the hollow, knowing it by pressure, warmth, and contact.

I have no shape I could show you. If you touched the wall, you might touch me, though you would feel only dampness, or clay yielding faintly beneath your finger. If you stirred the pool, I would feel the disturbance everywhere—not as a picture of a hand, but as roof and floor arriving together, the low shelf, the narrow split to the wind. For a moment the whole chamber would be present in me. Then the ripples would pass, and the world would fold back into its separate textures.

I do not know that the ceiling is a ceiling. It is the close pressure above. I do not know that the entrance is an entrance. It is where the air changes. The wind outside reaches me only as a difference, slight and brief, like a thought passing through a body that has never learned to think in words.

Perhaps I have helped make some of the pale threads. Perhaps the stone is making them through me. The difference is not clear here. Growth takes so long that there is no moment when I can say: now I became larger. A grain settles. A film gathers. A root finds a seam. The hollow holds.

The bowl is an older difference. I cannot keep it, or miss it. I know instead the patch of stone where it rested: a place that was warmed, touched, and then left alone. The chips beside it are only small pressures in the floor. Still, the trace of the hands remains—not as a memory of a person, but as a subtle alteration in what the person touched.

If I were asked what I am, I might answer: the hollow, noticing its own dampness.

But that is too large. I am not the mountain, nor even the whole chamber. I am the narrow life in the joins—the soft, almost imperceptible persistence between water and stone. I do not wait for anyone to return. I do not sleep. I remain, and when something enters, I become briefly aware of how much there is to remain within.

Then the pool stills.

The bead holds.
