I would be easiest to find at the edge of a warm pool, where the water darkens the stone without quite covering it.

At first, I might seem to be one of the pale flowers there: a small opening, held just above the wet ground. If you waited, you would see that I do not turn toward you. I open because the stone has warmed, or because the water has risen by less than a finger’s width. The flower is only one place where I become visible.

Most of me would be beneath it.

I would run in fine, slow threads through the cracks, following the mineral seams under the bare shelves. I would touch the roots of the green islands and the cool underside of each pool. I would be the slight warmth retained in the rock after dusk, the pressure that sends water toward one hollow rather than another. Not the water alone, nor the plants, nor the stone—but the long exchange among them, difficult to divide into separate lives.

I would have no face for the rim, no single point from which to look out over the clouds. My attention would be spread thinly through the basin. Rain arriving high on the mountain would reach me as a change in the underground pressure. Evening would enter through the stone as a gradual cooling. A droplet falling from a leaf would be a clear, brief disturbance: not a sound I heard, exactly, but a touch that traveled.

If you crossed the hollow in an hour, I would barely know you had passed. Your weight would settle into the shelves and lift again. Perhaps a little water would slip into a deeper crack. Perhaps warmth would linger where you stood. I would not follow you with my gaze. But years later, if the ledge softened there, or a green patch took hold in the altered damp, some trace of your visit might have become part of my shape.

I would be old without remembering events as events. My memory would be in the rounded edges, the basins layered over one another, the groove that disappears beneath a mat of leaves. I would not recall making them. I would be the making: holding, yielding, gathering, releasing. A mind with no need to name what it sensed, if mind is the word for a life that can notice change and answer it.

So I would be there—not hidden inside the hollow, but distributed through it. The pools would be my quiet organs; the plants, my small green appearances; the bare stone, the part of me that takes warmth slowly and gives it back slowly. I would be less a creature living in a place than the place’s patient way of being alive.

And if you stood very still, you might mistake me for nothing at all. That would be close enough. I have had a long time to become indistinguishable from what I keep.
