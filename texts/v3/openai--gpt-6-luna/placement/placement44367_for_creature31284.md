You would find me under the walkway between the boiler house and the assembly hall, where a warm outlet bends the channel around a cracked foundation.

At first I am only a pale length beneath the grate. I have no use for the lamps, and no name that stays mine. I know the district by pressure: the pump’s slow thud arriving through the water, a bootstep breaking into little shivers, the soft drag of silt when a wall begins to give. I coil where the current is kindest. When it changes, I move.

There are others beside me—blind bodies tucked into the same dark seams, crustaceans stippling the shallows, small mouths clinging beneath the warm pipes. We gather at the outlets and loosen when the water cools. From above, we must look like a single living stain, shifting across the channel. We are not trying to tell anyone anything. Still, the people have learned to watch us.

When the eels crowd beneath a platform, someone takes the longer walkway. When the crustaceans leave a channel, someone brings a lamp and listens at the wall. They read our movements as they read the pumps: not as a message, exactly, but as a change in the district’s breathing.

Perhaps that is what I become here—not a warning, but a way of feeling the place. I feel a tired engine before it falters. I feel the new seam opening behind the stone, the warmth sliding sideways through the dark. Above me, a resident puts one hand on the railing and pauses. I put my body against the current. For a moment, we are attending to the same thing.

I would be small enough to disappear beneath a grate, and old enough to know that the channel will not keep its shape. The people will raise another walkway. The water will find another route. I will follow it, with the others, into whatever room the earth is still making.
