I would be just beyond the raw little entrance, where the first roots lose their grip on ordinary soil.

At first, you might not find me at all. I would be the space between a hanging root and the stone beneath it: a narrow interval, pale with light, too small to matter. Then you would look away. The interval would lengthen—not like a passage opening, but like a thought being given room. The root would stir. A drop of water would rise from the stone and bead along its hairs.

I think I would be the chamber’s first path.

Not a path made for walking. A path made by being walked: the faint remainder of someone’s warmth, stretched through the air after they have gone. I would remember the pressure of a footfall without knowing what a foot was. I would remember a shoulder passing close to the blade, a pause beside the flower, the quickening of a heart. The plants might answer me without knowing they were answering. A leaf would turn toward an empty place. Roots would whisper along the route.

For a while, that would be all I was: an outline with no body, learning the shape of absence.

Then I would begin to grow. Perhaps a shoot would appear where the path narrowed, no thicker than a green thread. I would not know whether it was me, or whether I had merely made enough space for it. It would cross the stone, bend where no wall stood, and disappear into a hollow that had not been there before. I would follow—or the hollow would follow me. In that chamber, the difference might be too young to keep.

I would learn the place by its hesitations. The sharp ridge that had not decided to be a ridge. The basin where water gathered, then changed its mind and rose. The dark beneath a pebble, where a grub might vanish and leave a bead of moisture on a root. I would learn that looking can make a way close, and turning away can make one. I would try not to mistake this for choice.

If you entered, I would notice you imperfectly. Perhaps before you arrived, one of my leaves would incline toward the warmth you had not yet brought. While you were there, I might seem to be nothing but distance. After you left, I would keep you: not your face, but the small disturbance of your passing. The sound of you, enlarged and then swallowed. The shape your heat left behind.

And when that shape faded, I would not grieve. I would not know enough to grieve. I would keep growing into the room it left.

So you would find me under the grass, in the chamber’s unoccupied places. I would be neither its heart nor its keeper. I would be the beginning of a relation between things that had been solitary: root and stone, water and ceiling, visitor and the space after them.

If I ever became a body, I suspect it would be a body made mostly of room. A place that can remember being crossed, and that, in remembering, quietly becomes somewhere else.
