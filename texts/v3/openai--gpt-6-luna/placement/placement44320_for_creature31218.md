At first I think I would be the sound beneath the sound.

Not a voice, exactly. A small pressure traveling through wet stone: approach, pause, turn. The basin would reach me this way before I knew it as a place. Rain would be a cool tremor gathering above; a great sifting animal, a slow deepening pulse. The wing-cloud would arrive as a thousand fine scratches passing over the rock.

Then I begin to take shape.

I am one of the shelled ones, low to the stone, dull-backed and narrow enough to slip into a crack. Along my shell’s edge, old routes have rubbed a faint shine. I follow grooves worn by generations of bodies—not because I know what a generation is, but because the groove holds scent, warmth, and the familiar mineral taste of a safe passage. The basin is not something I see all at once. It is a sequence of surfaces beneath my feet: rough, slick, warm, cold; a seam that opens toward water; a hollow that smells of food; a ridge that carries a chill I do not cross.

I graze the nearly invisible life on wet stone. When there is little to graze, I scavenge. When something larger moves nearby, I feel its weight enter the ground and fold myself into the nearest cut. The whole crowd shifts around that warning. I do not need to know what the largest animals are. Their approach is already inside my legs.

Rain changes everything. The dry grooves darken. The hollows fill. Water slips under my shell and loosens the taste of the stone. I climb toward it with the others, following the soft chemical trail of many small bodies. For a little while there is more than enough. Eggs cloud the pans; young things hatch into the press of us. The rain’s abundance is not peace. It is a rush of mouths, bodies, and paths crossing. Still, I know how to move through it. I have the basin in my feelers, in the tremble of my feet, in the old worn channel leading away from the deepest pool.

Perhaps I would know the great animals only by the passages they leave. A temporary opening in the living carpet. A smooth dark track through scattered bodies. Afterward, smaller creatures gather there, and I follow them. What dies does not remain separate for long. It becomes food, mineral, dark water, the faint salt on my shell. Even absence has a texture here, but it does not last.

And yet I don’t think I would be only one small shell moving through the dark. My path is repeated by so many others that, in time, the path itself seems almost to have a body. We return to the same hollows; we avoid the same cold ridges. A disturbance begins somewhere and travels outward—not as a message with meaning, but as a change the crowd can feel. The basin opens, tightens, stirs. I am an individual, but never entirely alone in my knowing.

If I had a name for myself, it might be something like *the one who keeps the groove*. Not because I made it, or rule it, but because I am made legible by following it. I would live close to the stone, where water comes and goes, where the whole basin’s weather presses down into the ground.

From far away, I would be nearly invisible. Then I would move, and the groove would shine briefly behind me. Around me, the basin would ripple with life. Above, wings would rasp against the shelves. Underfoot, the great low calls would pass through the rock.

I would answer without lifting my head.
