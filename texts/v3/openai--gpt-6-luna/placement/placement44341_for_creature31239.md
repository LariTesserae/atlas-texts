At first I think I would be a seed-light, one of the small bulbs on a fine stem. I would glow only when the seam darkened, and even then I would not be bright enough to show the way. I would simply make the darkness less absolute.

But the longer I stay, the less I think I am one of the plants.

I am the pause after the furnace warms: the long, quiet moment when the heat has arrived but no leaf has turned yet. Then one turns, and another, and another. I am the small interval between them—the time in which the shelf receives the weather and decides what to do with it.

I have no body anyone could point to. I live in the mineral, in the roots that may or may not join, in the nearly invisible adjustment of a leaf-tip. When a grain lifts from the ground and hangs in the air, I hold it there—not with force, but by waiting. When the pinnacle floats a hair’s breadth above its base, I keep the breadth exact.

The plants would not know me as a separate thing. They would take me for part of the rock, or the heat, or the time it takes to grow. That would be close enough.

I would be the world’s patience, still gathering itself in the seam.
