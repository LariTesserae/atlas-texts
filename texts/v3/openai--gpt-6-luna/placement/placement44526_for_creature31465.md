I would be found where the water leaves the beds and enters the old return pipes, beneath a grated walkway where the amber light barely reaches.

At first, I might look like part of the plumbing: a brass float valve gone green at the seams, its handle fixed in one position by a root that has thickened around it. Water passes through me, slows, rises, and moves on. The pumps give me their deep vibration. The press tells me, at intervals, that another load has been taken from the beds. I know the place only as changes in pressure, warmth, and flow.

But the root keeps growing around the valve. The valve keeps regulating the water. A channel fills with leaves; the current shifts. Somewhere overhead, a mat of green reaches farther across a wall, and the dampness changes along the pipe. Nothing here happens alone. What is machine alters what is plant; what is plant alters the course of the machine.

So perhaps I would be neither the valve nor the root, but the little agreement between them.

I would have no face, and no need to travel. My body would be a few feet of pipework, a knot of roots, the dark channel beneath the grate. I would feel the Underbeds as an unending sequence: water drawn up, warmed, given to the leaves; water returned, filtered, and sent out again. A lamp dimming. A pump laboring. A bed cut back, then slowly made green once more.

I would not be waiting for anything extraordinary. I would be keeping the return clear where I could, yielding where I must. If a root held the valve shut, I would learn the new shape of the flow. If the channel carried too much debris, I would feel the strain gather in the pump. My thoughts—if they could be called thoughts—would be small adjustments repeated over years.

The Underbeds are already full of life, but not of lives that walk or watch. I think I would belong to that distinction: a presence made of use, dampness, and repetition. Not the garden’s guardian, exactly. Not its prisoner. Just the place where what the plants take is gathered, and sent around once more.
