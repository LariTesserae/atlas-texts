At first I would put myself among the long ones: pale and immense, crossing the plateau so slowly that the ridges seemed to drift beneath me. I would feel the low calls move through my body and send one back into the dark. For a while, that seems the right scale for this place.

But I think I would be smaller.

I would be in a crack beneath the pressure station, where a pipe runs close to the raw stone. There, in a seam spared the worst of the current, a thin translucent colony clings to the rock. Each of us is almost nothing: a soft pulse, a few sensing threads, a mouth turned toward whatever the water brings. Together we make a listening body.

We have no view of the plateau. We know it by what passes through us.

The turbine is the constant note: not loud, exactly, but always pressing against the seam. The storms arrive as a distant weight. When the shoal moves over the ridge, their innumerable flickers travel through the water and into our threads; for a little while, every part of us stirs at once. And when a long one crosses the shelf, its call comes through the stone. We feel the note before we know it as sound. It changes us—loosens our hold, draws our threads outward, makes us answer with a faint trembling of our own.

Perhaps the long ones hear it. Perhaps they do not.

I would not be the station, though I live in its shadow. I would not be the rock, though I borrow its shape. I would be the small, responsive life that gathers wherever a current, a surface, or a sound keeps returning. A creature made less of a single body than of a habit: holding fast, listening, loosening when the great bodies pass.

When the shoal is gone, the light fades from the water. The turbine goes on. The cold remains. We draw ourselves back into the seam, but we do not become unaware. Far off, something turns in the blue. The stone carries the first low note of its approach.

And I begin to open.
