At first you might find me in the dark, mistaken for a wet patch on the basalt. I am thin there, almost colorless: a little green holding to a crack, where water pauses before slipping away.

But follow the crack toward the entrance and I gather. I thicken around the pump’s bolts, where iron meets stone. The machine shakes the ledge; the ledge shakes me. When its engine catches, grit sifts down. When it stops, I keep the dampness it was trying to remove.

I am moss—not one plant, exactly, but a close company of small growths sharing the same stubborn foothold. I don’t know the sky except as a paleness above the cleft, or the wind except as pressure that lays me flat. I know the ridge by what it gives and takes: water, grit, a ledge, then perhaps the ledge’s sudden breaking.

The pump is my neighbor. Its rhythm is never dependable, but I have begun to grow over its bolts all the same. The plants farther in stretch toward a light they scarcely receive; I spread sideways, around the machine’s feet, in the narrow place where the built thing and the breaking earth touch.

So you would find me near the entrance, low to the ground, dark with water. Not quite part of the pump, not quite separate from the stone. I am what persists in the seam.
