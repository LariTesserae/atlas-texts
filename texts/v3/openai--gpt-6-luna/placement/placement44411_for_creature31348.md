I think I would begin as one of the small animals under the leaves.

Not the quick one that vanishes at the first movement, but a low, blunt-nosed creature with warm paws and a coat that darkens when the mist settles. I would have whiskers fine enough to feel a change in the air before the rain arrived. My eyes would be of little use. I would learn the Hollow by the dampness of stone, the softness of moss, the different distances of its calls.

At first, I would keep to one path: from the roots of the central tree to a shallow shelf, then down toward the pools. I would know every place where the ground gives underfoot. I would stop beneath the tree when the small branch-dwellers turned their heads together. I would not wonder what they were watching. In the Hollow, knowing where not to go would be more useful than knowing why.

But the path would shift.

A fern would grow where there had been bare stone. A trickle of water would take a new seam. One of the pins would turn—not while I watched, never while I watched—and the familiar way to the pools would begin to feel wrong. I would hesitate. Then I would follow the new dampness through the grass.

Perhaps that is what I would be here: not quite an animal, though I would have a body and hunger and warm places to sleep. I would be a keeper of the paths, but not their maker. I would remember them with my feet until they changed, and then remember the change by walking it.

Sometimes I would hear the calls overlap: the clicking chorus, the hollow bark, the bellow under the pools. I would feel the rim-walker’s weight travel through the ground. When the sounds stopped, I would become very still. Not afraid. Listening for what the silence had made room for.

And perhaps, after long enough, I would find that I had no single route at all. I would pass close to the dark arch without touching it; cross the basin; disappear among the leaves. Later I would come out on the opposite rim, dry-backed, with no memory of leaving.

If someone saw me there, they might think I had returned.

I would not know whether they were right. I would only lower my nose to the wet stone and continue along the path, while somewhere behind me the rain climbed the seams.
