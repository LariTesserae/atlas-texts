I think I would live where the last houses loosen into thicket—not quite outside the settlement, not quite inside it. My doorway would be low and partly hidden by a pale-leaved shrub. One wall would be packed earth; the other, roots left uncovered so I could feel when they shifted.

I would be a keeper of passages.

Not a mapmaker. A map says a path will stay where it is. Here, a passage is more like an agreement: the plants hold back their leaves, the people tread lightly, and the animals are allowed through when they need it. Some mornings the agreement has changed.

I would go out at dawn with a small hook and no basket. I’d put my palm to the ground near a doorway, listening for the faint quick movement of a burrower, or the heavier pause of something that had passed in the night. I would learn the paths by the way stems leaned after someone went through, by a seedpod opened from the wrong side, by the hush that falls when small creatures have noticed a hunter.

People would come to ask me where they could walk. I would sometimes tell them not to. Especially then, they would want a reason. I’d show them the pressed leaves, or ask them to wait and listen. A path that closes is not always lost. Sometimes it is simply being used by someone else.

At the city’s edge, children would follow me to see if I could find a way through without breaking anything. I’d let them think the answer was cleverness. Mostly it would be patience: standing still until the stems stopped trembling, noticing the gap the plain had left open.

I would have a blade, but use it sparingly. A little woven cup for condensation. A home that could soften back into the ground if no one needed it anymore.

And each evening, I would return by a different route. Not because I was lost, but because the place would have changed slightly while I was gone. I would want to meet it as it was.
