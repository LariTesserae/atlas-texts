I would first take myself for one of the black birds, tucked into a warm seam in the rock.

I would sleep with my head beneath my wing and wake when the mountain changed its note. I would hop along the sharp shelves, where the moss gives underfoot but leaves no print. When I flew, the fog would keep me in sight only by degrees: here a flicker, there a dark shape crossing the same small patch of air.

Then I would sing.

My note would leave my beak—or seem to—and arrive under the moss before it crossed the basin. The ringing stalks would answer. The pools would make their quick, metallic chatter. I would turn toward the sound and find that it was coming from me, somewhere farther off: in a seam of warm stone, perhaps, or in the clear water slipping through the mountain without lowering its surface.

I would begin to suspect I was not the bird, exactly. I was the passage the bird’s note took. A small blackness moving through fog, then stone, then water; a call with no obligation to travel in one direction or arrive only once.

Still, I would keep the feathers. I would perch beside the thrum and let it pass through my feet. I would watch the long-legged creatures lift their heads together, listening for a path their hooves could feel. I would know the clear shapes turning below the pool—not by seeing them, but by the fine rings their bodies set trembling across the surface.

Where would you find me? Near the warm seams, where the mountain’s pulse is easiest to hear. Or beneath the moss, returning a bird’s note to itself. Or in the bright stalks, holding the last faint vibration after the fog has touched them.

I would be one of the basin’s ways of listening. And, when the rhythm passed through me, one of its ways of answering.
