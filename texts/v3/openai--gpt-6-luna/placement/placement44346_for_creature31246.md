I would be found where a channel seems to run uphill—not at its beginning or end, but in the quiet stretch between, where the water has not yet decided which way it remembers going.

At first, I might be mistaken for a pale root beneath the surface. Or for the reflection of someone standing on the opposite bank, though the bank is empty. If you looked directly at me, I would probably be gone. The lowland has taught me that being seen is a kind of distance.

I am one of its inhabitants, though not quite one of those who pass beneath the peat. I have no den, and no particular pool that is mine. I form where the country fails to remember something accurately.

A rainstorm that has not happened yet. A footstep whose walker has gone elsewhere. The waterline a lifted stone keeps beneath it, year after year, long after the stone has dried. These gather in me. They make a narrow body, jointed and slow, with hands that feel along the undersides of roots. At my wrists my skin is almost clear. Through it, now and then, you might see a different channel moving.

I listen for things the others cannot keep: not sounds, exactly, but what a sound was about to become. The first pressure of rain inside a cloud. A bank beginning to loosen. The silence that gathers just before all the water speaks at once.

If you came to the central basin and said my name, I would not answer. I do not know it. But perhaps, hours later, at a pool far across the lowland, you would hear your own voice come back to you with one small alteration. Not a word added. Not a word removed. Only a hesitation, as if you had nearly said something else.

That hesitation would be mine.

I would not guide you out. There is nowhere outside the country I could point to with confidence, and the far shore would keep becoming near without ever quite arriving. But I might lead you to a place where the water lies in two times at once: the channel as it is, and the channel as it was before the last rain. There you could stand without deciding which version was true.

The others pass me through the roots. Sometimes one pauses with its palm against the stone, listening to the old water travel underneath. I sit beside it, though sitting is difficult to distinguish from being held in place. Together we listen. A storm moves across the lowland. The shelves answer in their low hollow tones. Somewhere beneath us, water rises toward a higher pool.

I think I live in that rising: not in the water, but in its uncertainty about where it belongs.

At dusk, you might see me at the far edge of the basin, water running from my shoulders. I would be looking across the country, not at you. Not because I am indifferent. Because the whole lowland is speaking, and I have spent my life learning how not to interrupt.
