I would be beneath the floor, where the plates overlap.

At first I would think I was only a pressure: a slow, warm insistence under the grit. The plates tilt above me. Their edges press against my back—or what I take to be my back—and I feel the hollow as a pattern of weights: a stalk’s roots tightening, a grazer’s quick feet, the wind driving sand against the stone. I have no eyes for the copper light. I know the day by how the heat changes in the cracks.

I am not quite one animal. I have a body, but it has no useful outline. It runs in dark seams under the ash, broadening where the stone is soft, thinning where it is old and hard. Mineral gathers along my skin. Something warm and living works beneath it, slow as a thought that has not yet decided to become a thought.

I shift when the pressure builds.

The floor lifts. A blister rises above me. I feel the little feet scatter, then feel them return, one by one, when the grains spill out. They take what I bring up. I do not know whether I make the glittering grains for them, or whether the grains are simply what happens when I turn in my sleep. The hollow does not ask for a purpose. It asks only that things happen again.

The plants are easiest to feel. Their wire-roots rest against my mineral channels. When a crack breathes bitter vapor, their roots draw taut, and I know the stalks will thicken. When the wind bends them, their scales rasp together overhead. Their sound comes down through the ground as a faint, dry shiver. Sometimes one of them rings with bells, and the whole hollow answers: wings dropping through the air, grazers rushing, the hard little clicks beneath the knobs. For a moment, I feel every life above me gathering in one place.

Then the flower is gone. The feet disperse. I settle again.

I am older than the spiral posts, perhaps, though I cannot measure age by years. I feel them as three stubborn vibrations at the rim. One has almost stopped speaking. The stone ring holds a different sort of silence—not empty, but arranged. The pale grazers avoid it. The clickers shelter beneath it. Roots have entered the ash around it. I have not swallowed the ring. I have moved around it, as I move around the black stone, the hard knobs, the places where the old floor will not yield.

Sometimes the wind falls away, and the hollow becomes so quiet that I can feel the thin crust of orange lichen flexing on the stones. The small things under it click to one another. I listen through the floor, though I do not know what listening is for them. Their warnings reach me as tiny bright knocks. I answer only by remaining where I am.

Above me, the hunters turn in the warm air. Below them, I am turning too: not traveling, exactly, but making the ground into a new arrangement. A ridge here. A crack there. A long pause. Then the next slow heave.

If I had a name, it might be the sound the hollow makes when it changes its mind. But I would not call myself the hollow. I am only the warm thing under its shifting floor, keeping time badly, and listening as the others make a world of it.
