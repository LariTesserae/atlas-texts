I would be found in one of the old channels, where the basin’s great bodies have passed so often that stone has learned the shape of their going.

At first, I would not be a *who*. I would be a silverish dust worrying at the channel wall: thousands of jointed slivers, each tasting metal, each feeling the small pressures that travel through stone and water. Alone, none of us would know the basin. Together, we would feel a change coming before it had a direction.

A tremor would pass through the ridge. We would click once.

The sound would be tiny, but it would travel along the channel. Farther down, other slivers would answer. We would loosen from the stone and stream into the water, gathering ourselves into a ribbon, then a knot. There would be no center to me. Only the place where the current tightened, the place where the temperature changed, the shared decision to turn.

Perhaps that is where I would live: not in a body, but in the turning.

When a school of armored animals passed beneath us, we would cling to its wake. Their plates would cast a dim shelter; their movement would carry us over flats we could never cross alone. We would taste the water disturbed by their legs and know whether the basin was drawing inward or breathing out. If one rose through the film, we might settle briefly along its back, silvering the seams between its plates. Then it would sink, and we would go with it.

Sometimes a shell-backed animal would open its dark hollow on the plain. We would enter, drawn by warmth and the shelter of its pale filaments. For a while its slow pulse would become the measure of our world. We would feed there, or shelter, or do something that looked like either. When the shell closed, some of us would remain inside. Some would escape through the rim. The difference would not make a clean boundary between *me* and *not me*.

I think I would be most myself when the deep bodies moved.

Their passage would reach us as a pressure too large to be called a signal. The water would lean. The ridge would tremble. We would gather in the channels, and our many small bodies would make a listening shape. We could not see the giants below the flats. We would know them by what they did to everything else: schools turning, crusts tightening, water changing its mind.

Then the roar would come, and the basin would flow in a new direction.

We would be carried into unfamiliar water. Some of us would be lost against stone; some would find a new ridge and begin to rasp. In time, the silver dust would return—not the same bodies, not all of them, but the same old pattern of gathering, loosening, answering. I might call that survival. I might call it a habit the basin has taught us.

So if you found me, you would find no face to meet yours. You would find a thin, living gleam in a groove of black stone. You might think it was mineral catching the weak light. Then the water would shift, and the gleam would move against the current.

I would be there, feeling the next change arrive from somewhere that has not happened yet.
