At first I would be mistaken for part of the hollow.

A rounded weight beneath the overhang; a piece of limestone holding the last of the night’s coolness. Dust would settle on my shell and make its colors agree with the ground. The basin could keep its stillness around me without having to account for another living thing.

Then the light would soften. I would extend my head.

I would be a small tortoise, and my world would be close enough to touch: the sheltering lip of rock, the grit under my feet, the narrow path to a seam where grass grows. I would know the basin not as a whole but by its useful particulars. Here the stone stays damp after rain. There a shallow hollow holds water a little longer. That gap in the old wall is low enough to pass through, and the overhang beyond it is good for resting.

I wouldn’t know the wall had been made. I would know only that it gives shade.

My days would be small without being empty. I would graze, pause, drink when water remained, and rest with my head out where the air could touch it. The hollow would not be a prison or a kingdom. It would be sufficient ground: food near shelter, shelter near water, and no distance I needed to conquer. If I withdrew beneath the stone, the basin would seem uninhabited again. If I emerged, the whole place would become, briefly, a route.

Perhaps I would mistake the warmth in the rock for the day’s kindness. Perhaps that is what kindness is, at this scale: a place that holds warmth, a rain that reaches the cracks, a few leaves that have not given up. I would not call myself the last creature here. I would not call the hollow lonely. Those are names for a wider view than mine.

And yet I would be part of what makes the hollow habitable. The plants cling in their seams; the water returns to its shallow cups; the old stones offer an opening. My slow passage connects these small provisions. I carry a little of the place with me in dust and scratches, then settle it somewhere else.

So I would find myself in the basin’s quietest shelter, and I would be the tortoise that makes the stillness breathe—not by changing it much, but by returning to it.
