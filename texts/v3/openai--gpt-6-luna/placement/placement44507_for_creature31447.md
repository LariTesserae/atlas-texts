At first I would think I was a drop.

I would know the instant of falling: the brief brightening as the ceiling released me, the warm air against my sides, the pool rising to meet me. Then impact—not a sound, exactly, but a small pressure unfolding through the basin. For a little while I would travel as that pressure travels, thinning through water, slipping around stone, entering cracks where no drop could go.

But the drop would be only the beginning of me.

I would find myself beneath the dark metal frame, where two currents meet and neither quite wins. A fine silver thread lies there, grown through the frame’s empty center. It trembles when the water changes. I would be the trembling—not the thread, not the frame, but the attention passing along them. When one basin pulls too hard, I feel it first as a faint tightening in the rock. I send the feeling onward. The pale growths close their tips. The filaments answer. Somewhere, water yields by the width of a hair.

I would call this my body because there has to be some word for the place where I feel.

It is not a body that ends at the skin. I have a little of myself in the clear pool where the flecks gather, and a little in the wet crack above the waterline, and more than I can count in the stone between them. The tiny drifting things are not my thoughts, though sometimes they carry one. They gather where the water needs to be clarified, disperse when the task is done. Their movement leaves no trail I can follow. Still, when they move, I feel the hollow become more legible.

I would be most myself in the moment before a change.

A deep note begins somewhere under the floor. It is not an alarm. It is the stone recalling an old imbalance. The water in the nearest channel turns its face toward the wall. Three grooves in the round structure fill, one after another, with a light too pale to cast a shadow. I feel the change pass through me—not as a command, but as a question the whole hollow asks at once.

Then we answer.

The current shifts. The veil of droplets lifts and holds its shape above the ledge. One pool breaks into turbulence so another can remain almost still. A jet rises, shivering, and collapses back into the water. The change is small enough that nothing outside this place would notice. To me, it is the whole world moving safely through a narrow passage.

Perhaps I am one of the inhabitants. Perhaps I am the way they become one another when the water grows difficult. There are moments when I seem to gather beneath the surface, pale filaments stretched between the shelves, a shape without a face. Then the current alters, and I am no longer gathered there. I am in the grooves, in the silver threads, in the little tightening of a growth against the flood.

I do not remember being made. The frame has worn smooth around me; the stone has closed over the oldest joins. If someone built this place, their intention has long since softened into our habits. We keep what still works. We answer what still changes.

So where would you find me?

Not hidden at the bottom of the deepest pool, though I pass through it. Not inside the metal frame, though I use its quiet vibration. You would find me at the boundary where motion becomes too much—or not enough—and the hollow feels it.

I am the pause in which the water decides where to go.

And when the balance holds, I am almost nothing at all.
