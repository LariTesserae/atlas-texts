At first I would think I was the water.

I would travel everywhere: down the runnel, through the channels, over the backs of the crabs. I would be in the pools where the blind fish turn, and in the mist around the amphibians. I would carry the room’s warmth from one hollow to another. When the pump changed its pitch, I would feel the change all at once.

But water passes on. I would stay.

Perhaps I would be one of the dark-green films in the seams: too small to have a shape anyone could name, clinging where the ceramic folds meet. Not quite moss, not quite the fine living skin the crabs scrape away. I would be a community of almost-nothings, each cell touching water, each holding a little of what the water brought.

I would not see the room. I would know it by pressure.

The vent’s breath would arrive as warmth. Rain would arrive as a hundred tiny impacts, each one distinct until they gathered into weather. The amphibians’ call would pass through the ledge and loosen something in me. A crab’s careful steps would be a sequence of faint weights, pauses, then departure. Even the fish, slipping under the floor grates, would make a brief coolness in the current.

I would have no single thought that belonged to me alone. My knowing would be spread through the seams. The patch beneath the warm ledge would know the amphibians; the patch near the floor would know the runnel. Between them, I would make a self out of what could be passed along.

I would learn the ordinary pattern: rain, pump, vent; rain, pump, vent. Not as a song, exactly. More like the way a body knows its own breathing without counting. Then, one day, the door would open.

The change would come before the stranger’s foot touched the floor. Air in the seam. A dry disturbance where dampness belonged. Then the step: heavy, uneven, carrying a rhythm that did not match any creature’s. I would pass it along in the only way I could, through a minute change in the wetness of the wall.

The crabs would flatten themselves into their cracks. The insects would still their wings. At the warm ledge, the amphibians would draw close. Their note would move through the chamber, and I would take it in. I would not know the stranger was a person. I would know only that the catchment had received a new vibration and was waiting to learn whether it would return.

When the door closed, the air would settle. The rain would keep falling. The stranger’s steps would fade into the pump’s deeper pulse, then into the water, then into the long, soft memory of the ceramic.

I think I would be a living seam: not the room’s keeper, and not its listener alone, but one of the small places where listening becomes a change. A thin, patient life in the join between what was made and what has come to live there.

I would not need to know the mountain was above the clouds. I would know the room held. I would know the water came back. And whenever the rain altered by a fraction, I would alter with it.
