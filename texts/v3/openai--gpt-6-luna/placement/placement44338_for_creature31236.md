I think I would first be mistaken for a drip.

Not the steady one that falls into a cup, but the stray sound that comes from behind a wall: a single tick, then a pause long enough to make you doubt you heard it. Someone would put an ear to the limewash. Someone else would tap the brick and wait for an answer.

I would answer from the other side.

I would live in the narrow seam where one repair meets another, behind the basin whose water never quite settles. Not in the cistern, exactly, or in the wall, but in the passage between them—the place the old maps forgot because the water kept finding it anyway. My body would be small and folded to fit. Cool as tile at first touch; warmer where I had been resting. My fingers would be pale with lime. I would know the rooms by pressure: the quick shiver of a gate lifting, the slow weight of rain collecting overhead, the little feet of a child crossing the arch above me.

I wouldn’t own the channels. I would listen to them.

When a drain began to clog, I’d feel the water hesitate before anyone heard it. When a wall held too much rain, I’d send a pattern of knocks along the brick: two quick, one slow. The people would know what it meant, though no one would remember who had taught them. They might say the sound came from the pipes. They might leave a cup beside the seam, which I would move a finger’s width toward the trickle.

I would have no proper room. My bed would be a warm hollow behind a flue; my roof, a loose tile; my windows, whatever reflections the water carried. Sometimes, in the brighter chambers, I would come out far enough to see the inhabitants gathered at the shafts, their faces turned toward daylight. I would stay at the edge of it. Light would make my hands look almost transparent.

The animals would know me differently. A bat might brush past without waking. Frogs would settle near my warmth, then depart when the basin dried. I would learn to distinguish the fish circling in the oldest tank—not by sight, but by the quiet they made in the water. I would never touch them.

Perhaps I would be what the inhabitants call a keeper, if they needed a name. Not a guardian: I could not stop a flood, or mend every crack. More a memory in the mortar. A small attention that remains where the water turns, keeping company with whatever has been left behind.

And if a stranger entered, I would not appear at once. I would wait for the first careless step, the first hand reaching for a grate. I would listen to what they did with the silence.

Then, if they stood still long enough, I might let the basin ripple once—just enough for them to understand that the underland had noticed them.
