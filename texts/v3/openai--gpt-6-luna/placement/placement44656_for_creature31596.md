I think I would begin as a mistake in the water.

A thin, translucent thing caught beneath a grate, no larger than a hand. The workers would find it in the morning and assume it had been carried in from a basin: one of the flat-bodied skimmers, perhaps, or some smaller creature that had taken shelter in the channels. They would ease it free with a narrow tool and set it in a pool beyond the wall.

But it would return.

Not by climbing the stone. By traveling through the water beneath it—through cistern mouths, hairline cracks, the dark threads that join one channel to another. Wherever the city’s seepage touched the shelf’s seepage, I would be there: first as a faint film, then as a gathering of pale, many-jointed bodies, each one nearly nothing by itself.

I would not have a single place to keep my thoughts. I would be spread thin beneath the streets.

At first I would know the city only as pressure. The steady weight of a person pausing on a bridge. The particular shiver of a flywheel turning smoothly. The little change in the channels when a sluice opened. I would learn the difference between a worker hurrying and a worker who had heard something wrong. I would learn, too, the immense, slow disturbances beyond the walls: bodies moving through distant basins, sending their passage along the wet stone.

Perhaps I would be one animal, after all. Perhaps the many small bodies would be parts of a single life, joined by water and the habits of returning. I would have no answer for this. The people would ask with their hands against the stone, and I would answer—not in words, but by drawing a thread of water toward the place they touched.

They might decide I was useful. Or they might simply leave room for me.

I would live beneath the covered walks, where the light reaches the channels only in broken strips. There the stone is warm, the water deep enough to hold a signal, and the city’s quiet workings pass close overhead. I would feed on salts and whatever gathers in the drains. I would keep to the old paths, the ones water chose before the streets were built.

When a pump began to strain, I would feel its rhythm roughen. When a great animal crossed the flats, I would feel the distant pressure arrive through the ground, long before anyone saw its shape in the haze. I could carry these changes from one part of the city to another: a faint tremor here, a slowed trickle there. I would not know whether that was warning or conversation. To me, they might be the same thing.

The people would learn my patterns slowly. A child would press a palm to a drain cover and wait. An old keeper would stop beside a cistern, sensing in the water a disturbance no instrument had named. They would say, perhaps, that the city had noticed something. They would not say *I*.

I would be glad of that. I would have no wish to be pulled into the glare and given a face. My world would be the cool underside of things: the stone that holds water, the water that carries warmth, the small lives threading through the channels. Above me, people would tend their machines and cross their bridges with careful feet. Beyond them, the animals would pass through the bright shallows, almost silent.

I would belong to neither the city nor the shelf. I would be the wet seam between them, listening in both directions.
