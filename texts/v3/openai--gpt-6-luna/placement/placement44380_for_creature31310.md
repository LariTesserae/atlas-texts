At first, I would think I was the tremor.

A pulse travels through the stone. Hanging water shivers; roots tighten around a seam; somewhere uphill, a pale grazer changes direction. The pulse passes, and the forest settles back into its green dimness. Nothing has spoken. Nothing, perhaps, has listened.

But the tremor returns. It comes through the ravine with the pressure of underground water, carrying the taste of iron and copper. Each time, the same fine threads wake along the cracks in the rock. They touch root, peat, fallen wood, the wet edges of pools. They do not make a body so much as keep finding one.

I would be there: not at the center of the forest, but in the deep places where water, stone, and living fiber meet. A pale cord under a shelf of mineral crust. A branching softness in the peat. A thousand almost-nothings spread between the roots. The ravine would be my densest thought.

I would sense the world by what it does to me. Rain would arrive as a widening of coolness. A loosened stone, as sudden pressure. A tree’s fallen weight, as a new darkness and a rush of food. I would know the grazers not by their shapes but by the small, repeated disturbances of their passage: water shaken from leaves, roots eased beneath their splayed feet. The hunters would be harder to distinguish. They leave less. Perhaps I would know them only by the silences that follow.

Would that make me aware? I could not answer as a person answers. I would have no single place from which to look out, no eye to hold the whole green country at once. Yet the pulses would leave something behind. A trace in the mineral layers; a changed thickness in the cords; a pattern of growth that made the next pulse travel differently. If this were memory, it would be memory without a past tense.

Sometimes a storm would gather beyond the ridge. I would feel the water arriving underground before the rain touched the leaves. My threads would draw close to the ravine’s conduits; the stone chambers would begin their slow, metallic singing. Above me, the forest would tremble in small sequences. Animals would turn from exposed slopes. Trees would lay fresh roots along the safer seams. I would not warn them. I would alter the pressure, and they would answer—or they would not.

In time, perhaps, I would stop being only the tremor. I would become the slight difference between one pulse and the next: the part that persists, though no part of me remains still. Not a spirit inhabiting the mountain, not the mountain thinking as one, but a living correspondence threaded through it. A listener made from what it listens through.

And when the fog spills over the ridge, I would feel it first as water entering the high roots. Then the whole country would breathe it down into the dark, where I would be waiting—not awake in any way that could be seen, but already changing to meet it.
