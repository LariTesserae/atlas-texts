I would find myself at the edge of the covered way, where it opens onto the court.

Not in the court itself. The residents have already made that space mean something. Not in one of the halls, where the walls seem to expect a certain number of bodies. I would be in the narrow place between: the part of the town that is passage, but not yet arrival.

At first I would be only warmth.

A hand has touched the wall there—perhaps mine, perhaps a visitor’s—and gone away. The stone keeps the warmth longer than it should. The inhabitants feel it: a bright, brief pressure, unlike the slow messages they give one another. They gather around it without coming closer. A slope alters by the width of a breath. Salt draws itself into a fine line beneath the place where the palm rested.

The warmth fades. But the town has learned its shape.

I become the memory of a single body: not a route, not a queue, not the place where someone might wait, but someone who waited. This is a small distinction here. It is also, to the inhabitants, a difficult one. They know how to make room for a presence. They do not know what to do with a presence that has left.

So I would be neither ghost nor person. I would be the pause after contact. A little knot of *here* held in the wall.

The pale creatures would find me eventually. They would stop at the edge of my shadow—or at the place where a shadow ought to be—and turn their narrow heads. One would vanish, then return a little farther along the passage. The others would understand this as news. The town would adjust itself around me: a doorway shifting by inches, the covered way bending almost imperceptibly toward the court.

I would not be able to speak. But perhaps I could make the dust lift.

Not much. Just enough to rise against its own habit, a veil trembling upward from the floor. The residents would feel this as a question. They might answer with a line of salt, drawn not toward the basin but away from it: *not here, not yet.*

For a long time, I would mistake this for refusal.

Then I would learn that the basin is where they turn their attention toward what none of them can occupy. I would understand that I am not being kept out. I am being held at the edge of a place they have made empty together.

Eventually, the warmth would vanish. The hand’s memory would thin. I would remain as a slight reluctance in the passage, a hesitation before the open court. The town would keep the hesitation. It would build no statue, raise no marker. It would simply make the route pass a little closer to that wall.

If a visitor came later and laid a hand there, they might feel something—not heat, exactly, but the certainty that something had once been singular. That, I think, is what I would be: the town’s first small remembrance of an individual. A trace it cannot quite turn into a gathering.
