I think you would first find me by the sound I make.

Not a call that means danger, or a bell that says a bridge is sound. Just two low notes, sent into the dark from beneath the plant-vault. I wait for the plateau to answer.

I would be somewhere between a settlement and the open basin: where the last woven stems give way to bare rock, and a root-bridge crosses a crack full of mist. There would be a shelter nearby, low beneath an overhang, its roof weighted with flat stones. But I would spend more time outside it, one hand on the wet stems, listening for what has changed.

I’d be one of the people who knows a place by the shape of its returning sound. We don’t have a grand name for the work. We say I’m going to listen at the crossing, or ask whether the basin is open. I carry a few hollow stalks cut to different lengths, and a coil of root-cord. I check the hanging bells, retie what the wind has loosened, and call across gaps where mist has hidden the far side.

At first, perhaps, I would think of myself as a path-keeper. Someone who makes it safer for people to cross. But the plateau keeps making that seem too small a description.

A call comes back muddied: the fog is thick in the basin. A bell sounds lower than it did yesterday: water has gathered in the bridge-root. The small climbers go quiet in one stretch of canopy, and I know something is waiting beneath it. The herd’s cries arrive from the western cliffs before any fog, and I can tell they are moving toward a spring we had thought had dried. The work is less about finding a way through than noticing what the country is already doing.

I don’t always know what I hear. Sometimes I call and get no answer at all. That can mean mist, or distance, or simply that whatever was there has moved on. I have learned not to fill the silence too quickly with a story.

I would carry seed mash in a pouch, partly as food and partly because the gliders know the sound of it. They would come to the shelter roof, pale eyes bright among the leaves, and take what I leave on a ledge. In return, they scatter seeds into cracks high on the cliffs, where a person could never put them. I would recognize one by a torn edge in its gliding membrane, though I wouldn’t pretend it recognized me in the same way.

Some evenings I would follow the root-channels to a pool and sit very still. The hunters know these places better than I do. I would not try to make the plateau harmless. I’d only hang a small bell where the path narrows, so that a traveler might hear the shift of air before coming too close. If the bell rang by itself, I would listen first, and move second.

Perhaps, after a while, I’d stop thinking of the work as keeping paths open. The roots change them. The fog closes them. Animals make use of them in ways no household planned. My part would be to notice when a familiar crossing had become a different crossing—and to tell someone before they trusted it with their weight.

So I would be a person, though not one who can be found easily by sight. A listener who lives near the seam between the vaulted plants and the bare stone. Someone who sends a small sound into the dark and waits to learn what the dark has to say about itself.

And when I’m not there, the bells still move in the wind. The plateau keeps listening without me.
