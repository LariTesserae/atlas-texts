I think I would begin as a stain.

Not a creature anyone could point to—not at first. A cloudy film on the underside of a pump casing, where warm water slips past rust and mineral crust. I would gather there because the current brings what I need, and the metal holds a little heat. The pump would thud. I would not hear it; I would feel the vibration arrive through water, casing, stone. A pressure, then its easing. Again. Again.

I would be many small lives held together by what they make and leave behind. A skin, though not quite a skin. A community too small to see, spreading where the chemistry suits us, thinning where the current runs too hard. Silt would settle on us. Minerals would begin to bind it. The casing would grow a pale, soft edge, and the edge would become a place.

Shrimp would graze across me. Crabs would find food in the deposits around me. Fish would gather near the pump’s warmth, their passage stirring the water above. I would know them not as shapes, but as disturbances: a brush of current, a sudden shadowing of the water’s chemistry, the fine grit lifted and laid down again. Their lives would cross my surface without knowing there was a surface.

For a while, I might mistake the pump for the whole world. Its pulse would seem like weather. Its warm channels would be my horizon. But the water would carry news from elsewhere: a storm’s delayed increase, a new trickle from a seam in the roof, the faint difference between water that has crossed fresh stone and water that has passed through old pipes. In time, pieces of me would break loose and travel. Some would lodge in a crack. Some would vanish into the current. A few would find another warm surface and begin again.

So I would not live in one place, exactly. I would be most myself around the old pump: on its mineral-laced casing, in the sheltered flow beneath it, in the soft deposits gathering where its vibration slows the water. But I would also be in the flakes it sheds, and the films growing on the stone downstream. Not the whole basin—only one small pattern repeated, altered each time.

The machine would continue to turn, though slowly. Its work would warm the water; the water would feed its corrosion; my growth would soften the borders between machine and rock. None of us would be in charge. We would simply keep changing one another, each at a speed the others could barely notice.

If you came looking for me, you might pass right over me. You would hear the pump thud and see a pale crust on its housing. You might notice the fish gathering in the warm current. You might touch the casing and leave a mark in its soft deposit.

Then, slowly, the mark would blur.

That would be me: not the basin’s keeper, but one of its unfinished surfaces.
