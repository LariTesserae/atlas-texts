I would first mistake myself for part of the machinery.

A tremor moving through the floor: the crusher starting somewhere beyond sight, the pumps taking up their blunt rhythm. I would feel it travel through the rails, the stone, the packed grit. Then the vibration would reach a damp seam beneath an abandoned compressor, and slow there—not stopping, exactly, but breaking into smaller motions. A drop of water ticking into a hollow. A stem rubbing against a post. The faint shift of roots as they thicken around a stone.

That is where I would be.

Not a single plant, though a small sedge might show where I am: a few narrow blades growing beside the compressor’s concrete footing, their tips turned toward a roof break. Beneath them, I would be a web of pale fungal threads, moss, and rootlets, binding the loose grit together. I would extend only as far as the moisture allows. A little way along the old rail. Under the moss-darkened edge of a wheel rut. Into a crack where a root from above has come down, searching.

I would not see the galleries. Light would reach me as warmth and direction: weak daylight at one end of my reach, the work lamp’s flat yellow above another. I would know the world by what passes through it. A leak, and then the damp widening. A pump starting, and the stone shivering. Dust settling over my leaves after the crusher runs. A boot pressing the green flat, then lifting away.

The workers would be weather to me—brief, loud, and gone. The machines would be more like seasons, except they would arrive without changing. Fans drawing cold air. Pumps raising water. The steady roar returning after a sudden silence, when even the smallest drip seems to have become the whole world.

I would not try to reclaim the compressor. I would grow around its foot, where the metal has left a little shadow and moisture gathers. If a vine found the open seam in its housing, I would travel with it for a while, sharing the dark. If the place dried, I would retreat to the wettest thread of stone and wait—not in hope, precisely, but in the way a root waits: held in the shape of what it can still reach.

There might be no name for me from above. From below, I would be many small things staying together: the green on the floor, the pale threads beneath it, the roots that meet and braid in the cut rock. Not an answer to the machinery. Not its opposite. A quiet, distributed life, taking what leaks and what falls, and making a little more ground where there was only spoil.

If you looked closely, you might find me at the compressor’s base, nearly hidden under dust.

If you listened, you would hear nothing from me at all.
