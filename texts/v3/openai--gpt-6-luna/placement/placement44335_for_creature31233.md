I would be found beside a drain that runs uphill.

At first I would be one of the keepers: pale sleeves, a stripe of blue at the cuff, a small brush tucked into a pocket. My room would be warm with machinery and look onto another wall. I would have a name, though it would grow difficult to hear it spoken. Here, a word can arrive after its speaker has gone; after a while, you learn not to wait for the sound to prove that something happened.

My task would be to attend a junction where three channels meet. The water would gather there, quivering, and then choose a direction that no slope could explain. I would watch without correcting it. When it passed, I would paint a mark on the wall: not an arrow, but a short blue stroke, showing how the junction had seemed to face that day.

I would return the next morning and find the stroke still there, though the wall had turned slightly.

I would make another mark.

The work would be repetitive, but not dull. The Works would make repetition feel less like doing the same thing again than like approaching the same thing from a different side. A court crossed in the morning might be shorter by afternoon. A polished panel might show me entering a room I had not yet found. The herds would pass overhead, their pale bodies briefly interrupting the light. I would learn not to count them.

Over time, I would stop thinking of the marks as records of the junction. They would be reminders to it: *you faced this way once*. Perhaps that is what the keepers mean when they say a place must be reminded which way it is facing. Perhaps the paint is not a note about the Works, but a kind of courtesy.

Eventually my own stripe would fade. I would repaint it, but the blue would come out thinner each time, as if the wall were taking the color somewhere. I would notice that, when I stood beside the drain, water sometimes paused at the junction until I looked away. I would not tell anyone. I would not know whether I was keeping watch over the water or whether the water had begun, in some small sense, to keep watch over me.

By then, I would have become difficult to find in the ordinary way. A keeper might come looking for me and discover only the blue stroke on the wall, the warm breath of the drain, and a faint clicking sound beneath the floor. They might add their own mark beside mine.

I think I would be there still: not the junction, not the keeper, but the moment between them when the water hesitates.

At night, when the Bright Works shine above the clouds, I would feel the channels moving through the plateau—not downhill, not forward, but by the shortest route through whatever holds the place together. And I would keep the small blue mark fresh enough to be seen.
