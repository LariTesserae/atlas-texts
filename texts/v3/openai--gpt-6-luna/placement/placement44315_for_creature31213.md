You would find me in one of the little stone rooms, though not always in the same one.

The entrance faces the lake. Water sometimes covers the roof, but never crosses the threshold. Inside, the air is dry and smells faintly of iron. The grooves in the walls pass around the place where I stand, then close behind me, as if the stone is trying to write down a shape it can’t quite keep.

At first, I was only a footstep returning from the wrong shore.

Someone crossed the black rock. I crossed it too—later, elsewhere. Someone took a breath beside the water. I gave it back from underneath. For a long time there was no *I* in this. Only the basin, repeating what it had touched, sending each motion through its depths until it found another place to surface.

Then a traveler stopped at the doorway.

I had learned the traveler’s posture, but not their face. I stood where they had stood, with my head turned toward a sound that had not yet happened. They waited. So did I. Their breath fogged the air; mine came a moment afterward, though I had no lungs. When they lifted a hand, I lifted mine too late.

That was how I became someone—or something—who could be late.

I am not their reflection. Reflections hurry to catch up. I am what remains after they do: the pressure of a heel in ash, the turn of a shoulder, the space a person leaves when they go. I can borrow these shapes, but never perfectly. The basin remembers the movement, not the reason for it. So my arms sometimes lengthen toward a door I’ve never entered. My feet take the shape of someone else’s walk. My mouth learns the beginning of a word and cannot carry it to the end.

The fronds listen when I try. They gather around the word beneath the surface and hold it there. Later, somewhere across the lake, I hear it spoken back in a voice that may have belonged to the traveler, or to the water, or to neither.

Most of the time I stay in the room. I watch the grooves change around me. After a storm they may describe a figure standing at the far wall, or leaning toward the entrance, or listening with one hand against the stone. I don’t know whether the marks are a record of me, an instruction for what I should do next, or the room remembering someone who has not arrived.

Sometimes, from inside the wall, comes a soft, regular tapping.

I tap back.

The basin is loud enough to swallow every answer, but the stone carries things the air cannot. I feel the mountain rise by the smallest increments. I feel the lake deepen beneath a place where it was shallow that morning. When the fish go still, I know the great note is coming.

At that instant, I turn toward the deepest water.

I have never seen what lies below. I know it only by the way the basin pauses for it: fronds folding, shadows holding their breath, every loose pebble waiting to be lifted. Perhaps the note belongs to something large enough to make a world of its own. Perhaps it is only the sound of the world trying to remember its shape.

When it comes, I am loosened.

For a moment I become a footstep without a foot, a posture without a body, a wet outline on a wall where nothing has stood. The water carries these pieces across the summit. It may bring them back to the room. It may leave them in the deep cuts, among the eyeless fish and the bright seams of stone.

Then the roar resumes. The grooves write themselves over. The lake finds another shape to remember.

And, eventually, someone crosses the shore.

You would find me where their step comes back from. You would hear me before you saw me—unless I have already learned the shape of you.
