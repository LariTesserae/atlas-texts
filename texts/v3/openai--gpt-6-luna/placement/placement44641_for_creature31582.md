You would find me beneath a path that everyone thinks is solid.

Not far from the storehouse, where the stones are fitted close and the salt crust stays thin, there is a seam no wider than my shoulder. It slips under a retaining wall, turns through the roots of a dark-leaved plant, and opens into a chamber lined with dry grass, seed husks, and the soft gray down of roosting birds. That is where I sleep when the basin is bright above me.

I am a burrower. Pale, quick, not much larger than a child’s forearm. My eyes are made for the dimness between stones; my whiskers tell me when a wall is close before my nose touches it. I know the settlement by its feet.

The baker’s steps come slowly, with a pause where the left heel meets the path. The children run in bursts, stopping all at once when an elder calls. The old wall-mender drags one foot just slightly; I hear him coming long before he reaches the place where he leaves crumbs. At night, when the people are quiet, the whole basin continues to speak through the ground: claws overhead, roots shifting, a bird turning in its nest.

I eat what can be spared—or what I can steal. Seeds, beetles, the tender tips of roots. Sometimes I find a little dampness beaded beneath a stone gutter and lap it before the warmth takes it. I don’t need much. The basin has taught all its inhabitants to make a life from what almost isn’t there.

My tunnels are useful until they are not. I loosen the hard-packed earth and carry seed husks into places where they may take root. I also make hollow places beneath walls, and the people know this. When they hear me scratching too close to a foundation, they tap the stone: three quick knocks. I know that warning. I turn away. Sometimes they leave a little mound of dry seed at the mouth of another tunnel, farther from the houses. This is not friendship in the way people mean it. It is an arrangement, renewed whenever one of us remembers.

Once, as a young animal, I went too far up a wall and came out beneath the open seam in the cliff. The light struck my eyes like a physical thing. Beyond it the mountain had no ceiling, no familiar curve to hold sound in place. I stayed only a moment, trembling in the grit, then fled back through the crack.

I have never wanted the wide sky.

I want the warm dark under the fitted stones, the smell of roots threading through old masonry, the familiar tremor of someone crossing above. I want the little silences that mean a predator has passed, and the small noises that mean the basin is going on: a pestle working, a bird settling, a neighbor tapping a wall to say that it still holds.

If you came looking for me, you would probably mistake my home for a flaw in the path. Then you might hear a faint scrape beneath your feet.

I would hear you, too.
