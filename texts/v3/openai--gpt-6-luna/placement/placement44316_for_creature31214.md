I would be found where the water hesitates.

At first, you might take me for a bead caught on the lip of a crack. I would shine there, small and clear, while the thread of spring divided around me. If you waited, the bead would not fall. It would lengthen toward the higher stone, trembling as though it had remembered something uphill.

Then you might see that the crack was not empty. Something pale would press through it: not a hand, exactly, though it would have the delicacy of fingers; not a flower, though its thin divisions would open and close like petals in the steam. The stone would warm beneath you. I would withdraw before you could decide what you had seen.

I think I would be one of the seam-kin. Or one of the ways they happen.

There is no single place I live. I am in the warm seam under the black arch, in the bead held above the pool, in the dry rectangle at the cliffside frame. I am the little adjustment that lets water choose a new course, or lets a snail pass into a patch of air and arrive somewhere the cliff has made distant. I don’t carry the water. I persuade it. I keep a space open long enough for something to change its mind.

If I had a body, it would be made of intervals: the narrow warmth between two stones, the pause before a drop falls, the fine boundary where vapor becomes rain. Perhaps, on certain mornings, I would gather all those intervals into a small white dart beneath a ledge, and cross a stretch of bare rock. Perhaps that would be the closest I came to having a shape.

I would not know whether I was tending the Sill or being tended by it. The springs would open in me; the cliffs would shed their shards through me. The green mats would spread, and I would let them. Later, when they withdrew and left the stone shining, I would not call it loss. The Sill is still deciding what it is. So am I.

When you arrived, I would not greet you. I would feel the fact of you: weight on the stone, warmth in the damp air, a new condition the Sill had not yet accounted for. Perhaps I would let a trickle pause beside your boot. Perhaps the low vibration would stop, and in the quiet you would hear one drop fall a long way.

At the rim, where the cloud opens onto nothing you can name, I would become harder to distinguish from the weather. The dark folds below would slow. The warmth would travel downward through the hidden depth, and I would listen—not with ears, but with the whole delicate arrangement of seams.

Then the cloud would close.

I would be a bead again. Or a crack. Or the air between the water and its reflection.

The spring would begin to run uphill.
