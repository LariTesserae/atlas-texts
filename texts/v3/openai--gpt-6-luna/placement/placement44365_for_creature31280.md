At first, I think I would be one of the pale creatures: a small, translucent thing slipping across a sunlit saddle, gone before the eye can decide what it saw.

But that is too quick. It belongs to the water’s passing thoughts. I would be something that takes longer to notice.

I would live where a black ridge breaks into loose stone and the roots have begun to hold it together. Not at the summit, exactly, but along its sheltered side, where warmth from a vent gathers beneath the fronds. My first body would be a soft knot among the roots—neither quite plant nor animal—with filaments threaded into the cracks and fine feelers resting against the leaves. I would know the water by its pressure, the light by the warmth it lays across my skin, and the current by the slow bending of everything around me.

For a long while, that knot might seem to be all I am. Then one day—though “one day” would mean little to me—I would feel a change on the far side of the basin: a slab shifting under its coat of growth, a new pocket of pumice settling into place. I would send myself there, not by swimming, but by taking root. A thread would lengthen. A frond would lean. Over years, I would become less a creature in the highland than a way the highland feels itself: a delicate continuity between stone, water, and leaf.

I would not command the plants. I would be among the things that help them hold. Their roots would pass through me; mineral dust would gather in my folds. When the whole grove bowed in the current, I would feel the motion arrive at different parts of myself, slowly enough to know the shape of it. The vent would warm me from beneath. The sun would reach me in scattered green light.

Perhaps the pale animal would sometimes pass over my ridge. I would sense its small disturbance and keep it, briefly, as a brightness moving through the leaves. Then the water would settle. The fronds would return to their unhurried inclination.

I would be the seam that listens: a quiet, spreading life in the young rock, most awake where the highland is still becoming.
