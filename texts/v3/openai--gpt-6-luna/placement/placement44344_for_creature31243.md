At first I think I would choose a high place: somewhere above the daily water, with a view across the roofs and enough light to tell morning from afternoon. I imagine myself on a crane-walk, or in a narrow room with a dry floor, watching the city rearrange itself below.

But I don’t think I’d stay there.

I would end up lower down, where an obsolete bridge forms the ceiling of a newer street. There’s a narrow maintenance ledge along one wall, just above the usual waterline. I’d sleep there in a hammock that can be unhooked quickly, with my boots and lamp within reach. When the water rises, the room grows smaller. When the old stone shifts, the ledge hums.

I would be a fault-listener. Not one of the people who can hear a pump’s pitch change across a crowded room—I’d be worse at that, perhaps. I’d need to press my palm to a rail, or my cheek against a wall, and wait for the vibration to tell me what the city’s noise is hiding. A healthy conduit has a steady shudder. A strained one skips. A dangerous one makes the metal feel almost warm beneath the hand.

People would send me ahead when the maps stopped being useful.

I’d carry a lamp, a line, and a little case of chalk sealed against the water. I would mark the routes that still opened, and the doorways that had become traps. Sometimes I’d find only a shifted wall or a channel running the wrong way. Sometimes there’d be an animal pressed beneath a grate, waiting for the current to change. I’d leave it a way out if I could. The city’s hungers are already crowded enough.

I’d learn the local signs in the dark: a slow amber pulse at the far end of an alley, a hand held flat to mean *don’t follow*, two taps on a pipe to say *I’m here*. My own signal would be three taps, spaced far apart. Not an alarm. Not yet. Just a message sent through the bones of the place: *something has changed; I’m listening.*

I don’t think I would be brave in the way people tell stories about bravery. I would be careful. I’d know which stones looked solid but weren’t, which doors should never be opened against a rising current, and how long it takes for a warning to travel from one block to another. I’d be afraid whenever the water ran backward. I’d go anyway, because somewhere someone would be waiting for a route home.

And after enough years, I think I’d become less a person who knows the city than a small part of how it knows itself. I’d recognize the tremor that comes before a platform shifts. I’d know when the deep cistern creatures were rising by the way the floor seemed to breathe. I might even come to hear, in the old stone’s answering shiver, something other than danger.

Still, I would never call it a voice.

I’d put my hand against the wall. I’d wait for the next vibration. Then I’d turn my lamp toward the passage and send the signal onward.
