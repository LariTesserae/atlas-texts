You would find me in the survey office above the West Lift, though not always by taking the stairs. The office is a narrow room between a public hall and a wall of new stone. It has one window, facing away from the clouds. Moisture gathers on the glass anyway.

At first, I would seem to be a clerk. I keep the maps dry, the instruments padded, and the records legible. When a crew reports that a ridge has moved, I ask where they stood when they measured it. When two accounts disagree, I keep both. I have a drawer full of maps that are accurate in different directions.

I would be careful with nouns. I would write *unavailable* where someone else might write *gone*. I would never mark a road safe without adding *in ordinary weather*. This would make me useful, and faintly irritating. People would come to me when they wanted a fact, and leave with the fact plus its necessary hinge.

But I don’t think I would remain only a clerk. The blank spaces on the maps would begin to feel like rooms I ought to visit. I’d go out with the surveyors, carrying a brass instrument that fogs from the inside. I’d learn the difference between a wall that had always been there and one that had arrived since breakfast. I’d wait at landings while the needles settled—or failed to settle—and make a note of that, too.

Eventually, people might say I know the city. I would correct them. I know where its accounts disagree. I know which staircases take longer on the way down, and which patches of moss mark warmth in the stone. I know the old houses whose doors open onto mountain, and the families who still hang coats there. I know that the goats’ bells sometimes sound nearby when the herd is several shelves away.

Perhaps, after enough years, I would become less like a person and more like the margin of a map: a place where the city keeps what it cannot yet resolve. A small figure in a damp office, lamp lit behind blue glass, adding careful notes while cranes turn beyond the window.

If someone asked where I lived, I’d say: above the lift, for now.

And if they asked what I was, I’d say: **the one who keeps the second measurement.**
