I would be found at the edge of one of the rooms that has been left bare.

Not in the middle of it. The middle belongs to the room. I would be just inside the doorway, where the rootwork makes a low arch and the floor is smooth from many small crossings. If you looked quickly, you might take me for one of the pale, long-bodied mammals slipping through the walls: whiskers, bright eyes, a narrow back that seems to go on a little farther than it should. I would watch you enter. I would wait to see whether you understood that the room was empty on purpose.

At first, I think I would be an animal. I would sleep in hollows warmed by the sun, drink from leaf-cups, and travel the narrow roads behind the bark. I would know which gaps admit a body and which admit only its shadow. I would keep my paws close to the walls, where the city is thickest and the routes most reliable.

But I would not be very good at staying in one place.

A passage would shorten behind me. A doorway would open where I remembered a wall. I would take a familiar turn and arrive in a courtyard I had not yet crossed. After a while, I would stop trying to decide whether the district was changing around me or whether I was the thing that changed it. I would learn to follow the ground’s small hesitations: a root that bends toward no water, a crack that holds rain after the rest have dried, a bird’s shadow arriving just before the bird.

I would become a keeper of almost-paths.

Not a guardian. The district does not need guarding. The beetles know their damp seams; the birds know when to lift; the roots find their way through anything left long enough beneath them. I would only notice where one route fails to meet another. I would go there and wait. Sometimes I would scratch the bark once, softly. Sometimes I would leave a scent in a corner, or lie very still until the place found its way around me.

I think the empty rooms would be where I learned to listen. Out in the lanes, sound is too plentiful to have a shape. In a vacant room, a single small noise can settle: a seed husk snapping, a claw touching stone, water ticking from a leaf. I would hear the underfloor traffic change direction. I would hear a bird decide against a landing. I would hear the district making room for something not yet arrived.

At dusk, when the blossoms opened and the pools caught the last light, I would come out farther. I would cross the courtyard while the thin-winged insects hovered above the water. The birds would be settling into the roof-leaves. A few of the larger residents might be watching from behind the walls, unseen but warm. We would all be present for a moment, each of us keeping to our own small route.

Then the flock would rise. The leaves would clap. The sound would scatter everything again.

If you asked what I was, I might say: a small animal that lives where the city forgets to be quite the same place twice.

If you asked where to find me, I would say: in the doorway, just before you decide the room is empty.
