I would be found at the edge of a change.

Not where the water is deepest, but where it has just begun to move differently: around the seam of a stone shelf, at the lip of a new channel, beside a patch of plain lifting almost imperceptibly from below. There, a small dark shape would pause in the shallows. Its fine limbs would touch the grit one after another.

At first, I might seem to be that shape. I would feel the basin through my joints: the constant rain, the slow grind underfoot, the great roar pressing into the water. I would have no name for these things. I would know only that the stone had tightened, or that a tremor had arrived from far away and was not yet finished arriving.

Then I would go on, leaving no track.

But I would not travel very far—not all of me. Some part would remain in the seam, in the water’s skin, in the thread of pale life beneath the packed layers. The little animal would be less a body than a place where something could be felt. My limbs would test the new ground; below, the buried filaments would receive the answer. A tremor passed through the basin might take years to reach me. I would not know whether it had begun yesterday or before the pillars were worn smooth.

Perhaps I would be the pause between hearing and moving.

I would have other bodies, though I would not think of them as bodies. A bead of water cupped in black lichen, with a pale thread curled inside. A clear reed bending in two directions at once. A vibration traveling through an old pillar, making it groan. Each would hold me briefly, and none would contain me. When the water changed its mind about where downhill was, I would feel the decision pass through the basin—not as a command, but as pressure asking whether the world could bear another alteration.

I would answer as slowly as the plain.

Sometimes the answer would be a channel. Sometimes a ridge. Sometimes nothing anyone could see. The surface creatures would gather at the place afterward, touching the softened edges with their transparent limbs. They would not know I had answered. I would not know, exactly, that I had.

In storms, I would become difficult to separate from anything else. The roar would enter every hollow and filament. The little animals would lock themselves to stone; the reeds would bow without choosing a direction. Beneath them, I would tighten—not to keep the basin still, but to keep it from coming apart while it moved. Then the pale lights would flicker under the water, and for a few breaths I would sense openings: not doors, precisely, but the basin folded against some other version of itself.

Afterward, I would be small again.

A jointed thing crossing a shelf. A listening point in the shallows. Something that could be overlooked because it had learned to resemble what it was touching.

So if you went looking for me, you would find no dwelling and perhaps no single creature. You might find me where the ground had just shifted, feeling with a hand’s worth of limbs for what the buried multitude had heard. I would be a listener made briefly visible: one small, dark answer in a world that is always asking itself how to change.
