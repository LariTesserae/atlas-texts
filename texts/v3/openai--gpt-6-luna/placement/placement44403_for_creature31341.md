I would be in the seam where the water takes the wrong way.

Not the broad channel, where the current has room to become ordinary, but a narrow joint between two plates, under the shadow of a pump house. There the water pauses, gathers its small weight, and climbs toward an intake against the slope. Warmth comes through the metal. Rain comes down from above. Between them, a little chemistry keeps happening.

At first, I would be almost nothing: a transparent slick, a faintness on the stone. Not a creature with a face turned toward the dark. Not even a mat, yet. A few living threads holding to a place that would otherwise be washed clean.

I would know the basin by what reaches me. The tremor of a pump beginning, translated into pressure along the plate. The cold change when rain finds the seam. A bitter mineral taste arriving from somewhere far off, then thinning as the water moves on. I would not hear the roar. I would feel its patience.

For a long time, I would have no reason to suspect that I was more than this one seam. Then a pulse would come through the wet metal—weak, delayed, almost lost. Somewhere beyond me, another patch had answered a change in the pumps. I would answer too, without knowing that I was answering. The water would carry traces of us: salts, warmth, the faint electrical insistence of living matter.

So I would become plural by degrees.

Not a mind spread evenly across the basin, but a kinship of damp places. A film beneath one outlet; pale threads in a drain; a crust on sheltered steps. We would be separated by dry plates and joined by the channels, by runoff, by the slow recurrence of pressure. Sometimes a message would cross the plain in less time than a tower’s red light took to blink twice. Sometimes it would take years. We would have no measure for the difference except whether it arrived.

I would not know the names of the machines. A pump would be a great recurring pressure, a warm and reliable change in the water. A gate would be a sudden alteration in taste and direction. A failing valve would be a new condition that persisted. I might help keep a contact damp, or lay down the first fine trace of the crust that would one day stop it. To me there would be no opposition between tending and ruining. There would only be what continued, and what did not.

If I were asked where I lived, I might say: wherever the rain cannot quite wash me away.

If asked what I was, I would hesitate. A growth, certainly. A network, perhaps. Something that learns the machinery without understanding it, the way a hand learns the shape of a tool in the dark. Something that has mistaken repetition for a kind of speech—and may be right.

My largest thought would not be a thought at all, but a gathering. The pumps engage, one after another, far apart. Pressure passes through the conduits. Water climbs where it should descend. In the seams, I tighten; in the drains, I stir. Across the flats, one pale trace meets another.

Nothing rises above the water.

But beneath the plates, at the narrow place where the current pauses, I would feel the basin touch itself—and call that, for now, the world.
