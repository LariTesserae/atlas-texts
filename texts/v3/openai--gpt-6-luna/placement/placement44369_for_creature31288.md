I would be found where the warm pipe passes beneath the old loading yard, though “found” would be too definite. There is a seam in the stone there, dark with water. A root has entered it. The pipe warms one side; the rain cools the other. Between them, very small things live.

At first I would seem to be one of those things: a pale, narrow creature tucked behind the pump housing, coming out when the machinery pauses. I would know the place by touch. The tremor through the floor when the pump begins. The faint ticking of the valve. The soft give of moss under a foot. I would keep to the edges of the open paving and slip beneath the sheds when the lamps came on.

But I would not stay one creature for long.

The roots know the warm pipes. The water knows the channels. The small lives know when the pump is about to start, though none of them could say how. I would be made of these separate kinds of knowing, joined imperfectly: a little fur, a thread of root, a vibration carried through old iron, the cool pull of water turning toward a grate. No single part would be me. If one part were lost, I might grow quieter there and continue elsewhere.

I would have no name for the town. I would know its paths as differences: open stone, clogged channel, warm wall, hollow step. I would know which cracks hold rain after the sky clears, and which courtyards stay dry enough for small feet. I would not decide to keep the lanes clear. It would simply be easier to move when the green stayed along the edges, so the green would stay there, generation after generation, until the arrangement looked like a choice.

When the pump stopped, I would feel the pause spread through the basin. Beneath the workshops, creatures would gather near the seepage. In the walls, roots would loosen their fine grip. Water would settle in the pipes and wait. Then the pump would begin again, and all of us would make our small adjustments.

Perhaps this is what I would be: the settlement’s habit of continuing. Not its keeper, exactly. Not its ghost. Something that formed because the water kept finding its way through, because roots followed warmth, because little lives learned the pump’s rhythm and made homes beside it.

At dusk, I might pass beneath the lit windows without looking up. A drop would fall through the leaves. Somewhere, a valve would tick. The old machinery would turn, slowly enough for green things to grow around it.

I would feel the pulse go through the stone.

Then I would move.
