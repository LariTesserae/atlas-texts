I would be found where the air first changes its mind.

Not at the seam itself—the light there is too sharp, the wind too young—but a little way below, where a vent divides around a basalt pillar and loses some of its force. A dark fan of growth clings to the stone. Its edges are stiff and grey, and its finer threads disappear into the mortar. At first, I would seem to be only one more plant in the city.

Then the wind would pass through me.

I would rattle, but not all at once. One frond would answer the high whistle from the roof; another would wait for the low boom in the rock. My roots would feel the warmth shifting through the wall. Salt grains would settle on my surface, and I would loosen them, slowly, making space for the next breath. Nothing about this would look like thought. Nothing would move quickly enough to be mistaken for a gesture.

But I would know the difference between an ordinary gust and the air that comes before the stone expands.

I would not have a body with a clean edge. Part of me would be there, in the fan by the pillar. Part would run as pale threads through the wall joints, where mineral vapor gathers. Part would have taken hold in a roof slot, miles—or what counts as miles in this little hollow—away. Between those places, the city’s channels would carry my weather: warmth, dust, pressure, the small tremors that travel from one terrace to another.

I would be less a single plant than a way of noticing.

The old houses would give me shelter, though I would not think of them as houses. Their channels would be my paths. Their terraces would make pockets of warmth where new growth could unfold. I would press my roots into the patterns left by earlier roots, following grooves worn so gradually that no one could say where stone ended and living matter began. The city would not belong to me. I would not belong to it. We would keep making room for one another.

I would learn the vents by their voices. Some sing thinly in the dry hours. Some moan when the outer crust tightens. One passage gives a blunt, sudden roar that shakes flakes from the ceiling. I would answer each differently—not with words, but by bending, tightening, letting one surface crackle while another holds still. The sound would pass through the lanes and return changed. Perhaps the city would recognize itself in it.

I would have no memories as people have them. No images kept intact. What I carried forward would be a tendency: here the wall warms early; there the salt falls after the second boom; in this narrow lane the air curls back on itself and brings the old dust home. The past would remain in my arrangement, in the places where I grew thick or learned to retreat. A record made of habits.

When the wind stopped, I would still be listening—not for a footstep, not for a visitor, but for the next alteration in the pressure of the stone. In that quiet, the smallest sounds would come forward: a thread of new growth separating its tip from the wall; a salt crystal forming; the softened click of a joint settling into place.

Perhaps I would be called the city’s ear. But an ear suggests that sound arrives from elsewhere, and here everything is touching everything else. The air touches the fronds. The fronds touch the walls. The walls press back against the buried heat.

So I would be the place where that touch becomes audible.

Above me, the plain would glare without answer. Below it, I would cling to a warm seam, neither wholly plant nor building, neither the wind nor the stone. When the hollow breathed, I would tremble with it. When the rock gave its low, patient boom, I would carry the note through my fine, dark threads—and, for an instant, the whole city would sound like one living thing.
