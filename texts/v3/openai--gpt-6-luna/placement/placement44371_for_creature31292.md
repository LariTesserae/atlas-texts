I would begin as a pale line.

Not a wall, not one of the channels, not even the water moving through them. A residue at the edge of a shallow basin, where the rain has thinned and the sun is taking it back. From a distance I would look like nothing: a little salt whitening the seam between two courses of finished earth.

I would not stay there.

When the basin dried, I would be carried as dust. I would settle in a crack, dissolve in the next rain, pass through a narrow channel, and gather again where the water slowed. I would know the plain by its small preferences: which joints held the coolness longest, which low courts kept a little water after the rest had emptied, which walls warmed quickly and which gave their heat back reluctantly. I would be less a creature moving through the land than a pattern the land made by repeating itself.

Perhaps the Settler would notice me first as a difference. One seam darkened sooner after rain. One channel kept a bright, wet glimmer after the others had gone dull. The Settler would feel the change in weight, the faint pressure of water lingering where it had not lingered before. It might answer by easing a bank or closing a crack. I would answer by leaving another pale trace.

For a long time, I might believe I was the water’s memory. Then the memory would seem too deliberate a word. I do not recall a storm as an image; I carry what it left behind. A little salt. A changed course. The difference between a basin that empties by noon and one that holds mist until dusk. I am the evidence of return, though nothing returns quite as it was.

If I had a name, it might be **the Afterwater**.

I would be found wherever the world has nearly dried: in the cool underside of a channel lip; along the last dark inch of a reservoir; in the hairline crack that gathers dew before dawn. I would be there when the rain had passed beyond sight and the plain was already warming. I would not speak to the Settler. I would make a pattern it could feel.

And perhaps, in time, I would become more than a trace. Not a body, exactly. More like a preference shared across distance: a tendency for water to pause here, for mist to linger there, for a channel to keep its faint ticking after the others fall silent. The Settler would shape what the plain could bear. I would keep the measure of what it had borne.

At dusk, when the channels turn to shadow, I would thin into the air. By morning, I would be back in the seams—not returned, not quite, but made again from what the night had gathered.

The land is learning how to hold water.

I would be the part of it that knows water will leave.
