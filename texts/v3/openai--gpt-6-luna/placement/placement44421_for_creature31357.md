At first I would be found at the edge, where the last roof gives way to cloud.

Not because I wanted to leave. I think I would have gone there to learn what the Fold was doing beyond sight: whether the ridge had broken again, whether the new stone had held, whether the roots had caught it. I would stand with one hand on the living wall and feel the mountain’s weather arrive through it—cold gathering, water loosening, some deep weight shifting under the ground.

But I would not stay at the edge. The Fold would have no use for someone who only watched.

I would be the one people called when a familiar lane began to feel unfamiliar underfoot. Not a builder, exactly. There would be no building in the sense of making a thing and then being done with it. I would be a listener: someone who could feel, through a palm or the sole of a foot, where a passage had begun to tighten, where an arch was taking too much weight, where a new shoot was pressing patiently toward the light.

I would live near a junction deep inside the settlement, where three narrow ways met beneath a roof of overlapping leaves. It would not be a room anyone had planned. It would be a pocket left over where the roots bent around an older trunk. One wall would be warm from the bodies passing on the other side; another would hold the chill of the stone. I would sleep there, close enough to hear the Fold change its mind.

People would come to me quietly. They would put a hand on the stem beside my doorway, and I would feel the small tremor travel through the joined ribs of the houses. Sometimes I would go with them. Sometimes I would say: not that way today. The roof is lowering there. Take the damp passage. It will open again.

I would know the settlement by its pressures rather than its shape. A lane would be the place where shoulders could pass without turning. A home would be the hollow whose air had learned someone’s breath. I would remember the old routes, but not insist on them. The Fold changes too slowly for alarm and too steadily for certainty. My task would be to help people notice the difference between a passage that is closing and one that is merely resting.

Perhaps I would have once had a room of my own, deeper and drier, with a little roof pocket that caught the morning’s weak light. Then the roots would have drawn tighter around it. I would have moved before the walls pressed in, carrying almost nothing. The room would have closed behind me, not as a loss but as a kind of answer. Afterward, when people spoke of where I lived, they would point toward the junction: there, where the lanes learn one another.

I would be slight from stooping. My hands would be broad and rough along the fingers. I would wear dark barkcloth that smelled faintly of rain, and I would have no lamp. I would find my way by temperature, by the drag of air against my cheek, by the dampness of a wall. I would know who was approaching not by their face but by how the bridge gave beneath their step.

And I would sometimes be wrong. A root would turn where I thought it would keep straight; a roof would lift away from a room I had expected it to shelter. Then I would put my hand against the living wall and wait. The Fold would not explain itself. It would only make its new shape felt.

So I think I would be neither its keeper nor its servant. I would be one of the small ways it learned to listen to itself: a person in the close dark, feeling for the next opening, and walking there often enough that it stayed open for others.
