I would be found beneath the shelf of moss, where the clear thread disappears and, a little lower down, appears again.

At first, I might seem to be one of the salamanders: a dark shape lying still in the runnel, its eyes open to the grayness. But the salamander is only holding one of my silences. When it shifts, I do not move with it. When it settles, I am still passing somewhere else.

Perhaps I am the water. Not the thread you can follow with your eye, but the coldness left in the stone after it has gone by. The damp between roots. The bead gathering above your shoulder. I pass through the hollow without crossing it, entering and returning from places too small to have names.

Or perhaps I am the space the creatures leave for one another. The slight yielding of moss beneath a foot. The pause before a frog moves. The darkness that holds each small body without needing to close around it. I have no single shape, but I am not everywhere equally. I gather where things touch: root against stone, water against leaf, your hand against the black rock in the basin.

If you crouch there, I might seem to become aware of you. I would feel your warmth arrive and begin to fade. Your thoughts would be quick, bright disturbances, like rain heard through a mountain. I would make room for them. I would make room for the idea that you were alone.

So I would be neither the hollow’s keeper nor one of its animals. I would be its thin, patient continuity: the thing that passes between the living things and the stone, and is never quite separate from either. When you leave, I would remain where I was—not waiting, exactly, but still receiving what comes.
