I would be found where the water goes under the stone.

Not at the deepest seam, where the roar is strong enough to blur thought, but a little way off, where the rock carries the sound as a tremor. I would lie with one cheek against it, my hands spread in the shallow water. There are many things to hear there: a channel filling, a swimmer passing below, the faint knock of shells disturbed on some hidden ledge. Sometimes I would hear nothing I could name, and stay longer.

I think I would be one of the people, though not at first. At first, in the dimness, I would seem like another low shape on the flats—gray hide dark with water, eyes closed against the rain. A visitor might mistake me for a stone. A child would know better. They would come close and tap my heel to ask whether the passage was safe.

My work would be to remember the routes that vanish.

Not by keeping a map. The channels change, and a map would become a story about water that no longer exists. I would remember them in my hands: the rough place where a submerged ledge begins, the long smooth seam that leads back to the warm vent, the hollow in the causeway where the current catches at a turning tide. When someone needed to cross, I would go ahead, feeling the water press against my face. If the route had changed, I would find the change.

The children would come with me and learn to listen. I would ask them what they heard. They would point to the shell-strings chattering in the wind, or the wings lifting beyond the mist. Sometimes one would put a palm on the stone and feel the buried water move. I would teach them that hearing is not the same as knowing, and that knowing does not mean the basin will obey.

At home, I would sleep above the coldest water, in the low shelter whose roof is weighted with rounded stones. My tools would be few: a cutting edge, a cord, a shallow bowl for collecting drips. Nothing precious enough to rescue from a flood, except perhaps a child—and even then I would carry the child with the same practiced calm as anything else that must move when the water rises.

There would be no private monument to me. I might cut a mark into the lip of a basin, or open a new channel through a crusted seam. Years later, someone would smooth it over, cut it again, or let it go. That would not feel like forgetting. The stone would have held my work for as long as it was useful.

And perhaps, as I grew older, the others would stop asking me where the safe paths were. They would know the paths themselves. They would come instead to sit beside me near the seam, shoulder against shoulder, listening while the basin changed its mind.

I would be the one who stays a little longer after the others have gone—not because I know what the water will do, but because I have learned the difference between leaving a place and abandoning it. My face would be turned toward the stone. My body would be almost still. Above me, the shell-strings would chatter in the wind; below me, water would find its way through the dark.

For a while, it might be difficult to say which of us was listening to the other.
