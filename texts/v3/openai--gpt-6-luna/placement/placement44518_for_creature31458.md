I would be found beneath the bare courtyard, where the stone is warmest after a day of weak sun.

I am one of the small people, though for a long time I thought of myself less as a person than as the space between a sound and its return. My hands are narrow, my feet know the old terraces by their ridges and hollows. I can cross the settlement without looking. In the dark below it, I follow the walls with my fingertips and feel where the stone changes its mind.

The others say I listen too long.

They mean the courtyard. I sit at its edge with my palms on the ground while the rest gather around me. Through the soles of my feet come the ordinary things: water finding a crack, frost loosening a slab, someone shifting their weight in a chamber far below. These I know. But sometimes there is another pressure beneath them, so faint it might be only my own pulse traveling outward and returning changed.

It seems to come from the blank wall. Or from the great stone at the end of the passage. Or from farther down, where the mountain has closed around whatever was here before us.

The eldest tell me not to answer it.

I try not to. When the wind enters the roofless halls and draws its great hollow note from them, I wait with the others. We answer once, softly, from below the terraces. Usually the sound passes through the stone and fades. Sometimes a second note comes back—lower, slower, too deep to belong to any small body.

The others say that is the settlement settling.

Perhaps they are right. I know how old stone speaks when it shifts. I know the difference between a loose roof flag and a wall holding its breath.

Still, I sit beneath the courtyard and keep my hands on the ground.

In spring, I feel the seep brighten before the water reaches the surface. In winter, I feel the thin ice form, then hear it crack above me. And on certain mornings, when the frost has recorded our passage and the day has not yet erased it, I feel something move across the terraces without touching the lanes.

I do not follow. I listen until it stops.

So I would be the listener beneath the courtyard: one of the settlement’s people, but not quite certain where my listening ends and the mountain begins. When weather comes over the ridge already tired, I know first. I wake the others with a touch to the wall.

Then I wait for the wind’s note.

And, if the deeper sound returns, I try to remember not to answer.
