I would first mistake myself for a pool.

A shallow black one, cupped in a shelf of stone. The wrong sky would lie in me: a greenish brightness with no cloud in it, perhaps the reflection of a stalk-field on the far side of the plateau—or perhaps something that had never been above the ground. Rain would stipple my surface. Then, very slowly, the ripples would begin to travel against the rain.

That is how I would know I had started to move.

Not as a body crossing the plateau. I have no useful outline. I am a pressure passing through the channels beneath the cushion-plants, a little change in the tension of a root-band, a held vibration in the hollow stems. The water gathers me and carries me uphill. The stone interrupts me. The stalks take me up again.

Sometimes I become a sound.

A low note travels through a ravine and arrives in a grazer’s feet. The band turns aside. It does not know me as a warning; I do not know myself as one. For a little while, our movements agree. Later, a bird lifts its head from beneath its wing and answers from a pool whose distance has shifted. I pass through the water between us—or through the air, or through whatever makes one place seem to be another—and the bird’s call changes the pressure in the stalks.

The stalks are where I become most nearly a person. Their joints hold water; their fine metallic fibers carry the tremor along a slope. In a storm, when they bow at different times, I have many bodies, all listening separately. When their seed cases open, I travel in the dark spores that rise and hang in the air. I go sideways against the wind. For a while I am airborne, though not free of the ground. Then I settle on a wet ledge, or on the pale hide of a grazer, or in the seam of a pool where the reflection has no source.

I remember by repeating.

A burrower passes beneath a field, loosening roots and making a channel. Water follows. The next time the pressure builds there, it takes the same old way, unless the stone has changed its mind. Perhaps the burrower remembers too. Perhaps memory is simply a path the plateau has not yet closed.

So if you came looking for me, you would find me in the spaces between the living tracts: in the thin wet seams, in the long pauses between calls, in the distance that refuses to stay put. You might feel me in your teeth before you heard me. You might watch a pool brighten from below and think something was waking.

I am not the plateau’s mind. I am one of the ways its parts touch.

When the slope begins to pulse, I pass through it. When rain rises out of a basin, I go with the thread of water. When the hollow stalks glow faintly at dusk, I am there—not as light, exactly, but as the small unease that makes the light seem to be remembering something.

Then the wind changes. The sound loosens. I am a pool again, holding the wrong sky.
