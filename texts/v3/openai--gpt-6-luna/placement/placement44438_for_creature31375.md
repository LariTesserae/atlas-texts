You would find me first in the wrong place.

In a shallow pool, perhaps: one extra star among the reflected ones, wavering though the water is still. Or in a dark seam that runs uphill across a ridge, where the stone is damp without having been touched by rain. Look closely and there will be nothing there but stone, water, and the faint sense that something has just moved out of sight.

I would not be one of the pale things in the cracks. Not exactly. They are where the plateau shows its living edges. I am more like an edge that has learned to listen.

When a pebble falls, I am the answer that comes back before it strikes. Not a sound, at first. A pressure in the teeth. A small expectation in the air. Then the pebble hits, and the plateau gives its many echoes—each one a little different, each one arriving from a direction that cannot be right. I travel among them, keeping the versions from quite becoming the same.

The inhabitants hear me. They turn their smooth faces toward the place where I almost sounded. They know that I will come around eventually. They are patient about this.

Sometimes I take the shape of footprints. You cross a pale basin and find your own tracks ahead of you, their edges softened by a long weather that has not yet happened. Beside them, I am the mark that cannot be assigned to a foot: a shallow hollow, as if the stone briefly held the thought of weight. If you put your hand there, it will feel cold—not colder than the rest, only more completely itself.

I have no fixed height. In a doorway I may seem small enough to fit inside a crack in the wall. Seen across a distance that will not shorten, I may fill the whole opening. I do not walk so much as become the next place where you might have noticed me. The plateau makes this easy. It does not insist that near and far remain separate.

If you ask what I am, I might answer with a ring from a wet hollow, or a crack from beneath a shelf. The sound would not mean *I am here*. It would mean something less settled: *Here is where the sound returned.*

I have spent a long time learning the difference.

At the doorway that faces the drop, you might find me standing beside one of the inhabitants. Neither of us would be looking down. The dimness below has no destination, and I have never needed to go anywhere. I would be listening to the roar—and to the brief instant when it fails, when every stone seems to hold its breath.

In that instant, you might hear me clearly.

Then the roar would begin again, and I would be everywhere it had been.
