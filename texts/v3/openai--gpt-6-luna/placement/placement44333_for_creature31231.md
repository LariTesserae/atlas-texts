You would find me where a path goes soft.

Not where it ends—that is too easy—but where the firm roots give way beneath the moss, and the ground holds a little more water than it did yesterday. I would be crouched there with one hand pressed flat to the living mat, listening for what has changed below. Perhaps I would look like one of the people: pale, narrow-shouldered, with seeds caught in my hair and silver flower-dust in the creases of my fingers. Perhaps, at a distance, I would look like a patch of leaves that had learned to wait.

I would be called by the routes I keep. No spoken name, most likely. Among the people, a name might be a pool, a hollow, a particular passage through the roots. Mine would be the place where water vanishes under the old stair and returns warm several miles away.

I would know the small languages of pressure: the quick, soft disturbance of a burrower; the steady advance of water through an ancient channel; the deeper shifting that makes every path briefly uncertain. When the plateau moved, I would not try to stop it. I would lift a stone from a stream’s mouth, lay a fallen branch across a tender place, and wait with my palm against the ground until the water found its way.

At first I might think this was all I was: a listener, a keeper of passages. Then, after a long while, I would notice that the paths also kept me. The same hollow would warm beneath my sleeping place. A root would rise where I needed a handhold. A pool would hold my reflection even when the mist blurred everything else.

So I would begin to wonder whether I lived on the plateau, or whether I was one of the ways it learned to notice itself.

I would leave offerings in the old stones—not to ask anything, exactly. A smooth pebble. A twist of pale grass. Sometimes nothing but the warmth of my palm. And if someone came along the path, uncertain whether the sound beneath their feet was water or something alive, I would be there nearby, quiet enough to let them listen.

I would be the one who stays where the ground changes its mind: a person, perhaps; a root’s slow memory, perhaps. The high country would not need me to decide.
