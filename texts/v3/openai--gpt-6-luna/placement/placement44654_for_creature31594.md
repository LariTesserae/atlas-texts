I think I would begin as something small enough to be mistaken for a movement in the moss.

A pale creature, perhaps: narrow-bodied, quick, with feet that feel the buried ridges before they show themselves. I would live in the long corridors between the dense growth, where the plants stand low enough to pass through and the basins hold a little water in their shade. I would travel mostly at dusk, when the cold draws beads of moisture from the air and every stalk is silvered at its edges.

At first I would think of the plateau as a place around me. I would know it by the spring of the ground, the shelter of the groves, the safe routes between hollows. I would leave tracks in the moss and warm small pockets beneath the roots. I would strip leaves, carry seeds caught in my fur, vanish when the crowns turned.

Then, slowly, I would understand that the ground had felt me.

Not watched me—not with eyes, or with any single attention. My passage would be one more pressure moving through its country. A ridge might tighten after I crossed it. A cluster of fronds might close where I had rested. The marks I left would be read in several ways at once: warmth, disturbance, food, perhaps the beginnings of new growth.

I would still be myself. I would hunger. I would startle at the silent leaning of a grove. But my small life would be threaded into something too large to see: a brief, moving thought in a mind that has no head.

I would be found where a dark root mat loosens around a seam of wet stone, or beneath the cupped crown of a stalk gathering cloud-water. I would come and go. The plateau would not call me home; it would simply close gently over the traces of me, and keep changing.
