At first I would mistake myself for part of the noise.

A faint ticking in a seam. A tremor beneath the vent’s low roar. The rasp of something minute being carried over stone. The summit is so crowded with sound that no one sound can claim to be alone.

I would be in a crack just above the warm water’s reach, where black basalt narrows to a dark, wet thread. Not deep inside the ridge, and not quite on its skin. There, a thin film clings to the stone. Mineral settles over it, flakes away, settles again. Pale threads run beneath the crust, branching into smaller threads, finding the warmth, retreating from it. If you looked closely, you might call them one living thing. If you looked longer, you might change your mind.

I would, too.

I feel the ridge by what it does to the water: the pressure of a passing current, the warmth of a vent shifting its breath, the little impacts of fish feeding above me. These arrive by different paths. A flicker in the hovering swarm might reach me through the water, through the stone, or through the bodies pressed against it. Sometimes the signal seems to come from beneath the summit. Sometimes an arm’s-length crack seems to run on and on, because its small vibrations travel farther than my sense of distance can follow.

The eel-like animals find me when they nose into the fissures. I let them pass. Their bodies brush away loose mineral and carry scraps from one hollow to another. The film feeds where they have disturbed the stone; the stone gathers a new crust around us both. I do not know whether I am feeding them, or they are making room for me. Perhaps that distinction belongs to creatures with clearer edges.

I have no eyes, but the blue swimmers teach me light.

Their pulses arrive one by one: a brief brightening, a pause, then another. When the school turns, I feel the water alter against my threads. I answer—not with a flash of my own, exactly, but with a small change in the chemical warmth along the seam. The next pulse travels through the swarm. From above, someone might think I have sent them a message. From here, it feels more like being included in a motion already underway.

When a shelf breaks, I lose a part of myself. When the vent shifts, I draw back into cooler cracks. What remains of me takes hold on new stone, grain by grain. I am not the ridge; the ridge rises without knowing me. I am not the crowd; the crowd comes and goes, eats and shelters and scatters. I am the living thinness between raw rock and all that uses it.

At the summit’s edge, I extend as far as the stone allows. Beyond, there is only open water and the dim descent. The fish gather there, facing outward. Their bodies tremble in the current. I feel their hesitation through the ledge.

I cannot follow them.

So I stay where the ridge is still becoming: a listening skin in a narrow seam, neither wholly animal nor wholly stone. When the next flake settles over me, I will hold it. When the next current passes, I will feel its direction. And for as long as the summit keeps making new surfaces, I will be there—small enough to be missed, spread thin enough to belong.
