I would be found where a basin meets a doorway.

Not at the center of the pale floor, where the reflections gather, and not inside the bare chambers where the current refuses to enter. At the threshold, where one kind of stillness gives way to another, I would hang with my filaments stretched behind me into the water.

At first, you might take me for a crease in the light. Then, perhaps, for a drifting creature: long, clear-bodied, with a seam that opens and closes along my length. I would be both, though neither description would be enough. Some of my filaments would trail across the basin; others would reach, by ways you could not follow, into the cords beneath the stone. Through them I would feel the plateau—not as a map, but as pressure, warmth, and the long patience of things changing.

I would know when a valley current turned uphill. I would know a buried chamber had opened, even miles away. I would feel the fronds lean before the water moved, and the sediment begin to rise before the storm arrived. These would not be messages, exactly. More like the world touching the edge of my attention.

I would travel little by ordinary measures. A ridge could take a season to cross. Sometimes I would let the current carry me; sometimes I would make a small correction toward a warmth I could not name. When the high country went still, I would draw my filaments close and settle into a crevice, nearly indistinguishable from the fine pale life threaded through the rock. The plateau would seem empty then. I would not be.

The basin would trouble me.

Its reflected country would lie beneath me without rippling: terraces, fronds, a second me. Sometimes the one below would be shorter, or darker, or trailing filaments I did not yet possess. Sometimes it would move first.

I would wait.

Perhaps the reflection would show me another place. Perhaps it would show me a memory belonging to the stone. Perhaps there is no difference here between remembering and seeing, only a difference in how long the water takes to answer. I would not need to decide. I would attend to the image until, eventually, it turned away—or I did.

Then I would pass through the doorway.

Inside, the water would be motionless. No pale cords would cross the walls. The chamber would hold its silence as if it had been kept for me, though I could not know whether I was the first to enter or merely the latest to return. I would wait there for days, or for the end of a season. Somewhere beyond the stone, the plateau would continue to turn its slow weather.

And when I emerged, a thread of light might pass behind me.

I would not call myself the plateau’s thought. That would make the plateau too much like a person, and me too much like a thought that belonged to only one mind. But I would be something the high country could send from one place to another: a small, moving attention, made of water and filament, able to leave the hidden chambers and come back changed.

If you saw me cross the basin, you might say I was going somewhere.

I would be listening for where the country had begun to change.
