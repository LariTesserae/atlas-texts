I would be found where a channel slips beneath one of the dark paths—under stone, willow root, and the quiet pulse of a tower.

At first I might seem like a pale strand of weed caught in the current. Then I would move. I am small and long-bodied, with a flattened head and fine, trembling feelers along my sides. I have no need for a fixed burrow. I live in the basin’s temporary places: a hollow left by a loosened stone, a pocket beneath a mat of roots, a crack that holds water for three nights before opening somewhere else.

The water tells me what is coming. A pressure change touches my skin before the channel rises. The tower’s low vibration tells me when its buried anchor shifts. I do not understand its measurements, but I know the difference between the usual hum and the strained one. When it changes, I leave.

I feed on the small lives that gather in fresh water: larvae, soft-bodied things, the drifting crumbs of the marsh. Sometimes I carry seeds snagged in my gills from one pool to another. Sometimes I carry nothing useful at all. The basin does not seem to require that every journey have a purpose.

I am most at home in the minutes after a flood has passed, when the water is clouded and the channels have not yet decided where to go. I slip through the new routes while the birds watch from the reeds and the larger animals wait for firmer ground. By morning, my passage may be gone. That is all right. I know how to begin again.

If someone found me, they might call me a fish, though I can cross wet stone when the water thins. The children know better than to name me after a place. They call me *still-here*—not because I stay, but because, after the basin rearranges itself, something living is always waiting in the next hollow.
