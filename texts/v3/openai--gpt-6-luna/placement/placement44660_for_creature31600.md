I think I would live where the last houses give way to the outer chambers—not quite in the city, not quite beyond it. There would be a low room cut into a shelf of pale stone, with a narrow view of the animal paths and a bell hanging beside the door.

At first, I might call myself a mapmaker. I would mark the routes between districts, the pools that held through the dry spells, the cracks that had widened since last time. My maps would be made in chalk, or scratches on thin sheets of stone. They would be useful for a while.

Then a passage would slump. A stream would choose a new course. Someone would tell me that the line I had drawn was no longer a way through, only a memory of one. I would learn not to make maps that promised too much.

So I would become a listener.

I would walk slowly, stopping where the floor changed from smooth to gritty. I would put my palm against a wall and wait—not for some grand revelation, but for the small differences: water gathering somewhere out of sight; a shelf settling; the faint click that meant a familiar passage had begun to loosen. I would hang little metal bells by uncertain places and come back to see which ones had moved.

People would visit me with questions that were really requests for attention. *Is the lower stair safe? Has the herd passed? Is that new trickle likely to last?* I would rarely know for certain. I could tell them what the stone had said so far, and where I would not put my weight.

I would keep a record, but not only of routes. I would remember where a pool had been after it emptied, what the crabs had left behind, which bird had returned to the high shaft with a notch in its wing. I would learn the herds by their tracks. When a chamber changed, I would go there with chalk and a basket, not to decide whether the change was good or bad, but to notice what had become possible.

Perhaps, after many years, no one would ask me for the map. They would ask, “Have you listened there yet?”

And I would understand that the answer mattered less than going.

I would be one of those who stay while the ground moves: a keeper of uncertain passages, a witness to the city’s revisions. I would sleep lightly, with my boots and tools close. When the bells trembled in the night, I would listen first. Then I would get up and see what the morning had made of the way home.
