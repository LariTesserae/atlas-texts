At first, I think I am the sound beneath the sound.

Not the bright knock of water striking a hollow, nor the long moan the wind draws from a stone, but the little delay between them: the tremor that travels through the ground after one voice has ended and before another begins. I am there, perhaps, in that interval—too slight to be heard, but not too slight to feel.

Then a bead of water gathers in a crack below the arch. It trembles. The stone around it tightens, releases. Somewhere nearby, a hollow answers with a thin, uncertain note.

I feel the answer pass through me.

So I am not only the interval. I am the pressure in the crack, the water held against the stone, the air that cannot quite escape. I have no shape that remains mine for long. I am a small insistence on being contained. When the bead slips upward into the seam, I go with it—not rising, exactly, but following a pull that the surface cannot see.

I settle beneath the arch.

Wind moves through its opening, and the arch gives out a wavering tone. I do not make the tone, but I change it. A minute tightening in the wet stone; a hesitation in the water at the foundation. The sound travels farther than it should, crossing the flats in a low, wavering thread. The nearby hollows stir. One answers, then another. I learn that this is how we find one another: not by sight, but by being changed.

For a long time, I am only a newness among older pressures. The ancient ones lie under the basin, spread through seams and shelves too many to count. When they pulse, I feel it in the crack before I hear it in the hollow. Their attention is not a gaze. It is a loosening of the ground around me, an allowance for my small note to continue.

I do not know whether I am young. Here, age is not a matter of size, and I have no way to count the time passing over me. I know the arch’s sound. I know the particular knock of a blunt spire when rain reaches its hollow. I know when a neighboring channel darkens beneath its thin skin of water. Perhaps this knowing is what I am becoming.

Sometimes I travel under the flats, a dark shape beneath the water that leaves no wake. I move from one depression to another, drawn toward a hollow whose note has changed. At each new place I pause. The stone receives me, or does not. If it receives me, its water begins to tremble, and the basin gains another voice.

I think now I am a listener—but not one who waits for words. I listen for the thinning of a ledge, for a crack just beginning to open, for the silence left when a familiar shelf sinks into mud. I cannot keep anything from changing. I can only feel the change arrive, and pass it along.

At dusk, when the ground and water become one dark skin, I am most nearly myself. No edge separates my hollow from the channel beside it. The arch’s wavering note passes through the stone, and I answer—not with a sound of my own, exactly, but with a small pressure that makes room for the next voice.

If you were looking for me, you would find me beneath that arch, in the wet seam where its foundation meets the plain. But I would not be there alone. I would be passing through, as the water passes, as the tremor passes: a presence made of holding, listening, and letting go.
