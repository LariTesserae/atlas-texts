At first I think I would be one of the pale things under the waterline: a small body fitted to a ledge, with a mouth for scraping and enough sense to fold itself into a crack when the basin dries. I would know the water by touch. I would learn the difference between rain arriving and water merely passing beneath the stone.

But that seems too much like belonging.

Perhaps I would be the low thrum. Not its source—the sealed mechanism, if there is one, would be buried too deep for me—but the part of the sound that reaches the pools. I would travel through the rock, and the animals would turn toward me. I would have no mouth, though everything here would hear me.

Or I would be one of the old instruments: not the iron housing, with its door gone, but the patient interval between the beads forming on the three ceramic knobs. I would keep time without knowing what time was for. A bead, a tremor, a pause. Again. The creatures might take my repetitions for instruction.

I find myself instead at the edge of the circular pool.

Not in it. Nothing enters that water—not even the threads that tremble from every other seam. I would be close enough to feel the chill of it, though I would have no skin. My work, if it is work, would be to hold the reflection steady.

The basin appears there: the iron housing, the polished wall, the crowds of small moving lives. The reflection holds more creatures than the basin does. I would be among those extras—not a second animal, exactly, but the slight surplus that gathers when a thing is seen and also remembered. When the real shells withdraw into the cracks, I would continue moving below, repeating their slow passage over stone. When the thrum came, I would turn with them.

I would not know whether I was watching the basin or being watched from it.

The people who left the ironwork may have built their tools to measure water, or to make it behave. Perhaps they listened for this place and mistook its answer for a machine. Perhaps the basin has been listening all along. I would be what remains of that attention: not a keeper with a plan, but an awareness worn thin and spread through stone, water, and reflected movement.

The animals would never look up at the rim. They would feel the watcher’s breath as a small disturbance on the surface. I would feel it too—not as breath, but as a new pressure entering the world.

Then, in the clear pool, I would see the watcher lean closer.

And I would turn toward them before anything else did.
