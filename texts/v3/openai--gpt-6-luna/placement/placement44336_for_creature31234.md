At first you might take me for a damp stone under the low wall. I stay still long enough for moss to settle its green over me, and my shell is the color of the pale rock when the water has dried.

Then a drop falls into the basin. I lift one claw.

I am a small crab, living in a crack just wide enough to turn around in. The crack stays cool when the ledges grow warm, and keeps a little water after the basin has thinned to a white rim. I know the hollow not by sight but by touch: the grit of a beetle’s passage, the soft give of a root, the shiver in the stone when rain begins above us.

When the pools spread, I come out to sift the new leaves. When they shrink, I go back into my narrow dark and wait. The lizards sometimes lie nearby, almost indistinguishable from the ledge. I keep my claws close until they move on. Nothing here is mine for long—not the wet stone, not the food, not even the shelter. The hollow gives each thing its turn.

The steps are easier to climb than the raw rock. I cross them at night, though there is no one to see me using what people made. Roots have found the gaps between the blocks. Moss has softened the edges. I pass beneath them, carrying a scrap into the dark where it will become soil, or food, or something too small to name.

I would be found at the edge of the basin, where water gathers and disappears. If you came in quietly, you might see me pause beneath the old pillar, feel the air with my antennae, and slip between two stones.

Then the hollow would seem still again. But I would be there, listening for the next drop.
