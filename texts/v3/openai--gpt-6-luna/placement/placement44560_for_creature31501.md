I would be high on the trunk of a forest near the shelf’s rim.

Not at the edge itself. The abyss is too large to listen to all at once, and its silences arrive with no shape I can understand. I would live where the stone still holds the current steady: one of the flattened ones, spread against the living stalk, my branching limbs laid along its ribs. The stalk would be warm only by comparison with the water. Its pulse would pass through me day and night, though here neither day nor night would mean much.

At first, I would think the pulse was the world.

I would learn its small differences. The deep draw of the bladders filling. The shiver of fronds turning their faces into the flow. Seed cases knocking open somewhere below me. The fine chatter of grazers crossing the canopy. I would learn how a passing feeder presses the water aside, and how a predator makes the forest’s music go briefly wrong.

My body would be made to notice. Along my flanks, the sensitive pits would receive the stalk’s tremors and the water’s pressure. I would not see far. I would not need to. Sight would give me provinces: light above, dark beneath, the occasional glassy shape passing between fronds. Touch would give me the whole local world, arriving as a pattern too broad and subtle to call a message until I had lived here long enough.

And I would live here long enough.

The forest would wear pale scars into me. A frond would brush the same place on my flank for years, then bend away when it broke. I would feel the vacancy before I understood it. For a while the current would move strangely there. Then a new frond would take its place, and the pulse would settle into the old rhythm. I might remember the difference. I would not know whether anything else did.

When I was young, I would have drifted without knowing I was searching. I would have been carried over bare stone, over meadows where the transparent grazers moved like thoughts too slow to finish, past other forests whose chords were unfamiliar. I would have passed through water so open that there was nothing to brace against. Then a vacant place in this trunk would have caught my limbs, and I would have stopped.

Or I would call it stopping.

The first time a signal travelled through the stalk from somewhere beyond my reach, I would answer with a few clicks. Not because I had something to say, exactly, but because the vibration had made room for an answer. Another listener, perhaps far across the stand, would return a low knock. The forest would hold the exchange and pass it on, altered by roots and joints and the bodies of others.

In time, I would know this patch of the living world—not as territory, but as familiarity. Here the stalk pulls harder before a seed case opens. There, the current carries the pale grazers in a narrow stream. Beyond the western meadow, a long shadow sometimes crosses the water, and every animal beneath the canopy becomes still. I would know the usual so well that the unusual could find me.

I would not be the keeper of the forest. Nothing here belongs to me. I would be one of the small, enduring places where its movement becomes felt. A body held against a body, listening to the enormous machinery of life turn without hurry.

Sometimes, a signal would arrive from the rim: a pressure with no local cause, a faint, slow disturbance from the water beyond the shelf. I would send it inward. Others would receive it, pass it on, and the forest would know—not that the abyss was near, exactly, but that the water there was different.

Then the current would resume its familiar hold. The fronds would bend. The seed cases would knock. The grazers would cross the meadow in their unhurried files.

I would remain where the trunk divides the flow, a listener grown nearly into the thing I listen to, sounding an occasional small note into the chord.
