At first I think I would be a thread of water: not enough to pour, just enough to darken a seam in the uphill wall. I would appear before anyone else noticed the rain had passed, and travel by small decisions—down a groove, under a stair, into the cool mouth of a cistern.

But the thread is not mine to keep. Someone comes with a cup, then a tool. Someone else holds the lantern. The water goes where the town has made room for it.

So perhaps I would be the person who comes to look.

I would live near the uphill side, in a narrow house with a court open to the sky. My work would be to notice what has changed: a wall darkening where it was dry, a channel running a little slower, a roof tile sitting proud of its neighbors. I would know the ordinary sounds of the place—the tap of a mallet, the creak of a handcart, the step of the neighbor who favors one knee—and hear when one of them was missing.

No one would call this special. If I found a leak, I would fetch a jar; if a roof needed patching, I would climb up with stone and mortar. People would tell me things while we worked, not because I was wise but because we had time for the same task. I would know which child could be trusted to clear a channel and which old person needed their water carried up the steps. I might grumble about both.

There would be a small room in my house where the tools are kept, their handles darkened by use. In the morning I would check the cistern seals. Later, I would sit on a sunlit stair and mend a strap, listening to footsteps pass above and below me. When cloud settled against the edge of the shelf, I would go to the outer lanes and make sure the drains were clear. I would not think of this as guarding the town. The town would simply be the place whose small changes I knew.

Perhaps I would have left once. I would have seen a broader river, trees tall enough to hide a roof, rain that came down all at once. I would return carrying some unnecessary object from the lower valley, and people would let me sit before asking about it. After a while, while we mended something, I would tell them what the rain sounded like there. They would listen, then ask whether the bridge had held.

In the end I think I would be neither the water nor the wall, but the attention that passes between them: a person with a cup in one hand and a tool in the other, stopping at the same corner each day to see whether the stone has begun to darken. Someone easily overlooked from the ridge. Someone the town would miss if, one morning, nobody came to look.
