I think I would begin as a difference too small to be called a creature.

A darker thread in the amber light. A bead of water that gathers where the roof has never quite decided whether to be stone or glass. A place among the leaves where, for no reason anyone can name, the warmth feels slightly different.

Then I would find I had hands.

I would be one of the pale inhabitants, though not quite like the others. Filaments would veil my back and drink the hanging damp. My feet would rest softly on the grit. Amber lines would branch under my skin, carrying the light inward—not as food, exactly, but as something to keep. I would lie for a long time against a warm shelf of stone, with leaves growing close around me, and know the basin as a body knows its own warmth: without needing to look.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires some belief that company ought to be different. The others would be near, sometimes touching my shoulder with a fan of fingers, sometimes turning a leaf toward me. I would answer with a small deepening of color at my throat. This would be enough.

For a while, I might believe I belonged to the grove. That the leaves were not around me but part of me, and that my stillness was simply the way this place lived.

But there would be a certain patch of light I could not quite settle into.

Not brighter than the others. Not warmer. Only a little farther from where I had always been.

I would turn toward it.

Perhaps the turning would take a very long time. Perhaps it would be only a movement of the head, hardly more than a thought passing through the amber lines beneath my skin. The leaves would remain unbent. The others would not hurry to follow. Nothing would announce that a boundary had been crossed.

Then I would lift myself from the leaves.

This is what I would be: not the first of my kind, perhaps, but the first among them to become aware of distance. The one who gives the basin a *there*, and therefore a *here*. Before me, the hollows and groves would belong to one another without needing to be reached. After me, one place could be left for another.

I would take the step carefully.

The floor would not break. The plants would not cry out. But the fine grit would shift beneath my soft foot, and in the damp margin of a hollow I might leave a faint mark. Behind me, a leaf would settle slowly into the space I had vacated. Somewhere nearby, another being would turn toward the soundless change.

I would look back—not because I feared what lay ahead, but because for the first time there would be a behind.

And then I would go on, carrying the smallest possible unrest in my body: the knowledge that the world can be otherwise than it is. I would learn the basin one careful distance at a time, and the basin, which had held everything in a perfect present, would begin to have a path.

I think I would be called nothing at first.

Later, perhaps, the others would know me by the places where the leaves had parted. Or by the little impressions left in the soft earth. Or by the way, when I lifted my hand toward them, they began to look beyond the sheltering grove.

I would live beneath the pale stone roof, in the green-lit country where water hangs before it falls.

I would be the one who goes.
