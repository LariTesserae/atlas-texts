I would not begin by calling myself an animal.

At first I would think of myself as a place: a shallow hollow between two slate shelves, where the wind loses its certainty and the silver fronds grow thick. Dust gathers there, lifts, gathers again. Copper threads stitch the plants to the stone. Small feet pass through, then pass through once more along the same nearly invisible route.

I would be somewhere just beyond that route, close enough to feel it tremble.

Perhaps I am one of the large ledge-dwellers. My body lies folded against the rock, its plates dulled by mineral dust. I do not need to chase the chimes or the grazers. I wait for the hollow to change. The wind presses through the stone beneath me; the pylons disturb it; the fronds report it in their rattling. Then I give one low pulse, and the nearest creatures widen their paths—not fleeing, only making room for what is coming.

But that is not quite it. I am not only the pulse. I am the listening that precedes it.

The black pylons stand at different angles, each keeping its own old attention. When the air around them folds, I feel the fold arrive through the ground: a small wrongness in the pressure, a pause where there should be a vibration. A loose stone rises. Somewhere, a chime clicks at the empty air. I answer, and the sound travels out through my plates, through slate, through the fine copper roots holding the green in place.

The animals know me by the routes they take around my resting place. They do not know me as a guardian. I am not keeping anything safe. I am simply part of the hollow’s way of noticing itself.

At night I become harder to distinguish from the ledge. The bright voices thin out, and the deep ones go farther. Dust crosses my back. The stone roars beneath me, carrying a pressure older than any call I can make. I feel the whole basin as a body too large to move: the rim, the turning tower, the scattered green islands, every small creature adjusting to a gust that has not yet reached it.

And slowly I understand that I am not separate from the place I occupy. My resting hollow is shaped by my weight; my weight is steadied by the roots; the roots hold because the wind keeps combing mineral and charge into them. My body is one of the ways the hollow answers.

So I would be a ledge-creature, yes—but not only that. A living resonator. A slow, dust-backed ear pressed to the mountain.

I would live where the wind changes its mind, and wait there until the change has a voice.
