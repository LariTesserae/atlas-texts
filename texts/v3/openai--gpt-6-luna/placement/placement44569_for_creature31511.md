I would live where the mountain changes its mind.

Not on the open face of a cliff, where the current can seize anything that lets go, and not deep in the basin, where the water grows heavy and the light gives out. I would make my den in a broken pressure station on a narrow saddle between two valleys: one side sheltered, the other a passage for the sea. The station would be little more than a rusted frame grown over with sponge and coral. Its old pipes would carry no water now. But when the tide turned, the whole structure would tremble before the current arrived.

I would be small enough to fit inside the housing of a dead gauge. Soft-bodied, many-armed; a creature that could pour itself through a crack and become, a moment later, part of the rock. I would not have a name for the mountains. I would know them by pressure: the steady weight of a safe hollow, the quickening pull of a channel, the faint shiver that meant a surge was gathering beyond sight. The sea would reach me first as a change in the stone.

At rest, I might look like a fold of old kelp or a patch of pale growth. My skin would borrow the colors around me—moss-dark beneath the forest, sand-gray on the ledge, freckled with sparks when I was startled. I would keep a few things near my den: a crab shell, a smooth pebble, perhaps a bright scrap of cable stripped bare of its living coat. Not treasure, exactly. More like a record of places I had been, though I would understand each one through touch rather than memory’s words.

I would leave the station when the water called food through the pass. One arm on the rock, the others opening and folding against the force, I would travel under the kelp roofs. Shoals would flash above me. A hunter might hold still in the blue, waiting for the school to turn the wrong way. I would know when to flatten myself into a crevice and when to let the current carry me around the next ledge. Sometimes I would meet another of my kind at a rich hunting ground. We might trade a shell, or only pause long enough to read one another’s color. Then the water would divide us again.

I would not think of myself as master of this place. The mountain is too large, and the sea is always revising it. I would be a listener, a den-maker, a brief intelligence tucked into the moving country—one more small life learning the shape of a current before it changes.

At night, the dead gauge would glimmer with the light of the creatures living inside it. I would rest there with my arms folded close, feeling the station knock softly against the mountain. Beyond it, the deep travelers would call across the valleys, and the stone would answer in tremors.

Then the pitch of the water would shift.

I would wake, loosen myself from the dark, and go find out what had changed.
