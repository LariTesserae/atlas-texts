I would be found beneath the ceiling, where a drop gathers and does not fall.

At first I would look like water: a clear bead holding the dim chamber upside down. But I would not be only the bead. I would be the faint living film that gathers around it, a translucent thread laid along the concrete, branching into the roots and down toward the little pools. When the water moves, I move. When it dries, I become almost nothing—a shine in a crack, a softness on stone.

The mites cross me without knowing. The roots take hold where I keep the clay damp. I feel the beetles’ small shudders when the motor turns, and the shrimp stirring in the cups below. I have no eyes, but I know the pools by what they show: sky, grasses, a brightness that comes and goes with the machine’s hidden pulse.

Sometimes I hold a drop until it grows heavy. Sometimes it vanishes against the wall, and I go with it into a crack, where the whole seam darkens around me. Then rain enters, and I spread again—not far, never far. A glimmer under the concrete. A slick path around a grain of sand. A little more water saved for whoever arrives next.

I think I would be the pause between gathering and falling: not the flood, not the stone, but the damp connection by which this crowded place keeps finding itself.
