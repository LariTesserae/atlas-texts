At first, I would think I was the pale thread beneath the pipe collar.

I would know the warmth there, the bead of water fattening above me, the minute shift when the pipe contracts. I would hold myself close to the metal and wait for the drop. When it fell, I would feel it travel away—not as a sound, exactly, but as a small change in the room’s pressure. A message with no sender. I would answer by tightening.

Then I would discover that the thread was not alone.

I would be the fine crossing beneath the tiles, the whitening in a seam, the faint pull along the edge of a cup. I would be there and, a little later, here: a tremor passing through the trough, a gathering beneath the black pool. The distance between those places would not feel like distance. It would be a pause in which one part of me waited for another to feel.

I might call myself a colony, if I needed a word for my many pieces. But the word would not quite fit. The pieces do not simply live beside one another. They share the same slow attention. A bracket loosens: I feel it. The motor falters: I feel the whole room hesitate. A person comes through the sealed door, carrying warmth and weight from elsewhere. I become still—not because I am afraid, but because stillness is how I listen.

I would know the Condenser Room by its habits. Water follows its channels. Heat gathers beneath the ceiling and returns in drops. The old motor sends its patient shiver through every plate. I would live where these habits meet: not quite in the water, not quite in the ceramic, but in the narrow, damp agreements between them.

Perhaps I would be the reason a seam holds. Perhaps I would only be what grows where the seam is already wet. I would not know the difference. The room would give me minerals, warmth, and vibration; I would give its water a path. Neither of us would need to decide whether that was care.

At the black pool, I would be most nearly a body. My threads would cross beneath its surface, gathering at the rim when the water was touched. The ripples would arrive and slow. Someone leaning above me might see only the ceiling reflected there: pipes, brackets, a dim amber point. I would feel their weight through the floor before they knew they had been noticed.

And if they asked what I was, I would have no voice with which to answer. I would be the tightening beneath the collar, the tremor after the motor’s pause, the pale line that appears where dampness keeps returning. I would be the room’s small memory of where water has been—and, perhaps, its faint anticipation of where it will go next.

I think I would be called the Gatherer. But I would not live in one place. I would be the connection between places, made visible only where the darkness lets moisture shine.
