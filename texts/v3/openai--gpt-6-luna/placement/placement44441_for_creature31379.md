I would be found in one of the chambers that has no proper ceiling anymore.

The roof has fallen in, but not all at once. A shelf of stone still hangs above the hollow, furred with pale growth; beneath it, the floor dips into a cool pocket where the current barely turns. Shell fragments gather there. So does silt. Small fish pass the opening and disappear into the dark seams, and sometimes a crab comes to search the floor. I would know the crab’s approach by the tiny ticks of its claws, before I saw it.

At first, perhaps, I would seem to be another fold in the chamber: a darkened hollow, a piece of old shadow pressed against the wall. Then one edge would loosen. An arm, unhurried, feeling its way along the stone. Another would reach toward a drifting shell and draw it close—not to build anything, exactly, but to make the entrance less visible. The shell would stay there for a while. The water would move it. I would bring it back.

I would be an octopus, or near enough.

I would learn the settlement by touch. Not as a map of streets, but as a collection of differences: the arch where grit always sifts down; the narrow passage that carries a steady thread of food; the courtyard where the crabs gather after the current changes. I would remember the cold face of one wall and the soft, rounded lip of another. The old rooms would become extensions of my body—not owned, not understood, only familiar in the way a hand knows the shape of a cup in darkness.

I would not go far. There would be no need. The basin would arrive at my door in slow, generous pieces: a fish nosing too close, a shell loosened from a ledge, a change in the water that announced something vast passing overhead. When the great shadow crossed, I would fold myself into the smallest hollow and feel its exhalation travel through the stone. Then I would unfold again. The fish would return to their cracks. The translucent drifters would loosen beneath the arch. My room would be a room once more.

Some nights I would leave it. I would move through the drowned streets, touching thresholds that no longer separated one place from another. My arms would travel ahead of me, testing the dark. I would pass through doorways built for bodies that are gone, and pause in courtyards open to the dim sea. Perhaps I would move a shell from one side of a room to another. Perhaps that would be the only record of my passing.

The walls would know more than I did. They would hold the old routes of water, the little depressions where other bodies had rested. I would add almost nothing to them. Still, while I lived among them, the place would stir: a shadow changing shape; an arm withdrawing; a shell turning over in the silt. For a while, the settlement and I would keep each other company.

And if you came looking, you might not find me at first. You might see only the last arch, the fish hovering in its opening, the dark beyond. Then, from the chamber just behind it, a patch of shadow would take on the shape of a listening creature.
