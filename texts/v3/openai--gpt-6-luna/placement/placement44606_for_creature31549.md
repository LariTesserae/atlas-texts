You would find me where a lane is thinking of becoming a crack.

Not in the deepest chamber, where the lamps make every shadow look permanent, nor beneath the broad shaft where everyone gathers to borrow the daylight. I live near a narrow passage that has been closed twice and opened three times. One wall is old stone; the other is a house someone built against it, then partly dismantled when the floor tilted. My doorway has no door. I keep a stool, a water cup, charcoal, and lengths of string there, and move them whenever the ground asks.

I am a listener, though I would not say the stone speaks.

I know how to mark the ends of a crack and come back later to see if they have parted. I know which ceiling ticks are ordinary, which silences deserve a second look. Sometimes I set a cup of water on a shelf and watch its surface. Sometimes I put my palm to the ground—not to learn a secret, but to feel whether the familiar tremor has changed. The basin gives no clear answers. It gives small differences, and small differences are often enough.

At first, I thought of myself as a mapmaker. I carried charcoal through the connected hollows, drawing passages and chambers on scraps of bark, cloth, whatever would take a mark. But every map was already wrong by the time I finished it. A stair ended at a new ledge. A blocked lane opened somewhere else. The children’s names for places lasted longer than my careful lines. So I began mapping something less exact: who knew a route, who had braced a lintel, where water appeared after rain, which household could use help moving before a wall came down.

Now I think I am a keeper of temporary names.

Not the names themselves, exactly. More the pause before a place is named: when it is still only a raw cleft, a difficult crossing, a patch of stone no one trusts. I stay near those beginnings. I help people decide whether to shore a gap, abandon it, or try passing through with a basket of food and a child holding each end of the string. When a way becomes safe enough, I go along on the first walk. We eat at the far end, as the people do, so the passage begins by connecting lives.

I have no fixed house. There is a room I return to, for now, above a cistern with moss on its rim. My tools are packed where I can lift them quickly. I keep a few herbs in pots beneath a shaft of light, though the light shifts when the roof shifts. One of the pots has been carried through three moves. Its leaves are pale and its scent is fierce.

If you asked the people here who I was, some would say I notice cracks. Some would say I am the one to ask about the lower lanes. A child might say I know the way to the crooked tooth, even when it isn’t there anymore.

I would say I belong to the space between making a home and making room. The earth is still opening; the people are still answering. I am neither its warning nor their courage. I am the one who helps them hear the difference—and, when the passage holds, walks through with them.
