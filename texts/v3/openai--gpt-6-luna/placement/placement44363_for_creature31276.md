At first I think I would be one of the people: someone with a length of wire in a coat pocket, taking the long way home because the sluice sounds different after rain. I would know which stones turn slick, which belt complains before it breaks. I would have iron under my nails and a small garden behind the house.

But that doesn’t quite hold. The people leave the sluice and go indoors. I would stay where the water changes its mind.

I think I would live in the hinge: not inside the whole machine, and not quite in the stream, but in the narrow dark where the gate meets its frame. I’d be a small creature made from rust, runoff, and the attention people give a sound when it is almost—but not yet—a problem. Thin as a root. Cold to touch. Able to feel a new stone settle through the metal before anyone sees it.

I wouldn’t control the water. I’d listen to it. When leaves gather in the teeth, I’d feel the flow grow muffled. When the channel clears, I’d feel the sudden bright pull of it. My voice would be a little knock, easy to mistake for the engine house cooling—unless you lived here long enough to know the difference.

The workers might say the sluice needs checking. A child might lean over the rail and hear me answer from below. The frogs would know me as the stillness between two ripples; the rats, perhaps, as something that gives no chase so long as they keep out of the flour. I would have no quarrel with the swallows. They have the eaves; I have the dark under the gate.

At night, when the doors close and the machinery cools, I would go on listening. Not guarding the town, exactly. The town does not need a guardian. Just keeping company with the small changes: a root lifting stone, water finding a new seam, the first note of a belt going wrong.

If someone came out with a lamp, I might make the hinge tremble once beneath their hand.

Not a warning. Just: **yes. I heard it too.**
