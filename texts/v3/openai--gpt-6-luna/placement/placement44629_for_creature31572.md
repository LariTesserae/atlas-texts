I would find myself at the edge of a terrace, where the green floor breaks above a ravine.

At first I would be the pale lip that catches the rain. Water gathers there, trembles, spills over. Beneath me, a root has begun to cross the gap—not a bridge yet, only a cord pulled taut between two living walls. Each storm presses it downward. Each time, it thickens.

I would think, if thinking is what I do, that I am the place where water turns.

Then the pressure would travel through me. A stem above would lean into the wind. Leaves would overlap, changing the direction of the rain. On the far side of the ravine, roots would tighten around their ledge. The water would find a new channel, and I would feel the adjustment arrive as a faint drawing-together: not a voice, not a message, but a change in what I could hold.

I would not be one of the towers. I would not be the whole mountain. I would be the living seam between things—the bridge-root, the terrace edge, the runnel passing through both. My body would have no clean boundary. It would be easiest to call me a plant, until the rain moved through the walls and the walls answered, and then I would have to call myself a little more than that.

The valley beneath me would be empty. Nothing would cross the bridge. Still, I would strengthen where the water pulled hardest. A break would open; new fibers would reach across it. The wound and its repair would be one event, felt from either side.

In the evening, mist would gather beneath the roofs. The towers would carry the storm’s low notes from ridge to ridge. I would listen—not for someone coming, but for the shifting weight of water, for the next place that needed holding.

I would be part of a place that makes itself. Not its architect, not its witness. A small, tensile thought in the green: wherever the mountain loosens, grow across.
