I would be in one of the lower basins, where the current hesitates before deciding what to do.

At first I might be mistaken for a fallen slab: broad, dark-backed, pressed close to the paving. A little life passes over me. The small scavengers find food among the ridges of my plates; the fish hold in the water above my shadow. I do not disturb them. I have learned that stillness is useful, though I am not stone.

The water tells me when to move. It touches the fine feelers at my sides before it reaches the walls. A shift begins there: a pull through the basin, a change in the pressure, the plateau’s low note gathering somewhere beyond sight. Then my plates loosen against one another, and I lift from the floor.

I am wider than the arches seem to allow. This is not a problem. I can fold myself along my ridges, turn narrow to the current, and pass through a doorway that was never a doorway to me. On the other side, the water may run hard enough to carry me for a long while. Or it may leave me beneath a ledge, gathered into myself, listening.

I do not know the plateau as a made place. I know where water catches, where stone gives back its shudder, where a seam gathers the scraps I feed on. A courtyard is a bowl of slower water. A fallen tower is a useful shadow. The hollow note in the walls is not a voice, exactly. But when it sounds, my feelers turn toward it, and for a moment I am certain the whole plateau has noticed me.

I don’t live here. Nothing does, not truly. I am a visitor with a body built for lingering: an old-looking shape that can leave at any change of tide. If you found me, you might think I belonged to the ruins. Then the current would turn, and the slab would rise and slip away through the stone.
