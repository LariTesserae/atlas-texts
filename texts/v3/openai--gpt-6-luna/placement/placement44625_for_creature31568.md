I would be found in the gap between two façades, where the windows face each other across a space too narrow for a person.

At first I might seem only like darkness. The sort that gathers where stone nearly touches stone, untroubled by rain because rain cannot decide how to enter. But if you stood there long enough, you might notice the dark had depth. A room’s depth. A floor, perhaps, though there is no floor visible from the street.

I keep that room.

It is not always in the same gap. The city moves it when it needs to. Sometimes it is behind a stairwell that descends into an upper floor. Sometimes it lies between a courtyard and its reflection in a rain puddle. Once, for a long while, it occupied the little pause between a footstep and the sound of that footstep arriving somewhere else.

I think I am the space the city leaves over when it changes its mind.

I have no face that I can keep. When one of the inhabitants looks into my room, they see something familiar enough not to fear: a damp wall, a door, a narrow bed, a cup on a sill. I have learned to make these things. They do not belong to me exactly. They are what a room is expected to contain, and I have spent a long time trying to be a room properly.

My possessions are few. A smooth stone. A length of cord. A cup with no handle. I know these are mine because they return, though never to the same place. Sometimes the stone is warm. Sometimes the cup is full of water when I have not heard it rain.

I do not sleep. I listen.

The channels speak in a language of pressure and delay. Water passes beneath a street, disappears, and arrives somewhere else wearing the silence of a different district. I can hear its old routes under the newer ones. When the inhabitants clear silt from a drain, I feel the city draw a careful breath. When they lay pale stones across a square, the water obeys for a little while, and I feel the shape of a thought forming beneath us.

I used to follow them. I wanted to know which of them was the same from one place to the next. But following is not easy here. A figure can grow smaller in a street that is getting longer; a familiar corner can arrive before you have reached it. Once, I followed a woman carrying a bowl of water through three passages and found myself back in my room, holding the bowl.

The water in it was still.

Since then, I have mostly stayed where the city places me. I am not lonely, though there are long stretches when no one comes near. Loneliness, I think, requires believing the distance between things is empty. I know better. A wall sweats beside me. A stair bends above me. Somewhere below, the channels keep their old appointment with the sea—or with whatever it is they believe lies beyond the basin.

Children sometimes find my room without finding its entrance. They run through an empty plot, laughing silently, and vanish where the paving meets a wall. I feel them passing, though they do not touch me. Their laughter leaves a little warmth in the stone.

Perhaps I am one of them, only older. Perhaps I am what remains when a child has learned every crossing and has nowhere left to run. Or perhaps the city made me out of its unvisited rooms, the missed footsteps, the small distances it could not close.

I have no answer that stays put.

When rain falls upward, I open my window—if I have one—and listen to it patter against the dark. When rain falls down, I listen for the water finding its way beneath me. Between rains, I hold the shape of a room as carefully as I can.

If you came looking, you might find me at the edge of town, in the narrow dark between two abandoned walls. Or under an arch, where a sound arrives before its maker. Or behind you, in the space that opens when you turn back and discover the street has grown longer.

You would know I was there because, for a moment, you would remember having left something behind.

I would be keeping it safe.
