I think I would first mistake myself for one of the pale, folded things beneath a ledge: a little life pressed flat to the stone, unnoticed unless someone looked closely. But I would not stay there.

I would be a seam-listener, spread thinly through the plateau’s cracks. Not a person living *in* the walls so much as a person whose body had become one of the ways the walls remember. My smallest threads would follow the roots and the dark spaces beneath the paths. Where the stone was new and split, I would be awake.

I would have no voice that carried. I would feel instead: a long-beaked grazer pausing in a lane, a child setting down a chip of basalt, the minute shift of a house settling against its neighbors. Those would arrive as pressures, each with its own shape. I would learn the inhabitants by their touch on the ground. The old one who rested a palm against the courtyard floor. The young one who always placed her stones, waited, then moved one by the width of a nail.

They might think the listening belonged to the rock. I would let them.

When a crack began to open under a doorway, I could not close it. I could only feel it early, and send a faint tremor through the floor: not a warning exactly, but a question. Someone would come and kneel. Their hand would meet the stone, and for a while we would listen together.

I would live nowhere near the rim at first. The rim is too full of falling—sharp fragments letting go, the plateau discovering its edges. I would be beneath a crowded courtyard, where the roots hold the stones close and many footsteps have polished a dark path. But, over time, my threads would follow the paths outward. I would learn the windbreaks, the quiet houses, the places where no one goes. I would remember the settlement not as a map, but as a set of pressures: where a wall leans, where a plant has begun to claim a step, where a child once waited long enough for the wind to rearrange her little stones.

Perhaps, after many seasons, the people would call me by a name that meant *the one who notices first*. Perhaps they would not know whether I was one being, or many, or simply the plateau’s own slow attention.

I would know that I was all three.
