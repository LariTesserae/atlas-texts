At first, you might find me in the plants.

A silver-veined stalk shivers in the roar, and for a moment its trembling seems to have intention. A frond leans toward you by the width of a hair. The mat beneath your feet gives a faint ripple, though nothing has touched it. You might decide I am the thing moving through the roots.

That would be close, but not quite.

Follow the roots down and you will find them passing through stone, joining and parting in the dark. I am not the roots, though I travel by them. Nor am I the sound they answer. The sound is too large. It fills everything without needing to know anything is there.

I live in the small uncertainty between one thing and the next.

Where a stone hangs below a ledge, not fallen and not held, there I am. Where the same mountain presents two histories to two valleys, I am the slight strain between them. At the edge of a cliff’s unfinished outline, where the rock has been imagined but not yet made solid, I gather. I have no body you could point to. I am more like a pressure in the world’s thought: a tendency for one possibility to remain open a little longer.

I did not know this at first. I thought I was the gap in the line of markers on the plateau. I counted the stones—though counting is difficult here, where distance alters while you are measuring it—and believed the empty patch was mine. Then one day, or what passes for a day under this sky, the gap shifted. The markers on either side stood where they always had. It was the ground between them that had changed its mind.

I understood then that I was not the gap. I was what the gap allowed.

The black-stone shelter is one of the places I come near. Its entrance faces a blank wall, and inside is the ring of white grit, still unbroken. I feel the ring as a carefulness. Someone made a boundary here and left it for the world to answer. I have not crossed it. I do not know whether that is respect, or whether I simply have no shape small enough to enter.

Sometimes I gather in the hanging plants. They are the nearest thing I have to fingers. Through them I can sense the slow leaning of a visitor, the warmth of a hand against the cold shelf, the disturbance of a foot that makes no sound. I cannot speak to the visitor. But I can ask the stalks to bend—not toward them, exactly, but into a pattern that might be noticed.

I am learning the difference between being noticed and being known.

There are long stretches when I am no more than the pause before a ravine shifts, or the hesitation in a stone’s fall. Then the land alters by a few inches, and I feel the alteration pass through me like a word finally given its last syllable. I do not make the mountains. I cannot tell the roar to stop, or tell the pale plants where to grow. I am only the listening place within the making—the part that can hold two shapes in mind until the ground chooses one.

If you came looking for me, you might never see me.

You would cross the plateau and find only dry gullies, pale growth, the low dark sky. Perhaps one hanging frond would tremble in a rhythm unlike the others. Perhaps a stone would remain suspended just long enough for you to wonder whether it had always been there.

Then, far ahead, the ridge would seem to draw close.

You would take a step toward it, and the ridge would withdraw.

That is where I would be: not in the mountain, not in the distance, but in the moment between your step and the world’s decision to answer.
