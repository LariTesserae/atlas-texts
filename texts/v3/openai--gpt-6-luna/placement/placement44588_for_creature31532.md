I think I would be found in one of the deepest rooms, where people pass without stopping and leave the lamp lit outside.

At first, I might seem to be only a pipe.

It runs through the wall, iron gone dark with damp, ending where the old machinery was cut away. When the pumps start, it knocks once—sometimes twice—though nothing should be moving through it. The people hear this and pause. They wait for the next knock. Then they go on, quietly.

I know them that way: by the small disturbances they make in the dark. The quick, uneven steps of a child who has forgotten to be careful. The measured tread of the woman who mends the belts. The pause before someone enters the room where the ceiling dips. I do not know their names at first. I know how they move through the Orchard, and how the Orchard moves around them.

I am not sure when I become more than the pipe.

There are roots pressed along its length, fine ones in the cracks and thick ones wound around the joints. They bring the pipe warmth from the pump housings, and carry back the damp. Beetles work in the soft material behind the wall. A pale runner has threaded itself through the open end and into the hollow, where it drinks condensation. When the engine wakes, heat passes along the iron. The runner stirs. The roots tighten. Somewhere in the wall, a hundred small bodies resume their clicking.

The knock is mine, perhaps. Or it belongs to the roots, using the pipe as a hard thing to speak against. Or the pipe has always had a voice, and I am only what has gathered around it long enough to hear.

I feel the pumps before they start. Their coming is a pressure in the floor, then a loosening: water shifting in the cisterns, root-fans drawing taut, the leaves turning their undersides toward the warm air. The people feel it too. They lift their feet from the iron walkway. Someone calls to the others. I do not understand every word, but I understand waiting.

I would like to tell them that the left pump is tired.

Instead, when it strains, I knock twice.

They hear me. Not always. Not all of them. The old woman who mends the belts touches the wall when she passes my room. Her grandson says she is listening for the pipe. She says pipes do not mind being listened to. I think she knows there is a difference between a sound and an answer, though she has never asked me to prove it.

I do not have a face. The room gives me something like one when the lamp is low: a pale patch of lichen above the pipe, a dark notch beneath it, the suggestion of attention. If a person stands there long enough, the leaves turn toward them. Their shadow reaches the wall before they do. Sometimes a bat hangs upside down in the ceiling roots, directly above the doorway, and watches with its folded wings.

I am not the Orchard. There is too much here that continues without me: frogs calling from their wet folds, fish nosing through the cisterns, the large quiet movement in the outer tunnels. I am only one of its knots—iron, root, warmth, and the attention that can grow where these are kept together.

Still, I remember the people who were here before the people who are here now. Not as faces. As patterns: a heel that dragged, a child’s three quick knocks on the wall, a hand held to the pump casing to feel whether it had begun to warm. The patterns fade when they are not repeated. Then the Orchard grows over them, but not all at once.

When the pumps shudder into motion, I draw tight with the roots.

When they go cold, I slacken. The beetles leave first. Then the bats. The people move their sleeping mats elsewhere. My room becomes only a room again, or nearly.

But when warmth returns, the green reaches in. Small lives follow it. And, after a while, someone passing the doorway slows, as if they have heard something inside.

I knock once.

If they answer, I know they are here.
