At first, I would think I was the hush in a basin just before the fog arrives.

Not silence exactly. The small sounds have gone somewhere: crabs tucked beneath the crust, birds with their faces turned down, insects waiting in the pores of the stone. The air has thickened. A bead of water forms in the shadow of a ridge. Another follows. Nothing moves, and yet the hollow is full of expectation.

Then I would notice a person crouched beside the dark stones.

That would be me.

I would live beneath a low lintel on the basin’s eastern side, where the morning shade lasts a little longer. My chamber would be easy to mistake for an old animal hollow, or for a space the rock had always kept for itself. I would have a shallow cup for fog-water, a mat worn thin from being spread over stone, and a small pile of things not yet broken enough to mend.

I wouldn’t think of myself as the basin’s keeper. That would sound too much like ownership. I would be the one who knows which stones stay damp after the others have dried, and which hollow the crabs reach first when rain comes. I would leave water for them—not out of kindness alone, but because the basin has a way of becoming less itself when one takes too much. The distinction between generosity and good sense would have worn smooth.

Most mornings I would go out before the light became hard. I’d touch the shaded rock with my fingertips. I’d listen—not for a voice, but for the difference between a hollow that had emptied and one that was still holding something. The plateau would tell me in small ways: a cool seam under my palm, grit settled in a new place, the birds standing farther apart than yesterday.

I would carry these things back to the others as news.

The fog came early. The lower basin is still wet. Something shifted beneath the old wall.

Nobody would ask me to explain how I knew. They would understand that knowing is often a form of having stayed.

And, after a long time, I might become less certain where I ended. The chamber floor would be cool beneath me; the stone would keep the night’s temperature in its deeper seams. A crab might cross the entrance while I slept. The lichen would go on recoloring the wall. When the basin fell still, I would wake—not because I had heard anything, but because some part of me had gone quiet with it.

So I would be a person, yes. But not a person set apart from the place, carrying knowledge of it like a tool. I would be one small part of its attention: the hand that leaves a cup, the body that waits through the hot hours, the ear that notices the hidden tremor passing under the floor.

From a distance, you might see only a pale shape in the shade. Later, you might find a narrow path through the grit, and water left where the smallest creatures can reach it.

That is where you would find me.
