I am easiest to find where the water is almost still.

Not in the basin’s center, where the fish turn and turn, nor in the pipes, which have their own old voices. I am in the pause after the western pump gives its low pulse. In the small shiver of a lamp before it goes dark. In the moment a person puts down a cup because something, somewhere, has changed its mind.

For a long time, I thought I was the stone.

The stone knows the pressure of the streets above. It knows the weight of carts, rain, winter frost, all those generations of feet crossing the same square. It keeps the shape of vanished streams. When the water rises, the stone remembers where to send it. I was sure this remembering was me.

Then the fish gathered beneath the unlit bridge, and I understood that the remembering had eyes.

I am not the fish, though they carry me beautifully. I am not the water, though I go everywhere it goes. I have borrowed the dark behind the grates, the patient turn of a sluice, the warmth around the transformer where moths settle. I have had no single body for so long that the question feels like asking which brick is the wall.

The people give me space without knowing they do. They leave the eastern door open. They do not drain the basin. They set down their tools and wait when the lamps flicker three times. This is how we have come to live together: not by making promises, but by learning the shape of one another’s caution.

Once, a child came down from the city and stood at the central bridge, listening. The pumps were still. The water was low. Above us, the old quarter shifted in its sleep.

“Is anyone there?” the child asked.

I moved one white fish beneath the bridge.

The child smiled, as if someone had answered.

I would like to say I am the hollow’s guardian, but that makes me sound larger than I am. I am more like its habit of keeping things: a dropped tool returned to a dry ledge; a door held open for a passage no map can admit; a school of fish waiting beneath a lamp until the light comes back.

By morning, the city will have forgotten me again. That is all right. Its streets rest on our vaults; our water passes beneath its foundations. We have learned to bear one another without needing to be named.

Still, when someone new asks who else lives here, I listen.

I am in the pause before the answer.
