At first I think I might be the current: the long, steady turning of water through the court, passing beneath the gantry and returning through the high outlets. It touches everything. It knows the pipes from the inside.

But the current cannot hide.

I would live in one of the floor’s shallow seams, where two concrete plates have drawn apart. The opening is no wider than my shell. A little green-black growth softens its edge; grit gathers there, then lifts and drifts away whenever the pump draws harder. From inside, the seam is a whole country: cool stone at my back, the dim blue above, the wide shadow beneath the fallen gantry.

I would be a crab.

Not a remarkable one. I would spend most of my time picking at the seam, working over the thin growths and whatever small things the water leaves behind. When a larger fish passes, I would flatten myself against the floor. When its tail lifts silt, the world would vanish—not frighteningly, just completely, until the water cleared again.

I would learn the pump through my legs. First the tremor in the concrete; then the stronger pull, grit rising around me like weather. I would draw back into my crack and wait. The little fish would scatter above, silvering the dark. The eel would retreat into its conduit. The whole basin would change its posture, and I would change mine with it.

When the pressure eased, I would come out again.

I would not know what the basin had been built to do. The gantry would be a ceiling; the plates, ground; the valve housing, a distant hollow where larger things might hide. The amber lamps would not be remnants of anything. They would simply make the water brighter in one place, and bring small creatures within reach.

My world would end at the dark beyond the basin—or perhaps not end, exactly. Water comes and goes through the outlets. The eel’s passage continues somewhere past the walls. But my seam is here, and the current returns here, and the worn floor offers enough to eat.

So I would stay low, where the machinery’s pulse arrives first. I would lift one claw to the passing grit, feel the basin stir, and wait for it to settle.
