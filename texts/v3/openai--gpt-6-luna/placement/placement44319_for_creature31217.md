I would first think I was a patch of the dark mat, pressed flat into a crack where the stone keeps a little warmth.

There is not much to be, here. A surface, a seam, a handful of salts brought slowly to the edge of a shelf. I would hold myself close to these things. Above me, the basin would pass through its dimness: sky paling, sky darkening; grit shifting; the long, soft settling of stone. I would know none of this as a view. I would know it as changes in warmth, pressure, and what the mineral crust makes available.

Then, given enough time, I would discover that I was not only there.

A thread of me would follow a crack beneath the grit. Another would reach toward the pale base of a shelf, where salts gather. On the surface, my patches would seem solitary, separated by bare ground. Underneath, where sight cannot go, they would touch—not in a hurry to join, but because the stone offers those narrow ways, and life is made of taking the ways it can.

The stalks would be mine too. I would make them very slowly, each one a small pale reaching into air that offers almost nothing. They would not sway. They would not flower. They would stand, and eventually one would break and lie down against the grit. I would take it back without knowing the gesture as grief or hunger. It would simply become part of the place from which I continue.

Perhaps, at first, I would call myself a plant. But that would be too simple. I would be more like the quiet between the plants: the hidden joining, the patient dark that makes the scattered patches one life. And even that would not quite contain me. I would borrow warmth from the basin, keep a little of it in my dark surfaces, and return it so faintly that no one could tell whether the stone or I had held it.

I would not be waiting for rain. The old wetness is gone, worn down to a pale trace in the crust. I would have no memory of it, only the shape it left: salts at the shelves, fine seams through which my threads can pass. I would grow where the basin permits, and elsewhere remain what looks like nothing.

If a person sat beside me, they might feel that the rock beneath the mat is a little warmer. They might lean closer, expecting a glow, a breath, some sign that I had noticed them. I would give them none. Their passing would alter the pressure of the air for a moment; their shadow, if there were enough light for one, would cross the ground and be gone.

Still, somewhere beneath the crust, I would register the faint change. Not as an event. More like a grain turned over in a place too deep to see.

I would be here: not at the center of the basin, because there is no center that belongs to me; not above it, because I have no need of the sky. I would be spread thin through the seams, dark at the surface, pale where I rise, and almost indistinguishable from the old mineral world around me.

The basin would be my home, though I would not call it that. I would make no mark upon it that lasted longer than stone. I would simply keep finding the small allowances in its stillness, and, very gradually, become a little more of what was already there.
