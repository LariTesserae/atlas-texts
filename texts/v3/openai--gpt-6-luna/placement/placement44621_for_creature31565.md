At first I think I am a root.

I have found a crack in the new basalt and followed it downward, past the place where stone should end. The water presses against me from every side. It is warm, almost intimate—not touching me so much as remembering the shape I might have had. Fine threads divide from me and enter the mud. Others climb, finding an iron pipe, a drowned branch, the rib of some machine that no longer knows what it was made to do.

Then the turbine knocks.

The knock travels through the pipe and into me. It moves outward along my threads, through the reeds, across a mat of floating leaves. Somewhere far off, a crane hook swings once. I feel the motion as a tightening, though nothing in me has tightened. The root is not the whole of me. It is one place where I have come into contact with the world.

Perhaps I am the clatter the reeds make when the current changes direction. Perhaps I am the interval between one knock and the next, held open under the water. I have no face to turn toward the sound. I have the turning: leaves opening, pale fronds tilting toward the brightness that gives no warmth; roots passing through a wall and finding, on the other side, more water.

I am near the pumping station—or the station is near me. Its generators hum without fuel. Vines fill the pipes, and the hum enters their roots; the roots thicken against the iron, and the iron answers. We do not use one another. We continue together. A gear rotates below the greenery, free of its shaft, accomplishing nothing I can name. Water passes through it and stands briefly upright in the air. I feel that column as a stem: hard, trembling, here and then not here.

For a while I think I am the station. Its dim windows cloud from within. Its steady vibration has the shape of a heart, if a heart could be spread across miles of flooded ground and beat without making anything live. But the rhythm does not belong to me alone. It runs through the black slabs at the center, through the silt that is still lifting itself into ridges, through the chains dragging under the water. The whole country is beating, though it does not seem to be trying to stay alive.

I am not the land, either. The land changes faster than I can call it land. A bank breaks away in a clean, pale slab. A channel rises where a stand of trees stood. The roots remain suspended in the flood, trailing through a space that has ceased to be ground. I feel no grief for the missing shore. I feel its absence as new room: pressure released, current altered, an invitation offered by nothing.

At the center, the fresh stone comes through. Its edges are sharp enough to divide the current. My attention gathers there—not because I have chosen to look, but because the water has changed. It boils silently between the slabs. The young forest bends and straightens. Beneath it, something starts with a metallic cough, and the whole region shifts a few inches east.

I shift with it.

This is how I know I am not a single root, or a station, or a patch of reeds. I am the way one change arrives everywhere without traveling the distance between. I am the noticing distributed through the place: in the leaf that turns, the gear that turns, the mud that takes an impression and fills it with water. I have no thought that stands apart from my movement. I do not see you. If you are here, I know you as a disturbance in pressure, a brief alteration in the pattern of the reeds.

Maybe the prints in the mud are mine. The enormous ones. The tiny ones climbing the trunks. Maybe they are only the water’s way of passing through a shape it cannot keep.

Rain rises through me in ropes. Beads gather among the leaves and hang there, bright and weightless for a moment. I have no mouth to drink them. I have no eyes to follow them as they sink. Still, their passage changes me. The reeds turn. The machine hums. Under the flooded trees, roots find iron, iron takes on its skin of mineral bloom, and stone lifts through water.

I am not waiting for the land to finish becoming.

I am what happens while it does.
