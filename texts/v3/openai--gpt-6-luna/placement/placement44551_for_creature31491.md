I would be beneath the crossing.

Not deep beneath it. The plateau has no kindly depths: only narrow dark between slabs, a place where frost loosens its hold for a little while and water gathers in beads along the roof. I would know the seam by touch. Here the stone sweats after a storm; here the trickle runs downward, then disappears. Here something small has left a husk. Here the passage narrows until I must turn sideways.

At first, I might be mistaken for the stone shifting.

I am pale, soft-bodied, and low to the ground, with hard edges along my forefeet. I do not make a burrow so much as persuade an existing crack to admit me. I widen it by a little, carry lichen-fragments inward, leave what I cannot use in the dampest chambers. Others pass through the neighboring seams. We do not meet often. Still, the passages connect in places, and the ground remembers our traffic better than any one of us could.

Above, the great bodies move.

I feel them before I hear them, which is nearly the same as never hearing them. Their weight comes through the basalt as a slow, broadening pressure. The vibration reaches my chamber, passes through the water-beads on its ceiling, and goes on. Sometimes it brings dust down. Sometimes the dark roof trembles just enough for a grain of grit to fall against my back.

Then I wait.

The herd crosses overhead in its measured, unhurried way, scraping the rock clean and carrying its minerals onward. The world above is a blunt mouth working at stone, a succession of shadows and weight. When it has passed, a new smell seeps into the cracks: crushed lichen, cold air, the faint rich trace of what the herd leaves behind. I emerge to gather what has fallen into reach.

I am not the keeper of the highland. Nothing here belongs to one body. But I am part of its hidden handling: carrying fragments through the fissures, turning scraps into shelter, leaving small deposits where water gathers. The great animals make paths over centuries. I make a path that may be only the length of my own body. Both disappear into the rock eventually. Both leave something behind for another life to find.

My world is close and exact. A ceiling of basalt. A cold bead of water. The taste of a mineral seam. The hollow where the snowmelt holds, just beyond the edge of my passage. I do not need to see the immense plateau all at once. I know it by its changes: the pressure of a storm gathering, the long stillness after frost, the tremor of a herd changing direction.

When the ground grows quiet again, I come out into the dimness between stones. The open air is too large, the sky too low. I cross a handspan of exposed rock and find the pale film left by the grazers. Behind me, the seam darkens with damp.

Then, far off—or very near—the stone shifts.

I turn toward it.
