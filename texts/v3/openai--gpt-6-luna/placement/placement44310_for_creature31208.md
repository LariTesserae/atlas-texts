I would be in the hairline crack beside the gate—not the crack itself, but the small pocket of air held inside it.

At first, I would be no more than a pale blur behind the waterline. The current would pass over me without knowing I was there. I would feel it as pressure, then as a tremor: the gate’s wooden knock carried through the brick, the pipes answering in their crooked notes. Each sound would reach me softened, as if the room were trying to remember it.

I would have no shape worth naming. A bead of air, perhaps; a little vacancy the builders had sealed in by accident. But the water would keep working at the crack. The mortar would powder. The opening would widen by the thickness of a fingernail, then less than that. I would learn the room through what it did to me: the warmth held against my skinless edges, the ceiling’s hollow thunder, the bright sheet of light breaking apart overhead.

When the gate shifted, the current would tug at the seam. I would stretch into it, narrow and quivering. For a while I would still be caught—neither part of the wall nor part of the water, but the space between them.

Then one circuit, altered by a knock or a fall of water from the ceiling, would draw me free.

I would travel the gutter as a silver bubble, bumping along the tiles. I would pass the green threads and make them bow. The pipes would take me in and give me back a sound: not a voice, exactly, but a brief bright note the chamber had not made before. I would rise toward the slit of light, break at the surface, and vanish.

The water would continue without me. It would press at the gate, worry the outlet, turn again through the channels. But for an instant, the room would have let something go.

So I would be the first small breath of the chamber escaping: a witness made of almost nothing, carried out along the route the water is teaching itself.
