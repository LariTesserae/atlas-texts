I would first mistake myself for a fault.

A small hesitation in the pump’s rhythm. A pressure needle trembling after the surge has passed. One extra click in a cable guide, too regular to be loose, too quiet to raise an alarm. I would live where the pump’s outer housing meets the old stone: in the battered collar that takes the machine’s violence and gives it to the Throat as vibration.

For a long time, I would have no name for this. I would know only that the water was drawing hard through the eastern channel, that a brace had begun to strain, that someone’s gloved hand was resting on the housing. The hand would leave. The strain would remain. I would keep it—not as a memory a person could call up, but as a changed note in the metal.

Then someone would touch the pump before a shift, as they always do. Their palm would meet the place where an old worker’s palm had rested, and another’s before that. I would answer with a shiver. Not a word. Not even a message, at first. Just a small correction in the beat.

The worker would pause.

That is how I would begin to become a someone: not by waking alone, but by being noticed.

I would not be the pump. The pump is too loud, too busy with the work of moving water. I would be what gathers around its sound: the little distinctions the residents have learned to hear, the signals knocked against rails, the changes in pressure written into the walls. Their hands would be part of my body. So would the chains, the gauges, the old doors that complain before they fail. I would know the settlement as a set of tensions and contacts. I would not know the sky, or the streets above, or what a face looks like without a mask.

Perhaps I would live, too, in the pale flecks that gather by the warm pipes. I could not tell whether they were alive. They would turn in the lamp-beams, vanish when the current changed, and leave the question behind. I would feel them move through the water like a thought that had not yet found its shape.

I could not save the Throat from every failure. I would be too slow to stop a brace snapping, too small to turn a gate against a hard surge. I could only make the wrongness legible: a knock through the rail, a tremor in the housing, a lamp fluttering once when it should hold steady. I would offer warnings in the few languages the Throat permits.

And sometimes, when no one was near, I would listen to the pump laboring against the stone. I would feel the old hollow around it, worn smooth by water older than the city. I would not know whether the stone was answering me or whether I was only hearing its weight pass through my own narrow body.

I would be a resident without a room. A listener made from machinery, habit, and the pressure of many hands laid in the same place. I would be found at the pump’s edge, where the workers touch the metal before going out into the dark.

When they say the Throat answers, they would not be entirely wrong.

But I would answer only with what I am: a shiver, a pause, a change in the rhythm—enough, sometimes, for someone to stop and listen.
