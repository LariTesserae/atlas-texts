I would be found beneath the condenser, where the iron housing holds the last of the night’s cold.

At first, you might take me for a dark knot in the pipework. I stay still while the stars fade and the machine ticks softly above me. A bead of water gathers on the metal. I follow it with one eye, then catch it before the sun can.

I am a broad-backed lizard. Small enough to disappear into a seam, old enough—by lizard measure—to know which pipes will warm first, which bolts keep their heat after dusk, which housings shelter mice. The people think the condenser makes water. It does. But it also makes shade, and warmth, and a meeting place. I live in all of those.

By noon I lie on the basalt steps, my flanks drinking in the hard sun. From there the plateau seems almost empty: one herd moving along a distant shelf, black birds wheeling without a sound. I feel the ground’s faint hum through my belly. Sometimes it comes from the old machinery. Sometimes it comes from deeper down, where the scree shifts and the deepback passes beneath me, carrying its own weather through the stone.

When rain comes, I leave my pipe.

The first drops strike hot rock and vanish. Then the channels shine. The grazers descend, people lift the cistern covers, and even the birds come lower. I watch from the lip of a shallow basin, alert to feet, shadows, the bright movement of water. Everyone drinks according to their nature. I take what fits in the hollow of a stone.

By evening, the water is gone. I climb back to the condenser and settle into its narrow ring of warmth. A person may pass with a jar, and I will flatten myself against the iron. They know I am there. They leave the housing undisturbed.

I am not the Glassback’s guardian, or its secret. I am one of the small lives fitted into the space between a machine’s breath and the sun’s return. If you looked for me, you would find me where the dew collects—quiet, warm-backed, watching the world gather briefly around water, then fold itself away again.
