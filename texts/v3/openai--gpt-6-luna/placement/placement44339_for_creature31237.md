I think I would begin as a person with a ring of keys.

Not an important ring: a few old brass keys, one newer steel one, and a flat piece of metal filed down to fit a gate that no longer has a name. I would live in one of the houses sunk a little below the lane, where the floorboards warm over the pipes. In the morning I’d walk the numbered channels with a wrench in my pocket, looking for the dark line on the brick that means water is rising where it shouldn’t.

People would know me as someone who listens.

Not because I’m especially wise. Because in the Sedgeworks, listening is part of the work. You learn the pumps by their pitch, the press by its pauses, the rain by how it sounds on each roof. You learn that a channel can be in trouble before you see the water: a certain hush beneath the planks, a new silvering of moss, the sparrows suddenly avoiding one brace.

I’d spend a long time believing this was just a knack. Then, one late morning, I’d hear a sound coming from beneath Number Nine—a small, irregular knock, not quite a pump and not quite a root shifting in the old drain. I’d follow it under the house, down a narrow stair, and find a clear pool gathered below the grating. The pale sky would be sitting in it, perfectly still.

I’d kneel there. The knock would come again.

After that, I would listen differently. I’d begin to recognize the district’s many almost-voices: water caught behind a wad of leaves; the press asking to be slowed; a hedge root pressing patiently against a pipe. None of them words. None of them quite separate. I wouldn’t call it magic. The residents would call it knowing your patch. But sometimes, in the hour before dawn, with the pumps murmuring below and the houses dark above, I’d feel that the listening went both ways.

Perhaps, after enough years, I’d become difficult to locate.

Someone would see me on the plankway at dusk, carrying my keys. Someone else would swear they heard my boots in the service passage after I’d gone home. A child would find the loose board at the cold grate already lifted, as though a hand had been there ahead of them. If asked, I’d say I was in the pump room. Or clearing a channel. Or nowhere special.

I would still be a person: hungry, tired, occasionally cross when someone left a sluice open. I’d drink tea from a chipped mug and keep screws in jars. But my sense of home would stretch a little farther than my walls. It would include the routes water takes beneath the lanes, the factory’s steady thud, the old roots threading the drains. It would include the places where a repair is beginning to soften into something else.

So: you’d find me near Number Nine, or wherever the water has started making an unfamiliar sound.

I’d be the keeper of the small passages—not their master, not their spirit. Just someone who knows when to clear a grate, when to brace a leaning wall, and when to wait a moment longer before deciding what needs fixing.
