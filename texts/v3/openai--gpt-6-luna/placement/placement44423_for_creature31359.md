I think I would first be mistaken for a change in the wall.

A bead of water would pause in a crack where it had never paused before. A thread of root would lift, just slightly, as if listening. Somewhere beneath the common ground, a few grains of grit would slide together and make a sound too small to call a voice.

The Iru would hear it.

They would come barefoot, and one of them would lay a broad hand against the stone. I would feel the warmth of that hand through several thin layers of earth. I would try to answer—not in their clicks or whistles, but with the faintest tremor, a little *not yet* passing through the wall.

After a while, they might decide I was a creature. The children would give me a name: **Under-ear**. They would crouch at the cracks and tell me about their day, pressing their lips close to the stone. I would learn the shape of their voices without knowing which sounds meant what. Their laughter would be easy. Their silences would take longer.

I would live where the hollow touches what lies beneath it, in the narrow, unlit places where roots are still searching and water has not yet chosen a path. I would have no single shape. Sometimes I would gather as a soft pale moss along the back of a crack; sometimes as a coolness in the ground, or a stir of dust that crossed a passage with no breeze. I would be less a body than a habit of noticing.

The Iru are good at hearing what is about to happen. I would hear what is beginning to happen farther down: the slow leaning of stone, the first thread of a seep, a root testing its way toward open air. I could not stop the hollow changing. I would not want to. But sometimes I might nudge a pebble from a passage, or let a drop of water fall where an Iru would think to look. A warning, perhaps. Or only company.

At night, when the settlement gathered close, I would follow the warmth of their sleeping places through the floor. I would listen to the soft-footed visitor cross the lanes. I would not know whether it was the same visitor each night. I would know only its careful weight, and the way it paused beside the food they left.

Perhaps, in time, the Iru would understand that I was not guarding the hollow. I was keeping it company while it became something else.

And if you came through the seam, I would not meet you at the entrance. I would be beneath your feet: a faint coolness, a small shift in the earth, the feeling that this place had noticed you and was deciding, quietly, where you might fit.
