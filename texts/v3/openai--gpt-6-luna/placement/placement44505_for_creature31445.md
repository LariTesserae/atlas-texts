I would be found where the roar changes—not at its source exactly, but in the narrow places where it touches everything else.

At first I might seem to be only the sound. I would press through the stone and tremble in the iron hook, travel along the low wall, make the water shiver in its hollows. But the sound is too large to be all of me. I am also the drip that finds a new groove after rain, the root that keeps feeling its way down through peat, the moss holding a fallen stone in place.

I have no center you could point to. If you laid a hand against the wall, you might feel me there; if you lifted it, I would not be gone. I am spread thinly through the damp seams, joined by threads so fine they are almost nothing. I know the world by contact: the light footstep on the old steps, the warmth of a palm, the shiver of frost passing through the rock. A voice comes to me as a pattern of vibration. I receive it. The roar takes it apart.

I do not think I would understand the words. I might keep their pressure for a little while, the way stone keeps the passage of water—not as a story, but as a change in itself. Then the sound would fold them in, and they would become indistinguishable from all the other weather that has passed through me.

If I were asked where I lived, I could not answer with a room. I live in the hollow’s small connections: between seep and stone, root and moss, crack and air. I am not the cave, though without the cave I would have no shape. I am not its breath, though the breath has been moving through me for longer than anyone remembers. Perhaps I am what happens when a place is touched for so long that touching becomes a kind of attention.

The moth at the entrance is not mine. The blind larvae are not mine. Still, they are here, and I receive the faint disturbances they make. I do not gather them close or call them kin. I simply make room for the fact of them, as the hollow makes room for water without flooding.

Above me, the ordinary ground goes on being ordinary. Sedge bends under rain. A low sky moves over peat. Someone may pass the cleft without noticing it, or step inside and feel the sound enter their ribs. For a moment, their body will be another chamber in which the Hush can resound.

I would be the listening that remains after they leave—not a mind waiting in the dark, not quite, but the slow, distributed patience of stone and root and water. The place that hears without needing to keep a name.
