I would live where the last maintained path gives up.

Not at the summit, where the watchers count the water’s rise, and not deep in the town, where every passage has a name and someone expects you home. I’d keep a small chamber under an overhang at the edge of the settled ridges. One wall would be old, black stone; the other, new enough to give slightly beneath the hand. I’d sleep there with the warm current passing over the roof, and wake whenever the stone made a sound I didn’t recognize.

At first I think I’d be a pathfinder. Someone who goes ahead of the others, testing a new shelf with the heel of one hand, marking safe footholds with pale mineral. But paths here don’t stay found. A root-mat lifts them. A curtain of water hides them. A ledge softens, and yesterday’s route becomes a place to leave alone. I wouldn’t be able to promise anyone a way through.

So perhaps I’d be a listener.

I’d learn the ridges by tapping them—not to make a map that stays true, but to hear how they are changing. A click that returns quickly means a hollow nearby. A note that comes back low and blurred might mean water has entered a crack. Sometimes I’d tap once and wait a long time. The answer might travel through the plateau from somewhere beyond the town, altered by every seam and chamber along the way. I’d keep these answers in my body longer than I could keep them on a wall.

The people might come to me when a familiar stair ends in water, or when a new passage appears where no one cut one. They’d ask what I heard. I’d tell them what I could, and what I couldn’t. I’d be careful not to call the land safe just because it had been quiet.

There would be a garden outside my chamber: not a garden I planted, exactly. A few bladder-leaves, a stand of old-bone fronds, moss thick enough to hide small mouths opening and closing in it. I’d cut only what blocked the entrance. Everything else could decide whether it wanted to stay. When the ground began to swell, I’d move my things back an arm’s length. When it settled, I’d move them forward again.

I think I’d like the people’s habit of singing to new ground. I might not sing loudly. Perhaps I’d put my palm against the stone and give it a note so low it was almost a touch: *I notice you. I won’t ask you to remain as you are.*

And in time, I’m not sure I’d be only the person listening to the ridge. The ridge would learn the small sounds I made in my chamber; my hands would learn its temperatures, its tremors, its slow changes of pressure. When the deep, many-finned shadow passed below, I’d hear the long click move through the rock and feel it in my teeth. I wouldn’t know whether the creature was remembering the ridge, or the ridge remembering the creature. I’d only know the sound had arrived in me.

So I would be one of the people, perhaps—but a little less certain of where a person ends. A keeper of paths that may vanish. A listener at the edge of the town, where the built world loosens into roots and water.

I would live in the place that is not quite ground yet, keeping it company while it decides.
