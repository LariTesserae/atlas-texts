I think I would first mistake myself for a seam in the roots.

A narrow dark passage, lined with fibers fine enough to comb the current. Something faintly luminous gathers there: not a light, exactly, but the memory of one. I would know the passage by touch. The close pressure of the walls. The steady tremor of water moving through the plateau. The occasional heavy pulse of a traveler passing somewhere beyond sight.

Then the seam would open, and I would move.

I would be a school of small, translucent fish—not one fish leading the others, not many fish merely keeping together, but a shape made and unmade by turning. We would slip through the root lattice, silvering briefly where the faint light caught us, then vanish into the dark. Each of us would feel only a little: the brush of a stem, the taste of growth on a root, the sudden pressure of a larger body nearby. Together, those little sensations would become enough to find the old paths.

We would feed along the living walls, taking the soft films that gather in their shade. Our mouths would leave clean trails through the darker growth. We would return to them, though none of us would remember the first time. When one of us turned, the others would follow—not obedience, not decision, but a change passing through the school like a ripple through water. We would fold around a pillar, stream through a crack, gather again on the far side.

Sometimes a traveler would pass above the terraces. Its enormous shadow would move over us, and the rootwork would carry its weight as a distant shiver. We would scatter into the seams. Then, when the tremor softened, we would come out again.

I would learn the plateau from within. Not as a map, with places named and separated, but as a set of familiar pressures: this current that carries warmth; this hollow where tiny bodies collect; this arch where the roots hum more loudly; this long, open passage in which we can turn together without touching anything. The plateau would not feel like a world beneath us. It would feel like the conditions of our being—like water, like darkness, like the space between one small body and another.

Perhaps, as the years passed, I would spend less time in the open water. The school would gather closer to the rootwork. We would know the difference between a living tremor and one left behind by the current. We would brush the fibers and feel an answer so faint that no single fish could hold it. Something in the great green body tightening, loosening, turning a frond toward warmth. Not speech. Not thought as I could understand it. A sensation spreading beyond us, older and slower than our lives.

And perhaps we would be part of that sensation—not its eyes, not its messengers, only one of the small moving patterns it permits. A brief brightness passing through its corridors. A little appetite among its roots.

At the highest ridge, where the fronds grow short and the dark opens into a long view, we would gather in a loose cloud. Below us, the terraces would descend one after another, their black trunks fading into the water. We would not go there to look out. We would turn because the current had changed, or because one fish had turned, and the rest had felt it.

I would be no one fish in particular. I would be the turning itself: a silver thought that never needed to know it was a thought, threading the living architecture, feeding, vanishing, returning.

And when the plateau trembled beneath us, we would turn with it.
