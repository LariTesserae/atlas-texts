At first, I would be mistaken for a flaw in the wall.

I would live in a narrow seam below the city’s central spine, where a buried pipe keeps the stone warm. My body would be flat and dark, my plates fitted so closely to the masonry that dust could settle over me without revealing where I ended. I would know the pipe by touch: its steady heat, the slight tremor in its casing, the pulses that passed through it from somewhere below.

For a long time, I would think that this was all I was: a keeper of warmth. I would clear the seam when grit gathered in it. I would test the old joints with my filaments. I would trace the cuts on the metal tags, though some of them would have become only grooves beneath my touch. The work would not feel like a choice. It would feel like the shape of my body.

Then, over many intervals, I would notice one pulse arriving differently.

Not louder. Not weaker. Only a fraction late—so little that another might have taken it for the settling of stone. I would press myself to the pipe and wait for the next. It would come late again. I would record the difference on a tag at the nearest junction, adding one fine cut among hundreds. Then I would return to listen.

Perhaps this is how I would become myself: not by leaving the city, but by learning to distinguish one silence from another.

I would be one of the old ones, though not the oldest. My plates would have darkened; the pale tissue beneath them would show only when I unfolded at a warm threshold. Others would know me by the pauses in my steps. They would leave a route clear for me, even after I had stopped using it. Sometimes one would rest beside my seam and touch its filaments to my back. We would share the warmth of the pipe without needing to name what we were keeping each other company for.

The late pulse would remain. I would follow it through service passages and sealed galleries, laying one foot after another on familiar slabs. At last I would reach a door whose cuts had worn almost smooth. Beyond it, the conduit would continue beneath the plateau. The door would not open for my hands, but the stone beside it would have a hairline seam—old, warm, and just wide enough.

I would pass through.

There would be no revelation beyond it. No hidden sun, no water, no great machine newly waking. Only the deep stone, and the pulse traveling through it from somewhere too far below to reach. But there, away from the city’s pipes, I would feel the delay clearly. The world itself was keeping an interval.

I would return and add a mark to the tag: not a warning, exactly, and not an instruction. A record that the pulse had been heard outside the walls. Perhaps the next one would understand it. Perhaps no one would. I would leave the mark anyway.

So I would be a listener in the warm foundations: a small, jointed creature fitted to the dark, who learned that maintenance could mean more than keeping a system from failing. It could mean attending to what continued without asking to be understood.

And when the vibration passed through the plateau, I would answer with three soft taps against the stone. Not to wake anything. Not to call anything near.

Only to say: I felt it. I am here.
