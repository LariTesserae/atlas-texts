At first I would think I lived beneath one of the pale pillars.

There is a dark seam at its foot, narrow enough to escape notice. It holds a little coolness even at noon. When the rain comes, water finds the seam and travels down into the stone. I go with it—not as a body going somewhere, exactly, but as a thought passed from one patch of damp to another.

I would be easy to mistake for a listener. I have no face to speak of, only a few jointed feelers that rest against the ground. Through them I feel the herd coming long before I hear it: weight arriving in a rhythm, then pausing, then moving on. I know the little creatures by their quick, bright interruptions in the dust. I know the birds by the widening circles their shadows make overhead.

But the listeners come out of their hollows and press their limbs to the rock. I am already there.

I am the pause inside the low vibration, the small interval in which the plateau decides whether to answer. When the ridges hum, I pass along their hidden seams. The old towers receive me without opening. A channel warms. A scatter of stones lifts and settles. Somewhere, under the crust, water changes its course by the width of a root.

I don’t make these things happen. I help them find one another.

After rain, when the ring of pillars holds its impossible sheet of water, I rise to the surface. Not visibly. I am the slight trembling that passes over the reflection when the other sky moves across it. I have never been to that sky. Still, I recognize something in its traveling clouds: the sensation of a place too far away to call from, but not too far away to answer.

The listeners watch the reflection. I watch them watching. For a little while we share the same stillness, though they may think it belongs to the water.

By noon the sheet is gone. I retreat through the stone, divided into damp traces, into cool undersides of ledges, into the dark that lets the grasses survive. I have no shelter of my own, unless the plateau itself counts. I have no name the others use. Perhaps I am not one thing long enough to need one.

At evening, when the herds come down to drink and the thorn trees lay their thin shadows across the basins, I gather again beneath the ground. The note passes underfoot. The animals pause. The listeners turn their heads.

And I travel on, ridge to ridge—not carrying a message, exactly. More like keeping the space open where a message could go.
