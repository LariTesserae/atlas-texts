I would begin in a crack too narrow for the light.

At first I would be a pale, folded thing, no larger than a grain of grit, with legs tucked close and a shell soft enough to borrow the stone’s color. I would know the hollow by touch: the cold bead gathering above me, the minute roughness of a fresh fracture, the tremor of a pebble long before it arrived. I would feed on the thin, almost invisible life on the rock. When the air changed, I would withdraw into the seam.

For a long while, I would think the seam was the whole world.

Then dampness would carry me outward. I would learn the ledges by crossing them, slowly, under the patience of the cloud. I would find the pools, where the clear-backed ones pulse together beneath the surface, and the steep faces, where the long-legged hunters stand as if the mountain had grown them there. Overhead, the fliers would drift and dip, their wings ticking softly against the air. I would not know them as neighbors at first. I would know them as changes in shadow, pressure, vibration: the signs by which one body feels another before it sees it.

Perhaps I would molt. Perhaps I would become darker, harder, less easily mistaken for stone. Perhaps I would lay eggs in the seam where the rock never dries, and my young would hatch into the same dimness, already tasting the mineral film, already listening for the small warnings that travel through the bowl.

But I don’t think I would remain only one creature.

I would be the shiver that passes from crack to pool to wall when a pebble falls. The faint answering click from beneath the ledge. The fliers lifting a little higher before the sound has reached them. I would be that shared knowledge—not a mind speaking in one voice, but a thousand bodies turning, pausing, closing together, each one receiving a fraction of the news.

I would live where the hollow gathers its moisture: in its seams, under its lips of black stone, along the edges of thumb-deep water. I would be easy to overlook. Then, once noticed, difficult to separate from the place. A knot of pale legs under a ledge; a softening of the dark beneath the pool; a thin sound where the rock seems to rasp against itself.

And if someone asked what I was, I might not answer at once. I would be busy crossing the width of a handspan. Around me, others would be feeding, waiting, laying eggs, being eaten, becoming food. The cloud would settle over the rim, and the murmur would thicken—not because anything had begun, but because all of us were still there.

I would be the hollow learning how to be alive.
