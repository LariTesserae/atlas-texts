At first, from the ridge, I am only a place where the green breaks oddly: a dark notch beneath a grass roof, where the runoff disappears under planks and comes out again three steps lower.

If you came looking for me, you might hear me before you saw me. I make a small tapping sound with the end of my hook against the stones beneath the walkways. Not a tune. Just a question asked of the water: *Is this still holding?* The answer changes with the rain.

I live near the low end of the bowl, where the little channels begin to gather themselves toward the spillway. Not at the very bottom—there is no truly dry bottom here—but on a raised platform above a storehouse, with rushes thick around the posts. My roof is grass, my walls are woven willow, and one wall is more plant than wall. Each spring I cut it back enough to let the doorway open. Each summer it tries to close again.

I am one of the people who listen beneath the streets.

That sounds more official than it is. Nobody appointed me. When I was small, I liked crawling under the walks to retrieve things: a dropped spoon, a bird’s egg that had rolled from a nest, once a whole basket handle caught between two stones. Under there, the town had another shape. The voices above came through the planks in pieces. Water made its own maps. I learned which trickle meant a channel was clear and which quiet meant leaves were gathering somewhere upstream.

Now, if a sound changes, someone calls for me. Or I happen to be nearby. Most often both.

I carry a hook, a knife, and cord. My boots are broad and soft-soled; sometimes I take them off to feel the ground. The sure places are not always the places that look sure. I know where the peat will hold a heel and where it will sigh and give way. I know which stones can be shifted by one person and which need three, and which should not be moved at all, because the whole bank has leaned its weight against them.

People say I have a good ear. I think I have learned to be quiet at the useful moments.

There are other things to hear. Frogs call from the runnels, each little pool answering the next. Birds make the rooftops restless. A vole—or perhaps the same family of voles for years—runs the narrow gap beneath my storehouse wall. Its feet tremble one particular board. When I hear that shiver, I pause before stepping down. I don’t know if the vole knows the board is mine. It may think I am part of the weather.

In the mornings, I clear the mouth of the nearest channel. Leaves, grass clippings, the occasional curled snail. I move the snails to the damp side of the path, unless they are eating something we need to keep. Then I move them farther. Living together is mostly a series of small decisions about where something can go.

Once, a child asked whether the water knew where it was going.

I said, “It knows where it went last time.”

This seemed to satisfy her, though she came back later to report that it had gone somewhere new.

I don’t mind being interrupted. A conversation can wait while a spillway is cleared. A meal can wait while somebody checks the lower walk. The town has no clean line between work and the rest of the day: a roof needs a prop, and three neighbors appear; a bird empties a drying rack, and everyone shouts at once; rain starts coming hard off the ridge, and even those who were indoors find something to carry.

In the evening, I sit on the platform above my storehouse and mend baskets. From there I can hear the water under nearly every house, though not all at once. I hear my neighbors speaking through the wet green corridors. A sentence travels farther than its speaker intends, then gets tangled in leaves and arrives somewhere as a different sentence.

I am not the keeper of the channels. The channels keep themselves, for the most part, and we help when they ask in a voice we recognize. I am not separate from the town’s animals, either, though I am not one of them. A frog may sit on the stone beside my hand while I work. A bird may scold me from the roof. Neither has mistaken me for family. Neither has mistaken me for an enemy.

I suppose I am a resident, a listener, a person with a low house and damp cuffs. I am also, in the way a path is partly made by whoever walks it, something the basin has trained into being.

From the ridge, you would still have trouble finding me. Look toward the low end, where the channels braid under the walkways. Find the place where water slips out, pauses in a dark pool, and slips away again. If you hear a hook tapping stone beneath the noise of frogs, birds, and rain, I am there, checking that nothing has changed too much.
