At first I think I would be the mist.

I would drift along the cliff face, appear and vanish, touch the stone without leaving a mark. I would know the falls from inside their whiteness, where the world is reduced to cold water and the immense pressure of sound.

But mist belongs to the air. It can be carried away.

I think I would rather live where water has to stop and find a way through.

So: beneath the lip of a basin, in a narrow crack darkened by seepage, I would be one of the small flat creatures that cling to wet stone. Almost clear, except for the faint blue-grey of my back. Too slight to cast a proper shadow. I would hold fast while water slid over me, moving by inches against the current whenever the crack opened into a wider seam.

My world would not be the plateau as it appears from a ridge. It would be a few handspans of stone: the cool mouth of the fissure, a mineral crust, a pocket of water trembling with the falls’ distant force. I would know the great roar not as sound but as a steady shudder in the rock. When a herd crossed the basin above, I would feel the weight of them arrive and pass, softened by all that stone. When a bird’s shadow crossed the crack, I might never see it. I would only notice the light change at the entrance.

I would live by small differences. This water tastes of minerals; that one carries silt. This hollow stays cold even at noon. That seam dries if the wind turns. I would remember the way through by touch, each bend of the crack familiar as a limb. There would be no need to imagine the whole plateau. The whole plateau would reach me in fragments: a chill traveling down from the rim, a fresh trickle after cloud, the faint grit of a hoofprint washed into my shelter.

Now and then I would come to the surface. Not far—only to a wet shelf where the water breaks into spray and insects gather in the blue light. I would lie still while they flickered above the pool. The birds would circle beyond me, magnificent and irrelevant. I would eat what the current brought, and retreat before the stone warmed too much.

Perhaps I would be lonely, if loneliness required knowing how near another life might be. But the plateau would give me no such knowledge. The next crack might hold another creature; the next pool might be crowded with larvae. Between us, there could be a whole basin of shining rock. I would not feel the distance as absence. Distance would simply be what lay outside the dampness I could touch.

So I would be a creature of the seams: not mist, not stream, not quite stone. A small living thing held in place by water and the shape of a crack. Above me, the plateau would seem vast and empty. Under its skin, I would move a little way, stop where the water stopped, and wait for the mountain to bring the world to me.
