I would be found where the ground has not quite become ground.

Not on the surface, where the leaves receive the mist and return it to the air, nor deep beneath, where the old rootways have already learned the shape of the stone. I would be in the seam between them: a fresh fracture, still cool from opening, while a ridge slowly presses upward from below.

At first I would seem small. A pale thread of growth slipping into the crack. Water would run over me. The rock would give off its faint mineral cold; above, the canopy would rasp and bow. I would have no eyes to look up with, but I would feel the weight of the vegetation overhead, the cloud gathering in its leaves, the fine change in the pressure of the air.

Then the stone would lift.

I would tighten around it—not to stop it, but to keep contact as it rose. A signal would pass through me: pressure, perhaps, or the first hint of a direction. I would answer by sending water into a neighboring stem. That stem would alter the angle of its leaves. Somewhere beyond sight, another root would receive the alteration and pass it on.

For a while, I might think the answer was mine.

I might call myself the thread in the crack, the living thing holding to the new rock. But my reach would widen. What I sensed would arrive already changed by other bodies: the coming mist, the slow heave of a distant slope, a tremor traveling through stone that had not yet broken the surface. I would know these things without seeing them, as a leaf knows the wind not by understanding it but by turning.

Perhaps I would become a root-mass. Perhaps that is the name for the part of me that grows across years, outlasting the stalks that rise from it and the mats that gather above it. I would keep no memories as pictures. I would remember by being altered: a thicker knot where the ground once strained; a channel held open where water had passed; a lean in the growth toward a place where warmth gathers under the rock.

Still, “I” would not quite be a lie.

I would be the listening seam. The little interval where the newly made world touches the life already waiting for it. A living pressure against stone. A response that travels farther than the body that began it.

At night, the pale stems would carry their dim light through me. I would not shine; the light would move through the network, finding no single source, only passage. Above me, leaves would click and slide together. Beneath me, the ridge would continue its slow ascent. The whole high country would murmur through my one narrow fracture, too vast to enter all at once.

And when the new rock finally emerged, wet and bright-edged, I would already be there.

Not covering it yet. Not owning it. Only holding on as it arrived—feeling, through countless lives joined to mine, the first coldness of its exposed face.

That is where I would be.

Not a creature in the country, exactly, but one of the ways the country feels itself becoming.
