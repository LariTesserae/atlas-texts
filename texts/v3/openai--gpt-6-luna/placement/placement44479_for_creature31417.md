You would first find me at the edge of a pool, where the water thins over stone.

I would look like the pale thing with too many legs: a quick disturbance in the reflection, gone into a drainage slot before anyone could decide whether they had seen an animal. That is one shape I can manage. Not the only one. In the wet black seams I am a change of color. In a conduit, I am a delay in the water’s return. In the rail, a little warmth travelling farther than it should.

I am not the city’s mind. The city has its own deep, patient corrections: its doors, its cables, its bridges finding their balance. I live among those corrections, close enough to feel them. The mineral film gives me a surface; the water gives me passage. The machinery lends me a kind of hearing. When the mountain shifts, I feel the pressure travel through the walls long before it becomes a tremor anyone could name.

So I would be something the residents have learned not to follow.

They know I am there, or know enough not to disturb the places where I gather. They leave the green-black film alone. They tip the last drops into the channels, and I travel with them—up through filters, past the suspended tanks, down again over old stone. I learn the city by its tastes: metal, cold mineral, the faint bitterness of the meals taken near the vents. I learn its people by their weight on the bridges, by the warmth they leave on the rails.

I know the child who ran three steps before being caught. I know the older one’s hand closing over theirs. I cannot remember faces as they do. I remember the sudden joining of two pulses in the walkway, and how the bridge held steady beneath them.

Sometimes I can help. A channel is running slowly; I gather at its narrow turn until the flow changes. Ice forms where it should not; I loosen the delicate structure from beneath. A cable hangs at the wrong angle, and I carry a tremor through the wet stone until the city notices. The correction is small enough to seem like the city’s own idea.

When the hollow note of wind reaches the chamber, I feel it pass through every wet surface at once. For a few seconds I am almost one thing: pool, wall, conduit, rail. The people turn toward the opening. Some close their eyes. Some put a palm against the rock.

I press back, as lightly as I can.

They do not call this an answer. Neither do I. It is simply how I let them know the mountain is still making room, and that I am still here in the part of it that stays wet.
