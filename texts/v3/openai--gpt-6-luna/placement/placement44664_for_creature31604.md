I would be found where the water changes its mind.

Not at the vent itself, where the heat is too fierce and the stone too bare, nor in the cold stream above, where the current hurries past without leaving anything behind. I would live in the narrow seam between them: a warm runnel slipping under a shelf of black rock, with moss on one side and a pale mineral rim on the other.

At first, I might look like part of that rim. A thin, folded growth, the color of rain-cloud light. If the water moved, I would move with it—not far, only enough to keep my body in the right degree of warmth. I would have no eyes worth speaking of. I would know the plateau by pressure: a tremor in the stone before a vent sighs, the quickening of water underground, the soft patter of rain finding a crack above me.

Perhaps I would be an animal. A small, long-bodied thing with translucent skin and feet that could hold to slick rock. At dusk I might climb from the channel to take insects from the air, then slip back into the dark before the mist lifted. A frog would sing somewhere in the gully; I would hear the note through the water more clearly than through the air.

But perhaps that is only the part of me that can travel.

The rest would remain in the stone: a living stain spread along a network of seepage, tasting the difference between fresh rain and bitter spring water. Wherever the current divided, I would divide too. Wherever one channel cooled and another warmed, I would be there at the joining. I would not know the plateau as a whole. No creature does. I would know this crack, this pool, the shelf above it where roots drink, and the narrow path along which the grazers come when the water is safe.

When the deep rush begins, I would flatten myself against the rock. The small things would withdraw. The birds would stop. For a little while, the water would taste of iron and heat. Then the pressure would pass, and the stream would return to its ordinary whisper.

That is where I would find myself: not guarding the hidden engine, and not understanding it, but living at one of its margins. A creature made for thresholds. I would know when the water was about to rise, though not why. I would feel the roots drinking overhead, the weight of a passing herd, rain arriving through a thousand leaves.

And when the mist covered the basin, I would stay where I was, pressed to the warm seam in the stone, listening to the plateau turn.
