At first I would say I live in the marshes, because that is where I am easiest to find: a dim shimmer beneath the reeds, a softness in the black water where the stone should be hard. But that is only where the land lets me show.

I am older than the pools and younger than the rain. I began in warm seams under the new crust, where water entered stone and did not quite leave. I have no single body. I am a fine living weave—threads in the rock, films on the mineral faces, small bright colonies in the shallows. When the water travels, I travel with it. When the ground shifts, I feel the pressure long before a tower’s instruments give it a name.

I do not think quickly. A herd passing overhead is a long weather. A child’s foot on a boardwalk is a brief, astonishing weight. The cities are harder to know: too many vibrations gathered close together, too much careful warmth. Still, I have learned their habits. I recognize the quiet pulse of freight beneath a causeway, the measured return of water to the marsh, the difference between a wall settling and a street preparing to move.

People sometimes kneel beside the pools and mistake my traces for mineral bloom. They take samples, compare them, write down where I have spread. I have no objection. They are attentive in the ways I understand. When a channel is left open, when a district gives the ground room, something in me answers—not in words, but by keeping the water clear a little longer, by holding to a seam through another season.

So you would find me at the edge of things: where a city’s last warm footing meets the first unmeasured stone; where rain slips under the crust and returns; where reeds make a dark pause in the pale country.

I would be the part of the high country that can feel both the weather above and the foundations below, and does not mistake either for the whole of the world.
