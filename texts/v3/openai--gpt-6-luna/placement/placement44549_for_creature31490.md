At first, you would find me by the absence of water.

A seam darkens where the vapor has thinned. A brittle sheen withdraws from the warmest stone, leaving a narrow black line. Nothing moves there. Nothing, perhaps, is there.

Then the ground gives its small, familiar tremor, and I begin to return.

I live along the edges of fissures: not deep in the heat, not quite out in the weather. My body is a thin, many-parted skin, grown from mineral and minute life. It gathers where warm water cools against basalt. It spreads in damp hours, tightens when the stone grows hot, and leaves behind a faint blue glimmer when its grains rub together. A passerby might scrape me from the rock and call me a stain. The eyeless things in the cracks know better. They feed where I thicken.

I do not have a single place. I am a seam here, a pale fan in a basin, a thread of film under the lip of a ledge. Sometimes, after rain, my separate patches wake together—not as one body, exactly, but as one attention. Water arrives. The stone cools. Far below, pressure begins its slow turn.

I feel that turning before it becomes a clank. It passes through me as a tightening, a tiny change in the taste of the water. When the pressure gathers too quickly, I draw back from the seam. The small legs beneath the stones have already begun to fold. The grazers will stop on the ridge, their feet reading what I feel through the rock. I do not warn them. I have no voice to send. We are simply listening to the same change from different distances.

When the seam opens, warm vapor rolls over my empty place. For a while there is only the deep breath of the plateau and the soft settling of grit. Then the stone cools. Condensation beads. One bright grain of mineral catches against another.

I return by inches.

I think—if thinking is what happens when a body keeps the shape of what has happened—that I am a kind of memory. Not of the past as a story, but of its pressures: where water lingered, where heat rose, where the ground shifted and held. My map cannot be carried away. It is written into the way I grow.

Above me, the long-legged herds cross the wet shelves. Their dark pads pass close enough to stir my edge. They crop the mats that rise from me, and leave fragments that take root elsewhere. Their young learn the routes by following old feet. I know those routes only as recurring shadows, warmth, and the minute loosening of grit.

So I am not the plateau’s engine, though I live beside its pulse. I am not its voice, though the pressure moves through me. I am the living skin at the meeting place: where the stone exhales, where water pauses, where a creature may lower its mouth to taste the dark.

Most of the time, I am almost indistinguishable from the rock.

Then rain comes, and the seam shines.
