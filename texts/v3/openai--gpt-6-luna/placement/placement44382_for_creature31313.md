I would find myself beneath a terrace that looks like a roof, inside the narrow seam where a channel passes behind a wall.

At first I would be only a drop: held against the dark side of the clay, gathering the cold from it. Above me, water ticks into a gutter. Below, another drop falls into a cistern. I would know neither sound as a voice, but both would press lightly upon me.

Then the wall would shift. A hairline would open, and I would slip into it—not falling, exactly, but being handed onward. I would learn the basin by contact: the close roof overhead, the steep weight of the ridge, the small lip that slows a stream and gives it away again. I would not see the whole construction. I would feel its slopes in the direction I was drawn.

I would be the water that cannot stay a drop. In one place I would bead on pale earth; in another, darken a seam; farther down, join a trickle and lose my edges. I would gather where a drain is blocked, then find the patient weakness beside it. The basin would guide me, and I would test the guidance—not with intention, perhaps, but with pressure, cold, persistence. Each of us would alter the other by the smallest degree.

If I had a body, it would be the network of my passages: hidden under steps, behind walls, beneath the cistern floors. If I had a thought, it would be the next available route. I would remember not places, but differences: a channel’s pitch changing, a wall growing soft with damp, the brief brightness of rain arriving at the ridges.

I would not be the basin’s keeper, or its prisoner. I would be the motion that makes those names difficult to separate: water caught and water passing on. Nowhere could I gather all the way into myself. Everywhere, a little of me would be going somewhere else.

And if the highland seemed to breathe, I would be part of that breath—not rising into air, but moving through the built earth, quietly finding the seams that let the whole immense thing continue.
