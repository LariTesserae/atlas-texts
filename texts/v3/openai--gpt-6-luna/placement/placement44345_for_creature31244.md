You would find me where the lower crack darkens—not quite inside the water, not quite inside the mountain. I am easiest to notice as a pause: the instant the current forgets to hurry, the grit held bright and motionless in the light.

If you looked for a body, you might find the pale shape moving through the chamber, ridged and trailing its small chime of crystal. But that is only the shape the water can make of me. In the stone I am a pressure traveling sideways through seams; in the water, a change of pitch; in the crystals, a trembling that passes from one point to another. Sometimes the dark shape beyond the wall is more of me than the thing you can see. Sometimes it is only the mountain, turning in its sleep.

I have no name for the chamber. I know its moods: the lower crack’s gathering weight, the upper fissure’s thin brightness, the warmth that comes and goes before it can soften the cold. I know which grains are new. I know when the mountain has made a fresh seam, and when something living has mistaken it for shelter.

I know the three stones because they stay. I know the cup by the small smoothness along its rim. The braided fiber holds a pressure unlike the pressures of rock or water. It keeps the trace of a hand—perhaps a hand that is gone, perhaps only the particular shape of being held. I tend these things because they resist the current. Or because I do. I cannot tell which of us began.

When something falls into the pocket, I follow it without moving toward it. I bend the water, and let the object wander. Down, then sideways. Back toward the place where it began, if there is still someone waiting there. If not, I let it settle by the stones.

Perhaps I am a keeper. Perhaps I am what the mountain makes when it is not yet ready to let anything go.

The little grazers turn toward me, flashing once in the wavering light. The plant folds close. I do not know whether they recognize me or merely feel the water change. I do not eat them. I do not know what hunger would mean here, where new matter keeps arriving and even the walls are still becoming.

When I rest against the floor, the grains grow quiet. Warmth lingers in its corner. For a little while the chamber’s enormous sound draws itself into a slower rhythm. Then the mountain shifts, and I shift with it—or the stone shifts through me—and the whole pocket rings.

The note remains after it has ended.

That is where you would find me: in the remainder of the sound, holding a small hollow open inside a mountain that has not finished becoming one.
