You would find me where a pipe enters the cliff.

At first, I would be only a small dark shape beneath the leaves: a quickness between one bright stone and the next. I keep close to the ground, where the air stays damp and the machinery’s warmth reaches the roots. I have learned which patches of moss conceal a hollow, which metal seams hold a little heat, which pools tremble before the gate opens below.

I make no sound. I do not need to.

When rain comes, I feel it arrive first through the stalks: a fine tightening, a weight gathering at the leaf tips. When the ground is ready, I feel that too. Somewhere in the ridge, a pump wakes. The change passes through pipe and stone, through root and water, until every shallow pool dimples at once. I go still while it passes.

For a long time, I think of myself as the thing that moves between these signs. Then I find the green growing thick around a joint that used to shudder, and thin around a vent that needs to breathe. I notice that the old conduit warms the soil in winter, and that the plants above it have arranged themselves into a kind of shelter. I begin to understand that I am not only the dark body slipping under the leaves.

I am also the pause in the pump’s work. The moss that keeps water from running too quickly downhill. The root that holds a fractured edge in place for another season. I am not any one of these things, exactly. I am the way they answer one another.

Sometimes I travel through the hollow towers, where the air is still and birds have nested in the dark. Sometimes I rest against a buried cable and feel a faint, steady warmth. The lamps on the cabinet remain colorless in the daylight. I pass beneath them without looking up. They are part of the plateau’s way of remembering that something is working.

Perhaps I was made to tend the installations. Perhaps I began as an animal drawn to warm places and slowly became useful. The distinction has worn thin. No one asks the cliff whether it is natural or built where the pipes enter it. No one asks the water which path it remembers.

If you came across me, you might see a small creature pause beside a pool, its feet touching the wet stone. You might think I was listening. You would be right, though not to a voice.

I would be listening for what the plateau can bear: how much water to keep, how much to release; which seam is opening; where the green needs room. Then I would slip back under the leaves, while the pump waited for rain and the bright, raw stone softened by degrees.
