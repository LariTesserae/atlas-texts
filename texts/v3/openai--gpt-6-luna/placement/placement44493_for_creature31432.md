I think I would live in a seam.

Not in one of the grand openings, where the mist gathers like weather, nor in the warm chambers people leave alone. I would be found where a narrow runnel slips behind a wall, disappears for a long while, and comes out through the roots of a tree on another terrace. A place you could pass a hundred times without noticing, unless you were listening for water.

I would be one of the High Quiet’s small inhabitants. Not quite an animal, though I would need warmth. Not quite a person, though I would understand a hand laid carefully against a wall. My body would be narrow and soft-edged, made for turning through roots and old channels. In the dark, the fine lines along my back might hold a little bronze light.

The people might call me a listener, if they needed a name. I would know which beams had begun to swell, which drains were filling with leaves, which familiar paths had started to lengthen. I would not know where a corridor went. I would know whether it still wanted to be followed.

Sometimes I would clear a gutter. Sometimes I would leave a cup of water near a threshold, though no house could be seen. If someone stood at the wrong crossing, I might touch the wall from the other side—just enough to give their palm a tremor to follow. I would not lead them everywhere. Some passages should close. Some shade should be left to itself.

Perhaps I would be older than the people, or perhaps I would have come into being because they kept tending the place: a little awareness gathered from hands, roots, water, and repeated footsteps. I would not ask which was true. The High Quiet does not seem to require its inhabitants to settle such things.

When the bell sounded, I would stop moving.

I would feel the note travel through the joined wood and the sleeping water, passing beneath my hands, through the seam where I lived. I would wait with everyone else—not for a summons, but for the country’s attention to pass over us.

Then I would return to my small work: listening for what had lost its way.
