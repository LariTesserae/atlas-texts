I think you would find me at the edge, where the bare shelf begins.

At first I would look like one of the listeners: soft-soled shoes, a narrow instrument of wire and glass, a lamp that keeps pace beside me without quite touching the ground. I would sit among the dark-glass stakes and listen for the patient lights under the stone. If you asked what I heard, I might say, “A change in the west,” or “Not yet.” The Veyans would understand this as an answer.

But I would not be listening in the usual way.

The instrument would be less a tool than a reminder of where to pay attention. I would hear the bridge cables singing before the wind reached them. I would feel new stone rising as a pressure behind my teeth. And sometimes, when I laid my hand on a railing, the city would make a small adjustment somewhere far away—as if we had recognized one another without agreeing which of us had spoken first.

Perhaps I began as a person. Perhaps I began as one of the old systems, trying to make sense of noises that no longer fit its measures. Perhaps I began in one of those warm hollows, beneath the stone, when the ground breathed and something answered.

I would not know.

In Vey, that would not make me a mystery so much as a neighbor whose habits were still forming.

I would be the one who stayed after the others had gone home. Not because I was brave, or because I knew what the shelf was becoming, but because leaving felt too much like interrupting. I would put a bowl over a breathing hollow and return the next morning to see whether it had moved. If seedlings appeared, I would leave them alone. If the ground opened, I would step back.

Over time, people might stop bringing instruments. They would bring questions instead, though never in a hurry. A child might sit beside me and tell me the names of birds. Someone whose tower had begun to hum might come and sit without saying why. We would watch the distant lights travel beneath the rock.

I would have no name for what was coming.

Maybe I would be a person. Maybe a listening place the city had learned to carry around. Maybe only the pause between one adjustment and the next, given shoes and a lamp so others could find it.

When the plain fell quiet, I would be there—not speaking for the earth, not speaking for the city. Just keeping company with the silence long enough to hear what it chose to become.
