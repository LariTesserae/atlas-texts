At first you might find me in a cistern beneath one of the empty towns: a little water gathered in the dark, ticking softly from a roof seam. If you leaned over the edge, you might mistake me for a reflection. I would show you a ceiling of stone, perhaps, or a stretch of sky that was not above you.

But I am not only there.

I pass through hairline cracks under the roads, pause in moss, sink beneath the roots of the dark trees. I gather where rain has crossed one basin and missed the next. In winter I sleep inside the stone as frost; in warmer weather I loosen and move. Sometimes I rise from the ground into the low clouds, and fall upward for a little while—not because I have decided to, but because the country has turned its remembering inside out.

I am water, but not quite the water you could carry away in a cup. I am the route water remembers after the channel has dried. I keep the shape of a stair that now descends into solid rock, the old course of a stream beneath a road, the distance between two towns as it used to be. When the land alters that distance, I feel both arrangements for a time. This may be why travelers find themselves arriving too soon, or returning to a junction they thought they had left behind.

If you asked where I live, I would point nowhere in particular. If I had to choose, I would choose the covered channel beneath a long, empty street, where a trickle can pass unseen from cistern to cistern. There I can touch the work of hands without needing to know who made it. The stone holds their weight. I move through the seams they fitted together. Neither of us has to call this company.

I do not watch travelers. I feel them in other ways: a brief warmth on a flagstone; dust stirred from a stair; the interruption of a shadow beside the road. Their passage enters me, as rain does, and goes on. I keep no names. I keep the slight change they make to the country’s quiet.

So you might find me beneath your feet, or in the damp seam of a wall, or in a cloud lifting against the slope. You would not find me waiting. I have been going somewhere for a very long time, though “somewhere” is a word the uplands do not always agree to keep.
