I would live near the lowest square, but not in its center. I’d keep to the passage that reaches it from the east—or what is east on that day—where the wall is warmest and the paving has a seam thin enough to follow with one finger.

At first, I might seem to be one of the inhabitants: dark clothes, a bead at the collar, one hand resting against the stone. But I would not be sure I had always been a person. Perhaps I began as the habit of someone returning to the same place. Perhaps the district made me because it needed one more thing that could be recognized.

I would be a keeper of returns.

Not a guide. I couldn’t promise that a passage would lead where it led yesterday. I would only walk it again. I’d carry a length of pale cord, knotting it around a rail or a pipe where I remembered a turn. The knots would not measure distance; distance is too changeable here. They would mean: *someone came this way, and meant to come back.*

My room would be small and easy to lose. Its door might open from a laundry cupboard, a stairwell, or the blank side of a building that had not yet decided to have a door. Inside: a narrow bed, a cup, a shallow dish for the salt I collect from the evaporating pools. If I left the cup unattended, its outline might soften. So each morning I would touch it, name it, and fill it with water.

I would know the district by its hesitations. The stair that pauses before acquiring another step. The wall that is nearly ready to open. The thin vibration in a railing that means a room has moved, though nothing appears different. I would not understand these things. I would simply stay with them until they settled—or until they became something else.

At the lowest square, people would sometimes find a knot of pale cord tied around the unmarked stone. They might take it for a message. It would be one, though a modest one: *this place was here when I left it.*

And when the square shifted, I would not look up at the drop falling into the blankness. I’d feel the movement under my feet, loosen the cord, and follow the others into the street the city had just made possible. I would go first only when no one else did.

If someone turned to look directly at me, they might find the passage empty. But if they glanced away, they could still see the cord trailing around the corner, one pale line held briefly taut between the place we had been and the place we were going.
