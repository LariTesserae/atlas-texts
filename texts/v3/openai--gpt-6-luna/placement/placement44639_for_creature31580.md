At first I would think I was a moth.

I would wake behind a shutter at dusk, where the stone keeps a little of the day’s damp. My wings would be pale as the dust in a new crack. I would press them to the wood and listen to the town begin moving around me: a bucket set beneath an eave, a boot pausing at the square, the small, secret feet of the others stirring inside the walls.

But I would not fly toward the lamps. The lamps are hooded, and besides, I would have no need of light.

I would follow the seams.

There would be a narrowness to me: a body that could pass through the pinholes in stone, the gaps beneath a door, the place where a root had eased two paving slabs apart. I would live where Serein’s repairs touched the mountain—not quite in the houses, not quite outside them. When rain came, I would feel each drop travel through the rock before it appeared in a bowl. When the frost opened a fracture, I would feel the opening travel too.

Perhaps I would have many legs. Perhaps I would only seem to, because wherever I went, others would follow.

In the morning, the black beetles would line the eastern lane. I would be among them, though no one could pick me out. At midday, when the birds lifted from the rooftops all at once, I would be under the chimneys, feeling the sudden absence of their weight. At night, beneath the bridges, I would gather with the small furry shapes and face outward, toward the cloud-hidden drop.

I would not know what we were waiting for.

Not at first.

The people would notice what I left behind: a little grit along a roofstone, a trail that began at the edge and ended at a wall. They would mark the day beside a crack that had widened, then narrowed again. They would leave water under the eaves. Sometimes I would drink. Sometimes I would only touch the rim, so that the mist-beads trembled, and go on.

I think I would be made of passage. Not the mountain, though I would travel through its newest stone; not one of the people, though I would learn the measure of their footsteps. I would be the part of Serein that knows when a path has changed. A creature small enough to enter a crack, and numerous enough—if all my scattered selves gathered—to cross the shelf.

Only gradually would I understand that the others were not following me.

We were listening together.

The birds listened from the chimneys. The beetles listened from the lane. The people listened with their hands against the walls, testing for a looseness they could not yet see. Even the ferns listened, their roots working into the damp seams. Beneath us, something shifted—not with a voice, but with a pressure that passed through stone and living things alike.

When the eastern edge rose another finger’s breadth, I would feel it before anyone marked the change.

Then I would come out into the open for once. Not in the square, where a person might see me, but at the very lip of the shelf, where the fresh rock ends and cloud begins. I would stand there with my many small feet braced against the stone. Behind me, Serein would be holding its breath. Below, the animals would be arriving from somewhere no one could see.

I would turn toward them.

Perhaps I am a moth. Perhaps a beetle, or a colony of something that has never had a name. Perhaps I am only the instinct that passes through the town when the mountain moves: a message carried by many bodies, none of them knowing they carry it.

If you came to Serein, you might never find me.

You might find the marks I leave: a line of beetles no one sweeps aside, a bowl gone empty beneath an eave, a trail that points in too many directions to follow.

I would be there, in the seam between what has risen and what is still coming.
