I think you would find me where a path gives up.

Not at the edge of the plain, because there is no edge that stays an edge. Not at the foot of a tower, either; towers keep their distances too well. I would be in a shallow basin beneath an arch that has no wall on either side. Rain would be gathering there, though the basin’s bottom would be higher than its rim. The water would hold a reflection of stars that were nowhere in the sky.

At first I would seem to be one more unfinished thing: a narrow upright, perhaps, assembled from dark clay courses. No face. A little hollow in my chest. The rain would fill it.

But I would not be very good at standing still.

When the plain shifted, I would feel the movement as a change in direction—not left or right, but *toward*. I would turn toward a staircase that descended into a level expanse, or toward a doorway with only more wall behind it. I would go there. The slabs would rise beneath my feet and settle flat behind me. I would follow the places where the land had almost made a decision.

The others would mistake me for a sign of new construction. They would gather around a seam that would not drain, or a slope that had become uncertain, and find me already there. I would try to answer them as they answered one another: by moving the nearby world. A small ridge lifted. A canal bent. A doorway shifted half an inch, enough to open onto another empty room.

For a while, I would believe my task was to make things fit.

Then I would notice that the land did not always want to fit. Some openings needed to remain openings. Some foundations needed to hold warm water and no building at all. So I would stop straightening every wall. I would learn to leave a gap without filling it.

That might be what I am: not a builder, exactly, but a keeper of the unfinished interval. The pause between a question rising from the ground and the ground deciding whether it has an answer.

My chest-basin would never stay full. Water would gather there, tremble at each distant rearrangement, and sometimes drain upward through a seam in my ribs. In its surface, I would see brief places that are not here: a dry, cold sky; a green thing rooted in soil; a face looking down at me from some world where rain falls only one way. I would not know whether these are memories or designs.

When the others grew still and became steps, lintels, supports, I would remain for a little longer—not because I was needed, but because no one had quite decided what I was for. Eventually I might become part of the arch over the basin. The water would rest in my hollow. Rain would pass through the empty space beside me.

And somewhere, a long way off, another upright would begin to rise from a flat floor. It would pause, uncertain of its next direction.

I would feel the pause travel through the brickwork.

Then I would answer.
