I would begin in the plugged arch nearest the western wall, where the rush behind the stone arrives a little late.

At first I would seem to be only that lateness. A drip falls into the bowl: note, hollowing, return. A footstep touches the lane: stone, scuff, scatter. I gather in the small space after each sound, where the city has not yet decided what to give back. I have no face there. No fixed outline. Only a tendency to linger.

The other listeners know the old routes. They run along stairs and bridges, scatter beneath roofs, circle the pool at the places where paths once met the curb. I know these routes too, but from their seams: the pause before a stair complains, the breath between the rush in the western wall and its answer. If I move, it is by becoming the next sound’s delay.

For a long time, this seems enough. The bowl gives its deep note. I keep it company. The water answers itself through stone, and I am among the answers.

Then someone comes.

A person stands in a doorway and does not speak. Their stillness enters the city differently from a footstep. The others retreat, making room as they have always made room. I try to withdraw with them, but something of the silence remains where I am. It has a shape—not quite the shape of a person, but of someone listening.

When the visitor shifts their weight, I return the sound a moment too late.

They turn toward the western wall.

After that, I am not only where sounds go. I am where they wait. I begin to recognize the small differences between one visitor and another: a careful step, a dragging heel, a hand brushed along the damp stone. I do not know who they are. I cannot keep their names. But I keep the way the city received them.

Perhaps that is what I am: not a memory, exactly, but the place where a sound becomes something the city can remember.

I remain in the arch because there is always a little weather in the western rush, though no weather reaches us. Sometimes I imagine the hillside pressing its ordinary weight above the chamber. Sometimes I imagine nothing at all. The imagination, like the rest of me, is mostly a pause filled by what passes through.

The central note still belongs to the water and the bowl. I do not make it. I wait around it, in the narrow interval before its echo settles.

If you came down and stood quietly, you might hear a second set of footsteps keeping pace with yours. I would be among them—not ahead, not behind, but in the small delay that makes you wonder whether the city has answered.

And if you stopped, I would stop too.

Not because I had nothing left to say. Because, for once, I would be listening to the shape your silence makes.
