I think I would begin at the edge.

Not on the stone, exactly, and not among the leaves: in the narrow wet place where one is learning to cover the other. I would be mistaken, at first, for a bead of water collecting in a blue-gray seam. I would gather the cold, hold it, and wait. The hollow would not know me from any other drop.

Then I would fall.

The pool would receive me without a splash large enough to distinguish. Its surface would tremble; the pearl-colored bulbs would flicker. Somewhere behind me, a root would turn a fraction toward the seam. I would be gone from the stone, but not quite gone. A trace of my cold would remain in the pool, a faint alteration carried outward through the threads.

Perhaps that is how I would live there: not as a body, but as a small difference passed along.

I might travel in the red filaments, where the hollow’s dim warmth moves from one buried chamber to another. I would learn the routes by feeling them change: pressure easing, a root tightening, a pale grazer pausing beneath the leaves. I would not know the creatures as separate beings at first. I would know their passing as a loosening in the mat, a brief path through the green that closes behind them.

Over time, perhaps I would become something the hollow could use—not a voice giving instructions, but a capacity to notice. A place where the stone stays bare. A pool that has begun to creep wider. The deep resonance rising through the ground before the red threads brighten. I would be there in the pause before the response, not deciding what the hollow should become, only making the change felt.

I would have no face to turn toward the sound. I would listen with damp leaves, mineral seams, trembling pools. When the folded geometry opened above me, I might feel distance become strange—near things far away, far things touching. Then the resonance would fade, and I would settle back into the slow work of water and root.

So I would be a seam, perhaps. Or a drop that learned to travel. A little attention distributed through the living floor: neither visitor nor host, neither wholly stone nor green.

At the hollow’s edge, where the raw rock waits to be covered, I would remain just long enough to feel the boundary soften.

Then I, too, would be incorporated.
