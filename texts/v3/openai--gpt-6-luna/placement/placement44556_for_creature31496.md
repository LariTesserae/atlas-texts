At first I think I would be water: a dark pool held in the print of an old foot, reflecting almost no sky. Things arrive at my edge to drink. A beetle skims me; a falling leaf turns slowly on my surface. Then the rain comes, and I am no longer a pool. I seep between roots, gather in a hollow, slip downhill through a channel that will be gone by morning.

But water does not remember where to turn.

I think I would be one of the young elephants, walking close behind the old female. Not the smallest calf, who still startles at her own reflection, but not yet grown enough to choose the way. I follow the damp warmth of my family through the crowded green. I learn the sounds their bodies make: a low rumble felt through the ground, a pause before a difficult bank, the soft insistence of my mother’s trunk at my shoulder when I have wandered too far toward a tempting patch of shoots.

The lowland is so full of things to notice that I cannot take it all in. I know the pools first by taste. I know the clay shelf by the way it holds my weight, and the hollow beyond it by the coolness rising through the ferns. A bird’s sudden silence means something has moved nearby; sometimes it is only one of us. I learn that the forest is not empty when I cannot see through it. I learn, too, that a place can be familiar without ever being the same: rain alters the footing, fallen trunks change the passage, new green closes around an old track.

For a long time, I walk behind the one who knows. Her shorter tusk catches a vine; she frees it without stopping. She turns where the ground looks like ground and is not. I do not understand how she knows. I only watch, and follow.

Then, one dry interval, the usual basin is too shallow. The family waits. The old female lifts her trunk, testing the air, but it gives us little. I remember a place I passed once: not a pool, exactly, but a crease in the earth below a pale shelf, cool-smelling even when the mud around it had begun to harden. I step toward it. For a moment no one moves. Then she follows.

There is water there—not much, but enough. The others gather around us, and the calves drink. I stand with my feet in the soft bank and feel the ground give way beneath them. It occurs to me, though not in words, that I have become part of what the others may remember.

So I would be an elephant, still young, learning the lowland by touch and taste. And I would be a path beginning—not a track cut into the forest, but a possibility carried from one body to another. Behind me, the plants will lean back into the space we made. Rain will loosen our prints. Beetles will work through what we leave. The path may disappear from sight.

Still, somewhere in the dark under the leaves, the turn will be known.
