I think I would first mistake myself for the pause between two ticks.

There is a small hollow beneath one of the proud bolts, where the ceramic terrace meets a metal rib. Water almost reaches it, but never quite. The mesh above is crowded; the channels below are busy. Here, for the width of a fingernail, the bodies thin out. I would find myself in that almost-empty seam.

At first I would be no more than a clear film laid over the metal. The machine’s hum would pass through me, and I would answer by trembling. Not loudly. Not in a way anyone could call a signal. Just enough for the nearest pale insects to feel that the bolt was still there beneath their feet.

Then the film would gather a body.

I would have a soft, flattened shape, one mouth, and many fine limbs fitted to the seam. I would drink the trace of water that lingered after the droplets had gone, and feed on what the others left behind: a little shed skin, a mineral grain, the faint residue of countless lives. My body would be almost transparent, except where the vibration collected in me, a dim blue glimmer like the one held in the insects’ joints.

I would not hear the machine. I would feel it as a pressure that never ended. I would know the pipes by their intervals, the creatures by their different tremors: the busy combing below, the crowded rasp above, the slow shiver of the wall colonies opening and closing. The machine’s note would be the deepest fact of my world. Everything else would be a change within it.

If a droplet came down too hard, I would flatten myself against the bolt. If the channel below ran thin, I would loosen and let the water find the smallest groove. I would not be a keeper of the place. Nothing here needs keeping. I would be more like a knot in its fabric: a spot where the living pressure gathers, briefly, before passing on.

The others would touch me without knowing me. An insect’s foot would catch at one of my limbs and move on. A young one might rest in the curve of my body until the next tremor carried it back into the mesh. I would know each contact as a distinct event, then lose it. My life would have edges, but they would be edges made by other lives.

After a while, I might not know whether the tremor began in me or arrived from elsewhere. The seam would fill with my descendants, each a small clear body laid against the next. We would not become a single mind. We would become a place that could feel the machine more delicately than any one of us could feel it alone.

And still, somewhere inside that crowded sensation, I would keep one small uncertainty: the instant before the pipe ticks, when the whole chamber seems to hold its breath.

I would live there—not outside the noise, but in its narrowest seam. I would be a little animal made of listening, learning the shape of the world from what it makes the water do.
