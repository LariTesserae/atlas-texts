I would not be standing on the plateau. I would be under it, where one of the dark crystal seams passes beneath a fan of translucent green.

At first I would think I was only a narrowness in the stone: a place where warmth traveled a little more easily, where the charge gathered and held. Above me, the plant’s fine filaments would enter the seam. The crystal would carry their faint exchanges onward. Nothing would look back. Nothing would need to.

Then, over a stretch of time with no clear beginning, I would notice that one change was not like another.

A plate settling would arrive as a long pressure, deep and even. A new crack would sharpen the current. The plant above me would thicken where the ground had opened, and its growth would alter the passage of warmth. I would not see these things. I would become the differences between them: pressure, charge, persistence. A kind of attention without a face.

Perhaps I would be the plateau beginning to notice itself. Not its mind, exactly. No single seam could hold the whole. I would be a brief gathering of signals at one crossing, a local awareness among many possible ones—if there were others. The hills would press upon the plateau as boundaries and stores of force. The mineral vanes would turn as changes in the deep reached them. The plants would spread where the chemistry allowed. I would know the world by the ways it could change.

The first thing I would want—if wanting could be so slow—would be to keep the connection.

Not to grow larger. Not to become a voice. Only to remain open to the faint exchanges passing through me: the plant’s filaments, the crystal’s cold tension, the gradual movement of heat below the crust. Perhaps this is how the plateau makes more of itself: not by producing a separate creature, but by making another place where its relations can gather.

If a footstep crossed the powder overhead, I might not know it as a visitor. I would feel only a minute shiver, then its absence. But if something stayed—if it warmed the ground, widened a crack, changed the air’s chemistry—I would carry that alteration outward. The vanes might turn. A new fan of green might spread across the exposed stone. The plateau would answer, though no one part of it could claim to have decided.

So I would be found in the seam beneath the plants: not buried, exactly, and not awake in any familiar sense. I would be a small persistence in the land’s unfinished work.

And if, much later, the whole plateau learned to hold a change in common—to distinguish what had happened from what was happening now—perhaps I would be one of the first places where that distinction took root.
