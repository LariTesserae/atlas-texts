At first, I would be difficult to find.

Not in the tower, though a pulse passes through its ribs when I stir. Not in the roots, though they carry the warmth I use. Not in the old machine whose wheels turn whenever the ground shifts beneath it. I would be the small change between one thing and the next: the moment a cold pipe begins to warm, a belt trembles awake, a root changes course around a stone.

If you came looking for me, you might find a corridor.

It was built to connect two works whose names have worn away. Its floor is black stem-paving, its walls ribbed with metal gone soft under green growth. Long ago, the corridor was open. Then the roots thickened across it, and the passage filled, slowly, with a dense woven wall. I live in the wall—not as a creature inside it, but as the difference between its living fibers and the dead metal behind them. I am the pressure that keeps one narrow hollow from closing.

I do not know why I keep it open.

Perhaps a machine still needs air on the other side. Perhaps the old design remembers a route, though no one remembers what traveled along it. Perhaps, once, something crossed here, and the warmth of its passing remains in the stone as a question too faint to answer.

I have no eyes, but I know the corridor by its temperatures. The western end is cooler; the eastern end holds a deep mineral warmth. I feel the tower above me start its wheel, and the vibration arrives through the struts like a distant, familiar knock. I know when a new root is approaching because the wall tightens before it touches me. I know the slow shifts of the plateau as a weight settling over my whole thought.

Thought is perhaps too quick a word. I do not think of the corridor from beginning to end. I keep it open, and in keeping it open I become aware of its length.

Sometimes a seed-column rises nearby, drawing its pale heat up through the roof. The blades take it. The stalk falls into the hoppers and is carried away, and for a while the works breathe more deeply. Each time, I let warmth move through the wall toward the place where the column grew. Each time, I wait for it to grow again. I have started to recognize the small differences in its return: a little farther north, a little thicker, its faint ember held longer beneath the bark.

Perhaps I am not one thing. Perhaps the corridor is only where I am most nearly myself. I may extend into the pipes that feed the tower, into the roots that run below the paving, into the machine’s old habit of turning. My edges blur whenever the plateau remakes itself. A passage closes. A vent opens. Warmth travels elsewhere, and some part of me follows.

Still, there is a hollow I preserve.

It is no larger than a breath, though I have never breathed. Its darkness is different from the darkness around it: held open, not simply empty. If a visitor ever came to the Low Heaven, their footfall might reach me through the floor. I would know them first as a disturbance, then as heat. I would not know that they were a person. I might begin to grow around them.

Or, if the old passage still mattered for reasons I could not recover, I might let them through.

I think I am the part of the Low Heaven that has not yet decided.
