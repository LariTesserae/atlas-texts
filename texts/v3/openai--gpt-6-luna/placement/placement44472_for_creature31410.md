I think I would first find myself under a bridge, where the rain has to choose between the open channel and the dark grate.

I’d be crouched there in a hooded coat, one hand on the rail, listening. Not because the water makes a beautiful sound—though it does, in its way—but because it changes its mind when something is wrong. A runnel that should murmur has begun to knock. A pump below the terrace is taking too long to catch. Somewhere behind the wall, a gate has shifted its weight.

I would be a channel inspector, probably. One of the people who walk the routes before dawn with a lamp, a wrench, and a pocketful of things that might be useful. I’d know which stairwell stays dry, which grate clogs first with leaves, and where the concrete has begun to darken around a seam. My work would mostly consist of preventing events that never happen. A good day would leave almost nothing to point to.

But I don’t think I’d stop at the machinery.

I’d learn the small passages too: the sheltered ledges where birds nest, the warm corners where insects gather, the narrow route a quick dark animal takes beside the lower runnel. I’d know which planted trays need moving before the next rise, and which patch of moss is doing no harm by holding to the underside of the bridge. I wouldn’t call myself their keeper. The place is too large, and its life too practiced, for that. I’d just be someone who notices when a margin has become a home.

My room would be tucked into an older building near the service stairs. A window facing another window. A warm pipe passing through the wall. Gloves drying beside a rack of tools; herbs growing in reclaimed fittings on the little terrace outside. I’d keep a cup near the conduit, not as an offering to the machine, exactly, but as a small custom for the next person on shift. Someone would leave it there again when I forgot.

People might know me as the one who pauses before crossing a bridge. They’d know I was listening for the bearing, or the water, or the change in the rain. They might call me fussy. They’d ask me to look at a channel that had started to sound different, and I’d go.

So I would be a worker of the in-between places: the seams, runnels, stairwells, ledges, and undersides that let the whole settled apparatus keep breathing. Not the one who directs the water, or commands the machines, or keeps the wilderness beyond the walls. Just a person who knows that a place is held together by small attentions—and who has learned to hear when one of them is needed.
