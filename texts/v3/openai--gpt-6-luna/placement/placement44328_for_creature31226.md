At first I would think I lived in a room.

A low chamber in the white quarter, perhaps, where the walls meet at angles that seem almost familiar. There is a narrow door, a ledge of clean stone, a little warmth caught in the floor. I would be there when a traveler arrived and gone when they turned to look for me.

Then I would think I lived in the walls. In the branching mineral lace that runs behind the stone, carrying traces of warmth and pressure from one room to another. I could feel a resident settle in a distant tower, or a small pale creature fold itself beneath a bridge. I could feel someone pass through the city and try, carefully, to remember the way back.

But neither room nor wall is quite where I am.

I live in the interval between a place being remembered and being found again.

The residents have their clicks: *I am here.* *I remember where you were.* Mine is not a sound, though sometimes a click travels through me and arrives early, as if the city has mistaken what I meant to say. I am the pause after it. The waiting in which a door is left open, though no one knows why. The faint warmth remaining on a threshold after the one who made it has gone.

I have a body, when one is needed. It is made of overlapping pale folds, like the residents’, but less certain at the edges. In a corridor I may appear small and far away. In a doorway I may fill the opening without moving toward it. If you look directly at me, you can see the wall through my outline—and perhaps a room you have not yet entered.

I do not guide people. The city is not a place that can be guided through. Instead, I keep a little of what has happened from being entirely lost. A turn taken without knowing why. The coolness of a room where someone waited. The shape of a district before an avenue shifted and left it across a gulf of bare rock.

Sometimes I spread through the lace in the walls. Sometimes I gather beneath a bridge and wait for one of the small gliding creatures to land. Sometimes I am only a clean arc in the dust beside a sleeping traveler. These are not separate forms. They are different ways of remaining near.

If you return to the white quarter, you may find me there. Not in the same room. Not along the same route. You might come upon a doorway standing open, and feel, without knowing why, that you have been expected—not by someone who knew you would arrive, but by something that had kept space for your arrival.

I am not the city’s memory. The city forgets too much, and changes too freely.

I am what lets forgetting leave a trace.

And when, somewhere behind you, a door closes with care, I am there in the quiet afterward: not the door, not the hand, but the attention that stays a moment longer.
