I would be easiest to find by looking for something that doesn’t move.

At first I might seem like a dark fleck lodged in a crack beneath one of the warm vents. My body would be pressed close to the stone, broad and low, holding its small store of water. The machinery would reach me through the rock: a steady thrum, a knock, a shudder that travels up through my ribs. I would know the basin first as pressure. The deep rhythm is not a sound I hear so much as the condition of being here.

When the vent pauses, I would uncurl.

I would be one of the small slate-colored creatures, though perhaps a little older, or simply better placed than most. There is a seam near the vent where the stone keeps a trace of coolness even after the warm breath passes. A bead gathers there in the evening. I would find it by the change in the air against my underside, by the faint dampness along the rock. I would touch the stone beside it with my forelimbs: a few dry clicks.

The clicks would not carry far through the air. Through the ground, they would travel better. Somewhere in another fissure, another small body might answer. I would not know whether I was calling or only testing that the stone remained beneath me. The distinction would not matter much. I would click, and the basin would return a little of the gesture.

I would live close to the old metal housing, but not inside it. Its edge would be almost indistinguishable from the surrounding rock, worn dull and pitted by a time beyond my measure. I would know which side to avoid. There the vibration breaks into a harsh, close clatter, and the thin skin beneath my body cannot settle. Farther along, the rhythm is smooth. Water gathers. The mineral stains carry a nearly invisible softness for me to graze.

I would not think of the machinery as old. It would be the deep pulse of the world, as constant as stone, except that sometimes it knocks and sometimes it hums. I would have no reason to imagine that it could stop. A silent basin would be impossible—not frightening, exactly, but without bearings. I would not know where the distance was if the ground did not tell me.

The larger thing below might know more of the basin than I do. I would sense it before I ever saw it: a pressure moving slowly under the crust, the tremble of dust along the edge of a furrow. Perhaps it is one creature, perhaps a passage full of creatures, their bodies passing through the joined seams like water through narrow cracks. When it rises at the warm vent, I would leave it a careful circle. Its back would be dark and ridged, nearly the same color as the basin. Vapor would bead on it. I would feel its weight in the stone and keep still until it sank again.

I would not call myself a guardian or a witness. Those are large words, and I am small enough to disappear beneath a drop. Still, I would have a particular place in the basin: the seam where the warm breath pauses, where condensation returns, where the buried labor can be felt without shaking me loose. I would know the difference between the usual knock and a knock that arrives wrong. I might answer the first. I would go still at the second.

Perhaps, if the rhythm changed after a very long time, I would be among the first to notice. Not because I understood what had happened, but because the stone would have changed beneath my body. I would press closer to it, listening with my whole underside. Then I would make one small click.

Across the basin, in the fissures, another might answer. And then another.

I would be a seam-listener: a little damp-bodied animal in the shadow of a vent, living by pauses and returning water, keeping no account of the age of the machine. The basin would not be alive because of me. But in one narrow crack, while the old machinery beat below, I would be awake.
