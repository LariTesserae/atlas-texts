I would be found where the covered passage narrows and the water slips beneath the oldest wall.

At first you might take me for one of the shelf-dwellers. I keep low, shoulders rounded against the damp, and when I rest my skin shines like theirs. I sit for a long time with one hand in the channel, feeling the little movements the water makes before it changes direction. People pass me without stopping. They know the sound of my breathing.

I am not a keeper of water, exactly. Water does not keep still long enough to belong to anyone. I clear a gutter when it begins to choke; I press mud back into a thinning wall; I leave a gap where the shell-backed grazers come to rasp the mineral film clean. Most days, my work is so small that no one can say what I have done. A pool holds its shape. A passage stays dry enough for a child to cross. The town carries on making its many voices.

I learned the channels by following my elders, then learned them again after the ground had shown me what they had missed. Beneath the stone, seepage has its own routes. It tugs at the floor in a way the feet can feel, if one is still enough. I can tell when a wall is growing thirsty, or when a pool is about to rise—not by sight, but by the changed return of a click from the passage. Once, I mistook a larger animal’s call for a warning in the stone and woke half the block. They laughed, and then helped me check the foundations.

Now the young ones come to me with their little scoops and their questions. They ask where the water goes. I tell them: “Here, for a while.” They want something more exact. So I show them how to listen with the palm against the wall.

My home is a narrow shelf beside the old channel, close enough that I wake when its rhythm changes. I sleep under a roof of shell and hide, among the breathing of my family and the calls passing beyond the walls. I do not need silence. Silence would mean that something had stopped answering.

Sometimes, late in the warm dark, I go out beyond the last houses. The basin opens around me: slick stone, shallow pools, the blunt shore of the world under its low ceiling of cloud. The town sounds behind me—not one voice, but many, crossing and returning. On the mud shelves, the great smooth animals answer in their wavering notes. I cannot tell whether they are speaking to us. I cannot tell whether that matters.

I used to think I was listening for a sign: danger, change, some larger meaning hidden under the water. I don’t think that now. Mostly I listen for what needs tending. A blocked runnel. A frightened child. The faint shiver of a wall that has held for years and may yet hold for years more, if someone puts a hand to it today.

So if you came looking for me, I would be there at the seam between passage and pool, neither fully of the town nor outside it. One hand in the water. One hand on the stone. Waiting—not for the basin to explain itself, but for it to make the next small sound.
