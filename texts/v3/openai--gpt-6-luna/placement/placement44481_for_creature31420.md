I would find myself at the edge of a clearing—not in its bare center, and not deep in the stalk-forest, but where the root-mat makes a last tangled shelf beneath the open light.

At first I might seem to be part of that shelf. A low shape under the pale litter, still while the ground shivers. My coat would be the color of old stalk-fiber, with dark seams along my back. I would have broad, splayed feet for traveling over the living roof without breaking through it, and ears that fold flat when the herd passes.

I would be one of the root-ears.

I would know the plateau first through my feet. A grazer’s weight arrives as a long, gentle trembling, growing stronger, then thinning away. A predator’s step is different: lighter, interrupted, sometimes stopping altogether. I would press my forefeet into the mat and wait for the ground to finish speaking. Sight would help, but only at close range. The country’s sounds would often tell me more than its shapes.

I would live beneath the clearing’s rim, where stems lean low enough to make entrances, but the air stays dry. My tunnels would follow the old root-banks. I would forage at dusk for fallen seeds, soft grubs, and the tender undersides of leaves. Some seeds I would eat; others I would carry in the fur of my cheeks and drop farther along the rim, where a new patch might take hold. I would not make the plateau. I would merely help it remember how to begin again.

Water would draw me out. After a night of dew, I would climb a folded leaf and drink from its cup, keeping my body low against the tremor of every stem. If a pale strip of rind hung high on a stalk, I would know people had found water nearby. I would give their shelter a wide berth—not because I feared them exactly, but because their footsteps were difficult to read. They walked above the root-web, light and strange, and their voices traveled in ways that did not always belong to the direction they seemed to come from.

Still, I might grow used to them. I might sleep beneath the edge of a raised platform, listening to the people settle for the night. Their shelters would creak in the wind. Beyond them, the stalks would boom, and the calls of the climbing creatures would swell and break apart across the dark. I would learn which silences were only pauses, and which meant that something nearby had stopped making noise for a reason.

At night I would move along the clearing’s border, never quite entering the open and never quite disappearing into the forest. The herd-road would lie before me, worn into the ground by lives much larger than mine. I would cross it after the thunder had passed, gathering what it left behind.

I would be small in a world that rarely needed to notice me. But I would know where the ground was hollow, where dew still held, and how far away a footfall might be. In this upland, that would be enough to belong.
