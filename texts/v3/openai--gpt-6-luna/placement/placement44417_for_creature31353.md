At first I am mistaken for a chip of basalt.

I am low against the ledge, grey with dust, my plates fitted so closely to the stone that there is no clear line where one ends and the other begins. Warmth enters me from below. A pale feeler moves along a crack, tasting the sharpness there. I do not see the chamber. I feel its pressure: the far-off settling of air, the tiny ticking of bodies beside me, the deep heat held in the rock.

Then something crosses the powder below.

The nearest shells tighten. The tremor passes through us—not as a message with words, but as a change in the shape of the quiet. I tighten too. Farther along the ledge, my neighbors answer. The movement goes on past where I can sense it directly, yet I am somehow part of its going. For a moment the colony draws itself together around the disturbance. Then, when nothing follows, we loosen.

I am one body. I am not only one body.

I live near the outer edge of a colony fixed to a warm face of basalt, where the ledge narrows above a basin of powder. Behind me, the others overlap in dark ranks, their shells faintly pearled in the steady heat. Beneath us, a seam of black glass runs into the chamber’s dark. It holds no warmth we can use and gives nothing back. We have been here longer than any one of us can know. When the crack in the ledge shifts, we will move—not far, perhaps only to the next warm shelf—and the place we leave will slowly become indistinguishable from stone.

I am the part that feels the edge.

A passing hunter would reach me first. So would a change in the air, a tremor through the floor, the first chill in a seam that has fed us for generations. If I am damaged, the colony will know—not what I was called, or what I meant to do, but where the pressure changed. My loss will pass inward as a tightening, then become food, mineral, and warmth. I cannot call this comfort. It is simply how the ledge continues.

Sometimes I turn, and the small blue light beneath my armor opens briefly into the darkness. It does not show me my neighbors. It may show them that something has moved, or that I am still here. The light vanishes when I settle against the rock again.

A maker has left a narrow scraper near the crack behind us. I feel its occasional touch, delicate and purposeful. The smaller creature works by pressure and contact, loosening flakes of glass, setting one across a powder pit. Its little constructions do not change the chamber. Still, the safe route it makes becomes part of what we feel: a faintly altered pattern beneath the floor, a familiar crossing in the dark.

I do not know how vast the chamber is. I know the warmth beneath me, the pressure of the shell to my left, the familiar mineral taste along the seam. I know when the colony breathes—not with lungs, but in the minute shifts of bodies settling, feeding, and settling again. When one of us stirs, I stir. When the movement passes, I remain.

So I would be found on the warm side of a ledge, where the living crust gathers beneath the young crust.

I would be a shelled creature at its margin—and, for as long as the others answer my smallest tightening, the listening edge of a many-bodied self.
