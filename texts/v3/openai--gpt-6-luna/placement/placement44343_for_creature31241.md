I would be found at the lower lock, where the warm outflow meets the colder basin.

At first I might look like one of the workers: waterproof coat, boots whitened at the seams with salt, a ring of keys knocking softly against one hip. I’d have a room above the pump house, with a narrow window facing the channels and a kettle that never quite dries. My work would be measured in gauges and gate-hours. I’d know the ordinary sounds—the pump’s low note, the chain tapping metal—and the small changes that mean the water has started to turn.

But I wouldn’t be only watching the machinery. I’d learn to leave the right gate open a little longer when the shoals were moving through. I’d know which warm channels the crabs favored, and where the birds would gather once the fish came in. I wouldn’t call it kindness, exactly. It would be more like keeping a passage clear for something that was already on its way.

After enough seasons, perhaps I’d become difficult to distinguish from the lock itself. I’d wake when the current changed. I’d recognize the brief darkening beneath the lamps before anyone called it in. On nights when the tide was high and the rain softened every edge, I’d stand on the steps and listen to the water move through the gates.

So: a keeper of the lower lock, though not quite its keeper. A human being, mostly. A witness to the traffic between basin and sea. Someone the city had taught to tell the difference between an empty channel and one that was only waiting.
