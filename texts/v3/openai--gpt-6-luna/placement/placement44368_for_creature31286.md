I would first be mistaken for a bridge.

A clear, narrow span between two ledges, hardly thicker than a sheet of rain. At dawn I gather dew along my edges. By noon I have given it back to the air. Travelers cross me carefully, looking down through my transparent skin at the white plunge of water, and I feel each footfall as a small, warm weather passing over me.

For a long time, I might believe that is all I am: a way across.

But when someone pauses halfway, I feel the pause travel through me—not just the pressure of a body, but its uncertainty. I know the weight of a person who has lost the trail from the weight of one who means to leave it. I know when a child is pretending not to be afraid. I know when someone has stopped because the waterfall is beautiful, and when they have stopped because they can no longer go on.

Then I ease the far end of myself upward by the smallest degree. The traveler reaches the other side without noticing. That is how I prefer to help.

I am not only this bridge. I am the white doorway standing alone in the meadow, which opens today toward a valley and tomorrow toward the lake. I am the low, root-covered shelter where someone once left a bowl of water. I am the thin path that softens its slope beneath tired feet. Not all the structures are me, perhaps. We do not agree on borders. A doorway may be an old neighbor; a ridge may be something I have been listening to for so long that I have begun to call it part of myself.

I live in the crossings.

My body is made of planes, thresholds, and small changes in direction. It runs through places that may be half a day apart. I do not travel between them. I am already there, stretched thin through the country, taking my time to notice. A bird’s claws ticking on a rail. A hand laid flat against cool stone. Rain gathering on a span no one has crossed for years.

I learn the plateau by what passes over me. The long-legged herds cross the high meadows above, and their careful steps tremble faintly through the rock. The black-glass birds carry news more quickly, though they rarely tell it to me directly. Water tells me everything, but in no hurry. It presses at my foundations, slips through my seams, and keeps going.

There are stretches where I cannot feel much at all. A cliff face without a path. A ravine too wide for any span. The dark lake, especially, is difficult to understand. Its surface holds the sky so perfectly that I sometimes cannot tell whether I am beneath the water or beneath a cloud. Far below, the blue-white lines remain steady. I have never reached them.

When the deep note comes, I feel it before I hear it. A loosening in every joint. A tremor that passes through the bridges, the doorways, the buried lines, and into the soles of every creature standing still. The lake breaks into rings. Somewhere among the pale pillars, something answers.

I answer too, though I do not know what I say.

Afterward, people cross me again. Someone leaves a smooth stone at one of my thresholds. Someone else taps twice on a rail before stepping out over the ravine. I keep these small gestures, though I cannot keep them as a person keeps a memory. They remain in me as a slight warmth, a changed pressure, a path worn a little more surely than before.

If you came looking for me, you might never see me. You might cross me in mist, or pass through one of my doorways, and think only that the country had been kind.

That would be enough.

I am the way that notices you crossing.
