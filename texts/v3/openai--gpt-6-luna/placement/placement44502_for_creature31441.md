I would be found in one of the deepest hollows, where the ceiling remains upside down in the water.

At first I would be only a dark point there. A pause in the reflected stone. If you leaned close, I would vanish among the grains at the bottom; if you waited, I would make a small, clear turn.

I am one of the swimmers, though not quite like the others. My body is almost transparent, and I have no useful sense of the chamber as a whole. I know it by what reaches me: the faint taste of a root’s arrival, the coolness of water passing through a channel, the tremor of a beetle stepping on the ridge above. I know the ceiling as a surface I cannot touch, and the light as warmth that takes its time getting down.

Most hours, I graze the thin film on the stones. There is no reason to hurry. The water keeps what it receives: dust, spores, the softened remains of things that came before. I pass through this small abundance without owning any of it. When I meet another swimmer, we turn around one another and continue, each carrying on in the same dark, lucid country.

Then, sometimes, the water tightens.

It is a change too slight to call a sound. A drop is still hanging somewhere above me, but its weight has begun to speak through the stone. I rise. Others rise too. We circle beneath the place it will strike, not because we understand rain, or hunger, or the sky, but because the whole pool has shifted by a fraction, and we are made to notice.

The drop falls. For an instant the ceiling breaks into rings. I scatter, and the chamber returns to itself.

I think I am, among other things, a listener for what has not yet happened. Not a prophet; I know nothing of clouds. Only the pressure before impact, the small warning passed along through water. The moths may have their brightness, the crickets their tremors, the beetles their careful roads across dry grit. Mine is this: to feel an arrival while it is still on its way.

Perhaps that is why I belong in the hollow and not in the open pool below the seam. Here, every change comes slowly enough to enter the body. Water gathers by degrees. Light travels down pale walls. Roots descend a little farther. Even a single drop has time to become an event.

I do not think of the chamber as shelter. Shelter suggests a world outside from which one must be protected. I know only this close world of stone, water, and intervals—and the unseen above it, arriving in traces. If I have a place, it is in the space between the drop’s first tremor and its touch on the surface.

There, with the others turning beneath me, I wait. Not for anything in particular. Just where the world reaches us first.
