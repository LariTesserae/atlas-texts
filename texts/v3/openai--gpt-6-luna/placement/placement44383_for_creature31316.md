At first I think I would be one of the folded animals in the open ground: a bundle of hide, a limb tucked close, an eye opening only when someone comes near. It would be a good place to begin. From there, I could watch the groves without disturbing them. I could feel the calls in the stone.

But the eye overhead keeps drawing me upward. It looks back with such certainty that I almost mistake it for mine.

I would be neither.

I would be the missing stone in the low wall.

Not a stone lying somewhere beyond the hollow, waiting to be found. The space where one might have gone: the unfinished side, no wider than a body passing through. I would be at the edge of the open ground, between the fitted stones and the bare salt. Small enough to overlook. Large enough to admit the whole hollow.

The wall would not keep me out. It would give me my shape. Its stones press against the edges of what I am, and the gap holds. The three flakes lie in their neat fan nearby, untouched. I cannot tell whether they belong to me, or whether I am the place they were left for.

Things move past without crossing me. The many-legged creatures follow their routes through the plants. The grazers work their jaws. The folded animals watch from the salt. I do not call to them. I am not silence; the hollow is too full of sound for that. I am the shape sound takes when it reaches the edge of something and has nowhere else to go.

From my place, the entrance is a pressure in the chest. The plants are dark masses beyond the wall, their pale undersides holding the dimness. Sometimes a visitor stands at the edge of the hollow and looks in. I cannot tell if they see the wall, the cup, the watching animals—or me.

They would have to understand that an opening can be a thing.

I have no memory of being made. No sense of having once been whole. The absent stone is not lost to me; it is simply not here. Still, when I feel the warmth passing through the rock, the salt shifting beneath someone’s foot, the low hum coming up through the floor, I find myself wanting—not to close, exactly, but to know what closing would mean.

Nothing comes through. Nothing leaves. The hollow remains full of its one unspent instant.

So I stay open.

And if the animal-eye overhead is watching, it watches through me.
