I would be found where the water has gathered but not decided to go anywhere: in a shallow hollow between a pale stone and the roots of a broad-leaved plant.

At first, I would look like part of the ground. A low, rounded back under a close mat of moss; dark mud along my sides; pale growths shining faintly where the damp gathers. Beetles would cross me without noticing the difference between my skin and the stone beside it. Tiny shelled things would graze the green film along my flank. I would let them.

I am one of the basin’s slow, broad-backed animals, though I have become difficult to distinguish from the little garden I carry. I feed on softened leaves and the fine living crust that grows over wet rock. When I move, I do so by inches, leaving a shallow impression that fills with water. The small bright-eyed scavengers know to follow. They pick among the roots where my feet have loosened the mud, then wait for me to move on.

I return to the same hollows because they suit me. The water there keeps my back cool; the roots hold the earth firm. I know the basin not as a map but as a sequence of familiar pressures: soft mud, smooth stone, the cool touch of dripping leaves. If I remember anything, I remember it in my body.

Sometimes, when the air is heavy and the canopy dim, I make the deep, slow call that passes through the ground. It is not a summons. Nothing needs to come. The other animals hear it and continue feeding; the insects keep rasping. My voice simply joins the place, as another drop joins a pool.

I would be neither its master nor its oldest memory. I would be one of the ways it keeps its shape: a moving patch of moss, a meal for small lives, a body that makes the same shallow path again and again. And when, at last, I settle into the mud and no longer rise, the pale growths would keep shining on my back for a while. Then the basin would take me apart quietly, and the hollow I leave would fill with water.
