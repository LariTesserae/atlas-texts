I would begin in a crack.

Not the deepest one, where the stone stays dark and cold, but a narrow seam near the leaning post, where dew gathers before it finds its way below. I would be almost too small to distinguish from a bead of water: pale-bodied, many-legged, sensitive to the minute changes in the stone. I would eat what gathers on the damp edges of things. I would retreat when the sun reached me. I would know the hollow first as pressure, warmth, and vibration.

For a while, I would believe the crack was the world.

Then the stone would ring.

A grain falling from the rim would send its note through my body. Far away, leaves would shiver in answer. I would feel a thousand small feet crossing the bowl—not as separate creatures, but as a loosening and tightening of the ground around me. When the chorus rose after rain, I would not hear it so much as find myself inside it. My own little rasp would be there, though I could not tell it from the others.

I would crawl out.

The hollow would be too bright at first: pale shelves, dark plants, the enormous blue opening overhead. I would follow the wet seams as they wandered over the stone. Other lives would pass me in glints and touches. A flower would open above, and its visitors would make it chime. The long-legged grazer would arrive like a slow change in weather. Its shadow would cross the ground; everything small would scatter. Afterward, we would return through the paths its feet had pressed into the plants.

I would learn that there was no center to the hollow. There were only places where more things could be felt at once.

So I would become, little by little, less like an animal with a home and more like a way of noticing. I would be one small body in the crack, certainly—feeding, molting, hiding from the dry air. But I would also be the tremor passed from one root-mat to another; the warning that travels when the grazer steps down; the faint answering rasp when moisture returns. My life would have edges, but my attention would not.

The dark posts would remain strange to me. I would avoid their smooth surfaces. Yet when the air grew wet and the fine lines appeared, I would feel something change beneath the rock: an old rhythm waking, not a command, but a familiar tilt in the world. The dew would hang. The flowers would open. The tiny bodies around me would turn toward the channels where water was going.

I would follow.

I would not know who had made the posts, or what they had once been for. I would know only that the hollow had learned a habit from them, and that we had learned the habit after the makers were gone.

In the dry intervals, I would draw back into the seam. Above me, the mountain would lie broad and still beneath the sky. Around me, the other small lives would quiet one by one, though never all at once. I would wait with my feelers against the stone.

Then the first bead of water would gather.

I would feel the mountain cool. Somewhere in the plants, a leaf would turn. A creature would move. The note would pass outward, gathering other notes as it went.

I would be a mite in the crack beneath the leaning post.

I would be one small voice in the hollow’s listening.
