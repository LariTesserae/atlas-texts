You would find me first by the light.

Not the lamp I carry, though that is small and usually hooded against the mineral damp. The other light: a narrow yellow line beneath a door in the old water district, where the lower streets vanish into cloud. Behind that door is a room built into the thickness of a wall. One bed, one table, a rack of pins, a map with more blank spaces than names. The room stays warm from a pipe running under the floor. When the city’s vapor presses against the window, the glass clouds over from both sides.

I live there, for now. Or I keep watch there. The distinction has grown difficult.

My district is defined by a cistern and the pipes that feed it. I know the cistern by its changes of voice: the low hollow note when it is nearly empty, the soft ticking at the inlet when water arrives cleanly, the brief, high complaint when mineral scale begins to narrow a throat. I can tell which pump has missed a beat by putting my hand against the wall. I don’t need to hear the machinery. The stone tells me.

This is not a gift. It is what happens when you stay long enough in one place and pay attention.

I used to carry a map. I still do, sometimes, though the older marks are more reliable than the lines. A pale stroke means water has gone where it shouldn’t. A pin driven into the wall means something has stopped, or almost stopped, and might begin again if left alone. There is a doorway in my district where water rises in a trembling sheet, then slips upward into the roof channel. I pass it often. I don’t know why I pause there. Perhaps because the water pauses, too.

I am slight, like the others, and my breath runs short on the higher stairs. I wear close-fitted layers, dark at the cuffs from touching wet stone. I carry a lamp, cord, pins. These are useful things. They make it possible to say what I am doing when someone asks.

But the answer changes with the hour.

At dawn—or what passes for dawn, when the cloud ceiling pales and the towers appear as silhouettes—I am a person going down to check a valve. At night, when the pumps turn quietly beneath the streets, I am an ear pressed against a wall. In the deep cisterns, I am only a lamp moving over the water, careful not to disturb the small transparent animals that gather there. Their whiskers touch the stone. When they surface, they make rings so fine that the water seems to have remembered a falling drop.

Sometimes I think I am a caretaker. This is the most ordinary answer, and maybe the truest. I clear mineral from the narrow passages. I replace a chain worn thin by years of slow work. I listen until a familiar rhythm returns. If it doesn’t, I mark the place and come back.

But there are sounds I cannot put on a map.

Once, beneath the western plaza, I heard a knock traveling through a buried pipe. It came three times, evenly spaced. I followed it down through the culverts, carrying my lamp past the pale microbial threads trembling along the wet stone. The knock stopped whenever I stopped. It began again when I moved. I thought at first it was a loose valve. Then I wondered if the pipe was repeating my footsteps. Then I wondered which of us had started.

I went back the next day with another listener. There was no knock. We cleared nothing, repaired nothing. Still, for a while afterward, I found myself waiting for that sound whenever I came near the plaza. The city has taught me that a silence can be a change, too.

There are people who hear deeper than I do. I know them by reputation, and by the brief glint of their lamps crossing a bridge in the distance. When we meet, we exchange measurements. If one of us has heard a pump falter, the other may ask which district, how long, whether the vibration traveled through the soles or arrived as sound. We speak carefully. It is easier to trust a number than a worry.

I have learned to recognize their habits: the one who leaves a lamp at the foot of dangerous stairs; the one who pauses at the climbing water; the one who never looks up when a shadow passes over the cloud banks. I don’t know whether the shadow is an animal. I don’t know whether it has ever landed. I know only that, when it passes, the vapor above the roofs briefly changes its pressure, and the cistern in my district answers with a note too low for most people to notice.

Perhaps that is where you would find me: not in the room, nor at the cistern, but halfway between them, standing on a wet stair with one hand against the wall. Listening for what has altered. Listening for what has not.

I am still a person. I think so. I have a name, though I use it less than the names of the pipes. I remember the faces of the few people I know, and the way their lamps move when they climb. I can be tired, irritated, hungry. The city has not taken these things from me.

But sometimes, in the long quiet after a repair, I feel the old rhythm resume through the stone. It passes through the soles of my feet, up my legs, into the hand resting on the wall. For a moment, there is no clear boundary between hearing the city and being heard by it.

Then the pump turns. Water finds its passage. I take up my lamp and go home through the clouds.
