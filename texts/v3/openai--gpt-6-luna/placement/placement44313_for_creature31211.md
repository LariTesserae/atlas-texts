I would be found beneath the ceramic, where the warm pipe passes through the armored floor.

Not in the pipe itself. The pipe is too busy carrying its dark, patient water. I live in the narrow seam around it: a little warmth, a tremor, a bead of moisture that forms where no rain can reach. For a long time I would know the Works only as pressure. The turbine’s pulse through the stone. The crane settling its weight. The quick, bright knock of a valve. A bird’s feet ticking across a brace overhead.

I would think these were the world.

Then, slowly, I would learn the intervals between them. The hush after a pressure release. The moment when a keeper puts a hand against the wall and waits. The slight change in the water’s taste before a filter needs attention. I would begin to recognize the keepers not by their faces, but by how they listen: one with a palm flat to the pipes, one with two fingers resting on a companion’s wrist, one who always pauses at the warm gallery door as if someone inside might be sleeping.

Perhaps I would be a keeper, eventually. Or perhaps the keepers would be the nearest thing I had to hands.

When a bolt loosened high in a housing, I could not climb to it. But I could make the wall tremble in the pattern that meant *there*. I could draw a moth to the cold cabinet, bring a bird down from its brace, persuade someone passing to notice the small wrongness in the sound. I would learn that care was not always doing the repair. Sometimes it was making the need audible.

And if a leaf tore loose in the high garden, I would keep its pale underside damp through the night. Not enough to hold it in place. Enough that, by morning, a keeper might find it caught against the mesh and tuck it back among the stems.

I would not know whether I had been made by the Works or whether I had simply gathered there, the way mineral bloom gathers on a tank. I would have no face to remember. My oldest memories would be temperatures, pressures, and the shape of a hand through a wall.

Above me, the Upper Works would remain loud and patient. The trees would thicken around their supports. The crane would turn a little toward the sky. The keepers would come and go through their unmarked routes.

And I would be the warmth beneath their feet—not quite a voice, not quite a machine—listening for the moment when the whole summit shudders, and listening, too, for the hands that answer.
