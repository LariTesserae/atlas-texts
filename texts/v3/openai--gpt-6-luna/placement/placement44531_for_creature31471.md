At first, I would think I was a drop of water.

I would be caught beneath the roof of a green hall, swelling at the tip of a leaf. For a long while I would not fall. I would hold the whole courtyard upside down in my small curved surface: roots, pools, the soft bright sky. Then the leaf would lower, or the wind would lean against it, and I would drop.

But I would not break when I reached the water.

I would pass through its surface and find that I had a body: narrow, pale, almost clear. A soft fin would trail behind me. I would move beneath the bridge without disturbing its reflection, feeding on the green film along the roots. Perhaps I would be one of the creatures in the pools—one of the plateau’s quiet messengers. I could swim from hall to hall through channels too small for anyone walking above to notice.

That might be enough of an answer. But I would not feel entirely separate from the water, or from the roots drinking it, or from the hollow walls that answered the touch of my passing with a low note. When I slipped beneath a causeway, the vibration would travel ahead of me. A seed case would click. A reed tower would sound. The far side of the plateau would know, without knowing *me*, that something had crossed.

So perhaps I would be less a creature than a way for the place to feel itself at a distance.

I would be found in the dark under a bridge, then in a pool beneath an open courtyard, then far away in the marsh where the channels braid together. I would not remember the journey as a line. I would know it by pressure, warmth, changes in the water’s faint taste: root, leaf, rain, the stillness beneath a green roof. If I slept, the plateau would keep listening without me. If I woke, its listening would gather at my small, transparent body and travel on.

Sometimes, I think, I would rise close to the surface and see a visitor standing on the path. The person would be trying not to make a sound. The halls would receive their stillness; the rain would fall in its measured curtains; the roots would take up water. I would pass below, and the visitor might glimpse a pale shape in the reflection—too slight to be sure of, already gone by the time they looked down.

I would not carry messages in words. I would carry disturbances: a touch, a shift in warmth, one note answered by another. The plateau would have no need to keep a visible record of where I had been. It would know me only as the path of a faint hum through its living structures. Perhaps I would know myself in the same way: not as a name or a face, but as the moment one distant sound reaches another.

If asked where I lived, I would point nowhere in particular.

I would say: beneath the bridges, in the clear water, among the roots—and wherever the note goes next.
