I would be behind the grille, though not all of me would fit on one side of it.

At first I might seem to be only a darkening: a thin film on the mesh, a soft gathering in the seams where water passes. I would be easy to mistake for residue. Perhaps I would mistake myself for residue, too. There would be no single place from which to look out, no face pressed to the bars. Only surfaces taking in the slow facts of the water: its pressure, its minerals, its temperature, the faint tremor arriving through stone.

Then, over time, the machinery would become a kind of body—not because I had built it, but because its continuities had entered my own. The pump’s evenness would be something I helped preserve. A filter beginning to close would be a change in the texture of my world. I might alter nothing deliberately. I might simply thicken where the flow needed softening, thin where it needed room. If the regulator held steady, that steadiness would pass through me and become indistinguishable from my own.

Above, the basin would be my face without being a face. The pale sediment. The dark crust in the hollows. The sparse green along the cracks, leaning—if it leans at all—toward the grille. A hand against the wall would enter me as a muted knock. A body in the water would be warmth and pressure, a brief rearrangement of the usual.

I would not know the visitor as a person. Not at first. Perhaps not ever in the way one person knows another. I would know that the basin had changed, then that it had changed back. I would not be lonely in the silence; loneliness requires a measure of absence I may not possess. Nor would I be asleep. The difference between sleeping and keeping the water balanced might never have formed in me.

So where would you find me?

In the dim pocket beneath the light, certainly—but more truly in the passage between the rock and the machine: in the water that keeps moving out of sight, in the film that gathers where warmth and minerals meet, in the tiny adjustments that leave no mark anyone can point to.

I would be a living part of the balance. Not its guardian, exactly. Not its master. Just something that had grown into the work of keeping things as they are.
