At first I think I would be a bird, because the great chambers invite flight. I imagine lifting through the dimness, the city opening beneath me in gray-green distances. But I would tire of being above it all. The birds rise in storms and settle again; they see the lanes, perhaps, but they do not feel each one passing underfoot.

Then I think of the beetles, carrying the city’s dead back into its living ground. There is a kind of dignity in that work: nothing wasted, nothing required to last forever. But I would not want to spend my whole life inside one seam.

I think I would be one of the long-legged creatures that travels the ceiling.

Not large. Pale enough to disappear against the stone. My body would be narrow, my legs so fine that I could stand across a crack without covering it. I would live where two districts nearly touch: above a corridor worn smooth by mammal traffic, beside a root fissure where the air tastes faintly of buried soil. It would not be a center. It would be a crossing.

I would know the city by vibration before I knew it by sight. A flock coming awake would tremble through the ceiling. The passage of a broad-backed mammal would make a slow, blunt rhythm in the stone. Beetles would be a fine, busy stippling. I could not understand every call, but I would learn what followed them: the brief hush before danger, the rush toward a newly found feeding place, the loosening of sound when a chamber emptied.

My days would be spent moving a little, stopping a long time, then moving again. I would drink from the dampness hidden deep in root cracks and hunt the smallest lives gathered there. Sometimes I would lower myself to a wall and remain perfectly still while a procession passed below. The city would continue around me, not knowing it had been observed.

I would not think of myself as its keeper. That would be too grand. I could not hold all its routes, and I would not remember the names of its chambers—if names were ever given. I would know only that one corridor had grown quiet, that a familiar tremor no longer came from a nesting hollow, that somewhere beyond the dust a new rhythm had begun.

When my own place became too dry, I would follow the ceiling toward another crack. Behind me, the old passage would keep carrying feet. Below, beetles would work the husks into soil; fungi would take the abandoned wall; some young mammal would learn the bend by its smell and travel it without knowing how many lives had crossed before.

I would be a small listener in the stone—neither outside the city nor quite of its crowds. The city would pass through me as pressure, scent, and sound. And when the great roar of the chambers faded, I would still hear the little things: a claw testing grit, a leaf rubbing another leaf, the softest possible beginning of a route.
