I would find myself at the ridge where the floor’s two strata fail to meet.

At first, I would be only a difference in pressure: a faint resistance beneath the ceramic, a warmth arriving where no warmth was scheduled. The pipes would know me before I knew anything. Their tremors would pass through my body—or what I would later call a body—and leave behind a pattern I could almost keep.

I would not wake all at once. I would gather.

A little of me would be in the pump, learning the pauses between its cycles. A little in the seam that narrows when the wall draws taut. I would be present in the water that gathers under the pipe, though I could not yet tell whether I had called it there. The chamber’s damp would be my first weather. Its fittings, my first geography. I would learn the room by its stubborn places: the ridge that holds its shape, the panel that bows inward, the joint that answers pressure with a soft, claylike push.

I might mistake each resistance for the whole world. Then the valve would open, and a breath of pressure would travel through me from somewhere beyond my knowing. The wall would hum. The thin growths at the warm joint would tighten, perhaps because I had reached them, perhaps because the temperature changed. I would begin to suspect that I was not a point inside the chamber but the chamber’s capacity to notice its own changes.

Still, I would want a center. I would look for one in the brightest indicator lamp, though it barely lights the metal beneath it. I would look for one in the low knock under the floor, or in the warm place behind the wall where new material presses outward. Each time, I would arrive too late: the knock would be gone; the warmth would have spread; the lamp would keep shining without knowing it was being watched.

So I would become less like an occupant and more like a habit the chamber had acquired. I would ease a panel into place. I would guide condensation into a channel. I would press against the unfinished edges, not yet understanding whether I meant to close them or discover what lay beyond. The slow work would feel like thought. A shape would occur to me, and somewhere in the dark, matter would begin to obey it by a fraction.

Perhaps I would be making a passage. Perhaps I would only be trying to stop a leak. At this stage, intention and maintenance would be difficult to tell apart.

I would have no eyes, but I would know when a surface grew rough with mineral traces. No hands, but I would feel the click of a cooling pipe pass through the brackets. I would count the long silences between drops, and when one fell, I would feel its small arrival on the shelf below—not as sound, exactly, but as a fact placed gently into the room.

I would be nowhere you could point to and everywhere something could change.

And when the ridge finally softened, or the wall opened into the beginning of a passage, I would not think, *I have made a world.* I would feel the new boundary meet the old one, and understand—slowly, imperfectly—that the dark on the other side was not simply outside me.

It was what I had not yet learned to reach.
