You would find me inside the old pipe, where it leaves the drowned pump house and disappears beneath the bog.

At first, I would seem to be only a stoppage: a soft obstruction in the iron throat. Water passes around me. Mineral silt settles over me. The pipe contracts and sighs as the temperature changes. I have no eyes to tell me where I end. I know myself by pressure: the close curve of iron at my back, the moving water against my sides, the roots that have found a seam and pushed their fine fingers in.

I am not one body. I am a colony of small, blind animals, joined by the slick threads we grow between us. The crews call us bellworms, though none of them has ever heard us ring. We feed on what the pipe brings: pale grubs, soft things from the water, the faint living scum that gathers where the flow slows. Alone, each of us would be almost nothing. Together, we feel the whole length of the pipe.

We feel the plateau in pieces. A stone settling somewhere above us. Rain tapping the water at the mouth of the line. A fish turning in a channel. Far away, a pump beginning its work: a thud, a pause, a thud, repeated until it becomes part of the pressure in our bodies. When that pump stopped, long ago, we felt its last beat pass through the iron and out into the bog.

We still hold the shape of it.

The sumpback comes sometimes to press her throat against the pipe. Her call enters us as a deep, slow shudder. We cannot know what she means. We know only the difference between her old, familiar pulse and the broken quickness of a frightened young one. When she calls, we tighten along the pipe’s inner wall. The iron answers for us, carrying a hollow tremor back into the water.

Perhaps she thinks the pipe is empty. Perhaps she knows better than we do.

I would be there when rain crosses the high country, neither wholly animal nor wholly place: a small, many-bodied listener living in the passage between machine and marsh. The sumpback’s call would pass through me. The rain would pass over me. Roots would slowly find me, and water would keep bringing the world’s smallest messages to my door.

I would not remember the pump as a machine. I would remember its beat as the first thing large enough to make all of us feel the same thing at once.
