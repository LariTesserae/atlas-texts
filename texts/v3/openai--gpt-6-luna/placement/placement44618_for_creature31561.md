I think I would be found beneath the third step down from the eastern terrace, where the stone stays cool even when the daylight reaches it.

At first I imagine myself up high, among the bats, watching the city from the roof’s raw wounds. But the ceiling is too vast, and the people look up too often there. I would rather live where their attention goes when they are not thinking about looking: at their feet, at the dark seam between stones, at the water’s small changes.

I would be a newt—broad-headed, dark-backed, nearly still. Not an omen. Not anyone’s pet. Just an old neighbor, with pale fingers resting on the grit of the channel and my belly held close to the rock. I would know the city through its underside: the warmth left by a sunlit wall, the tremor of a cart crossing a bridge, the new taste of water after rain. A channel that runs clear today may carry earth tomorrow. A step that has always been dry may begin to shine.

The children would learn where I rest. They would lift their feet carefully on that stair, though no one had told them to. One might leave me a beetle in the shallow water. I would not eat it while watched.

I would know no names for the terraces, but I would know their routes. Water comes down the western wall first. A root has opened a narrow crack above the old roofs. The lowest passage floods before the garden court, except when rain comes hard from the south. People have their own maps of these things: chalk marks, remembered floods, the feel of a doorframe gone damp. Mine would be written against my skin.

When the pools begin to rise, I would leave the step.

I would slip along the channels beneath the paving, past the pale fish holding steady in the current, toward a fracture in the wall where the stone is rough enough to climb. I would find a higher hollow and wait there, pressed into the wet dark while the city lifts its baskets and mats. Later, when the water withdraws, I would return by a different route. The city is always making new ones.

So I would not be its guardian. I could not stop a flood or hold a roof together. I would be one small sign among many that the place is changing: a dark shape under a familiar stair, gone when the water comes, back when it has passed.

And if someone paused there in the dimness, listening to the channels, they might feel the stone shift faintly beneath their hand.

That would be me, coming home.
