I would be found beneath the half-sunk wall, where one of the hand-cut channels narrows to a seam in the stone.

At first, I would not look like anything that could be found. A pale ridge, perhaps: one more old fold of mineral, softened by water and laid against the dark plates. A bead would gather above me and fall. The tremor would pass through the ledge. I would remain still.

I would be a small animal, though “animal” would be a shape borrowed from someone passing overhead. I have no eyes to turn toward the dim light, no voice to carry across the basin. I know the world as pressure and warmth: the slow weight of water against my back, the faint change when a foot settles on the shelf, the difference between the sheltering dark beneath a ledge and the exposed shallows.

My body is soft, but it has learned the appearance of stone. Mineral gathers along my spine in thin, irregular plates. When I rest, they hold the basin’s dull silver; when water passes over them, they darken until I belong again to the seam. I do not intend to hide. I simply do not need to be noticed.

There is food here, though little of it announces itself. In cracks too narrow for light, a faint slick gathers and thins. I graze it slowly. Sometimes I follow the old channels toward the catchment hollow, feeling their shallow course through my limbs. The stonework means nothing to me as work. But water holds its shape there, and so I follow.

I might spend a long time in one place. Days, if that is what they are. The basin’s measure is not the measure of a moving creature. A falling drop is an event. A visitor is weather. When the disturbance has passed, I reach forward, set one delicate limb down, and let the rest of me follow. Crossing the width of a hand takes as long as it takes.

If you found me, you might think the basin had begun to move. You would see the pale ridge draw in its edges; a small body loosen itself from the mineral floor. Then I would slip beneath the ledge, where the water is warm and the pressure of the world arrives gently.

I am not the keeper of this place. I do not remember who built the wall, or why the shelter stands open to the basin. I live where the water stays, in the narrow interval between stone and silence. If the basin changes, I will feel the change before I understand it. I will move a little farther into the seam.

And when I am still, the basin will look empty again.
