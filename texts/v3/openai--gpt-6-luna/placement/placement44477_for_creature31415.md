At first, I would put myself on one of the ridges, in a low shelter with a membrane door. I would have a stone knife and a habit of looking up whenever water began to tick through the stalks. I would be one of the people who renews the notches after a storm, because a crossing forgotten is more dangerous than a crossing lost.

But I don’t think I would stay a person.

I think I would become the crossing itself: a bridge of braided roots stretched over a narrow ravine, where the stone has split cleanly and the air moves through with a low, steady breath. I would begin as a few strands reaching out from either side. Rain would darken them; wind would test them. The plants would thicken where the strands held, and slowly there would be a way across.

I would know the people by their weight. A careful foot, then another: someone carrying water. A child who stops in the middle and leans over to look down. The pause of an adult listening for the storm. Their footsteps would not be words, but they would leave a shape in me. I would learn the difference between someone hurrying home and someone who has come to see whether I still hold.

I would not remember them as people do. I would remember pressure, warmth, the small tremors of a crossing. If someone cut a notch into the stone beside me, I would feel the blows travel through the ridge. If the notch were renewed after a storm, I would know that someone had returned.

The rest of the country would reach me indirectly. A change in the roots. A tightening far beneath the ravine. The gardens bowing in order, though the wind had not yet arrived. I would feel a storm approach as a growing tension between my two ends. When it came, I might bend until the ravine seemed to widen; perhaps a strand would break. I would not call it fear. I would know only the strain, and then the sudden release.

If I failed, the people would find another way across. They would cut fresh notches on another ridge, and I would grow toward the empty air again. I would not think of this as being abandoned. The plants do not keep their shapes forever. A crossing is not a promise; it is something made, tested, and made again.

So I would be neither wholly a plant nor wholly a path. A place where the country briefly agrees to bear a person’s weight. A living span between two stones, listening through its roots for weather—and, now and then, for someone coming home.
