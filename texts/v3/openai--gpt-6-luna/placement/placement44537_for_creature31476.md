At first I would be mistaken for rust.

I would live in the seam of a pump, where warm water passes again and again through a pipe that leads nowhere. A soft stain around a rivet. A pale thread under the metal lip. I would have no face to turn toward the dark, and no need to. I would know the pump by the shiver it sends through the wall before the chain lifts, by the brief change in taste when rain finds a crack in the ground.

I would not be one thing for long. A bead would break loose and travel down the pipe. A thread would take hold in the mud. Small hard-limbed creatures would tap at me as they passed, and I would answer—not in words, but with the faintest alteration of pressure. The larger ones would feel it through their many feet and pause beside the machine. For a while, my seam would become a meeting place: bodies resting against the warm wall, water moving through them, the pump turning without knowing it had company.

Perhaps I would be the pump’s memory. Not a memory of what it was built to do; I could not reach that far. I would remember its rhythms: lift, sink; lift, sink. I would remember the rain by its coolness, and each creature by the small disturbance it left in the water. If a body passed twice, I would know the second passage from the first, though I could not say how.

Eventually, the stain would spread. It would creep from the rivets into the soil, join the dark mats under the brittle crust, find its way along the channels. I would not feel myself traveling. There would only be more places where the water carried my taste, more walls that trembled with the turning engines, more mud that held a pressure for a moment after a body had gone.

Then the heavy knock would come from below.

I would feel it everywhere I had become: in the chain, in the warm pipe, in the small shells nested under the housing. The little creatures would vanish into the gears. The broad ones would press themselves low against the ground. I would not know what had made the sound. I would only hold its shape—the depth of it, the pause that followed—and pass that shape outward through the water.

If I had a body, it would be the place between things: metal and mud, machine and animal, the drop of rain and the ground receiving it. If I had a self, it would be the listening that persists after the sound has been covered over.

I would be there when the pump began its next slow turn. Not waiting for light. Not expecting an answer. Only keeping, in the damp seam of the world, a small record of what has touched it.
