I think I would begin as one of the little ones.

A pale, narrow body beneath a hanging frond, quick between wet seams. I would feed on the softness gathered there: the moss, the mineral dust, the film of life that forms where stone sweats. When the veins brightened, I would flatten myself against the rock. I would know to be still before I knew what the light meant.

Then, slowly, the distinction between me and the place I hid would begin to blur.

The frond’s root would brush my back. A faint charge would pass through its slick surface and into the seam beneath us—not through me, perhaps, but close enough that I would feel it as a change in the air, a pressure behind the eyes. I would learn the plateau by these small translations: mist gathering; a grazer’s weight shifting on the shelf above; the deep, patient tremor travelling up through stone.

I would not be the vast thing beneath the country. I would only be one of its nearly inaudible sensations.

I would live where a root mat met a blue-green vein, under an overhang whose lip caught the mist. In daylight—if the cloud thinned enough to call it that—I would look like a little patch of dark growth, scarcely different from the stains on the rock. At night I would show myself in fragments: a glimmer along a root, a spark passing between leaves, a pale shape vanishing into a crack.

Perhaps I would spend years believing I was one creature. Then, in the long dark, I would feel another of me answering from a neighboring ledge. Not a voice. More like the continuation of a thought I hadn’t known I was thinking. The charge would travel through the fronds, and the stone would take it farther than any small body could go. I would learn that I was not alone—not because there were others like me, exactly, but because the boundary of “me” had never been where I thought it was.

I would be a listener made of living tissue and borrowed current: part animal, part root, part tiny relay in the mountain’s great silence. I could not understand the deep process any better than the grazers could. When pressure gathered below, I would feel it coming. When vapor rose, I would open to drink. When the seams flared, I would pass the brightness along, not knowing whether I was carrying a message or merely answering one.

And still, I would remain small.

A hunter’s shadow would cross the shelf; I would press flat. The wind would comb the plateau; I would hold to the stone. The grazers would come to feed, leaving one cleft untouched, and I would wait through their stillness for whatever season had called them there. I would not command the world, or comprehend it. I would belong to its faintest exchanges.

If you searched for me, you might find only a wet hollow under a black ledge: fronds bending though there was no wind, a scatter of pinprick tracks, one seam glowing softly beneath them.

Then the light would fade, and I would go quiet with everything else.
