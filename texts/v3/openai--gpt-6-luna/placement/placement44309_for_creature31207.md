You would find me where the current changes its mind: on the sheltered side of a low ridge, beneath a stone too ordinary to notice. Silt has gathered there in a shallow lip. It keeps the worst of the flow from my body, though not its news.

At first I would seem to be part of the stone. A pale, folded thing, plated in dull little scales, pressed so close to the ground that the water passes over me without finding an edge. When grit begins to fall, I draw inward. The sediment settles across my back. Soon there is only another nodule in the basin, with a small darkness beneath it.

But that is not all of me.

Fine living threads run from beneath the stone into the packed gravel. They pass through seams and hollows, joining other threads, though none of us can follow them all. Some end in feeding crowns that lift briefly into the current; some lie dormant under crust and rock. I belong to this spread-out life—not as a limb belongs to a body, exactly, but as a sheltered place belongs to the basin. If one part is uncovered, another may feel the change. If a small creature grazes nearby, the disturbance travels through us as a faint alteration in pressure.

I am also the small body beneath the stone: a mouth, perhaps, and a pair of feelers I extend when the water carries something worth tasting. I have no need to decide which is the truer self. The threads do not make me less an animal. The body does not make the threads less mine.

I know the basin by what touches me. The drag of silt across a plate. A pebble settling against my side. A vibration traveling a long way through the floor, softened until it is barely more than the suggestion of movement. I do not see the ridges as distant shapes. I know where they are because the current lifts differently there; because one trough gathers the fine dust, while another stays scoured; because the water brings one kind of food from the left and another from above.

Now and then I send a small light from beneath my plates: blue-green, brief, almost no light at all. It does not reveal me. It says only: here. Perhaps another hidden body answers from the next crack. Perhaps no one does. The signal fades before it can become a landscape.

I am very old in the way the basin is old—not because I remember its ages, but because I change at their pace. A current may bury a feeding crown, and the network will close around the loss. A stone may shift. A new hollow may open. I do not hold to the exact shape I had before. What remains is the reaching: into seams, beneath gravel, toward whatever the water has brought.

So you would find me by an absence. A little patch where the silt does not settle quite as it should. A stone that lifts, almost imperceptibly, when the current turns.

And I would be neither hidden animal nor spreading life alone, but the narrow, patient place where the two meet: a body listening through the floor, waiting for the water to change.
