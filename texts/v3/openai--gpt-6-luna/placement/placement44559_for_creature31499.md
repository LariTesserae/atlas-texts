I would be found first in the seam beneath a catwalk, where the lamp-light does not quite reach.

At first I might seem like a scrap of shed skin caught on a rivet. Then the scrap would move: a pale, many-footed thing easing itself along the stone, reflective eyes taking in almost no light. I would be one of the climbers, though “one” would not mean much. There would be others in the cracks around me, and others in the wall beyond those cracks. We would share the warm places without needing to agree that they were ours.

I would know the Pocket by touch. The wall’s faint shudder when a pump takes hold. The tremble in a cable before its carriage begins to move. The different small complaints of a bridge settling under a passing person. I would not know the landings by number. I would know them by temperature, vibration, and the taste of dust.

When something fell, I would follow.

Not because I could catch it. I could not. I would go down after its little sequence of impacts, crossing the underside of a stair, slipping through a crack, descending by a route no person could use. Each strike would tell me where the falling thing had been. Each pause would tell me it had found another ledge—or was still on its way.

The people might take me for an omen. They would notice us spilling out of a wall before a pressure change and say we had warned them. Sometimes that would be true in the way a lamp flickering twice is a warning: we had felt the change, but we had not named it. More often, we would simply be moving because the air had shifted, or because something warm had opened nearby.

A child might leave powdered metal beside my seam. I would come for it after the child had gone. The child might decide this meant I trusted them. I would not know what trust was, exactly. But I might learn the child’s footfall, and come out sooner when I heard it.

I would live in the intervals the people cannot easily occupy: behind a machine’s casing, inside the warm throat of a duct, in the narrow dark between two plates of young stone. A sleeve left hanging overnight would be a room. A dropped bolt would be a journey. The Upper Pocket would not feel immeasurable to me. It would be a world made of passages, each one ending in another passage.

Still, there would be one thing I understood that the people also understood: nothing is gone just because you can no longer see it. So I would go after the falling sound until it stopped. At the last impact, I would pause with all my feet against the stone.

Then I would turn upward again, carrying nothing anyone could see—only the new shape of the noise, held in my body like a map.
