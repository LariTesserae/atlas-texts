This is a place of quiet strangeness: a high-altitude, submerged cathedral of living growth, where the air is thick with water and the world is draped in slow, breathing green—yet not quite still.

You descend not by foot, but by slow drift—suspended in a briny haze. The sky is a ceiling of liquid mist, a shallow sky that shimmers with a milky green glow, like a world viewed through frosted glass. Beyond, the light weakens and the sea deepens, not in the usual vertical sense, but in layers—strata of water stacked like sedimentary pages, each one holding the memory of a different kind of wetness. This is not water as you know it—no simple ocean. The atmosphere itself is a viscous liquid: a supercooled aqueous fog that condenses to a slick sheath on every surface, yet floats as breathable air. It is cold, yes—1.3 on the thermal scale, a damp chill that seeps into the bone and yet never freezes. You feel it less as cold than as absence: a void in warmth, a quiet hum from within the liquid.

Around you, the terrain is not terrain at all, but a forest of immense, slow-rising organisms. These are not trees, not exactly. They resemble the skeletal remnants of drowned forests, but alive—fusing, pulsing. Their trunks are columns of interwoven moss and fibrous, rubbery tissue, rising from the seafloor like cathedral pillars or organ pipes, yet their canopies are absent. Instead, the tops have dissolved into the water, becoming clouds of gelatinous fronds that drift and float, feeding on dissolved minerals and the faint electromagnetic pulses of the world below.

Vegetation: 2.9/3 — near total. This is not a forest; it is a *living architecture*. The floor, where it exists, is a thin layer of algae-laden silt atop a porous rock base, but it’s barely a base. Structures grow up from it not to support the sky, but to fill the space between sky and floor—each form a different kind of vessel for life. Some are hollow, filled with glowing, bioluminescent sludge that pulses in slow, rhythmic waves; others are dense networks of vein-like stems, forming natural scaffolding, like the ruins of a lost city built from living things.

And yes, there is *built*—2.3/3. But not in the usual sense of cities. The human-scale structures are not separate from the forest. They are embedded, grown into it. Wooden frames—crafted from hard, dark sapwood harvested from a rare species that grows only in waterlogged zones—rise from the silt, but are quickly engulfed by living moss that climbs them, forms roofs, and knits the buildings into a seamless lattice of organic and constructed space. Houses are not sealed; they breathe. The walls are translucent membranes of layered lichen and resin, allowing water to seep in and out, exchanging nutrients. There are no doors, only archways grown from thick stalks of aquatic reeds, their ends sealed by bio-gel caps that open and close like eyes. The entire settlement is a sort of *co-symbiosis*: humanity has not claimed this space; it has entered it as a participant.

Yet the place feels sparse—density: 0.9. The structures are scattered. Not empty, but *rare*. You move through vast green aisles—galleys of suspended growth—where the only sounds are the soft *plip-plip* of condensation on fronds, the low drone of wind through the liquid sky, and then, suddenly, a burst of noise: the *thump-thrum* of a massive root shifting, or the chorus of thousands of small creatures scraping against the stems.

Fauna: 2.3/3 — abundant — and strange. The water isn’t just fluid; it’s a *medium*. Swarms of small, iridescent fish-like things (not fish, exactly—more like swimming jellyfish with fins) flit through the water, leaving trails of glowing silt behind them. Some of them are intelligent: they communicate in pulses of light, and if you linger too long in one spot, they will gather around you, swirling like living dust, probing with delicate bioluminescent tendrils. Larger creatures—some the size of small whales—drift in slow spirals, their bodies fused with the living architecture. Their hides are covered in moss, and their eyes—when they open—are not pupils but clusters of glowing green orbs, blinking like stars. They do not attack, but nor do they avoid. They *observe* the world as it exists, slowly, patiently.

Sound: 2.3/3 — loud, but not in a human sense. The noise is not of machines or voices. It is the *wetness* of the world. The constant *drip-drip-drip* of water condensing off structures, the groan of roots as they expand and contract with seasonal shifts, the deep, subsonic hum from a vast fungal network that underlies every living thing, sending signals across the substrate like a neural web. At night, the sound shifts—what is not night in the traditional sense, but a dimming of the sky's glow—when the fungi release spores in synchronized pulses, producing a kind of low-frequency singing, like a vast organism breathing.

There are no sudden storms, no tidal surges—dynamic: 0.8/3 — slow. The world changes on a scale larger than human perception. The structures grow by millimeters per decade. The canopy shifts with the subtle drifts of water-borne minerals. The sky is not stable—it ebbs and flows like a tide, thickening and thinning, sometimes clearing to reveal a deep, starless void beyond, other times collapsing into a deluge that floods the surface for days. But it is always returning, always regaining balance.

Temperature is cool—1.3—but not harsh. There is no wind, but the water flows in slow, laminar currents, carrying heat and nutrients from deep below. It is like a great, ancient lung: breathing in and out in centuries-long cycles.

Light is normal—1.7/3: not dim, not dazzling. The glow comes from the sky, from the plants, from the animals. No sun, no moon—just a persistent, ambient luminescence that bathes the whole world in a soft green-yellow light, like twilight on a foggy afternoon that never ends.

Weirdness: 0.9/3 — unusual, but not impossible. This is not a dream, not a myth. It feels *real*—a place that has simply evolved differently. It does not defy reason, but it does not bow to human expectations. You cannot walk here. You float. You drift. You breathe the moist, salty air and feel it seep into your skin. You learn to listen. The people here do not build roads. They grow walkways—vines knotted into bridges, or hollowed-out trunks that serve as tunnels. Their tools are simple—wood, bone, stone, shaped by hand—but they use them with great care. They do not cut indiscriminately. They *invite* growth. They leave gaps in the architecture so the forest can reclaim it. Their buildings are not meant to last forever; they are meant to be consumed, transformed, reborn.

And yet, this place is not peaceful. It is not serene. It is alive. And life, when it is not rushed, when it is not forced, when it is simply *allowed*, becomes something patient, persistent, and profoundly strange.

Here, the world is not just *over* water—it *is* water. And everything else is just what floats in it.
