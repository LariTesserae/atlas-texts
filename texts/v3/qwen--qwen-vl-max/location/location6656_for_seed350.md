The world is a vast, liquid cathedral, but the cathedral is drowning. Water—not just water, but the water itself—has become a living, breathing organism of immense scale, rising beyond the sky, folding down like a thick, translucent fabric that drowns the world from above as much as it inundates it from below. It is not simply wet; it is *submerged* in the way that time is submerged in memory—everything is held in suspension, in a slow, viscous moment of becoming.

The terrain is low, barely perceptible beneath the churning sea of water. But what remains—what has clawed through the flood—are ridges of coral-like stone, jagged and new: **not geologically young**, but *biologically* emergent. These spines of rock are not rock at all, but calcified organs—fossilized pulses of a deeper biology. They rise like the vertebrae of some long-dead leviathan, their flanks grooved with veins of bioluminescent algae that glow in slow, pulsing cadence—green, violet, faint pink—like the inside of a nervous system. They are **eroded not by time, but by pressure**—by the ceaseless, silent crushing of the water, or perhaps by something deeper: the weight of unmade things. Their tips are sharp, fresh, as if the world is still being sculpted by a god who hasn’t yet finished its work.

Above and below and in between, **the water is thick with vegetation**, not just floating or growing, but *breathing* in the same way a forest absorbs light. There are no trees, but vast, rootless sponges that swell like lungs, drifting in slow spirals, absorbing and emitting light. These are **planktonic forests**, composed of gelatinous fronds, translucent filaments, and bulbous sacs that pulse with trapped air and slow-churning bioluminescence. They form **layers of density**, so thick they create a haze of color, like a reef seen through the wrong kind of lens—everything is refracted, blurred, saturated. At the surface, the water is so thick with suspended plant matter that light cannot pass through—only *down*, and only in fractured beams, like fingers of a dying god.

The **light itself is dim**, but not absent. It doesn’t come from the sun; the sky is obscured by the water, which now forms a dense, sky-high mist of rain-liquid—rain falling from a sky that is also the sea, and the sea that is also the sky. Light filters down from the **deafening, industrial heart of the world**—a vast, submerged complex of rusted pipes, broken elevators, and cracked conduits that glow with the sickly blue-green pulse of submerged electricity. **Light is not a presence—it is a vibration.** It hums through the water, vibrating the plant matter, making the algae flicker in sync with the deep thrum of machinery that is *somewhere*. Not *under*—*not* under. *All around*. It is the **sound** that anchors the place: a **deafening** orchestra of metallic groans, deep harmonic drones, the hiss of leaking pressure, the rhythmic *clank-clank-clank* of something enormous moving through the water, and above it all—**the scream of water**. Not waves, not storms. The *water itself is screaming*, as if it were a living thing in pain, or in ecstasy.

The **temperature is warm**, but not from the sun. The water is heated by the **internal fire of the world**—a subterranean engine, perhaps, or the byproduct of a civilization that tried to power the ocean itself. Thermal vents spew out not steam, but glowing, gelatinous blobs of organic sludge that drift in slow spirals, consumed by the floating forests. The warmth is felt in the skin, in the bones—like standing inside a womb. It is **comfy**, but also terrifying: it makes the body feel *too relaxed*, too open, too *available*.

This is a place of **high density**, but not crowded. It is *layered*—the water, the plants, the machinery, the light, the sound—each layer pressing against the next, yet never quite touching. There is **no fauna**. No fish, no birds, no insects. Nothing that moves under its own volition. But the world *moves*. The ocean is not still. It flows in a **turbulent loop**, folding back on itself, forming whirlpools that don’t drain, but *grow*. Water rises into the atmosphere, becomes mist, forms clouds of liquid that fall back down as rain, but only to be absorbed again by the plants, the machines, the stone. It is **chaotic**, **storm state**, **hyperactive**—like a bazaar of the subconscious, where nothing is stable, and every second something new is born and dies.

And then there are the **buildings**—not cities, not towns, but **scattered monuments**—partial remains, single structures, half-sunken factories with their roofs split open like jaws, revealing interiors that are glowing with electrical veins. Some are made of metal, others of glass, others of a strange, porous stone that absorbs light and emits low-frequency sound. They are not *built*. They are **grown**. Or perhaps *forged* by the water, by the light, by the pressure. They are **artifacts**, but not created by humans—more like *echoes* of human intention, imprinted on the world’s fabric.

But the **weirdness** is the true ruler. The **reality is broken**. Shapes change. A building may be a factory one moment, a forest the next, a lung, a heart, a mouth. The water sometimes *thickens* into solid gel, forming temporary landforms that dissolve minutes later. The light can be heard: you *feel* it in your ears. The sound can be seen: vibrations in the air form patterns that you can *touch*. Time is not linear. You may see a structure that was destroyed years ago—only to realize that you are *inside* it, and it is now functioning. You may *remember* a moment that has not happened. The **dynamic** is not just stormy, it is **self-creating**. The world is *making itself* in real time, constantly, chaotically, joyfully, tragically.

The **scale** is neither small nor vast—it is *middle*. You can see the curvature of the water, the horizon of the sea-mist, but it feels close. The sky is not high; it is *overhead*, pressing down. You are not a speck. You are a *participant*, and the world is *aware*. It *knows* you are there. It reacts to your presence.

This is a world that has **no top, no bottom, no inside, no outside**. It is a **dream of an old god of water and industry**, one that has awakened too late, too deeply, and now dreams with the **deafening noise of its own heart**.

It is **alive**. It is **angry**. It is **beautiful**. And it wants to be **remade**.

And in the distance, beneath the light, beneath the plants, beneath the screaming water, there is a **single, small, rusted door**—half-submerged, leaking electricity, its handle warm to the touch—leading nowhere. Or perhaps somewhere. Or perhaps into the world itself.

You stand here, and the water rises to your neck. The light hums in your bones. The silence is a scream. And somewhere, deep within the liquid cathedral, a machine *pulsates*, and a forest *breathes*, and the world *shakes* with the weight of what it is becoming.
