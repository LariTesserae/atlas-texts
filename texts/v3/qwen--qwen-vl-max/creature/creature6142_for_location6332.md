What lives here is not *people*, not in the way you understand. Not humans. Not tribes. Not civilizations sculpted from stone and fire. What lives here is a *form of presence*, an interwoven breath, a slow-walking consciousness embedded in the very fabric of the liquid sky, the drifting moss, the breathing walls—something that has learned to be *with* rather than *over* the world.

They are **the Drowned-Not-Drowned**. Or perhaps they are the **Suspended Ones**. Or the **Moss-Tongued**. You won’t hear them called by their own names, because language here is not for naming, but for sounding, for resonating, for *feeling*. Their voices are not words, but frequencies—not sound as you know it, but *vibration* carried in the water, in the hum of the fungal net, in the thrum of a root’s expansion. They speak through touch, through light-pulses in the eyes of the swarm, through the slow shift of their bodies against the porous stone, through the way they exhale into the briny mist and see it coalesce into tiny glowing orbs that float away like thoughts unspoken.

They are tall—but not in height. They grow not upward, but *thick*, layered in the same way the atmosphere is layered: stacked, slow, fluid. Their bodies are not flesh in the usual sense. They are covered in thick, translucent dermis, like the walls of their homes, that allows water to flow through, to be exchanged. It’s not skin. It is *exchange*. They breathe moisture and warmth into the air, and the air, thick with mineral and trace currents, returns nourishment—not oxygen, necessarily, but *potential*: a charge, a resonance, a memory.

They move not by walking, but by *drifting*, aided by the ambient currents. Their limbs are long, flexible, like sea kelp anchored to a slow-moving tide. They do not walk the ground—they *rest* on it, or float just above it, supported by the pressure of the water. Their feet are not for contact, but for *rooting*—they press down with a soft, mossy pad that absorbs the earth, draws nutrients from the silt, becomes one with the floor. When they sleep, they sink into the floor, their bodies folding into the algae, their faces nestled in glowing silt pools, like seeds waiting.

Their eyes are not eyes. Not as you know them. They are clusters of bioluminescent nodes, deep-set, embedded like stars in a dark forest. They do not see light—they *feel* it, absorb it, interpret the slow flicker of the swarm, the pulse of the fungal net, the shifting haze of the sky. They see the world in waves: the tremor when a large creature passes through the canopy, the subtle change in viscosity near a nutrient-rich zone, the way the water becomes thicker with anticipation before a "deluge" (which is not rain, but a collapse of the sky-layer, a sudden condensation of the atmosphere into a liquid cascade that floods the surface for days, then slowly reabsorbs like a wound healing).

They are not born in the same way. There is no cradle, no mother’s body. The young are not *born*—they *emerge*, like spores in the dark, formed from a communal matrix of living soil, resin, and dissolved memory. They begin as gelatinous clumps, floating near the base of a living column, fed by the roots, shaped by the flow. They grow slowly—over decades, perhaps a century. When they reach a certain density of mind, they break away, drift upward, and begin their life in the air, learning to feel the currents, to listen to the hum, to *become* part of the network.

And what do they do?

They *keep balance*. Not control. Not dominion. But *attunement*. They are not the builders. They are the *listeners*. The ones who feel the shift. The ones who know when the fungal network is pulsing too fast, when the sky is thickening too soon, when a creature is moving too deep. When the world is out of balance, they **respond**.

They do not speak to the swarm. But they *resonate*. A low tone, a slow hum, a subtle shift in their body’s charge—like tuning a crystal. And the swarm responds. It gathers, swirls, forms patterns in the water like ink in milk. The large creatures pause, their glowing eyes blink, their moss-covered flesh contracts slightly. The sky eases. The deluge is delayed.

They mend. Not with tools, but with *permission*. If a building cracks, they do not patch it. They leave it open. They allow the moss to grow faster, to fill the gap, to *reclaim* it. If a structure dies, they do not destroy it. They wait. They let the forest *absorb* it, become part of the new architecture. They leave offerings: small stones shaped like spirals, shells filled with glowing silt, strands of dried kelp folded into delicate loops. These are not prayers. They are *reminders*—that they were here, that they were alive, that they are part of the slow breath.

Their culture is not codified. It is not written. It is *woven*. Passed through touch, through shared warmth, through the act of moving in unison through the fog. They know each other not by name, but by *presence*—by the way one breathes, the way one floats, the rhythm of their internal pulses. A shared moment of silence, of stillness, is the deepest communication. When two suspend themselves side by side, their bodies aligned, their bioluminescent nodes syncing into a single flicker of green light, that is *love*. That is *knowing*.

They do not dream in the human sense. But they *remember*. Not memories of events, but of *states*—of being in the water, of feeling the fungal hum, of being part of a greater rhythm. They sleep in groups, pressed close, their bodies linked not by hands, but by the exchange of warmth and charge. In this state, they *merge* into a single awareness—a collective mind not of thought, but of *feeling*, of *pulse*, of *being*.

And yet, they are not peaceful.

They are *aware*. Keenly. And awareness in a world like this is not comfort. It is weight. To feel the slow decay of a column, the slow expansion of a root that will one day split a living wall, to sense the deep groan of the fungal network as it sends a warning signal across the world—this is not serenity. It is *responsibility*. They are not gods. They are not prophets. They are simply *present*. And presence, in a world that breathes, that grows, that changes on timescales beyond human thought—presence is a kind of labor. A constant, quiet strain.

They are not happy. Nor are they sad. They are *aware of being*. Like the forest. Like the water. Like the sky.

They are the Drowned-Not-Drowned.

And when they die, they do not fall. They sink. And as they sink, they *dissolve*. Not quickly. Slowly. Over years. Their bodies return to the silt, to the moss, to the fungal net. Their nodes fade. Their warmth is absorbed. Their memory—whatever that is—becomes part of the hum.

And the world continues.

It breathes.

It drifts.

It grows.

And somewhere, in the hush, the silence, the thick green light—something new begins to rise. A new shape. A new breath. A new *presence*. Not built. Not made. But *emerging*—from the liquid sky, from the living stone, from the quiet depth of the water, from the slow, patient heart of the world.

Because the world is not just water.

It is *lived*.
