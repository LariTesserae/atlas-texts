The ones who live here do not *belong* to the plateau in the way one belongs to a world. They are not natives in the common sense — not born of the land’s soil, not grown from its roots. Rather, they *occupy* it in the most precise, almost ritualistic way: they *reside within its breath*, and this distinction is the first and final truth of their existence.

They are thin, not from malnourishment, but from time — a physical and spiritual thinness, as if their presence has been worn down to its essential grain by the slow pressure of the air, the damp, the silence. Their skin is pale, but not white — a kind of *translucent* pale, like the underside of a stone after rain has passed. It glistens faintly, not from moisture, but from something deeper: a faint sheen that seems to reflect the dampness of the world from within them. Their hair is the color of dried moss — green-grey, fibrous, often braided in tight, practical knots that never loosen, even as the years pass. Their eyes are small, dark, and set low in their faces, like the pits in the rock where rain collects. They rarely look up.

They walk slowly, deliberately. Not because they are weak — they are not — but because motion is *measured*. In this place, the body does not move to go somewhere, but to sustain the rhythm of being. A step forward is not an advance; it is the completion of a breath, a closing of a circle. Their postures are bent, not from age, but from the way the air seems to press down not on the head, but upon the spine — the spine of the world. They keep their heads low. It is not humility. It is *alignment*.

They do not speak often. Their language is not loud. It is not intended for air, but for the stone, for the water, for the slow currents beneath. Words are not spoken; they are *placed* — a single syllable dropped into the well, a short series of clicks passed from hand to hand through a tunnel, a low murmur that is more like a sigh than a sentence. Their grammar is not syntactic but temporal — a sentence takes three days to form, and is only complete when the moss at the edge of a hearth has changed color. They do not name things. They *identify* them by their relationship — the well is not "the well," but "the place where water holds," or "the throat of the stone." A building is not a "house," but "the thing that holds the fire and the sleep of the three who dwell beneath."

They live in family-chains rather than families. A single lineage may stretch back seven, eight, nine generations — the exact number is not known, nor is it needed. They do not trace ancestry like lines on a map, but as *echoes* in the stone. A person remembers their own parents, their grandparent, and sometimes a great-grandparent — but beyond that, the memory is not theirs. It is the memory of the place. They carry it like a weight in the chest, not as grief but as *continuum*. Children are not born in celebration, but in *recognition* — a birth is an event that confirms a truth: the stone is still fertile, the water still moves, the flame still survives. They are not raised in homes. They are raised in the network — passed from one stone-structure to another, held by a dozen hands, fed by the shared stew in the communal fire-pit, instructed by silence and observation.

They wear no jewelry. No symbols. Their clothing is functional — woven from plant fibers, dyed with lichen into hues that mimic the rock — grey, cobalt, moss-green, the blue of stone just beneath the surface. Their garments are *woven into* their bodies — not sewn, but grown, as if the cloth were a second skin, slowly absorbed into the flesh. Some elders have garments so old they are more stone than fiber, their edges smooth from years of being handled, worn, and re-woven.

Their technology is not tools, but *continuations*. A flint knife is not a man-made object — it is a piece of the rock that was shaped, by a thousand hands, to serve a purpose. A bone needle is not a tool, but a fragment of a creature that once moved through this place, now repurposed to thread the fibers. They do not invent. They do not improve. They *maintain* — a verb so central it has five forms, depending on the object being held: to maintain the well, to maintain the flame, to maintain the tunnel, to maintain the memory, to maintain the silence.

They do not worship a god. They do not pray. They do not believe in spirits. But they *know* the land is not merely *a thing*. They know it is *held* in a kind of suspended awareness — not conscious, not sentient, but *resonant*. It is not a being; it is a *condition*. And they live *within* that condition. Their lives are not *lived* in the usual sense — not for joy, not for progress, not for legacy. They live in *attunement*. They maintain the balance not because they are forced to, but because they have no other choice. To be elsewhere is impossible. To do otherwise is *unthinkable*.

They have no leader, no king, no chief. Their decisions are made through the ritual of the well. When a choice must be made — where to dig for new clay, whether to repair the northern wall, whether to allow a new house to be built — the elders gather around the well. They do not speak. They stand, one by one, and place their hands on the stone rim. They wait. The water does not move. But sometimes — rarely — a single drop will fall from the edge of the stone, not from condensation, not from rain, but from *within*. It is said this is the land’s answer. It is not yes. It is not no. It is “so,” and from that, the people take direction.

They do not mourn the dead. They do not bury them. They do not burn them. When a person dies — often quietly, in their sleep, with the fire low, the breath still — they are carried to the edge of the settlement, beyond the threshold, and left. The body is not clothed. It is not covered. It is placed on its back, palms up, face toward the sky. Then the wind — the same wind that whispers over the rocks — takes it. It is not eaten by animals, not devoured by soil. It is simply *dispersed* — the bones dissolve into the stone, the flesh into the moss, the breath into the atmosphere. And after a time — no one knows how long — the spot where the body lay may be found by a small mound of earth, coiled like a spiral. No one knows why. But some say it is the mammal — the one that drinks from the veins — returning the body to the network.

And on those rare mornings when the air crystallizes…  

They do not react. They do not panic. They do not gather. They do not speak. Instead, they become immobile — not frozen, but *still as the stone*. They stand in doorways, in paths, on roof-edges, holding their breath. And in that moment, they are not *aware* of the land — but *part of it*. They feel the moss glow, the lichens ripple, the well pulse beneath their feet — not as a phenomenon, but as a *recognition*. And when it ends, they slowly resume their lives, as if nothing had happened.

But something has.

They do not dream of escape. They do not wish for change. They do not hope for the future. But they *remember* the morning the air crystallized. And in that memory, there is a kind of reverence — not for the land, but *for its capacity to remember*.  

They are not its keepers. They are not its children.  

They are its *continuity* — the living, breathing, silent thread in a fabric that has never been woven, never been torn, and never will be.  

They live not in time, but in the pause between breaths.  

And that, in the end, is enough.
