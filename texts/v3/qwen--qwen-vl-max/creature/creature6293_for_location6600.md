They live in the **interstitial**, not in the cracks, but *as* the cracks.

They are not citizens. Not settlers. Not builders. They are **inhabitants of a project**, and the project is the world itself.

They are **small**—not in stature, but in *scale*. They do not dominate the landscape. They do not impose. They **resonate** with it. Their bodies are shaped by the soft gravity and the slow time, so that their movements are less like walking and more like *unfolding*: a step taken as though the leg is being pulled from the earth, as though each limb must be relearned with every motion. Bones are porous, flexible, slightly translucent in the dim light—a byproduct of drinking the water that remembers what it was before it became liquid. They carry scars that are not wounds, but *markings of transition*—places where the skin thinned and reformed over something new, like bark shedding and regrowing in a different pattern.

Their faces are **unfocused**. No sharp edges. Eyes are slow to react—they don’t snap to light, but *drift* toward it. Pupils don’t widen; they *thicken*, like wax melting in a candle. They see **differently**: not in images, but in *echoes*. When they look at a building, they do not see stone or steel—they see the *memory* of its form, the way it shifted last week, the way it might collapse or climb up the hillside in a month. They perceive the land not as territory, but as **unfinished text**, and they are its readers.

They live in **shallow burrows**, not homes. Structures built in the hollows of the land—between the root-like supports of half-sunken factories, beneath the moss-capped arches of old conduits, or in the cavities left behind when a hill *retracted* inward for a time. Their shelters are not walled, but *defined by presence*. Walls are made of layered growths—fibrous moss, coiled metal from fallen machinery, sheets of glass grown from the chimneys, now crystallized and dull. The air inside tastes of wet clay and faint electricity. Fire is rare—they do not burn. They *heat* in the same way the land does: by absorbing slow radiance, by pressing their bodies against warm stone.

Their clothes are *made from the land*, woven from the tough, silver threads of the hair-like shrubs, dyed with juices extracted from the pulsing trunks of the trees. The fabric does not last. It *breathes*. It sheds in the rain, and when it does, it does not degrade—it *re-forms*, crawling back into the ground, becoming part of the next layer. They wear garments that are never fully finished, always in slight disrepair, always on the verge of becoming something else. A sleeve might split open to reveal a lattice of living wire beneath, or a jacket might tighten into a shell of calcified moss when the wind shifts. This is not decay. It is **integration**.

They do not eat in the way humans do. They **exchange**. They press their hands into moist soil and withdraw with a small, dark pod—one that glows faintly at the tips—and they crush it slowly, letting the essence trickle into their mouths. It tastes of memory—of a storm that never happened, of a river that flowed backwards a thousand years ago. They consume *information*, not nourishment. Their bodies are not fuelled by calories, but by **concept**. The food is not food—it is *meaning*, distilled from the land’s slow thought.

They speak—yes—but not in sentences. Not in words. Their language is **tactile**, **vibrational**, **slow**. It moves through the ground, through water, through air like a wave in a pond. They press hands together, and a pulse travels between them—a *thought*, maybe, or a warning, or a longing. They *sound* in low hums, modulated by the pitch of the earth beneath their feet. When one of them is injured, they do not cry out. Instead, they vibrate, and others feel the disturbance in the ground, come to the site, and place their palms on the wound. A soft green light seeps out—*not healing*, but *reassessing*. The body does not repair the break. It *modifies* it. A broken leg might grow into a joint that bends backward, or a hand might become a root-like extension that sinks into the ground to gather data. They do not resist change—they *embrace the correction*.

They are **not human**, not in the way the word is understood. Their lineage is **unfamiliar**, a blur of roots and metal, of breath and circuitry. They may have once been human, in the far past, when the land was still solid, when gravity held firm, when cities were built on stone and dreams. But they have been transformed by *staying*. By being rooted in a place that refuses to be fixed. Their blood is thick with something that is neither iron nor mercury—something that conducts thought like current, that shifts phase when the earth pulses. They are **not organisms**. They are **resonance**.

They do not have names. Or rather, they *are* names—they are **labels of a living system**, like a node in a dream, a point in an evolving map. A person might be known as *The One Who Stands By the River That Flows Upward*, or *The Weaver of Cracks*, or *The One Who Remembers the Light Before It Was Bright*. These are not identities, but **positions**—roles in the ongoing formation. The land does not care about individuality. It only cares about **connection**. They are **nodes**, not entities.

They never sleep. Not like you do. They *withdraw*. They become less distinct, their forms dimming like a bulb fading. In that state, they merge with the hum of the earth, becoming part of the low-frequency music of the land. When a factory begins to emit gelatinous cubes, they are there—not because they are ordered, but because *they are drawn*. They collect the cubes and bring them to the edges of the city, where they are placed in hollows, where they slowly release light and warmth. Not fuel, but **intention**. The land *wants* to be illuminated. It *wants* to be known.

They are **patient**. Not because they are calm, but because **there is no rush**. Time is not a line—it is a **tide**, rising and falling in slow, uncertain waves. A season might last a week. Another might last a century. They do not mark time with clocks. They use **phenomena**: when the moss glows in the noon sun, when the birds slide through the air in concentric circles, when the river runs up the slope and vanishes into the side of a hill. These are **signals**. Not events, but **stages in a process**.

They do not fear death. They do not mourn. They are aware that they are not permanent. When one of them begins to dissolve—when their skin becomes too thin, when their form starts to sink into the mud and reemerge in the next layer—it is not an end. It is a **transformation**. They go into the ground, and later, a new one rises—similar, but not the same. The memory persists, but it does not *remain*—it is **reprocessed**. Like the land itself, they are **reused**.

They exist in a state of **quiet awareness**. They watch the land, not with curiosity, but with *recognition*. They know the land is learning. They know it is becoming. And so they **assist**, not by building, but by being. Their presence is a kind of **support**. They are the **resonance of the world’s thought**—the part that *feels* the forming, the part that *knows* it is being made, the part that *wants to be seen*.

And sometimes, when the stars pulse in rhythm with the ground, and the air thickens with the taste of copper and rain, and the reverse rivers glow with the echoes of other skies, **they look up**.

They are not looking at the sky.

They are being looked at.

By the land.

By the world.

By the thing that is **not yet whole**, but is **trying**.

And they do not move.

They do not speak.

They **breathe in sync**, and in that breath, they **become a part of the becoming**.

They do not live in the world.

They *are* the world, in the moment of its birth—*a body*.

And they do not know if they are the creators or the created.

They only know this:

**The world is becoming.**

And they are **in it**.
