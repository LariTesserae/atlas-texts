The air is thin and tastes of iron and old dust, so high up that the sky is not a dome but a deep, crushing absence of stars—a darkness so absolute it feels like a physical weight pressing against the eyes. This is the Blind Caldera, a high-altitude hollow perched on the spine of the world, small enough that its curved walls are visible in the faint, ghostly phosphorescence of the life that clings to them.

It is a place of severe, dry heat. There is no humidity here, no moisture to soften the edges of existence. The heat radiates upward from the ground and downward from the air itself, a suffocating blanket that makes the skin prickle. It is a kiln without a fire, an oven heated simply by the pressure of being alive in a sealed, high-pressure atmospheric trap.

There are no plants. The concept of "green" is entirely absent. Instead, the floor and the lower walls are a jagged mosaic of weathered obsidian and basalt, smoothed by eons of thermal expansion and contraction. The erosion here is established; the land is old and settled. The rocks fit together like a broken puzzle, locked in a tight, permanent embrace. There is no soil, only the accumulation of mineral dust and the desiccated remains of the dead.

The silence is profound, mostly quiet, yet the silence is misleading. The place is frantic with motion, but the acoustics of the high, thin air swallow the noise. The cacophony of a teeming ecosystem is reduced to a muffled, rhythmic thrum, like a heartbeat heard through a thick wall.

And it is teeming. The density of life here is overwhelming. Every inch of stone is occupied. The fauna consists of "glass-feeders"—silicon-based arthropods that look like living mosaics of stained glass and chitin. They range in size from a grain of sand to the span of a dinner plate. They are packed shoulder-to-shoulder, a writhing, undulating carpet of iridescent black and deep, blood-red shells. They move in a constant, slow-motion tide; millions of legs clicking against the stone, but the sound is absorbed by the sheer density of their bodies.

They do not eat each other. They are grazers on the mineral wealth of the caldera. Using specialized acid mandibles, they scrape the rock, consuming the silica and iron, leaving behind trails of fine, glittering dust that coats everything in a grey film. The ecosystem is driven not by photosynthesis or water, but by thermal energy and mineral conversion. The "predators" in this system are scarce but terrifying: large, ambush-dwelling constructs of hardened crystal that lie dormant beneath the glass-feeders, snapping up dozens at a time with lightning-fast strikes of pincer-like limbs, blending perfectly into the obsidian floor until they move.

Scattered among the living tide are the only signs that something else once stood here. Massive, monolithic pillars of a different type of stone—perhaps marble, or a synthetic material that has long since fossilized—stand at awkward angles. They are partially buried, ground down by the slow, persistent gnawing of millions of tiny mouths. These structures are devoid of technology, devoid of writing, serving now simply as high-ground for the larger glass-feeders to molt.

The light level is near zero. The only illumination comes from the creatures themselves. As they scrape the rock, friction and chemical reactions within their shells cause them to pulse with a faint, heat-haze luminescence—occasional flares of dull orange or sickly violet. The caldera is a dark room filled with a shifting, bioluminescent fog of tiny moving bodies.

It is a claustrophobic, open-air tomb. The scale is deceptive; it feels like a cathedral, but the ceiling is the void of space. The air is too thin to carry a shout, but thick enough to cook a man. It is a biological engine running at maximum capacity in the dark, eating the world itself to survive, silent and screaming all at once.
