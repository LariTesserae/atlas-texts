The air here is heavy, not with pressure, but with suspension. It hangs warm and dim, like the breath held in a lung. You stand on a vast, high plateau, a shelf of the world lifted so far above the sea of clouds below that the horizon bends. This is not a mountain range piercing the sky, but a continent suspended within it—a vast, biological raft floating at the apex of the troposphere.

The light is a perpetual, gloaming gold. There is no distinct sun source today; the illumination is filtered through miles of thick, water-laden air that glows with a diffuse, amber luminescence. It is a permanent, quiet twilight.

Underfoot, there is no stone. There has not been stone here for millennia. The ground is a deep, spongy silence—a substrate of pure vegetation. You are walking on the roof of the world, but the roof is made of living matter. The soil, if it can be called that, is a dense matting of root-hairs, mycelial networks, and decaying leaves that have compacted over eons into a soft, yielding peat. It feels like walking on the back of a sleeping giant. Every step sinks half an inch, releasing a small puff of spore-scented air, a perfume of wet earth and blooming decay.

Water is the sculptor here, but it is a patient, slow-moving hand. The erosion parameter suggests an ancient, settled state, and indeed, the landscape has no sharp edges. The hills are rounded, swollen domes, rising like the humps of whales beneath a green blanket. Water does not rush; it weeps. From every vertical surface, from the massive boles of the towering Growth-Pillars, water squeezes out in slow, gelatinous tears. It is a weeping landscape. The humidity is absolute; condensation drips constantly from the canopy above, creating a rhythmic, infinite patter—*plip, plip, plip*—that is the only constant sound in the vast quiet.

The vegetation is total. There is no "view" that is not obstructed by life. The scale is so large that what you assume are distant mountains are actually the trunks of single trees, anchored deep in the spongy earth and reaching up hundreds of feet. Their bark is not rough; it is smooth and pale, stained dark green by the curtains of moss that drape from them like wet velvet. Between these titans, the space is filled. Vines thick as pythons weave nets that catch the mist, and epiphytes grow upon epiphytes in a chaotic, vertical city of leaves. It is a jungle so dense that it has become architecture. The air is thick with suspended pollen and drifting seeds, glowing motes in the dim light.

This place feels old. It feels like the end-result of evolution. The "erosion" is not of rock, but of energy; everything has settled into a final form. There is no struggling for light here, down in the lower tiers, because the biology has adapted to the low light. The leaves are immense, dark greens and deep purples, designed to drink up every photon. The ecosystem is closed, a perfect loop. A tree falls, but before it hits the ground, it is already being digested by the writhing masses of fungi and insects that live in the interstices of the bark.

And there is life everywhere. The fauna is abundant, but it is invisible unless you stop and stare. This is not a land of roaring predators or stampeding herds. The density of the vegetation demands silence and stealth. The creatures here are soft-bodied, membranous things. Frogs the size of dinner plates cling to the wet bark, their skin translucent, pulsing with the slow beat of their hearts. Large, silent birds glide through the mists on wings that span ten feet, catching the updrafts, feathered in soft greys that render them ghost-like. Insects hum—a low, thrumming vibration that you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears. They move in slow-motion, their metabolisms tuned to the warm, wet, slow rhythm of the plateau.

It is a strange place, possessing a weirdness that seeps into the mind. The "built" parameter is scattered, but present. As you move through the dense, wet foliage, you occasionally stumble upon structures that seem too geometric to be natural. They are not high-tech; they are primitive, rough-hewn things. Great circles of stone, or perhaps fossilized bone, sunk halfway into the living muck. Monoliths that stand twelve feet tall, smoothed by the rain, covered in a carapace of moss so thick they look like natural pillars. There are no tools, no machines, only these faint, haunting marks left by something that understood alignment and mass but has long since returned to the earth. They are altars, or perhaps boundary stones, now irrelevant in a world where plant-life has swallowed the concept of boundaries.

The dynamic of the world is agonizingly slow. You can see the weather systems forming in the distance—great banks of fog that roll over the rounded hills like slow-moving tides. Rain falls, not as a storm, but as a curtain that lowers and raises over the course of days. The growth is visible to the human eye if you stand still long enough; a creeper will uncoil a fraction of an inch per hour.

Here, the distinction between animal, vegetable, and mineral is blurred. The rocks weep water; the trees stand like monuments; the air is thick enough to taste. It is a vast, dim, warm, and wet womb of a world, existing in a state of deep, ancient equilibrium, utterly indifferent to anything small enough to walk upon it.
