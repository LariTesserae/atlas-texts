The sky is not a ceiling, but it is not open. It is a suffocating, luminous grey haze, pressed low by the sheer weight of the greenery above. This is the High Veldt, a place where the horizon is defined not by curvature, but by the fading of light into the dense, humid atmosphere.

Here, elevation is a physical pressure. You stand on a ridge of woven bark, two miles above the sea level of a forgotten ocean, yet the air is thick, warm, and heavy with the scent of wet loam and ozone. It is a tropical heat, trapped beneath the overarching fronds that blot out the sun. The light that does filter down is dim and spectral—a twilight filtered through a thousand generations of filters, turning the world into a monochrome of bruised purples and deep, breathing greens.

The landscape defies the logic of geology. There is no stone here, no granite bedrock, no mineral substrate. The ground is entirely constructed. Looking over the edge of the ridge, you see the face of the cliff: not layers of sediment, but strata of compacted roots, fossilized stems, and woven vascular tubes, pressed together over eons into a material harder than iron. This is the **Built World**, a megastructure of biological origin. It was not grown; it was erected. Gigantic pillars of dense, woody architecture stretch down into the unknowable mist below, supporting the plateau you walk upon. The geometry is too perfect to be natural—vast hexagonal grids of root-mesh form the plains, and archways of lignin span canyons that have not seen water in a thousand years.

Yet, for all its construction, it is entirely plant. The Vegetation score is absolute. There is no "soil" in the traditional sense, only a slow, endless breakdown of the previous layers of the structure. The ground beneath your feet is spongy, a living carpet of mosses and liverworts that digest the ancient, dead wood of the floor, converting it back into the rich, black humus that feeds the giant pillars. It is a closed loop, an ouroboros of flora consuming its own architecture to sustain itself.

And it is wet. The water here does not come from rain clouds, but from the world itself. Weeping Rocks. The giant pillars constantly exude moisture, drawn up from the deep biosphere and sweated out through pores in the bark. The cliff faces are perpetually slick with runoff. Condensation drips from the underside of the vast, interlocking leaves that form the pseudo-ceiling, creating a constant, rhythmic *plink... plink... plink* that is the only loud sound in a quiet world.

Because the Density is dangerously low. This is a landscape of vast, empty spaces. The canopy is a tight-knit roof miles above, and the floor is a solid, intricate weave, but in between, there is nothing but air. The pillars are spaced miles apart, creating hollow, cathedral-like voids where the wind moves slowly and silently. When you shout, the sound is swallowed by the porous, fibrous walls before it can travel fifty feet. It is a quiet that feels heavy, a silence that presses against the ears.

The scale of the place is disorienting. It feels as though you are standing on the back of a sleeping god the size of a continent. In the distance, you see movement—massive, slow tectonic shifts. But these are not tectonic plates. They are branches. The entire horizon is gently swaying, breathing. The "mountains" on the horizon are slowly adjusting their angle, tracking a light source that cannot be seen.

This is where the Weirdness bleeds in. The physics here are biological. Gravity feels inconsistent; near the massive sap-ducts that run through the center of the plains like rivers of amber, you feel lighter, as if the immense pressure of the moving fluid distorts space. Time, too, feels elastic. In the deep hollows between the pillars, shadows linger long after the light has shifted, and insects can be seen flying in patterns that seem to fold in on themselves, navigating non-Euclidean paths between the roots.

The ecology is abundant yet hidden. The fauna has adapted to this hollow, empty world. They are mostly gliders and climbers, creatures that never touch the ground. Birds with membranous, translucent wings that catch the dim light; arboreal rodents that can jump miles using the skin flaps that stretch from their wrists to their ankles, riding the thermal currents rising from the weeping rocks.

And there are people. The Tech is primitive—stone, bone, sharpened thorn. They live in the nooks and crannies of the architecture, small nomadic tribes that traverse the vast root-plains. They do not understand that their world is a machine. To them, the Weeping Rocks are gods providing water; the slow swaying of the mountains is the breathing of the Spirit. They build shelters of woven fronds against the sides of the giant pillars, safe in the hollows, insulated by the terrible silence of the void.

They hunt the gliding beasts with spears tipped with the neurotoxic spines of local flora. They move silently, their footsteps muffled by the damp, living moss. They know that to be loud is to disrespect the silence.

It is a world of constant, slow motion. The Dynamic is active, but on a geological timescale. The rivers of sap flow visibly, like molasses. The leaves of the canopy unfurl over the course of weeks. The "erosion" is not wind wearing down stone, but fungi digesting the cathedral, breaking the hard walls down into soft soil, a process as relentless as the tides.

You stand there, in the warm, dim twilight, mist slicking your face. The air smells sweet and cloying. You look out over the vast, empty expanse of the green void, and you feel small. Not small like a human in a forest, but small like a cell in a body. You are walking through the lungs of a world that has been asleep for millennia, dreaming in the dark, wet and warm and utterly, terrifyingly alone.
