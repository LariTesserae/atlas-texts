The air here is heavy, not with pressure, but with a profound, mineral dryness. It is a warmth that seeps into the marrow—a still, suffocating heat that feels like the breath of a sleeping giant. This is the Hollow, the vast subterranean expanse located deep within the crust of the world, so far below the surface that the concept of "sky" has been replaced by a ceiling of jagged, compressed stone.

The landscape is a desert of sharp edges. Because the erosion is young and the water nonexistent, the geology has not been smoothed by time or liquid. The ground is a chaotic armor of obsidian shards, jagged basalt ridges, and knife-like pinnacles that catch the faint light and glint menacingly. To step is to tread on broken glass; there is no sand here, only a billion billion years of crushed, dry rock. The terrain has the look of a fresh break—as if the planet split open mere moments ago and froze in that instant of violence, leaving the wounds raw and bleeding heat.

The scale is crushing. The horizon is not a curve but a straight line that fades into a gloom so thick it feels like a physical substance. Stalagmites the size of skyscrapers rise in silent forests, their tips so high they vanish into the darkness above. Between these monoliths, the space opens up into canyons wide enough to hold cities, yet there is a claustrophobic density to the place. The volume is vast, but it is crowded with the presence of the "inhabitants."

Vegetation is absent, or at least, unrecognizable as such. There are no leaves, no green, no soft cellulose. Instead, the ecology is built on crystal and stone. Great spiraling structures of quartz and gypsum erupt from the floor, twisting in geometries that suggest life but possess none of the softness. They are "statues" of growth, mineral trees frozen in a single moment of blooming. They do not sway; they do not grow perceptibly. They simply *are*, humming with a low, infrasonic vibration that you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears.

And the fauna—here is where the density truly lies. The air is thick with life, teeming with a silicate abundance. Small, shelled creatures scuttle across the razor-ground, their legs clicking in a rhythmic, ceaseless percussion. They are lithovores, evolved to consume the rock itself, slowly grinding the sharp landscape down into dust. They crowd the surfaces, a moving carpet of grey and brown carapaces, clambering over one another in a slow, desperate search for the mineral-rich veins that sustain them. Above them, larger, winged shapes glide on the thermals—bat-like, but with skins of polished slate and eyes that glow with the dim phosphorescence of the cave.

There is no water. The chemistry of this place runs on sulfur and silica. When creatures move, they do not leave tracks in mud, but scratches in stone. The silence of the place is deceptive; it is not empty, but it is quiet, save for the constant, dry susurrus of a million legs dragging over stone and the faint, periodic *clack* of a rock-eater cracking a geode.

In the distance, scattered among the natural spires, are the marks of the built. They are primitive, scarce, and jarringly simple against the alien backdrop. A ring of stacked stones here, a primitive hammer made from a heavier meteorite iron there. They are the remnants of a people who tried to exist here, perhaps long ago, or perhaps they are still here, invisible within the dense crowds of rock-eaters. The structures are low, defensive, and devoid of ornamentation—mere shelters against the oppressive, unchanging gaze of the mineral world.

The light is a ghostly, inadequate thing. It does not come from the sun, but from the rocks themselves—a faint, cold bioluminescence emitted by the lichen that clings to the highest ceilings, or the heat-shimmer of the thermal vents that pulse with a dull, red rhythm. Shadows are long and absolute, pooling in the crevasses like spilled ink.

Most striking is the equilibrium. Nothing changes. The heat has been the same for eons; the rock-eaters follow the same paths their ancestors did a thousand years ago. The dust hangs suspended in the air, unmoving by wind. It is a world trapped in amber—a vast, dark, sharp, and crowded stillness where time has stopped, and the only movement is the slow, eternal consumption of stone by stone.
