The space is defined first by its verticality and its rawness. It is a vast, subterranean hollow, a cavernous bowl of such immense scale that the far walls are lost in a humid, slate-grey haze. This is not a place shaped by the slow, abrasive hand of time, but rather a wound that has only just been opened. The stone walls are jagged and raw, showing the fresh, crystalline faces of rock cleaved from the earth only yesterday—or perhaps the day before. There are no smooth, river-worn stones here, no layers of sedimentary softness; the geology is embryonic, sharp, and aggressively new.

The air is cool and consistently damp. It carries the metallic taste of wet iron and the dusty scent of shattered bedrock. Moisture does not fall as rain so much as it precipitates out of the heavy air, slicking every surface with a glistening sheen of condensation. Water collects in the myriad divots and fissures of the raw rock, forming tiny, perfect pools that reflect the dim orange glow from the center of the void.

The light comes not from the sun, which is miles above, but from a massive, primitive industrial heart. In the very center of the depression, a great natural gas flue vents from the earth’s core, capped by an immense, lattice-work tower of timber and bronze. The fire burns with a steady, heart-like rhythm, casting a "normal" daylight that struggles to reach the periphery. It turns the mist into a shifting, orange curtain and illuminates the city that clings to the inner slopes of the cavern.

The settlement is urban, dense, and packed tight against itself. Because the floor of the hollow is unstable and constantly being reshaped by the earth’s violent birthing pains, the city has grown upward and inward, like a crust of lichen on a wound. Structures are hewn from the very debris of the excavation—rough-hewn granite blocks stacked with primitive mortar, bolstered by massive timbers cut from subterranean fungi forests that exist in the lower dark. There is no empty space; buildings are wedged into buildings, balconies overlapping, walkways bridging gaps with a chaotic, organic density. It is a squatter’s metropolis, a testament to the urge to shelter in the midst of creation.

The technology is strictly primitive. Here, leverage and friction are the only engines. Great wooden gears, lubricated by animal fat and the constant damp, turn massive winches to hoist stone buckets up the sheer cliff faces. The sound of the city is surprisingly quiet (0.4/3), a testament to the density of the construction and the absorbent nature of the wet stone and moss. The noises that do escape are muffled: the low, rhythmic creak of timber under strain, the soft hiss of the central flame, the clink of chisel on rock, and the constant, gentle drip of water.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to take hold in the fresh, mineral-rich chaos. Only the hardiest lichens and mosses colonize the shaded mortar between stones, and a few stunted, pale ferns cling to life where the condensation is heaviest. There is no soil yet, only ground-up dust and stone chips. Fauna is equally rare. The occasional blind, white crustacean scuttles in the dark gutters, and domesticated, flightless birds are used for their eggs and meat, but wild animals have not yet colonized this newborn world.

The dynamic here is one of aggressive, yet silent, growth. The earth is not static. The "Emerging" nature of the erosion means the landscape is actively changing. Small tremors are common, sending fresh showers of dust down from the ceiling. The city is not just built; it is constantly being rebuilt, shifting on its foundations as the floor of the world rises. It is a place of construction, of raw potential, where the rules are simple: build quickly, build tight, and listen to the earth. It is a cool, damp, echoing cradle of civilization, suspended in the moment before the rock settles into history.
