The darkness here is absolute. It is not the soft dark of a moonless night, nor the dusty dark of a closed cellar; it is a visual silence, a total absence of photons that suggests the sun has not existed in this sector of the universe for millennia, or perhaps that this place has simply forgotten to let light in.

Yet, it is warm. The air hangs heavy and tropical, thick with humidity that feels like velvet against the skin. It is a breath-held warmth, constant and unchanging, insulated by the sheer mass of the plateau upon which you stand. The elevation is significant—a high-altitude plain, perhaps a former mountain range that has been ground down by the heel of eternity—but there is no wind chill, no thinning of the air. If anything, the atmosphere is denser here, laden with moisture and oxygen.

You stand on the Old Floor. This is the terrain: a single, continuous sheet of stone that stretches out in all directions, smoothed by ancient erosion into a near-perfect plane. There are no jagged peaks, no rubble, no gravel. The stone is polished to a mirror’s sheen, dark as obsidian, and slick. It feels less like rock and more like the skin of a sleeping beast. This is the "ancient" state made manifest—geology that has given up the drama of upheaval for the boredom of equilibrium. It is the end of erosion, the final form of matter before it dissolves entirely into dust.

The ground weeps. The water parameter is a constant presence here. The stone is porous, or perhaps the water is forced up from beneath by pressures modern physics cannot calculate. From invisible pores in the obsidian smoothness, water bubbles up in silent beads until the surface tension breaks, sending a constant, slow trickle sheeting across the floor. It is a marsh-edge without the mud; a post-rain landscape that never drains. The water is warm, mineral-heavy, and possesses a strange viscosity—it clings to the rock like oil, reluctant to let go.

Because the light level is zero, your eyes are useless, or nearly so. You perceive this place through other senses, and through the strange, bioluminescent pressence of the life that clings to the darkness.

The vegetation is lush but paradoxically sparse. Because the density of the environment is so low, the plant life has compensated by growing colossal. This is the scale of the cathedral or the grove. You cannot see the walls, but you can sense them in the distance, a boundary where the air pressure changes.

Here and there, rising from the wet dark like the pillars of a ruined temple, are the Trees. They are not clustered; a single Tree might command a hectare of empty space, its roots drinking from the weeping stone. They are massive, towering structures that likely reach hundreds of feet upward, their canopies lost in the gloom. Their bark is not wood, but a dense, calcified material that hums with a low-frequency vibration. From every branch, from the vein-like structures of their leaves, glows a faint, cold blue light. It is not enough to illuminate the distance, only enough to outline the terrifying beauty of the organism itself. They are lush, dripping with mosses and epiphytes that shimmer in ghostly greens and violets, self-contained ecosystems in the void.

The sound here is loud, a cacophony of the microscopic and the slow. There is no wind to rustle the branches, so the soundscape is intimate and overwhelming. It is the sound of water: the *plink, plink, plink* of condensation falling from the colossal leaves; the *shhh* of the groundwater sheeting across the stone floor; the deep, resonant *gurgle* of the trees drinking. And overlaying it all is the tech—a sub-bass thrumming, a vibration you feel in your teeth more than you hear. This is the heartbeat of the plateau, the "beyond modern" energy that animates the geology. The stone itself seems to be processing information, ticking like a slow, planetary computer.

Fauna is abundant, moving in the spaces between the pillars. Blind birds navigate by echolocation, their sharp clicks adding a staccato rhythm to the wet ambience. They move in massive swarms, cloud-like entities that sweep through the empty air between the Trees. There are things that slither through the shallow film of water on the floor—long, sinuous forms that leave trails of bioluminescent slime in their wake, glowing hieroglyphs that fade within seconds. The ecology is frantic; life here is short, fast, and loud, burning through the energy emitted by the vibrating earth.

Scattered among the Trees are the Built artifacts—small structures, barely noticeable against the scale of the flora. They are not buildings in the human sense, but geometricperfect angles: cubes hovering just inches above the wet stone, monolithic doorways standing frameless in the open air, smooth surfaces that absorb all sound. They are remnants of a technology that engineered the very erosion of this plateau, machines designed to wait out the heat death of the universe. They are inert now, or perhaps operating on a timeframe so slow they appear dead, occasional flickers of static electricity arcing across their surfaces, the only sign that they are still dreaming.

The dynamic of the place is agonizingly slow. The trees grow inches in a century. The water level rises and falls over epochs. But the life within it—the birds, the slitherers, the insects—is frantic and fast, living out entire generations in the time it takes a single droplet of water to slide down a pillar of black stone.

You are standing in a dark, warm, hollow cathedral at the end of the world, where the ground cries warm tears, the trees hum electric songs, and the silence is loud enough to deafen you.
