The air here is heavy, a warm, breathable blanket that smells of wet dust and dissolved minerals. It is a low place, a hollow in the earth that feels more like a room than a valley—perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, bounded on all sides by steep, sloping walls of dull grey stone.

There is no sky to speak of, or if there is, it is hidden high above by a perpetual, low-hanging shelf of cloud that diffuses the light into a constant, dim twilight. The illumination is flat and shadowless, revealing a world that is densely packed, claustrophobically full. The floor is not a flat plain but a chaotic jigsaw of fallen boulders, slabs of shale, and eroded pillars jammed tightly together. There is no open ground to walk upon; one must clamber over the wet, slick shoulders of stones that fit against one another with the precision of a puzzle finished millennia ago.

Water is the governing force, saturating everything. It is not a place of rushing rivers or crashing waterfalls, but of weeping. The stone sweats; beads of moisture gather on the foreheads of boulders and trace slow, glistening paths down the flanks of the rock. In the crevices where the stones meet, shallow pools have formed—still, black mirrors reflecting the gloom. The humidity is absolute. The air is so thick with moisture that condensation drips constantly, a rhythmic, steady *plink… plink… plink* that is the only sound in the quiet. It is a sound that marks the passing of time, yet here, time feels suspended.

There are no plants. No moss, no ferns, no clinging vines. The stone is bare, scrubbed clean by the perpetual dampness, revealing only the geology—layers of sediment, veins of quartz, the smooth, water-polished bones of the earth. The vegetation score is near zero; this is a mineral world. The only color comes from the rock itself—greens of copper oxidation, rusts of iron, and the uniform slate-grey of the limestone.

Scattered among the natural debris are the faint marks of the built environment, though they are so old and weathered they barely register as artifacts. A crude, low wall of stacked stones, mossless and eroded, sits half-submerged in a stagnant pool. Nearby, the shattered remains of a ceramic pot, hand-thrown and fired in a primitive kiln, lie in pieces that have tumbled together with the river rocks. There is no metal here; the technology is stone and clay, simple and manual. These structures do not dominate; they are merely part of the debris now, accepted by the landscape as just more rocks to be wetted and warmed.

The warmth is tropical, muggy and enveloping, despite the lack of sunlight. The heat radiates from the stones themselves, which seem to hold the memory of a sun they cannot see. It is a stable, unchanging equilibrium. The water level never rises; the rocks never shift; the temperature never cools. The erosion is established—everything is rounded, smooth, and settled. The sharp edges of the world were worn away eons ago.

Life is rare. Occasionally, a small, pale crab or a blind, translucent slug might be spotted in the dark water of a crevice, moving with agonizing slowness, but fauna is largely absent. This is not a place of thriving ecosystems; it is a place of preservation and stasis. It is a quiet, dim, waterlogged room where the history of the earth is stacked in stone, sitting in the warm, wet dark, waiting for nothing.
