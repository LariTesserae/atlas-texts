The air here tastes of iron and cold spit. It is a dampness that doesn't soak, but clings—a fine, particulate mist that seems to have been sliced out of a heavier ocean and carried up to this height.

You stand on a spine of limestone, jagged and raw, a bone of the earth that has only recently broken the skin of the world. The erosion is young; the rock hasn't had the time to be smoothed by wind or water. It presents itself in aggressive shards, vertical plates, and scree slopes that look like they collapsed yesterday and haven't settled yet. The elevation is significant, high enough that the chest works harder to pull in the thin, chilled air, but it is not a mountain range. It is a singular, catastrophic uplift, a cathedral of stone rising abruptly from the clouds below.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight. It is not night, but the sun here is a diffused, silver coin behind a ceiling of unbroken slate-gray cloud. This lack of direct sun does not starve the life here; it forces it to become strange.

The vegetation is lush but startlingly sparse. You do not see a carpet of grass or a claustrophobic jungle. Instead, the life is gathered in solitary, triumphant explosions. Individual trees, impossible in their girth, grow from pockets in the sheer rock faces. Their bark is smooth and pale, like the skin of a shark, and their leaves are massive, dark green plates that hang heavy with condensed mist. Between these giant, isolated sentinels, there is only open, gray stone and damp air. It is a landscape of negative space, a room where the furniture is too big and placed too far apart.

Clinging to the side of the largest spire is the settlement.

It is a town, but a vertical one. Built by hands that knew only stone, bone, and wood, the structures are strapped to the rock with rawhide lashings and pitons hammered into the fissures. There are no nails, no smelted iron beams. The architecture is defensive, hugging the cliff face, a labyrinth of wooden platforms and hide-covered yurts connected by swaying bridges of braided vines. It looks less like a city and more like a parasite that has learned to build, perfectly adapted to the sharp, young stone.

And it is silent.

The silence is the most oppressive feature of this place. Given the density of the fauna, the quiet should be impossible. The ecology here is overwhelming in its biomass. Every surface, every niche, every hanging branch is occupied. But the occupants have evolved under a rule of absolute auditory stealth.

The fauna is a plague of soft things.

Thousands of creatures inhabit the damp groves and the eaves of the wooden town. They are six-limbed, covered in dense, velvet fur that absorbs the faintest whisper of friction. They have no hard shells, no clicking claws; their pads are soft, wide, and sticky. They are the color of the lichen—mottled grays and bruised purples. They swarm over the wooden walkways of the town, ignored by the inhabitants. They flow up the tree trunks like slow-motion oil spills.

It is an active, frantic ecosystem, but it plays out at a frequency that the human ear cannot catch. A predator—a larger, sleek-shaped thing like a weasel made of liquid shadow—drops from a branch onto a cluster of the grazers. There is a flurry of limbs, a tearing of soft flesh, but not a gasp. The victims die without shrieking. The predator consumes without crunching. The violence is a pantomime.

The dampness amplifies this hush. The water here doesn't babble; it slides. When the rain comes, it is a fine, vertical needle-stitch that hisses imperceptibly against the broad leaves. Puddles do not ripple; they thicken.

To stand on the edge of the wooden platform, looking out over the gray, jagged peaks and the isolated, towering trees, is to watch a muted riot. The air is thick with the movement of life—leaves shudder under the weight of invisible climbers, the bushes wiggle with the passing of burrowers—but the world produces no volume. It is a landscape of high definition and zero sound, a crowded, hunting ground wrapped in cold, wet wool. The people of the town move with the same practiced silence, their eyes constantly moving, watching the frantic, quiet dance of survival that plays out in the dim, misty space between the rocks.
