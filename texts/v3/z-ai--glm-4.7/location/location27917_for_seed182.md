The air here is heavy, a physical weight that presses against the skin, hot and damp like the breath of a sleeping beast. You are standing in a deep subterranean hollow, a space that feels less like a natural cavern and more like a wound in the earth that has been hastily bandaged with iron and glass.

The scale is deceptively intimate—a cathedral chamber, perhaps, or a single, sprawling room of a city that extends infinitely upward and downward. But here, at this elevation, the walls press close. This is the Below.

The first thing you notice is the freshness of the stone. Usually, the underworld feels ancient, crusted with the patience of eons. Here, the erosion is terrifyingly young. The surrounding rock is jagged and raw, snapped rather than worn. Great, white scars of granite and basalt rupture the gloom, their edges so sharp they look as if they might cut the shadows that cling to them. It is as if the ground split open only a week ago, exposing the wet, red meat of the planet.

Into this fresh fracture, the city has been packed.

It is an urban tumor, dense and haphazard. Structures are not built here so much as they are jammed into the available cracks. The architecture is industrial, brutal, and seemingly instantaneous—rivets driven into wet stone, iron girders bolted directly across fissures that still weep mineral water. There is no space between the buildings. They form a vertical hive, a precipitate of brick, rusted pipe, and damp concrete, layered one atop the other until they block out the distant, phantom ceiling.

Because the light is dim. It is a perpetual twilight here, illuminated only by the sickly amber glow of dying sodium lamps and the erratic pulsing of bioluminescent moss. The darkness is thick, but the density of the construction refuses to let it be total; light reflects off a thousand wet surfaces.

And it is wet. The water level (1.4) is deceptive; it is not a lake, but an environment of sweat. Every surface slick with condensation. The humidity is near saturation. Water doesn't just flow; it bleeds. It drips constantly from the rusted underbellies of the steam pipes that riddle the ceiling like the exposed veins of a giant. The hiss of escaping steam is the only constant companion to the silence.

The silence, indeed, is profound (0.6). For a place so packed with machinery, it is remarkably quiet. The great engines that must power this place do not roar; they hum. It is a sub-aural vibration, a bass note felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ears. *Thrum-thrum-pause.* A slow, patient rhythm. The dynamic is glacial. Gears the size of houses turn, but so slowly you cannot see them move; you only register their displacement by the groaning of metal that shifts once every hour.

The heat (2.7) rises from the bottom of the shaft, baking the condensation into a thick, breathable fog. It is a greenhouse temperature, stifling and intimate.

But where there is heat and water, there is life. The vegetation here is lush (1.8) and aggressive. It has adapted to the industrial grind. Vines as thick as a man's torso coil around the pistons, their leaves dark and leathery, drinking the oily runoff. Ferns unfurl from the joints of the machinery, their fronds tipped with copper filaments that conduct the dim electricity into the soil. The green is vibrant, almost unnatural, fighting against the rust. It is a symbiotic invasion; the machines provide the heat and the wet exhaust, and the plants bind the crumbling, fresh rock together, holding the city in place.

There is a strangeness (1.9) to the ecology. The plants seem too deliberate. The moss grows in perfect geometric grids along the brickwork. The vines pulse in time with the slow, deep thrum of the engine. It is as if the flora has been engineered, or perhaps the industry has been farmed.

Fauna is rare (0.9). You see no rats, no birds. Occasionally, something small and pale—a salamander or a blind, slow-moving arboreal creature—scurries across the wet pipes, but for the most part, the only movement is the mechanical creep of the city and the steady growth of the leaves.

You stand on a catwalk made of diamond-grate metal, slick with moss. Below you, the drop is bottomless, vanishing into the hot, white steam that rises from the earth's fresh break. Above you, the city is a ladder of buildings reaching toward a light they cannot touch.

It is a place of frantic stillness. A hot, wet, industrial lung that breathes slowly in the dark, suspended in a moment of violent creation that has decided to last forever.
