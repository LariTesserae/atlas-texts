The silence here is so loud it has texture.

It is a blinding, shivering roar of white noise, a constant, low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. The sky is a sheet of hammered brilliance, a flat, oppressive white that yields no shadows and reveals no depth. It is not the sun you know; it is a ceiling of raw, unfiltered luminescence, pressing down with the weight of an ocean.

You stand in the Dryness.

The ground is a ceramic hardness, baked by aeons of a heat that has no moisture to moderate it. It is a vast, low-lying bowl of pale, cracked clay and sintered dust, smooth as polished pottery where the wind hasn't scored it. This is the terrain: a barren, perfect flatness interrupted only by the geometry of the city and the violent green of the flora.

There is no water. There never has been. The chemistry of life here runs on mineral oil and liquid ammonia, sequestered deep within the tissues of the living things, but the air itself is a desiccating oven. To breathe is to inhale the scent of hot stone and ozone.

And yet, it is lush.

The vegetation defies the expectation of thirst. It explodes from the dry earth in thickets of glass and fiber. Imagine trees that are not wood, but bundles of translucent, silica-rich cables, standing rigid and tall. They are lush in the sense of sheer volume; dense thickets of crystalline "leaves"—sharp, geometric shards of refracting material—cluster around slender trunks. The light hits them and they sing.

That is the source of the deafening sound.

The world is an instrument. The high-pressure atmosphere, heated to a sweltering warmth, moves in slow, heavy bellicose currents across the bowl. When the wind strikes the forests of glass, the trees resonate. A thousand acres of silica chimes, humming in a key so low it feels like nausea, so high it sets the teeth aching. It is never quiet. The "grass" underfoot—millions of tiny, brittle quartz needles—shatters with every step, adding a crisp, percussive rattle to the ambient drone.

Scattered across this wide, empty bowl is the city, or what remains of the notion of a city. It is urban in its layout and organization, but primitive in its construction.

You see the structures: low, sprawling ziggurats and open-walled courtyards built from the same baked clay as the ground. They are handmade, mud-brick and rough-hewn stone, stacked by hands that knew no metal tools. The architecture is defensive, designed to cast deep shadows where none exist, yet the streets are vast and empty.

This is the paradox of the place: it is a city built for a crowd that is gone. The density of construction is high—clustered towers, intricate alleyways, networks of aqueducts that carry nothing but dust—but the *population* density is absolute zero. It is a ghost metropolis. The empty spaces between the buildings are yawning chasms of silence amidst the roar.

The scale of the landscape is deceptive. It feels like a vast open plain, a limitless desert, but there is a claustrophobic closeness to the horizon. You get the sense that this "world" is a pocket, a contained micro-environment perhaps only a few miles across, a sealed terrarium of a civilization that failed or fled. The erosion is "established"—the buildings are not crumbling ruins, but settled forms. The brick has fused with the clay floor; the edges have been rounded off by centuries of sandblasting winds. It is in balance. The city has merged with the desert.

Fauna is rare, almost non-existent. Occasionally, something stirs in the heat haze—a large, solitary shape. It might be a "stone-eater," a creature resembling an armadillo the size of a car, with a shell of polished basalt. It moves slowly, dragging its belly across the hot clay, feeding on the mineral secretions of the glass trees. It makes no sound; it is a mute, heavy observer in a world of screaming light.

The rules of this world are cruel and simple: *Resonance sustains.* The glass trees do not photosynthesize; they *audiocellize*. They feed on the vibration of the wind and the roar of the light. The deafening noise is not a byproduct; it is the harvest. The city, in its primitive way, was likely built to amplify this, to harness the energy of the song, though now the empty courtyards just echo the emptiness back at you.

It is a place of hostile beauty. To stand in the center of the clay bowl, surrounded by the shimmering, singing forest and the silent, baking city, is to feel small, baked dry, and shaken apart by the sheer volume of existence. It is a room that has been left on, humming loudly, waiting for someone to turn it off.
