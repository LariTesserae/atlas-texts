The air here is heavy, a physical weight you have to push through, warm and thick with moisture. It tastes of ozone and wet iron. This is not the damp of a swamp, but the humid breath of a cooling lung. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight that seems to emanate from the rocks themselves rather than a sky, creating long, bruised shadows that stretch across the ground.

You are standing on a high shelf, a plateau suspended in a void where the wind roars constantly but feels oddly disconnected, as if blowing through a grate far above. The scale is intimate, bounded; looking out, the horizon feels too close, terminating in a curtain of roiling mist or perhaps the sheer drop-off of a world that ends here. It feels like a room that has forgotten its walls.

The ground beneath your feet is raw—agonizingly new. This is a landscape of pre-formation, a geology caught in the act of deciding what it wants to be. The erosion score is near zero; there are no rounded pebbles, no smoothed valleys. Everything is jagged, sharp, and splintered. The stone is black glass and basalt, extruded violently only moments ago, its edges so keen they threaten to cut through the soles of your boots. It is embryonic land. The ground hisses, steam venting from microscopic fissures as the rock cools.

Water weeps from these fresh wounds constantly. It pours down the sheer, cliff-like faces of the obsidian spires in thin, constant sheets, collecting in the shallow, irregular bowls that have just begun to form. The condensation is aggressive; water beads on your skin instantly, dripping from your eyelashes. Every surface is slick, glistening under the dim light.

Vegetation is sparse, fighting for purchase on this unstable, slippery stone. There are no trees, no grass, only isolated clumps of what looks like rust-colored moss and towering, fungal stalks that pulse with a faint, bioluminescent violet. They seem to anchor themselves directly into the cooling rock, feeding on the heat and minerals rather than soil. They are spaced far apart, leaving vast, open stretches of wet, black emptiness between them.

This emptiness is rarely quiet, though. The sound here is loud and pervasive—a symphony of geological stress and biological frenzy. The rock groans as it expands—deep, subsonic booms that rattle your teeth. Overlaying this is a high-pitched, frantic chittering. The fauna is abundant, a teeming ecosystem of small, rodent-like creatures and skittering, multi-legged insects that have evolved specifically for this fresh terrain. They swarm the weeping rocks, lapping up the mineral-rich water. They move with frantic speed, their biological clocks ticking fast in this active environment. They are the scavengers of the newborn world, fearless and numerous, ignoring you as they focus on the feast of cooling stone.

Scattered amongst the raw geology are the signs of the built, though they blend almost seamlessly with the chaos. Monoliths of a dull, grey metal jut from the ground at impossible angles, uncorroded by the wet air. They are single, massive structures, lacking the complexity of cities, resembling needles or markers driven into the earth. There is no writing, only smooth, flawless surfaces that reflect the twisted lightning flickering in the dim sky.

And then there is the tech. It is beyond modern, invisible yet felt. It is not a machine you can touch, but a process. The "erosion" is not natural; it is being managed, or perhaps caused, by forces that current physics struggles to quantify. You notice that the water weeping from the rocks sometimes flows upward, defying gravity, tracing invisible magnetic lines in the air before falling to the ground. The occasional spark jumps between the biological mushrooms and the metal monoliths—a synapse firing.

This is a forge. A nursery for worlds. The high elevation and small scale suggest this is merely one node in a vast, disconnected lattice. The raw land is being printed, or extruded, or dreamt into existence by the invisible machinery humming in the subsonic booms of the rock. The fauna are the clean-up crew, the biological agents introduced to stabilize the raw output.

The atmosphere feels pressurized, like the inside of a diving bell. It is a place of violent birth. You are standing on a ledge that might not be here tomorrow, or might have grown into a mountain by then. The dynamic is constant: the ground shudders, a new spire of black glass bursts from the floor with a sound like cracking thunder, instantly weeping hot water, and the swarm of creatures shifts, surging toward the fresh heat, chattering wildly, oblivious to the high-tech sorcery that creates their home.

It is wet, warm, loud, and sharp—a dimly lit cathedral of creation where the walls are still moving.
