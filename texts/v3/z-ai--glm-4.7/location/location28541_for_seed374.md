The sound is the first thing you understand—a constant, tectonic roar that isn’t an earthquake, but the vibration of a million simultaneous actions. It is a wall of noise, deafening and absolute, pressing against the skin like the humidity.

This is the Hive-Mound, a basin of raw, embryonic earth that is only just now deciding to be land. The elevation is low, a great shallow bowl set below the horizon, trapping the heat and the water. There is no wind to speak of, only the heavy, still air of a greenhouse, hanging thick and wet.

The geology here is newborn. The erosion score is near zero; there are no rounded pebbles, no smooth river stones, no beaches of worn sand. Every edge is serrated, every face of rock is sharp and angular, looking as if it was shattered seconds ago. The ground is a chaotic jigsaw of limestone and basalt shards, rising in jagged, unstable spires that have not yet had the time to tumble. It is a landscape of raw angles and broken glass-like stone, the dust of creation still settling in the warm air.

Water is the medium in which this world exists. It scores a 1.5 on the scale—not a flood, but a pervasive, weeping dampness. The stone here is porous and sweating; water bleeds from the cracks in every vertical surface, creating a perpetual mist that clings to everything. The ground is soft with it, a gray slurry of crushed rock and mineral water that sucks at the feet. It feels like a marsh-edge, but there is no mud, only grey slurry and wet stone.

Because the land is so new, there is no soil. Consequently, there is no vegetation. The palette is strictly mineral: blacks, greys, and rust-reds. There are no trees, no grass, no moss, not even lichen. The world is naked rock and water.

And yet, it is urban.

The "city" is not built on top of the land, but carved directly into the rising spires of raw stone. It is a dense, vertical warren. Staircases are chiseled into the sheer faces of basalt needles, and bridges of rough-hewn timber span the gaps between the towering rocks. The technology is primitive—hand tools, chisels, stone hammers, levers, and rope. There is no metal, no glass, no electricity. The construction is crude but effective, a desperate, frantic clawing for space within the chaotic geology.

The density is extreme. The spires are packed with dwellings—small caves, mud-brick huts clinging to precipices, wooden scaffolding lashed together with vines. It is a town-size infestation inside a geological fracture. The light is dim, blocked by the sheer density of the structures and the perpetual haze of condensation. Torches and hearth fires smolder everywhere, casting long, dancing shadows against the wet rock, filling the air with the smell of smoke and wet ash.

The fauna is abundant, but it is not the wildlife of a forest. It is the people, and the things that live on them. The population is immense, swarming over the sharp rocks like ants. They are humanoid, primitive, their skin stained grey by the rock dust. They work endlessly. The "never changing" dynamic is the key to this place: it is a frozen equilibrium. The land rises, the water flows, the people build, and the noise continues, but the state of the world never shifts. It is a moment of frantic activity stretched out into eternity.

They share this space with the creatures that thrive in the damp and the dark—thick, pale amphibians that cling to the wet walls, insects that buzz in clouds thick enough to choke on, and scuttling, crustacean-like scavengers that clean the waste from the walkways.

The deafening sound is the heartbeat of the city: the rhythmic *thud-crack* of a thousand stone hammers hitting the rock, the roar of the waterfalls that cascade from the high rim of the basin, the chatter of a million voices, the hiss of steam, and the grinding of stone on stone. There is no silence here. Even in the deepest caves, the vibration travels through the wet stone, humming in the bones.

The atmosphere is stiflingly warm (2.2/3). The humidity is near 100%. The air is heavy, tasting of wet dust and smoke. It is hard to breathe, hard to see, hard to think. The heat radiates from the living rock beneath and the press of bodies above.

This is a place of becoming, frozen in the act of arrival. It is a raw, wet, screaming city carved from the earth's fresh wound, where the rock is still sharp enough to cut, and the water never stops weeping.
