The light here is not ambient; it is intrusive.

It falls from a jagged fissure in the stone ceiling far above, a singular, blinding column of white that penetrates the gloom of the hollow and strikes the floor with the weight of a physical object. This is the Below-Surface, a vast, sunken amphitheater that exists beneath the crust of the world, disconnected from the horizon, open only to the sky at a single, searing point.

Because of the elevation, the air is trapped. It is hot (2.6/3)—a stifling, breathless heat that hovers just this side of scorching. The temperature does not fluctuate. There is no wind to carry it away, no cycle of day and night to cool the stone. The atmosphere is in a state of permanent, tropical stasis.

The terrain is defined by a violent contradiction. The geology is raw (0.0/3), as if the ground beneath your feet cracked open only moments ago. The walls of the depression are sheared off in jagged, vertical planes of obsidian and basalt. These rock faces are sharp and untreated by time; they have not been rounded by wind or worn by water. They look like fresh wounds in the planet's skin.

And yet, covering every inch of this fresh, broken stone is the Green (2.6/3).

Vegetation here is not a collection of plants; it is a substrate. The total biomass is absolute. Where the rock split, a dense, interlocking mat of life rushed in to seal the fissure. It is a monolithic ecosystem of mosses, broad-leafed succulents, and climbing vines that have fused together into a single, continuous living carpet. There is no soil; the plants grow directly out of the damp air and the microscopic crevices in the raw rock, digesting minerals directly from the stone.

The "ground" you walk on is not dirt, but a spongy, resilient layer of interwoven roots and fallen fronds that have decayed exactly where they landed, forming a thick, springy humus. It is dense (2.2/3)—so thick that it is difficult to distinguish one plant from another. The space is saturated with chlorophyll.

The moisture level (1.3/3) is specific. It is not wet; there are no pools, no streams, no mud. It is an environment of dampness. The heat pulls water out of the living mass, creating a perpetual, heavy humidity. The air feels thick enough to chew. Water exists here as a film—a glistening coat on every leaf, a slickness on the black rocks, and a constant, fine condensation that slicks the skin. Occasionally, the heat building up in the fissure above causes a sudden plume of warm mist to rain down, a brief, localized shower that hisses as it hits the hot rocks, but for the most part, the water is just a breath in the air.

Scattered across the floor of the hollow are the "buildings" (1.3/3). They are not made of hands. They are architectural growths—massive, bulbous protrusions of the dense vegetation that have risen in arches, spirals, and hollow domes. They look like huts or temples, organic green cathedrals formed by the plants growing over and around themselves until they created structural voids. They serve no obvious purpose, standing as silent monuments to the plant's urge to occupy volume. Some have hollow centers you could crawl into, cool and dark, smelling of rich oxygen and wet dust.

It is incredibly quiet (1.3/3). The silence is heavy and absolute.

There are no birds. There are no insects buzzing. The fauna (0.8/3) is virtually non-existent. Occasionally, you might see a single, sluggish organism—a pale, eyeless lizard or a bloated, slow-moving beetle—trudging through the moss, but they are rare, isolated, and move as if under the influence of a sedative. The ecosystem has no need for grazers or hunters when the primary life form is a static, solar-powered geology.

The weirdness (1.2/3) of the place lies in its stillness. The landscape is dynamic (0.1/3) in its refusal to change. In a normal jungle, things rot, flowers bloom, leaves fall, predators hunt. Here, the sheer density of the vegetation seems to have locked the environment into a frozen equilibrium. The leaves on the vines do not wilt; they simply exist in a state of perfect turgidity. The bright light from above never moves, never dims, casting a shadow that never shifts its angle. It feels less like a living place and more like a diorama—a perfect, terrifyingly still snapshot of life that has paused in the middle of inhaling.

You stand in the bright column of light. The heat presses down on your shoulders. The damp air fills your lungs with the taste of ozone and green sugar. Around you, the green city of plants looms, silent and motionless, waiting for a change that will never come.
