This is the Under-Vault, a place that feels less like a natural cavern and more like the interior of a vast, hollowed-out lung. It is a landscape entirely of manufacture, buried deep beneath the skin of the world, where the air is thick and the walls are always weeping.

### The Architecture and Scale
The space is defined by boundaries; it is a singular, cathedral-sized chamber, roughly the footprint of a small hamlet, but enclosed entirely by masonry. There is no view of a horizon, only the claustrophobic, curving embrace of a ceiling that looms perhaps forty feet overhead. This ceiling is a masterpiece of primitive engineering—a vault of groined arches constructed from millions of hand-molded, mud-baked bricks. The mortar between them has eroded over eons, no longer rigid, but soft and yielding to the touch, crumbled into a gritty paste by the relentless humidity.

The floor is not natural earth. It is a constructed lattice, a grid of raised channels and troughs made of stone and hardened clay, layered over the actual bedrock like a second skin. Everything here is "built." There is no strata, no geological feature that hasn't been chiseled, stacked, or arranged by intent.

### Moisture and Atmosphere
The air is heavy and warm, holding a temperature that sits just above body heat—stifling, humid, and still. It smells of wet clay, old dust, and the musk of living bodies. The water parameter is intermittent but pervasive. It does not rain here, but the space weeps. Condensation slicks every surface. Droplets of water gather on the brick arches, swelling until they are too heavy to hold, then dripping rhythmically onto the floor and the structures below.

The sound is a constant, roaring pressure. It is not the sound of wind or waves, but the noise of density. The acoustics of the brick vault amplify the ambient chaos into a relentless drone—a "white noise" of skittering, chattering, dripping, and rustling that never ceases. It is the sound of a room that is packed too tight.

### The Warren: Density and Construction
The defining characteristic of the Under-Vault is the density of the built environment. The central floor is not open; it is choked by the Warren. Imagine a dense, multi-tiered apiary or a termitarium, but constructed by hand.

Thousands of small, cubicle-like structures have been stacked upon one another, reaching from the floor channels nearly to the ceiling. These are made of simple materials—wattle-and-daub woven around wooden frames, rough-hewn plank boards, stacked ceramic pots, and dried mud. They are crammed together with no space between them, sharing walls, supporting one another in a precarious, leaning city-within-a-room. It is a honeycomb of primitive tech: simple levers, pulleys made of twisted sinew and wood, and ceramic pipes run through the walls of this internal structure, directing the condensation runoff to unseen destinations.

### Ecology and Fauna
The "abundant" fauna are the architects and inhabitants of this chaotic meg-structure. They are the Mason-Rats.

These are creatures the size of small cats or large rabbits, covered in sparse, oily fur that glistens in the dim light. They possess dexterous, three-fingered hands that are constantly moving. They are everywhere. They spill out of the cubbyholes, swarm over the brick arches, and scuttle across the lattice floor. The density is staggering; in every square yard, you see a dozen of them.

They are the engine of the room’s dynamism. The ecosystem here is in constant motion. Mason-Rats are always repairing the softening bricks with fresh mud paste. They are always carrying things—bits of debris, scraps of fungi, stones—up the wooden ladders that connect the tiers. They are fighting, grooming, eating, and sleeping in shifts. It is a biological pace that never slows, a frantic, industrial hum of life.

### Vegetation and Light
Light enters the vault not from the sun, but from the architecture itself. Scattered throughout the brick ceiling are narrow ventilation shafts—tunnels that bore up to the surface. Through these pipes, columns of "normal" daylight beam down, illuminating shafts of swirling dust and steam in the stagnant air. The light is patchy, creating bright, humid islands where the beams hit the floor, and deep, velvety shadows in the corners.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling against the dominance of the stone and the fauna. Where the light beams hit the wet bricks, fuzzy mats of neon-green moss and lichen cling to the masonry. In the damp troughs of the floor lattice, occasional ferns unfurl, their fronds constantly trampled by the passing Mason-Rats. The plants are secondary, mere decoration on the hard bones of the built world.

### Erosion and Age
Despite the frantic activity, the place feels ancient and settled. This is not a ruin falling down; it is a ruin being held together. The "established" erosion is visible in the smooth, rounded edges of the bricks where countless Mason-Rat paws have worn them down over generations. The wooden pillars of the Warren are black with age and the grease of fur, polished to a shine. The space has reached a grim equilibrium: the structure decays, and the creatures rebuild it, in a loop that has been running for centuries.

### The Weirdness
There is a subtle, unsettling rule to this place. The Mason-Rats do not just build; they mimic. If you place a stone block in the center of the room, they will not ignore it, nor steal it. They will incorporate it. They will build around it, seamlessly integrating the foreign object into their chaotic lattice.

The weirdness lies in the feeling of being watched by the architecture itself. The thousands of cubbyholes, the dark gaps in the wattle-and-daub walls—they all feel like eyes. The density of the built environment creates a sense of paranoid compression; the walls feel closer than they should be, the ceiling lower, as if the room is slowly shrinking to accommodate the sheer volume of life inside it. It is a place that has been made so thoroughly that it has become a living entity, digesting the damp and the dark, pulsing with the noise of its own construction.
