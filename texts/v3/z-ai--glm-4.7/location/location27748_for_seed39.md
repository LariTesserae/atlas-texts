The air here is painfully thin, tasting of iron and ozone, suspended in a state of perpetual twilight. You stand on a ridge that feels less like earth and more like the scab of a giant healing wound. This is a world of verticals and negatives—extreme elevation where the horizon is not a line but a curvature of distant, jagged spires piercing a slate-grey ceiling.

The terrain is raw, possessing a brittle, embryonic quality. There is no soil, only the "emerging" geology: fresh, jagged basalt and rhyolite pushed up from the mantle with violent speed. The erosion factor is nearly nonexistent; the rocks have not had the time to be rounded by wind or water. They stand in knife-edged shards, stacked in chaotic, unstable piles that look like they froze in the middle of an explosion. The landscape is under construction, a frantic uplift of the crust that hasn't yet settled into the patience of geologic time.

It is profoundly cold—well below freezing—but paradoxically wet. The dampness does not come from rain, which would freeze instantly, nor from a marsh, which could not exist at this altitude. Instead, the moisture is atmospheric and thermal. The ground is warm. Beneath the fragile, sharp crust, the earth is hot. This heat meets the freezing air, creating a perpetual, heavy fog of condensation. Water weeps from the rocks, running in slick, shining sheets over the black stone before flash-freezing into translucent curtains of ice or pooling into super-cooled puddles that hiss when disturbed.

This is the source of the region’s "industrial" nature. There are no factories, no smokestacks, no buildings to clutter the vast emptiness. The industry is planetary. Deep below, massive tectonic engines are driving this new land upward, and the vents and fissures around you exhale with the rhythmic precision of pistons. Columns of steam blast from fissures in the rock, roaring like locomotives for thirty seconds and then falling silent, a clockwork cycle of pressure release. The ground shudders with a low-frequency thrum—a heavy, mechanical heartbeat that vibrates in the soles of your feet. It feels less like nature and more like an operation, a scaled production of new crust.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to gain a purchase on the slick, unstable stone. There are no trees, no bushes—only a low, cryptogamic crust of lichen and primitive moss that clings to the warmer vents. These patches are neon-green and phosphorescent in the dim light, the only color in a world of black rock and white steam.

Yet, where the green grows, the grey moves. The fauna is abundant, a teeming, engine-like ecosystem that has evolved to exploit this thermal energy. The creatures are hexapodal and low-slung, resembling wet, polished river stones come to life. They are the size of dogs but move with the segmented, rhythmic efficiency of conveyor belts. They swarm over the warm rocks, feeding on the bacterial mats and the mineral-rich slime that oozes from the vents.

They are not quiet, but neither are they cacophonous. The sound of the landscape is a textured hum—the clicking of thousands of chitinous legs against basalt, the soft wet slap of their bodies, the distant jet-engine roar of a steam vent, and the constant, high-pitched whistle of the wind cutting through the empty spires. It is the sound of a factory floor at a distance, muffled by the heavy atmosphere.

The space is open and hollow. You can see for miles across the plateau, but the density is near zero. Between the herds of rock-eaters and the clusters of steam vents, there is nothing but vast, freezing emptiness. The scale dwarfs the observer, making the swarms of fauna look like dust motes floating over a dark, wet machine.

It is a normal place in its physics, but alien in its execution. It is a world being born too fast, a wet, cold, industrial forge where the rock is still hot, the water is weeping, and life clings to the engine in the dark.
