The air here is not empty; it is a solid, vibrating wall of noise. It is a deafening, relentless cacophony—a low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the marrow of your bones, punctuated by the sharp, wet clicks of millions of mandibles and the high-pitched, discordant screeching of countless chitinous limbs rubbing against one another. To shout is useless; the sound of your own voice is swallowed instantly by the ambient roar. You do not hear the wind; the wind does not exist here, only the respiration of the mass.

You are in a low, broad basin, a geological bowl sunk deep into the crust of the world. It is cool—clammy and chill, like the inside of a damp cave—but the temperature is consistent, unyielding. There is no sun to warm the stone, and the thick blanket of living matter insulates the floor, trapping the geothermal warmth and the metabolic heat of the swarm.

The light is nearly gone. It is a world of deep twilight and absolute shadow. There are no stars above, only a heavy, oppressive ceiling of low-hanging clouds or perhaps rock itself. The only illumination comes from the life itself: irregular, spasmodic flashes of bioluminescence—sickly greens, bruised purples, and alarming reds—that flare and die out in seconds, brief beacons in the chaotic dark.

There is no soil as you would recognize it. The ground is slick, wet stone, worn smooth over eons by the slow, grinding movement of the tides of life that traverse it. The rock is porous weeping moisture—a dampness score of just enough to keep everything slick and glistening, but not enough to create pools or rivers. The water here is bound to surfaces, a sheen of oily condensation that coats every inch of the mineral world.

And that world is absent of plants. There is no grass, no moss, no trees. The color green does not exist here. Instead, the mineral landscape is overgrown, but not by flora. The "vegetation" is composed of vast, stationary colonies of sessile fauna—filter-feeders and scavengers that anchor themselves to the rock like barnacles the size of boulders. They form ridges and hillocks, a synthetic topography of calcified shells and hardened secretion tubes. They are pale, draining the grayness of the rock, standing like silent monuments amidst the frenetic motion of the mobile world.

The density is overwhelming. It is a carpet of life. There is nowhere to step that is not living, dying, or decomposing. The ground level is a seething river of carapace and flesh. Millipedes the length of a man’s arm curl around the ankles of large, blind crustaceans that scuttle sideways, snapping at the air. Hoppers with spring-loaded rear legs ricochet off the larger, armored grazers, bouncing through the chaos like sparks from a fire.

This is a predatory world, but the logic of the food chain has been warped by the weirdness of the ecology. There are no photosynthesis-based plants to support the bottom of the web; the base of the food chain here is the stone itself and the dampness. Lithotrophic grazers—creatures with grinding, millstone teeth—etch the living rock slowly, consuming the minerals and the chemosynthetic bacteria that slick the wet surfaces. They are the endless grazers, the "cattle" of this dark plain, moving in slow, grinding herds that scrape the basin floor.

Above them, the density of the predators is staggering. Spindly, multi-legged stalkers weave through the stationary barnacle-trees, plucking the softer grazers from the rock. But everywhere, always, there is the sound. The clicking, the hissing, the wet slap of flesh on wet stone. It is a deafening symphony of consumption.

There is a rhythm to it, however—a dynamic order within the madness. The grazers move in slow, rhythmic pulses, following the veins of minerals in the rock. The predators swarm in coordinated bursts, a flash flood of violence that washes over a herd and then recedes. It is an ancient cycle; the erosion is established, the rock polished to a mirror finish by a million years of mandibles. The basin is settled in its state, a perfect, horrible machine of survival. There is no built structure, for the creatures *are* the architecture. The towering spires of filter-feeders cast the valleys into shadow, their porous shells creating the windless corridors that the swarms run through.

To stand here is to be an intruder in a place that has no room for stillness. The dampness seeps into your clothes; the cool air bites at your skin; the darkness presses against your eyes; but it is the sound that defeats you. It is the roar of the world eating itself, over and over, forever.
