The light here is absolute. It does not come from a sun exactly, but from a pervasive, achromatic brilliance that saturates the atmosphere, eliminating shadows and flattening distances. It is a high-altitude glare, bouncing off the silica-heavy air and the pale, raw rock. The temperature is deceptively mild—cool enough to see your breath, a crisp alpine chill that nips at the skin but never freezes, kept in check by the dampness rising from below.

This is the Riven Crest, a landscape that refuses to look old.

The erosion is fresh, aggressively young. The mountains do not have worn, rounded shoulders; they are jagged wounds in the crust, shards of slate and granite thrust upward as if the earth had shattered only a century ago. The faces of the cliffs are sheer and white, showing the raw striation of the stone. There are no landslides of scree, no accumulated dust at the bases. The rock is clean, sharp enough to cut a hand, smelling of fresh-break and ozone.

Yet, despite the violence of the geology, the land is lush.

Vegetation has seized the sharp edges with terrifying speed. It is a dense, choking saturation of green, but not the green of a temperate forest. It is the green of extremophiles—mosses thick as wet mattresses, succulents with translucent, gel-filled leaves, and ferns with fronds the size of cars. They grow in dense, layered mats that carpet the vertical rock faces. The dampness (water 0.9) is intermittent but constant enough; condensation rolls off the machine-monoliths in the morning, misting the air, and water weeps directly from the pores of the rock itself, collected in deep, fist-sized pockets that keep the vegetation perpetually slick.

The ecology is strange in its solitude. The density of plant life suggests a riot of insects and birds, but the fauna is rare. You might wait all day to see a single thing move. Occasionally, a metallic beetle, iridescent and heavy, will click its way over a mossy boulder, or a silent, six-winged moth will flutter past with the erratic motion of a broken kite. There are no herds, no chattering flocks. The silence of the place is profound, a vacuum where the noise of life should be. The wind does not howl here; it is blocked by the sheer density of the growth and the erratic structures, resulting in a stagnant, heavy quiet.

This quiet makes the industrial presence feel ghostly.

The built environment is scattered, a graveyard of colossal ambition strewn across the high plateau. These are not small shacks or ruins, but the partial remains of mega-structures. A single smokestack, wide as a stadium, rises from a valley floor, snapped off halfway up its length. It is rusted a deep, burnt orange, contrasting violently with the pale stone and neon-green moss. Massive gears, half-buried in the fresh earth, sit exposed to the bright light, their teeth the size of houses. Pipes, thick as ancient trees, run across the landscape before diving abruptly into the soil or ending in frayed, twisted tangles of metal.

The technology level is undeniably industrial, scaled for production, but the purpose is obscure. These do not look like power plants or factories as much as they look like the engines of the world itself. As if the mountains were raised by these pistons, and the water is pumped by these silent centrifuges.

And they are not dead. They are just incredibly slow.

The dynamic of the world is glacial. If you were to stare at a massive, half-submerged drive shaft for an hour, you would see nothing. But if you mark its position against a distant peak and return in a week, you will see it has turned a fraction of a degree. The place hums with a frequency so low it is felt only as a pressure in the teeth, not heard by the ears. The condensation is not just weather; it is exhaust—cooling vapor from the massive, subterranean machines that are still heaving the land upward, creating the young erosion even as you watch.

It is a valley of slow-motion catastrophe. The bright, bleach-white light renders the scene with high definition, showing every rivet, every vein in the giant ferns, every sharp edge of the fresh-cut stone. It feels like a workshop abandoned moments ago, yet overgrown for a thousand years, operating on a timeframe where a century is merely a single rotation of a gear.

You stand on a ridge of limestone that smells faintly of sulfur and wet copper. Below you, a pipe the width of a highway traverses a gorge, burbling softly with water that glows faintly with bioluminescence. There is no one here. Just the damp, the bright, silent light, and the slow, grinding turning of the earth as it builds itself higher.
