The air here is heavy with a chill that seeps through clothing—a damp, mineral cold that smells of wet slate and curing mud. This is a place of low horizons, a shallow bowl of land that feels less like a valley and more like a wound in the earth that has only just begun to scab over.

The rock is the first thing you notice. It possesses a jagged, youthful aggression; erosion has not yet had the time to soften the edges. The stone formations thrust upward like broken teeth, sharp enough to cut careless hands. These are not the rolling, time-worn hills of an old world, but fresh, raw geology, exposed and vulnerable. The ground underfoot is uneven, a tangle of fractured stone plates and loose gravel that shifts with every step, as if the earth itself is still settling into its shape.

Water saturates everything. It is not a landscape of open lakes or roaring rivers, but a persistent, weeping wetness. The sky hangs low, a thick lid of grey that seems to constantly perspire a fine, cold mist. Water trickles out of cracks in the sharp rockfaces, collecting in the crevices and dripping with a rhythmic, hollow plinking sound that is the only constant noise in the quiet. Puddles do not sit stagnant; they are constantly refreshed by the seeping ground, turning the dirt paths into a slick, sucking slurry. Every surface is slick: mossy, shining, and dark with moisture.

Against this chaotic, wet backdrop, the settlement clings. It is a hamlet of claustrophobic density, a "urban" mass of primitive construction packed so tightly that the buildings seem to hold each other up. There are no streets in the traditional sense, only narrow, crooked gullies winding between the structures. The architecture is crude and immediate—walls of piled rough-hewn stone mortared with thick, pale clay, and roofs framed with heavy timber beams that look like driftwood scavenged from a forgotten flood. The tech is evident in the joinery: hand-hewn pegs, lashings of twisted gut, and tools marks left by adzes and chisels. It is a place built by hand and muscle, lacking the precision of advanced engineering but possessing a desperate, sturdy ingenuity.

The scale is intimate, almost suffocating. You can stand on the slightly elevated platform of the central granary—a squat, windowless tower of stone—and see the entire perimeter of the town. The boundaries are abrupt; the settlement ends where the sharp, barren rock begins, transitioning from the packed maze of huts to a desolate, muddy expanse without a buffer zone.

Life here is sparse and strangely quiet. Vegetation struggles to take hold in the sharp, acidic gravel. Where you expect gardens, there are only patches of grey lichen and clumps of tough, stringy moss that cling to the windward sides of the stones. There are no trees, only low, thorny shrubs that look more like wire than plant life. Fauna is rare; you might hear the skitter of a clawed foot in the rooftops, or see a solitary, sleek-furred otter-like creature slipping into a murky runoff channel, but for the most part, the animal kingdom is absent. The silence amplifies the sounds of the settlement: the scrape of a shovel, the groan of timber settling in the damp, and the relentless, weeping drip of the water.

There is a pulse to the place, an active dynamism that feels unsettling. Because the erosion is young, the land is not entirely stable. Small slumps are common; a pile of rubble that was a doorway yesterday might be a slide of mud today. The water level in the cracks rises and falls with invisible tides, independent of the rain. It is a world that is actively becoming, a landscape in the throes of creation, demanding that its inhabitants constantly adapt, repair, and rebuild upon the sharp, wet foundations of a world that hasn't finished deciding what it wants to be.
