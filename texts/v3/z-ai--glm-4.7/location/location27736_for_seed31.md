The air here is heavy with a warmth that feels almost like a physical weight, humid and thick, pressing gently against the skin. This is a high country, far above the sea level of the lowlands, yet the atmosphere retains a tropical, glasshouse quality. The sky is vast and open, a pale, washed-out blue that stretches endlessly over the horizon, unmarred by clouds but filled with a diffuse, pervasive light that casts no harsh shadows. It is bright, but not glaring—the moisture in the air acts as a permanent softener, turning the sunlight into a glowing constant.

The landscape is defined by its verticality and its youth. The mountains here do not look old; they look violent and fresh, as if the crust of the world only yesterday cracked open to reveal them. These are jagged, shard-like peaks, raw stone tearing upward in chaotic clusters. There is no smooth rolling here, no worn-down hills softened by millennia of wind. The rock faces are steep and angular, smelling of ozone and damp earth. Despite the sharpness, or perhaps because of it, the stone is dark—slate grey and deep basalt tones.

Water is the dominant sculptor, yet it seems to have lost its ability to erode. The geologic paradox of this place is the tension between the wetness and the sharpness. It rains constantly, but it is a slow, solemn rain—a drifting mist more than a downpour. Water collects everywhere: in the deep, scoop-like hollows of the rocks, forming clear, still pools that look like mirrors dropped onto the mountainside. Weeping rocks are common; great faces of granite that perpetually shed tears of condensation, creating constant, trickling melodies that are the only sounds in the world.

And it is quiet. The silence is profound and total. There are no birds to sing, no insects to hum, no distant chattering of monkeys. The absence of animal life (0.3) creates a stillness that makes the landscape feel like a painted backdrop or a meticulously kept garden. The only auditory texture is the water—the gentle *plink* of droplets hitting moss, the low hiss of runoff over stone, and the distant, rushing whispers of unseen waterfalls. Even the wind seems to respect the hush, moving through the high passes with a soft, breathy sigh rather than a roar.

Vegetation clings to the high ledges, lush (1.5) but sparse (1.0). It is not a choking forest; it is a scattered collection of perfect, solitary specimens. Massive ferns unfurl in the sheltered crannies, their fronds a vibrant, almost artificial green that contrasts sharply with the grey stone. Tall, slender trees with smooth, silver bark stand alone on promontories, their canopies pruned by the altitude into perfect, domed shapes. The ground between these outposts is bare rock, polished by the constant dampness into a slippery sheen, emphasizing the "emptiness" between presences.

Scattered across this regional expanse, nestled on the few stable plateaus and terraced into the cliff faces, are the structures of the city. The "built" environment (1.7) is extensive but low-density, a sprawling urban skeleton spread over miles of rugged terrain. It is a primitive place (0.7), constructed without mortar or metal. The architecture is cyclopean: massive, hand-hewn blocks of the local dark stone, stacked with such precision that the gaps are nonexistent.

The layout is organized and intentional, suggesting a society that values order and planning. Wide, paved processional ways connect the disparate districts, bridges of rough-hewn timber and stone arching over deep, mist-filled chasms. The buildings themselves are simple—square, functional, lacking ornamentation—but arranged in grids that follow the natural contours of the land. It looks like a city designed by architects who understood that nature was the landlord, building around the rock rather than cutting through it.

There is a feeling of eternal recurrence here. The "dynamic" nature of the world is frozen (0.5). The rain falls, the water flows, and the plants grow, but nothing seems to change. The sharp peaks remain sharp; the pools never overflow nor dry up. It is a world captured in a state of perfect equilibrium. The wetness feeds the lushness, the warmth sustains the life, but the clockwork of geologic time seems to have paused. It feels less like a living ecosystem and more like a terrarium—a high-altitude sanctuary suspended in a warm, wet, silent forever.
