The sound hits you before the air does. It is not the chaotic clamor of a construction site, nor the jagged roar of a battle, but a deep, seismic thrumming that vibrates through the soles of your feet and rattles the teeth in your skull. It is deafening—a constant, oppressive wall of noise composed of a million individual industrial processes: the grinding of tectonic gears, the high-pitched scream of pressurized vents, and the rhythmic, thunderous booming of vast pistons driving deep into the earth.

This is the Floor of the Iron Age, a place so low it feels less like a valley and more like the basement of the world.

Here, the darkness is nearly total, rating barely a half-point on the scale of luminance. The light that does exist is incidental and sickly—occasional arcs of violet lightning crawling across the ceiling of smog, or the dull, heatless orange glow of discharge flares miles away. These brief glimpses reveal a landscape that defies the natural order, for there is no bedrock here. The "ground" is entirely constructed.

You stand on a surface that feels like smoothed stone, worn round and soft by eons, but if you scrape away the thick, oily sludge that coats everything, you find not granite, but dense, layered alloys. The erosion here is absolute (2.7/3). This place has reached its final form. The sharp edges of industry have been rubbed away by the friction of their own operation until the machinery looks like natural topography. Vast, rusted pipe-work no longer stands out as engineering; it has eroded into canyons and hills, fused together by time and condensation. It is a "total built" environment (2.8/3) that has been abandoned so long it has fossilized into a geology of its own.

The air is cool (0.8/3), carrying a damp chill that settles into the lungs. It is a wet place (1.7/3), though it never truly rains in the traditional sense. Instead, the atmosphere is a heavy, saturating fog of condensation. The heat from the deep, unseen machinery clashes with the cold upper voids, causing water to weep constantly from every surface. Great, Gothic arches of black metal drip continuously, forming stalactites of rust that hang over the "valleys" below. Pools of oily, black water collect in the depressions of the floor, reflecting almost no light.

Vegetation is sparse (1.3/3), struggling to survive in the gloom and the vibration. There are no trees, no grass. In the few places where the thick layer of industrial sludge has thinned, patches of gray, leathery lichen cling to the riveted hillsides. They do not photosynthesize—they seem to feed on the vibration and the trace metals in the water, pulsing faintly in time with the thrumming of the machine-world. There is no animal life (0.4/3). No birds fly in the smog; no rats skitter in the dark. The biology of this place has been winnowed down to these silent, mineral-eating skins. It is a pure, lonely ecology of dampness and endurance.

Despite the sparsity of life, the place is active (1.8/3). The silence of living things is replaced by the noise of systems. Great venting towers, spaced miles apart, release plumes of superheated steam that hiss into the cool air with the force of a locomotive. The ground itself shifts imperceptibly—a creeping tectonics as the foundations of the megastructure settle into the mud of the deep crust. It is an open landscape (2.0/3), extending in every direction to a horizon blurred by smog and distance. You could walk for days and never see a break in the pattern of weeping rivulets and corroded gantries.

It is a strange place (1.8/3), possessed of a weirdness that lies in its familiarity. It feels like a ruin, yet nothing here has stopped working. It is a factory that has become a wilderness, a production line that has outlasted its creators and simply continued to churn, processing nothing but air and water, deafening the empty dark, waiting for a product that will never come. The water trickles down the rusted flanks of ancient pistons, and the great machine groans, satisfied in its endless, pointless cycle.
