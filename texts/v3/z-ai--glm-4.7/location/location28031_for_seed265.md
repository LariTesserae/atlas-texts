Here, the dark is not an absence; it is a medium. It has weight, it has texture, and it presses against the skin like warm, wet velvet.

You stand on a low shelf of land, a basin that feels vast without the comforting anchor of a horizon. The elevation is low, yet the air is strangely heavy, dense with humidity that verges on being inhalable liquid. The temperature sits just above blood-warm—a perpetual, sweating heat that never breaks into fever, never cools into shivers. It is the heat of an engine that has been running for a thousand years and has finally settled into a stable, glowing rhythm.

Because there is no light—0.0 on the scale, a total and absolute nullification of photons—the world is experienced through the soles of your feet and the hairs on your arms. The ground is treacherous. This is a landscape of young erosion, a geology only recently born or catastrophically fractured. The rock underfoot is sharp, sharded into flaking knives and jagged ridges that have not yet been smoothed by the patience of water or wind. It feels like walking on a field of broken obsidian or cooling slag, the edges fresh and keen enough to slice through careless boots. It is a "fresh" wound in the crust of the world.

And it is wet. Water is the constant companion here, though it rarely falls as rain. Instead, the atmosphere condenses. The air is so saturated that water beads on every surface, weeping from the fractured rocks, dripping in a slow, rhythmic cadence that marks the passage of time. The ground is slick with a thin film of slime and runoff, pooling in the hollows of the shattered stone. Every step squelches; every movement is accompanied by the wet slap of skin against wet stone.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to take hold in the sharp, unstable ground. There are no towering canopies, no sprawling grasslands. Instead, the flora is minimal, tactile, and alien. You encounter occasional growths that feel like damp suede or cold rubber, low-lying fungi or succulents that cling to the fissures in the rock. They do not smell of green or earth, but of ozone and wet iron.

The space is open, a regional expanse of this shattered, wet darkness, but it feels claustrophobically dense. This density comes not from the trees or the structures, but from the sheer saturation of the air and the abundance of life that moves within it.

The fauna is abundant. In the absolute dark, the ecosystem has abandoned sight entirely. The air is thick with the sensation of movement. You feel the displacement of air as large things glide silently past you. The ground vibrates with the scuttle of countless exoskeletons—things with too many legs, things that feel like dry leaves and wet stone when they brush against your ankles. There is a constant, pervasive sensation of being surrounded by living bodies, a crowded room in the pitch black. It is a silent crowd, though; the sound level is mostly quiet, a low hush of white noise punctuated only by the distant, rhythmic *drip… drip… drip* of condensation and the soft, wet sounds of things feeding or moving.

But you are not in a wilderness. You are in a machine.

The "scattered" built environment is felt before it is understood. Rising out of the wet, sharp rock are monoliths of industrial might. They are not whole factories, but the vast, scattered bones of them. A rusted gantry the size of a cathedral tower looms somewhere to your left, detectable only by the faint metallic resonance it makes when the dripping water hits it. To your right, a wall of smooth, riveted steel, warm to the touch, hums with a low-frequency vibration that rattles your teeth. These are industrial artifacts, scattered like toys across the basin, engines of such scale that their purpose is lost to time. They are warm, radiating a gentle heat that mixes with the ambient air, creating thermal currents that the blind fauna ride like invisible rivers.

The technology here is industrial, but it has stagnated. The engines do not roar; they breathe. They turn at a glacial pace. The dynamic of the world is agonizingly slow. Change happens over epochs. A gear might rotate a single degree in your lifetime. The water level rises by inches over a century. The fauna follows cycles of torpor and activity that span decades. There is no urgency here, only a slow, grinding perpetuation.

And then, there is the weirdness—the breakdown of reality.

In this absolute dark, physics feels like a suggestion rather than a law. The density of the atmosphere sometimes seems to solidify, making walking feel like swimming through syrup. Distances are deceptive; a step forward might take you ten feet, or ten inches, depending on how the geometry of the place has decided to fold in on itself at that moment. The silence is occasionally broken by a sound that is not a sound—a pressure wave that feels like a sudden flash of color in the mind, a synesthetic glitch where the machinery bleeds into the perception of the observer.

The boundary between the wet rock, the biological machinery, and the industrial detritus dissolves. You touch a wall that feels like flesh; you step on a stone that pulses with a heartbeat. The world is young and old at the same time, a fresh ruin existing in a slow-time darkness, waiting for a light that will never come to reveal whether it is a graveyard or a womb.
