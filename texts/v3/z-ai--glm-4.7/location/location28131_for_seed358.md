The roar is the first thing that exists—a constant, physical weight that presses against the skin before the eyes have even adjusted to the gloom. It is not the rhythmic crashing of ocean waves, nor the howl of wind, but a chaotic, industrial-decibel hydraulic thrumming. It is the sound of a million cubic tons of water moving under pressure through tight spaces, colliding with metal, and falling into deep, echoing hollows.

This is the Low Sump.

**Terrain and Geology**
The landscape lies well below the true surface of the world, situated in a tectonic bruise, a vast subterranean depression that feels more like a wound in the earth than a valley. The "ground" is not a floor but a chaotic jam of raw, angular stone. Because the erosion level is near zero—emerging, embryonic—there are no rounded pebbles, no smooth sands, no soil. Every rock is jagged, showing fresh, un-oxidized fractures where the earth has recently split apart. The stone is packed together with terrifying density; there is no open space between the boulders, creating a labyrinth of sharp corners and vertical slabs that leans inward, claustrophobic despite the regional scale of the cavern.

It is a geology that has not yet decided what it wants to be, frozen in the moment of creation.

**Water and Atmosphere**
The water parameter here is high, but it is not a lake. It is a saturation. The air is so thick with humidity that it feels like breathing warm soup. Condensation drips continuously from the unseen ceiling hundreds of meters above, creating a permanent, soaking mist. Water weeps from every rock face, turning the stone slick and treacherous. Pools of black, stagnant water sit in the hollows between the boulders, but the main force of the water is hidden—rushing through unseen fissures behind the rock walls, the source of the deafening roar.

**Light**
Visibility is poor. The light is a dim, dusty haze (0.7/3). It comes from neither sun nor moon, but from a sickly, flickering phosphorescence that grows on the wettest rocks and from the rare, distant strobe of failing industrial lamps far away. Shadows are heavy and absolute, hiding the sheer drop-offs that separate one cluster of jagged rocks from the next.

**Vegetation**
There is no forest here, no grass. With no soil to root in and precious little light, vegetation is sparse to the point of nonexistence. The only greenery is a slimy, superficial film of algae and primitive moss that clings to the wettest rocks, providing a slight friction against the otherwise slick stone. It is a biology of scum and slime, shallow and struggling.

**Built Environment**
Amidst this raw, chaotic stone, the mark of industry is scattered but jarring. Massive pipes, rusted to the color of dried blood, burst out of the rock walls at odd angles, leaking steam that adds to the humidity. They are remnants of a failed or perhaps ongoing attempt to drain the sump. Occasionally, a brutalist concrete piling stands incongruously against the natural rock, the base of a structure that was abandoned long ago, now half-swallowed by the packed stone. Ancient pump housings, silent and dead, sit like monoliths in the gloom, their gauges shattered, their iron casings pitted by the constant moisture.

**Fauna**
Where the plants are absent, the animals have taken over. The fauna is abundant (2.0/3) and aggressive. This is a kingdom of amphibians and insects. Giant, pale crabs the size of boulders scuttle through the crevices, their shells clicking audibly over the roar of the water. The air is thick with swarms of midges that draw no blood but buzz in clouds so dense they choke the throat. In the dark pools, eyeless fish and salamanders wriggle in dense clusters, packed fin-to-fin. It is a frenetic, teeming ecosystem, fueled by the constant heat and the detritus washing down from above.

**Sound and Dynamics**
The defining characteristic is the sound: deafening (2.7/3). It is a roar that never ceases. It drowns out speech, forcing communication to happen through shouts or hand signals. It masks the sound of predators. It is the sound of the world trying to crush itself.

Yet, for all the noise and the teeming life, the dynamic change is slow (1.0/3). The water level rises by millimeters a year. The rust on the pipes thickens imperceptibly. The rocks shift only on a geological timescale. This is a landscape of patient violence. It is loud and hot and frantic, but nothing actually moves very fast. It is a steam-powered purgatory, caught in a permanent state of becoming, waiting for the water to finally win and fill the hollow completely.
