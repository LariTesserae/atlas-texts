The sky here is a static, unblemished sheet of pale slate, hanging low over a landscape that does not so much sit upon the crust as protrude from it. This is a high, cold shelf of the world, a vast, continental plateau where the air is thin enough to taste like iron and sharp enough to cut the lungs. It is a place of absolute scale, where the horizon is not a curve but a geometric certainty, receding into a haze of industrial vapors.

The terrain is aggressively young. There are no rolling hills, no eroded valleys softened by time. Instead, the land is a chaotic network of jagged fissures and raw, upthrust stone plates, as if the planet’s crust has been shattered and frozen in the moment of breaking. These sharp-edged scarps are dark, glassy basalt, fractured into millions of knife-like shards.

Water is the binder of this broken world. It is a wet landscape, but not a drowned one. The humidity is total—a condensing atmosphere that weeps from the rock faces. Every sharp cliff edge is draped in curtains of constant, slow-trickling water. The moisture doesn't pool into lakes or flow in rushing rivers; it creeps. It coats the razor-sharp stones in a slick, treacherous veneer of glistening wetness. In the perpetual cold, this movement is arrested mid-fall. Icicles hang like crystal daggers the length of cathedral spires, frozen in the act of dripping, never growing, never melting. The water is a physical presence, a heavy, wet fog that dampens sound and absorbs the dim, normal light filtering through the cloud layer.

Against this backdrop of wet stone and ice, the vegetation defies logic. It is lush—a dense, vibrant carpet of moss and lichen that clings to every vertical surface. It is not the green of summer, but the bruising, electric verdigris of oxidized copper. This flora is thick, spongy, and deeply cold to the touch. It coats the industrial monoliths as thoroughly as it does the natural rock, blurring the line between the geologic and the manufactured.

And the manufactured is everywhere. This is an urban sprawl without a population, a city built for ghosts or gods. The "buildings" are not clustered in streets but stand in isolation, separated by miles of empty, wet mossland. They are colossal ziggurats of rusted iron and reinforced concrete, factory complexes the size of mountain ranges, and ventilation shafts that pierce the cloud layer like breathing tubes.

The density of the infrastructure is low—you could walk for hours across the wet spongy moss without seeing a pillar, only to suddenly find yourself at the base of a refinery tower that scrapes the stratosphere. This emptiness creates a profound sense of hollow space. The structures are not lived in; they are simply maintained, or perhaps merely operating.

Because it is loud.

The sound here is not the chaotic noise of a city, but a single, overwhelming, constant thrum. It is the vibration of a billion engines turning in unison deep underground. Massive pistons the size of islands shunt slowly back and forth, a low-frequency booming that you feel in your teeth more than you hear with your ears. Exhaust stacks belch plumes of white steam that rise and join the cloud cover, adding to the perpetual weeping of the atmosphere. It is a deafening roar that feels like silence because it never, ever varies.

There are no animals. No birds fly between the towers. No insects chirp in the moss. The ecology is limited to the copper-green carpet and the strange, pale fungal growths that cluster around the warm exhaust vents. It is a sterile, mechanical ecosystem. The only movement is the falling water, the rising steam, and the rhythmic, unchanging rotation of gears the size of moons.

And that is the truest nature of this place: it is frozen in a dynamic equilibrium. The water drips, but the level of the pools never rises. The gears turn, but the machine goes nowhere. The steam rises, but the clouds do not disperse. Time seems to have stalled here, caught in a loop of industrial production without consumption, a vast, high-altitude factory running on empty, processing the raw materials of a young landscape in a cycle that has no beginning and no end. It is a loud, cold, wet, and utterly still eternity.
