The air here is heavy, a humid, respiratory warmth that clings to the skin like a second layer of clothing. It is not the stagnant air of a sealed tomb, but the breath of something vast and alive. You are standing in a cavity so enormous that the ceiling is lost not in darkness, but in a luminous, pearlescent haze. The light is normal—daylight-bright, diffusing through a thick, mineral-infused fog that glows with a soft, constant phosphorescence. It casts no sharp shadows, only a blurring, omnipresent radiance that makes the distance hard to judge.

This is the Under-Song, a place of raw geology caught in the act of becoming.

The ground beneath your feet is not stable rock, but a slurry of calcifying mud and sharp, volcanic glass that has yet to settle. The landscape is embryonic. Great spires of stone thrust upward from the floor like jagged teeth, emerging from the wet earth even as you watch. You can hear the groan of the planet—the grinding tectonic plates shifting deep beneath, a subsonic vibration that rattles the teeth. Every few minutes, a section of the cavern wall will slough off with a thunderous crash, sending fresh landslides of grey sludge into the pools below. It is a violent, turbulent erosion; the world is destroying itself as fast as it is being built, a chaotic churn of stone and water.

Water is everywhere, yet it is not a sea. It is intermittent—weeping from the porous ceiling, dripping in rhythmic, deafening patterns, collecting in thousands of shallow, steaming pools that vanish and reappear as the terrain shifts. The humidity is absolute. Water condenses on the weird, glassy formations that hang from the roof, raining down in constant, random drops that plink against the chitinous armor of the residents.

And there are residents. The fauna here is overwhelming.

Because there are no plants—no greenery, no moss, not even a lichen to break the grey mineral monotony—the biomass has condensed into the animal kingdom. The floor is a moving carpet of life. Millions of creatures occupy every inch of the open space. There are pale, eyeless crustaceans the size of dogs, scuttling over one another in a frantic, clattering tide. Swarms of leathery, bat-like things weave through the air columns, their wings creating a collective wind that smells of ozone and wet fur. The noise is deafening: a cacophony of clicking claws, high-pitched shrieks, the wet slap of bodies against stone, and the endless, rhythmic dripping.

It is a density of life that defies logic. In the absence of vegetation, these things have adapted to eat the stone itself, or the chemosynthetic bacteria that crust the damp rocks, or, more likely, each other. The ecology is a frantic, hyperactive loop of predation. A swarm of spindly, many-legged arthropods will descend upon a dying slump-beast, stripping it to the bone in seconds, their mandibles clicking like a thousand shears, before the larger predators—massive, lumbering horrors with hides like granite—wade through the swarm to snap up the scavengers.

Amidst this biological riot, the marks of intelligence are scant and pathetic. Scattered across the raw, shifting landscape are the primitive attempts of built structures. They are crude, composed of piled basalt blocks and lashed-together bones of leviathans. They look like temporary waystations or crude altars, already crumbling and half-buried by the churning mud. They suggest a people who tried to tame this chaos, who perhaps mined the glass or harvested the swarm, but who were ultimately forced to remain nomadic, their technology barely rising above the sharp rock and bone they wield.

There is a weirdness here that prickles the skin. The fauna… they don't just move randomly. There is a pattern to the chaos. As you watch the vast, open valley of the cavern, you see that the swarms move in currents, swirling around the emerging stone spires like water around a ship’s hull. It is a bazaar of movement, a turbulent market of energy. The stone itself seems to guide them, or perhaps they are shaping the stone with their sheer numbers, carving the chaotic tunnels with their collective bodies.

The ground shudders violently. A fresh geyser of warm, mineral-rich water erupts from a fissure fifty yards away, sending a spray of scalding mist into the air. The sound is a roar that drowns out the chittering of the swarm for a moment. The creatures do not flee; they surge toward the heat, toward the nutrients, driven by a hunger that fuels this entire, unstable, fever-dream of a world.

Everything is damp, loud, and moving. It is a womb of stone and flesh, terrifying in its intensity, beautiful in its raw, unfinished hunger. You are deep inside the earth, but you have never felt more surrounded by the pulse of existence.
