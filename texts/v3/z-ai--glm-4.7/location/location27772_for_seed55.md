There is no surface here. There is only the descent.

This is a world drowned completely, a sphere of water held tight by gravity, without a single island breaking the tension. The "sky" is merely lighter water, fading from a bruised purple at the zenith to an absolute blackness in the abyssal depths. It is cold—a pervasive, shivering chill that hovers just above freezing, slowing the blood and quieting the mind.

You drift through the infinite volume.

Beneath you, the floor of the world is not rock, but masonry. It is a constructed seabed, a vast, flat tiling of hexagonal basalt stones, each one the size of a city block, fitted together with such precision that no weed grows in the seams. This is the "Natural" state of things: a planetary floor laid by hand. Time has settled it, however. The parameters indicate an ancient erosion; the sharp edges of the stones have been worn down by the eternal friction of the currents until the floor rolls like a gently undulating pavement.

It is dark. The light level is near zero, a visual starvation. Visibility is limited to what can be felt or sensed in the gloom. There is no sun to penetrate this deep; the only illumination comes from the water itself, a faint, phosphorescent decay of plankton, or the rare, bioluminescent pulse of the constructed flora.

And what flora it is.

Because the tech level is primitive, this garden is not made of glass and steel. It is made of stone, clay, and weave. The "vegetation" is colossal, a dense forest of kelp-like towers that rise thousands of feet from the basalt floor. But they are not plants. They are enormous, braided structures of rough-hewn fiber and dried reed, anchored to the floor with massive weights of carved granite. They are "grown" in the sense that they are added to, layer upon layer, by the unseen tenders of this world—a civilization that has perhaps passed, or simply moved to the deep.

These braided towers sway slowly in the current. They are dense. In a world where the density is high, you cannot swim freely. You must navigate the maze of rope and stone. The towers are encrusted with real, living mosses and slime molds, which thrive in the cold, dark water, feeding on the slow decay of the fiber. This is the lushness: a thick, velvety coating of life that blankets every artificial surface. It feels like swimming through a cold, underwater tapestry.

The scale is difficult to comprehend. The spacing of the braided towers suggests a logic of continental architecture. This is not a thicket; it is a submerged city of columns, stretching on forever. The water between the pillars is still, a vast, open hall of liquid silence.

There are no fish. No sharks dart through the gloom, no crabs crawling on the basalt. The fauna parameter is near zero. The only movement is the slow, rhythmic swaying of the towers and the infinitesimal drift of the silt. It is a quiet place, a mostly silent world where sound travels differently. The "noise" is just the low, thrumming pressure of the deep ocean, a vibration felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ears. Occasionally, a creak echoes from the deep roots of the fiber-towers as the strain of the current shifts—a sound like a wooden ship settling in a storm, but stretched out over hours.

Everything is built, yet everything feels ancient. This is an environment that has reached its final form. The erosion is settled; the construction has stopped. The tools used to carve the basalt and weave the giant stalks were simple—hammers, chisels, needles of bone or flint. There is no plastic here, no alloy. Just stone and organic matter, preserved by the cold and the pressure.

It is a world of perfect suspension. The cold water preserves the fibers of the towers from rotting quickly; the darkness prevents the growth of parasitic weeds that might choke them. It is a system in perfect, slow equilibrium. To be here is to float in a dark, cold cathedral made of rope and stone, a vast and quiet machine that has been running itself for ten thousand years, waiting for nothing, producing nothing, simply existing in the dark.

The water is the medium, the architecture is the structure, and the silence is the rule. You hang suspended in the black cold, surrounded by the slow, living ghosts of a hand-woven forest, listening to the creak of the earth.
