The air here is not air, or at least not the kind that invites breath. It is thin, sharp, and incredibly dry—a suspension of static dust and silica that tastes of ozone and old iron. You are high. Too high. The elevation is crushing, a verticality that goes beyond mere mountains into a realm where the horizon feels tilted, a sharp ramp disappearing into a bruised, purplish-grey gloom.

The light is dim, perpetual twilight without a sun to cast it. It seems to emanate from the rocks themselves, a faint, bioluminescent phosphorescence of failing geology. But to call it geology implies a history this place lacks.

The landscape is **emerging**. It is raw, embryonic. There are no rounded hills, no valleys smoothed by the patience of water. The terrain is a jagged labyrinth of splintered obsidian and hyper-sharp shale, thrust upward in violent, chaotic angles. It looks as though the crust of the planet has been snapped like a dry twig, revealing the wet, glinting innards beneath. The erosion score is non-existent; these edges are fresh, bleeding heat and radiation. It is a world caught in the act of being born, frozen in the moment of creation.

And it is loud.

The sound parameter is deafening, a roaring, tectonic bass that vibrates through the soles of your boots and rattles your teeth. Yet, the dynamic is slow. The sound is not the crash of thunder or the howl of wind, but the grinding of infinite masses moving at a glacial pace. It is the scream of continental plates shearing against one another in slow motion. The noise is a constant, physical pressure, a wall of sonic density that feels like it might crush your chest. But visually, nothing moves. The peaks remain stationary; the crystal trees do not sway. The roar is simply the friction of existence, the sound of reality trying to hold itself together against the weirdness.

There is no water. 0.3 on the scale implies near-total absence, and the land reflects that desiccation. It is a salt-flat nightmare, the ground crusted over with white alkali and grey dust. When you touch the ground, it feels warm and granular, distinctively devoid of moisture.

Yet, it is lush.

Vegetation here defies the lack of water. The "lushness" is a riot of crystalline flora. Forests of towering, hexagonal quartz "trees" erupt from the dry stone, their translucent trunks glowing with a faint, rhythmic pulse. They are not biological in the earthly sense; they are silicon-based life forms that drink the vibration of the deafening roar. The sound is their nutrient. The bass feeds the crystal lattice. Leaves are sheets of mica, tinkling faintly—a high, piercing melody that is barely audible above the earth-shattering drone. The ground is choked with glass-blossoms and mineral moss, sharp to the touch and glittering in the dim light.

The weirdness is absolute. Reality here is a suggestion, not a law. The distance between points is negotiable. You look at a crystal formation a mile away, glance down to check your footing, and when you look back, the formation is looming directly over you, or it has vanished entirely, replaced by a patch of empty sky. Gravity seems to function on preference rather than physics; dust motes hang in the air, motionless for hours, before suddenly shooting upward. The world flickers, a stuttering film reel where frames are missing. Sometimes the ground disappears for a microsecond, revealing a void of static green beneath, before snapping back into being.

There are no animals. The roar and the unstable physics would crush flesh instantly. But there are traces of something else.

The built environment is scattered and primitive, yet hauntingly advanced in its understanding of this place. You find small structures—low, circular huts built from piled basalt slabs, roofed with sheets of hammered copper. They are primitive in construction, dry-stone techniques held together by gravity and friction. But their placement is precise. They sit at the nodes of the sound, the points where the deafening vibration coalesces into a standing wave. Inside, the roar dampens to a hum.

Tools lie scattered around—hammers of meteoric iron, chisels made of diamond-hard substances. These were a people, or a force, that tried to carve the emerging geology. They weren't building shelters; they were tuning the landscape. They carved channels into the crystal trees to alter the pitch of the singing leaves. They built standing stones to redirect the slow, crushing vibrations.

But they are gone. Or perhaps they never were. The weirdness makes memory unreliable.

You walk carefully. The sparse density means you can see for miles, but the view changes every time you blink. The openness is a trap. You feel exposed under the dim, sourceless light, a tiny speck on the raw, bleeding bone of a world that is still deciding if it wants to exist.

The temperature is cool, a brisk, sterile chill that nips at exposed skin. It contrasts with the warmth of the vibrating rock. You are cold, but the ground is hot.

This is the Resonant Wastes. A desert of glass and stone where the silence of animals is replaced by the screaming of the earth. It is lush with deadly beauty, primitive in its tools, but ancient and alien in its hunger. It is a place where you do not drink water; you drink the noise. It is a place where the land is not yet finished being built, and you are just a ghost walking through the construction site of a god.
