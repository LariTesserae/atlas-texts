It is not a cave, though it is buried. It is a hollow of impossible brightness, a subterranean orchard of white light that seems to bleed directly out of the rock.

You stand in a space that feels intimate, almost claustrophobically personal—a single room of earth, roughly the size of a small chapel. The floor is uneven, jagged, and raw. This is a geology that has not yet learned the patience of time; it is *emerging*. The stone here is fresh, broken from the deep dark only moments ago, perhaps. There is no dust, no sand, no rounded edges smoothed by aeons of wind or water. Every angle is a knife’s edge. The rock faces shimmer with a wet, oily sheen, not from submersion, but from a heavy, static dampness that hangs in the cool air like a held breath.

The light is the source of the uncanniness. There is no sun above, no bioluminescent fungi on the walls. The illumination has no source point. It is simply a property of the atmosphere here, a vibrant, sterile noon-day brilliance that saturates the air. Shadows do not behave as they should; they detach from objects and linger a fraction of a second too long, or they stretch toward the light rather than away from it.

The walls are mostly bare—sparse, as if the world ran out of pigment before it finished this room. But in the niches and fissures of the raw stone, there are structures. They look like the calcified ribs of ancient engines or the petrified frames of small houses, scattered artfully around the perimeter. They are "built" in the sense that they possess geometry—right angles, arches, vertical pillars—but they are grown entirely of crystal and bone-white stone. They are architectural fossils, devoid of function, standing as silent monuments to a logic that does not apply here.

The silence is heavy, but the silence is a lie. The room is alive.

Because the vegetation is so sparse—only a few pale, translucent stalks growing in the cracks that look more like glass fiber than plant life—the ecology has turned elsewhere. The floor is a moving carpet of fauna. It is abundant, a teeming riot of macroscopic life. Thousands of small, pale creatures swarm over the sharp rocks.

They are things like trilobites, but transparent, showing the dull pulsing of organs within. There are soft, gelatinous slugs that inch along the raw crystal pillars, leaving trails of iridescent mucus that evaporate into the light. There are beetle-like things with carapaces of mirrored chrome that skitter frantically, their legs clicking against the stone with a sound so quiet it feels like a hallucination—a dry whisper in a sealed tomb.

There is no chirping, no buzzing, no roaring of wind. The sheer volume of biological motion happens in near-total silence. It is a mute pandemonium. You see a predator—a thing like a glass spider—ambush a smaller creature. The prey is wrapped in silk, dragged under a ledge. The struggle is violent, thrashing, desperate, but utterly soundless. It is like watching a tableau through thick glass.

The air is cool, enough to raise goosebumps on your skin, carrying the metallic taste of ozone and wet clay.

And then, the weirdness asserts itself. Because the erosion is non-existent, the world here does not hold its shape perfectly. The reality is thin. As you watch, a section of the floor near the far wall seems to hesitate—a flicker of static, a glitch in the texture of the stone. A cluster of the mirrored beetles walks into this glitch and vanishes, not into a hole, but simply ceases to be, their existence edited out of the frame. A moment later, the stone "renders" again, solid and seamless.

Elsewhere, a pillar that was standing straight a moment ago is now gently curved, bending like soft wax, though the stone remains rigid to the touch. The light pulses—not getting brighter or dimmer, but shifting in *frequency*, turning the air momentarily syrupy. The creatures do not react. They continue their frantic, silent consumption of each other in this pocket of raw, unfinished, illuminated madness, trapped in a room that is simultaneously a womb and a graveyard.

It feels as though you have stepped into the pause between two thoughts of a god—a small, bright, unfinished room where the physics were sketched in but never fully debugged, where the water weeps upward in defiance of gravity, and the silence screams.
