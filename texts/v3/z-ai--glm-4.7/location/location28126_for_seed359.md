The air is hot and smells of static electricity and pulverized limestone. It is a dry heat, a kiln-heat, entirely devoid of humidity. There is no moisture here; the concept of dew or sweat is foreign to the physics of this place. If you were to lick the grey-green stones, your tongue would come away dusty and perhaps slightly burned, tasting of old iron.

You are standing in a low, enclosed vault—a space roughly the size of a cathedral nave, but with a ceiling so low that it feels less like a sky and more like a lid pressing down. The floor is a paved mosaic of hand-hewn basalt tiles, fitted together with primitive mortar that has been driven deep into the seams by the weight of ages, yet the overall structure feels brand new. The edges of the tiles are razor-sharp; the corners of the room where the walls meet the floor have not been rounded by dust or time. This is a young geometry, freshly birthed, still holding the tension of its creation.

But the "nature" of the place has already begun to riot.

The space is lush in a way that defies biological taxonomy. Because there is no water, the "vegetation" does not photosynthesize; it mineralizes. The room is overgrown with a dense, choking garden of crystalline structures and metallic lattices. Thick, cable-like vines of braided copper hang from the rafters, heavy and rigid, fusing with the floor where they touch. Ferns made of green glass, their edges translucent and sharp as knives, sprout from the cracks in the masonry. There is no soil, only the manufactured substrate of the floor and walls, which the flora seems to devour directly, converting the stone and brick into more of itself.

The density is claustrophobic. There is barely a straight line of sight longer than a few feet. The metallic ferns overlap with webs of gold filament that catch the "normal" white light filtering from a source obscured above. The light is steady and unshadowed, casting no harsh glare, illuminating the intricate, crowded interior with a clinical flatness. It feels like being inside the mechanism of a watch that has been left to rust and bloom simultaneously.

Despite the abundance of life, it is mostly quiet. The sound is a low, near-subsonic hum—the vibration of the copper cables expanding in the heat, the slow microscopic shifting of the glass leaves. Occasionally, there is a sharp *tink*, like a spoon hitting a crystal glass, as a thermal fracture opens in one of the larger stone-pillars.

The fauna is abundant but small, scuttling in the undergrowth of wire and stone. They are things of chitin and ceramic, beetle-like creatures the size of a fist that scurry over the copper vines, their legs sparking slightly where they bridge a connection. They do not drink; they seem to graze on the oxidization of the metals, their mandibles scraping softly against the vines. They move with a jerky, clockwork motion, stopping frequently to absorb the ambient warmth.

The most striking feature of the landscape is the "weirdness"—the breakdown of reality. The construction of the room was primitive, executed by hands using simple chisels and levers, but the result is a violation of Euclidean law. In the corner where the density of the copper growth is highest, the walls seem to fold into one another. A granite pillar, clearly carved by a simple mason’s tool, protrudes from the floor, but halfway up its length, it seamlessly blends into a hanging tapestry of glass moss that defies gravity. The perspective is wrong; if you stare too long at the junction where a stone wall meets a cluster of quartz-flowers, your eye slips. The distance between two points is uncertain. It is a room that is larger on the inside than the outside, or perhaps loops back on itself, a space where the makers’ primitive understanding of architecture accidentally tapped into a glitch in the simulation.

Everything is static, yet dynamic in the extreme long term. You can feel the slow, geological pulse of the place. The glass ferns are growing—imperceptibly to the human eye, but if you were to turn your head and look back, you would swear a frond has extended an inch toward your face. The copper vines are thickening, slowly crushing the primitive masonry walls that built them. The heat radiates from the walls, a patient, baking warmth that encourages this slow, silent crystallization. It is a terrarium built by giants who didn't understand the seeds they were planting, left to simmer in a timeless, dry heat, waiting for the sharp edges to finally meet and seal the box forever.
