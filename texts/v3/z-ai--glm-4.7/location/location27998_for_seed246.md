This is the High Hollow, a plateau of ancient stone suspended in the freezing mist, where the architecture of the earth has worn down into a city of ghosts.

The elevation here leaves the air thin and razor-sharp. It is a cold that stings the skin immediately—a 0.3, deep and penetrating. It is not the dry, fluffy cold of a winter snowstorm, but a wet, clinging chill (1.4) that seems to leech heat directly from the bone. The dampness is omnipresent. It is not a place of heavy rain or deep lakes, but of condensation. The air is so heavy with moisture that it precipitates onto every surface in a constant, silent sheen of sweat. Water collects in the shallow, natural bowls of the stone, rimmed with delicate lace-like rims of ice that fracture if a shadow falls across them.

The ground underfoot is the star here. The erosion rating (2.8) speaks of a primordial patience. The bedrock has been smoothed into rolling, undulating hills of granite and basalt, worn down over eons until the sharp edges of geological youth are gone. There are almost no jagged peaks here; instead, the terrain is a series of vast, sleeping stone giants, rounded and polished by the wind and the eternal wet. It feels like the end of the world, a landscape that has reached its final, stable form and has settled into a long, geological senescence.

Because the scale is small (1.3), the High Hollow feels less like an endless wilderness and more like a singular, defined room—a vast, open-air cathedral bounded by distant, fog-shrouded scarps. You can see the boundaries of the place, the edges where the land drops away into the void below.

And yet, it is "urban" (1.5). As you walk through the mist, the arrangement of the stones strikes a chord of recognition. The erosion has left behind pillars and arches, causeways and plazas. There are clusters of standing stones that look like the foundations of a temple, and long, low depressions that mimic the layout of streets. It is a city, but a fossilized one. It is impossible to tell if this was built by hands or if the earth simply eroded into this shape, mimicking the order of civilization to mock the viewer. There is no tech here (0.2), no glass or steel, only the raw, grey bone of the planet arranged in complex, haunting patterns.

The vegetation (1.5) is lush only in a desperate, clinging way. Because it is so high and cold, trees cannot take root. Instead, the stone is draped in thick, velvet carpets of moss. The colors are shockingly vibrant against the greyscale rock—emerald, chartreuse, and deep bruised purples. Where the water collects in the hollows, the moss swells into floating islands of spongy green, soft and damp to the touch. It is a quiet, low-lying jungle that hugs the ground for warmth.

The density is low (0.4). This is an empty place. There are vast distances between the stone-pillars. You can stand in the center of the "plaza" and see nothing but rolling moss and rock for hundreds of yards in every direction. The space between things is a yawning chasm of open air. It feels hollow, like the ribs of a massive beast.

It is also loud (2.4). This is the contradiction that defines the Hollow. It is silent of life—the fauna (1.0) is rare, perhaps only a few hardy, white moths clinging to the moss, or the occasional distant silhouette of a mountain goat that freezes and watches you before vanishing. There are no birds, no chattering insects. But the environment itself is a roar.

The wind (2.1 dynamics) is the architect of this noise. Because the stone pillars are hollowed and ancient, the wind rushing through them creates a complex, acoustic instrument. The air currents move constantly, shifting direction with the erratic weather patterns of the altitude. As the wind hits the hollows of the stone, it creates a deep, resonant thrumming—a bass note that vibrates in your chest. Overlaying this is the high-pitched whistle of air shooting through natural fissures, and the constant "plink-plink" of melting condensation dropping into shallow pools, amplified by the stone acoustics.

The weirdness (1.7) lies in the resonance. The sound isn't just noise; it feels structured. The wind changes pitch as it moves through the "city," shifting from a mournful wail to a harmonic chord that sounds almost like a voice. Sometimes, when the temperature drops and the water begins to freeze rapidly, the rocks emit sharp cracking reports that sound like footsteps or breaking glass.

It is an active, dynamic place (2.1), but in a slow, terrifying way. You can watch the frost creep across the moss, turning it grey and brittle in minutes as the sun dips behind a cloud. The water level in the pools rises and falls with the rhythm of the condensation. The mist swirls, separating and reuniting, constantly reshaping the boundaries of the room. It is a place that is actively decaying, or perhaps actively breathing, the damp lungs of the stone filling with wind and exhaling song.
