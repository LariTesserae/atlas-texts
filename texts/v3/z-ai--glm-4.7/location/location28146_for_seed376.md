The silence here is absolute. It is not the peaceful silence of a library or the heavy silence of a tomb; it is the dampened, suffocating quiet of a world wrapped in thick insulation. The air is cold—biting, high-altitude cold that scrapes the lungs—but it moves sluggishly, weighted down by a perpetual, fine mist.

You stand on a high plateau, part of a regional expanse that stretches until it vanishes into the grey horizon. There is no stone beneath your feet, no soil in the traditional sense. The ground is spongy, resilient, and alive. This is the Total Green. The vegetation rating of 2.9 is not an exaggeration; it is a biological monolayer. There are no distinct trees, no separate bushes. Instead, the land is covered in a thick, continuous pellicle of plant matter—a densely woven felt of moss, lichen, and creeping vascular mats that have fused over millennia into a single, planetary dermis.

Because the vegetation is so total, the environment feels "built" without being constructed. The ground swells into hillocks that resemble the domes of architecture, then dips into hollows that look like excavated rooms. The "structures" here are purely growth, shaped by the relentless rules of this cold ecosystem.

And the ecosystem is industrial.

The "tech" parameter manifests as a biological emulation of a factory floor. This is not a garden of passive flowers; it is a landscape of engines. Everywhere you look, the plant mass is engaged in furious, silent production. Huge, tower-like stalks of fibrous cellulose rise twenty feet into the air, ribbed and reinforced like chimneys. They rhythmically contract and expand, sucking in the cold air and expelling warmer, humid jets of vapor from their peaks. These are the "lungs" of the plateau, pulsing with a slow, hydraulic precision.

Between the towers lie vast, flat conveyor belts of creeping runners. They shift horizontally, ferrying bright red berries and nutrient packets across the plateau in daisy-chains. The motion is constant—a turbulent, chaotic flow of biomass. This is the Solaris-like nature of the place: it is never still. A patch of ground might ripple as a new layer of fungal pavement foams up from beneath, hardens into a crust, and is then harvested by neighboring parasitic shrubs within the span of an hour. It is an anthill made of chlorophyll, operating at maximum efficiency.

Yet, for all this violent dynamism, the sound rating is zero.

The density of the vegetation—layered, packed, near-touching—creates a physical barrier that traps sound. The rhythmic thumping of the tower-chimneys is absorbed by the thick, spongy moss underfoot. The sliding of the conveyor-runners produces no friction noise, only a subsonic tremor you feel in your teeth. The rushing steam from the exhaust vents hisses visibly but makes no auditory impact, swallowed instantly by the heavy, damp air. You can shout, and the noise dies three feet from your lips, muffled by the wet, woolly atmosphere.

Water is intermittent but crucial. It doesn't rain often, but the condensation is constant. The industrial exhalations of the plants meet the freezing high-altitude air and precipitate instantly, turning the landscape into a glistening, dripping wonderland. Pockets of water collect in the hollows of the plant-architecture, forming perfectly still, black mirrors that reflect the normal, unimposing light of the sky. The water is cold and viscous, filled with algal thickening agents. It sits there until a root-tentacle dips in, drinks, and the pool vanishes, sucked dry by the vacuum pressure of the living mass.

The erosion is established; this is not a new growth. The landscape is weathered, balanced. The "factories" have hollowed out stable valleys where the thermal currents keep the frost at bay, while the high ridges remain crusted in permafrost-white fungi. It looks ancient, like a ruin that has been overgrown so thoroughly it has become the ruin itself. The colors are muted—industrial greens, bruised purples, the rust-color of oxidizing iron-rich sap, and the endless grey of the mist.

Fauna is rare. There are no birds in the sky, no insects buzzing. What animals do exist are likely solitary, deep-dwelling scavengers that move through the root-catacombs beneath the surface, feeding on the waste of the great green engines. You might see a ripple in the moss as something burrows away from the light, but the surface belongs to the plants.

It is a place of intense, quiet violence. It is a cold, open foundry where the machinery is made of flesh and the fuel is sunlight and water. It is beautiful in the way a ticking clock is beautiful—complex, rhythmic, and entirely indifferent to the observer. To stand here is to stand inside the lung of a sleeping, frozen giant, watching the mechanics of breath operate in total, deafening silence.
