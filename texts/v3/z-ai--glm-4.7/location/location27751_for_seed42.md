The water is the absolute here. It is not merely a setting but the total medium of existence—a vast, cool, hyper-oxygenated medium that replaces air. The light is normal, perhaps deceptively so, filtering down in long, unbroken shafts that pierce the upper depths and illuminate the landscape with the clarity of a terrestrial afternoon. The temperature is crisp, bordering on cold, a perpetual chill that keeps the metabolism of the world slow and deliberate.

The landscape rests upon a high seamount plateau, an elevated stage lifted from the abyssal plains below. Despite the depth, the water is startlingly clear, visibility extending for hundreds of meters. The erosion is established; the sharp edges of the geology have long since been worn away by the ceaseless currents, smoothing the basalt and limestone into rolling, flowing hills.

Here, the distinction between "city" and "forest" has collapsed. This is the Grove of Hollows.

The architecture is entirely derived from vegetation, adhering to a strict plant-as-substrate rule. Massive, tower-like organisms rise from the plateau floor—gigantic, hollow stalks of calcified algae and hardened kelp. They stand like skyscrapers, reaching up toward the distant surface, their trunks as thick as cathedrals. Over millennia, the inhabitants have carved into these stalks using primitive tools: hand-chiseled obsidian, shaped shell, and patience. Windows are simply holes where the softer inner pith has been scraped away, revealing the translucent, structural walls that glow faintly with stored sunlight.

The settlement is urban in its organization—distinct "buildings" connected by causeways—but sparse in its density. There is ample space between the hollow towers; one can swim freely from one structure to the next without touching another. This openness gives the place a sense of serene isolation. The city is small in scale, perhaps the size of a village, but the verticality of the plant-architecture lends it a monumental grandeur.

Fauna is abundant and integrated into the daily life. The city hums with biological noise. Schools of small, iridescent fish act as living swarms, moving through the open doorways like drafts of air. Larger, docile grazers—creatures resembling six-sided turtles with shells of living crystal—drift slowly between the towers, their shells encrusted with moss that the inhabitants cultivate and harvest. The sound is loud and pervasive: a constant, rhythmic clicking of the reef-dwellers, the deep, mournful thrum of the giant kelp resonating in the water column, and the rushing sound of the currents flowing over the smooth, rounded architecture.

Technology is strictly primitive. There are no metal machines or electric lights. Tools are crafted from the bones of larger deep-sea beasts and the harder shells of crustaceans. Weaving is a high art; nets of strong, fibrous seaweed hang suspended between towers, acting as hammocks, storage sacks, or passive catchments for drifting plankton.

The weirdness of the place is subtle but present. The vegetation that forms the city is not entirely passive. The "wood" of the towers pulses visibly, a slow peristaltic contraction that pumps nutrient-rich water from the roots to the high canopies. Living inside them feels less like inhabiting a building and more like living inside the digestive tract of a benevolent giant. The water itself tastes faintly of mint and ozone, a chemical peculiarity of the high-elevation plateau.

Everything moves slowly. The dynamic of the world is patient. You can watch a moss patch grow on a windowsill over the course of a day. The currents shift with glacial grace, redirecting the paths of the drifting jellyfish that float past like lanterns. The city does not fight the water; it accepts it. Doors are open holes; furniture is weighted stone; fire is impossible.

It is a place of breath-holding and slow adaptation, a cool, green-lit metropolis built inside the skeletons of living plants, where the rush of the ocean is the only weather, and the silence of the deep is never truly silent.
