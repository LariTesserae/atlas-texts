The air here tastes of cold iron and wet moss. It is a high, thin place, suspended in a basket of jagged grey peaks that rise like the broken teeth of some submerged earth-giant. The elevation is immense; looking down from the ramparts, the world below dissolves into a white, vaporous sea of cloud, leaving only this archipelago of stone floating in the upper atmosphere.

The temperature is perpetually cool, a crisp and unrelenting chill that settles into the joints. It is not freezing, but it is a sharp, attentive cold, the kind that demands you keep moving. Yet, it is damp. A strange, intermittent moisture slicks the surfaces of everything. Rain is rare, but condensation is a constant law. Water sweats from the raw granite, beading on the faces of cliffs and dripping rhythmically into carved basins. The stone is always dark with wetness, glistening like oiled steel in the normal, clear light.

This landscape is young—geologically newborn. The mountains haven't had the time to soften. There are no rounded hills, no worn slopes, no soil deep enough to cushion the step. The rock formations are angular, aggressive, and sheer. Cliffs end in abrupt, vertical drops. Ravines are V-shaped and deep, as if sliced by a god’s blade. Despite the water sliding over them, the stone does not yield; the erosion is paused, arrested by some strange property of the equilibrium that governs this place. The sharp edges remain knife-edged, seemingly immune to the smoothing passage of time.

Sprawled across these precarious shards is the city. It is an urban sprawl of primitive construction, built by hands without the aid of complex machinery or advanced metallurgy. The architecture is a rough masonry of stacked slate and timber, mortared with mud and straw, bound together with rawhide lashings and crude iron rivets. It clings to the mountainsides, a tiered labyrinth of huts, towers, and walkways. It feels dense in its organization—districts carved into ledges, bridges strung across chasms—but sparse in its occupation. There is a low density of living things; vast, empty courtyards sit silent between buildings, and wide boulevards are traversed by only a few solitary figures. The city feels like it was built for millions, yet is inhabited by thousands, leaving it open, echoing, and strangely spacious.

Vegetation, however, fights back against the stone with lush persistence. Where the water collects, in the fissures and crannies, life explodes in vibrant green riots. Ferns the size of men unfurl from cracks in the cliff face. Patches of moss carpet the rooftops, thick and springy. Hardy, twisted pines grip the vertical drops, their roots searching the damp air. The green is luminous against the grey, almost fluorescent in the high-altitude light.

And there is life. Fauna is abundant, yet the silence remains profound. Great, heavy-winged birds glide the thermals without a flap, riding the updrafts in slow, silent circles. Herds of shaggy, mountain grazers—six-legged things with dense, woolly coats—stand motionless on the ledges, chewing the tough moss. They do not run; they do not bleat. They simply exist, watching the world with heavy, lidless eyes. Insects hum in the ferns, a low, constant drone that underscores the quiet rather than breaking it.

The defining characteristic of this place, the source of its weirdness, is its total lack of dynamism. It is a world frozen in a perfect, eternal now. The sun moves across the sky, shedding its normal light, but the angle of the shadows never seems to shift on the city walls. The water drips, but the pools never overflow nor run dry; the water level remains fixed to the millimeter. The plants grow lush, but they do not overtake the structures; they reach a certain size and pause, held in check by an invisible tension. The wind blows, but it never strengthens to a gale nor fades to a whisper—it maintains a steady, respiration-like rhythm.

Nothing rots here. Wood stays hard; leaves stay green; stone stays sharp. It is a stasis that feels less like peace and more like a pause. The city is not ruined, nor is it new. It simply *is*. The people—if those distant figures on the ramparts are people—move with a slow, deliberate grace, performing tasks that seem ritualized and repetitive. They do not rush. They do not build, nor do they repair. They maintain the equilibrium.

It is a place of high, sharp suspense, a damp and verdant trap where the clock stopped ticking long ago, leaving only the sound of the water dripping, forever, into the same full basin.
