The sky here is not empty, but it is not blue. It is a heavy, pearlescent grey, pressed low by the altitude. You are standing on a vast, rusted roof—the roof of the world, perhaps, or merely the roof of a mechanism so large its foundations have long since eroded into the soil below.

The air is thick and warm, moving like syrup through the lungs. It carries the scent of wet iron, old lubricants, and the sharp, tangy musk of unwashed bodies.

This is the High Rust, a continental expanse of industrial plateau situated miles above the cloud layer. The elevation (1.6/3) means the air is thin, though the constant humidity (1.7/3) gives it a deceptive weight. It is a place that sweats. Massive pipeworks, thick as ancient oaks, protrude from the ground at jagged angles, weeping a steady, oily condensation that slicks every surface. The water does not flow in rivers here; it drips. It accumulates in the rust-hollows of the stonework, creating billions of tiny, still mirrors that reflect the normal, pervasive light (2.3/3). There is no sun to be seen through the perpetual haze, only a diffuse, shadowless illumination that allows for no hiding places.

There is no green. The vegetation rating (0.3/3) is absolute. This is a mineral kingdom. In the place of soil, there is a composite of crushed brick, ground-down slag, and ferrous dust. No grass breaks the monotony of grey and orange; no moss clings to the undersides of the bridges. The only "softness" comes from the oxidation itself—great, velvet curtains of rust that hang from the steel gantries like ancient tapestries, soft to the touch but staining the fingers with crimson dust.

The scale (2.6/3) is difficult to comprehend. Looking out, you see no horizon in the traditional sense, only a fading of the industrial lattice into the mist. The city—or infrastructure, rather—is built on a repeating fractal pattern. Silos the size of mountains rise in the distance, their tops lost in the clouds, connected by ribbons of highway and tram-ways that stretch for hundreds of miles. It is an urban sprawl (2.3/3) that has calcified into geology. The buildings are not discrete structures anymore; they have fused together through centuries of rain and settling, forming a singular, jagged landscape of canyons and mesa-ways made entirely of concrete and alloy.

And it is loud (1.7/3). The sound is not the roar of chaos, but the thrum of industry. Deep beneath your feet, massive engines (1.9/3) turn gears the size of islands. The sound is a constant, low-frequency vibration that you feel in your teeth before you hear it. Overlaying this is the rushing of steam venting from pressure valves, a sound like exhaling giants, and the rhythmic *clank-hiss, clank-hiss* of automated pistons working with a slowness (0.8/3) that suggests they are measuring time in epochs rather than seconds. Nothing happens quickly here. The erosion (2.5/3) is established; the steel has been rounded by the wind, the corners of the buildings softened by years of acidic rain until the architecture looks almost organic, like coral bleached by a dying sea.

The density (1.5/3) of the construction is claustrophobic. The streets, if they can be called that, are narrow crevices between towering walls of brick and ductwork. Wires hang low, thick cables black with decades of grime. It feels layered, a palimpsest of construction: you can see where new struts were welded over old brick, where older pipes were bypassed by newer, plastic conduits. It is a dense, tangled nervous system.

But the true life here is not the machinery. It is the fauna (2.7/3).

The absence of plants has driven evolution into strange, industrial niches. The overwhelming presence is the Chitter. Millions of creatures, ranging in size from a fist to a pony, inhabit the superstructure. They are arthropods—things with carapaces of steel-blue chitin and too many legs. They swarm over the weeping pipes, scraping the nutrient-rich algae and rust-blooms with rasping mouthparts. The sound they make is deafening—a tidal wave of clicking, hissing, and skittering that echoes off the canyon walls.

Larger beasts, sluggish and armored like living tanks, patrol the wider avenues. They feed on the swarms, their immense heads lowered as they trundle over the uneven ground. They move with the same glacial patience as the machinery, ignoring the human observers—or perhaps viewing them as just another static part of the landscape.

The rules of this world are simple: moisture causes rust, rust creates weakness, and weakness is exploited by the swarm. The dynamic is slow (0.8/3). A building does not collapse in a day; it groans for decades, shedding steel layer by layer, until one night it simply settles into the mire, becoming the foundation for the next generation of the city.

It is a warm, wet, metal cage, vibrating with the effort of its own existence. It is not a place that hates you, but it is a place that has no use for you. It is a machine that has been running for so long it has forgotten its purpose, consuming the rain and the dark to produce only heat, noise, and the endless, shifting scuttles of the things that live in its gutters.
