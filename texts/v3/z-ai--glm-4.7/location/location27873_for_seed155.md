The sky here is a pale, washed-out slate, pressing down on the horizon with the weight of a high-altitude ceiling. The air is thin and cool enough to bite at the exposed skin of your throat, tasting of ozone and distant snow. This is the High Veldt, a plateau raised so high it feels less like a mountaintop and more like the floor of a hard, cold heaven.

Here, the city does not sit upon the land; it *is* the land.

You stand on the "pavement," which is actually a flattened lattice of interwoven woody fibers, hardened over eons into something tougher than concrete. The vegetation parameter is absolute: there is no bare earth, no mineral soil visible. Everything is plant. But this is not a chaotic jungle; it is an architectural forest. The "buildings" are colossal, ancient trees—broad-leaved giants that have grown into the shape of cathedral spires, silos, and terraced housing blocks. Their bark has calcified, turning into a grey, stony armor reminiscent of weathered granite.

The city is vast, stretching out in a regional sprawl, yet the density is deceptively sparse. Between the towering bio-towers lie wide avenues of mossy, open space, giving the impression of a ruin that has overgrown its own boundaries, yet nothing here is ruined. It is merely settled.

Moisture is a constant companion, though it rarely rains properly. The air is perpetually damp, a heavy condensation that clings to every surface. Beads of water gather on the rimed edges of the giant leaves and drip rhythmically onto the woody streets below, creating a perpetual, syncopated drumming sound. The dampness keeps the moss plush and vibrant, a neon-green carpeting that contrasts sharply with the dark, fossilized wood of the structures.

Despite the lack of animal life—birds are absent, insects are unseen—the place is deafeningly loud.

The noise is industrial, a low-frequency thrumming that vibrates in your chest. It comes from the trees themselves. This is the reality breakdown: the vegetation is not just alive; it is mechanized. The "ancient" state of the erosion has allowed the machinery to fuse completely with the biology. Massive gears, caked in rust and lichen, grind audibly within the trunks of the larger towers. You can see pistons the size of train cars pumping rhythmically inside the translucent amber windows of the seed-pods, pushing a thick, luminous sap up through the vascular system of the city.

This is an industrial ecosystem. The exhaust vents of the factories are not smokestacks, but breathing stigmata on the sides of the ridge-structures, exhaling clouds of warm, steamy spores into the cool air. The hiss of escaping pressure valves sounds like the intake of breath from a leviathan.

Because the elevation is so high, the light is strange. It is "normal" daylight, yet it feels filtered through miles of thin atmosphere, casting sharp, surreal shadows that stretch for impossible distances. The geometry of the city feels off, too—a side effect of the weirdness. A avenue might look straight, but walking it takes twenty minutes longer than it should, the trees subtly shifting their angles when you aren't looking directly at them.

It feels like the end of the world, or perhaps the end of a life cycle. The architecture suggests a civilization that grew itself out of necessity and then calcified, leaving only the infrastructure behind. The scale is open, grandiose, and lonely. You walk down a boulevard lined with silent, ticking monoliths, passing open courtyards where ferns the size of houses unfurl slowly in the damp breeze. There is no movement of fauna, only the mechanical churning of the flora. The trees hiss. The ground thrums. The condensation drips.

It is a cold, wet, loud machine made of wood, sitting atop the world, slowly grinding its way through an eternity of processing nothing but its own existence.
