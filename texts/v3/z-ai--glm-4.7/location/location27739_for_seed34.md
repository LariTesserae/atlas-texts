The horizon here does not curve. Or if it does, the scale of it is so vast that the eye refuses to perceive the arc, accepting the land as a flat, infinite plane of stone and sky. This is the High Plinth, a continental table elevated so high that the clouds frequently snag on its jagged teeth.

The air is cool and thin, tasting of wet dust and ozone. It is a quiet cold (1.3/3), the kind that settles into the joints of the world, slowing everything down. Despite the elevation, the ground never truly dries. The atmospheric pressure holds a heavy, latent humidity (1.9/3). It is not a place of heavy rain, but of constant weeping. The moisture arrives as a thick, ground-hugging mist in the mornings, condensing heavily on every surface, and then spends the rest of the day dripping away.

From a distance, the landscape looks like the ruins of a civilization that covered the world. This is the illusion of the "Built" parameter (1.9/3). There are structures everywhere—towers, obelisks, winding colonnades, and sheer-faced walls. They stretch for miles, a dense thicket of architecture (1.8/3) packed so tightly that in places, the sky is visible only as a narrow, grey ribbon overhead.

But there is no mortar here. No glass. No metal. No tech (0.4/3).

As you draw closer, the city reveals itself to be geological. It is a karst labyrinth on a monstrous scale. The "buildings" are towering spires of limestone and basalt, sculpted by millennia of the wet, gentle breath of the atmosphere. The "streets" are the fissures and cracks where the stone has split and settled.

The erosion is established (1.5/3), settled into a deep, rhythmic balance. The stone is not sharp and new; it is rounded, smoothed, and fluted. The surfaces of the towers are etched with vertical runnels, like the pipes of a massive organ, where the condensation runs day and night. The water collects in the flutes, darkening the grey stone to black, creating the visual effect of dripping ink.

Move through the "alleys"—the narrow canyons between the spires—and the scale becomes oppressive. The walls rise hundreds of feet without a break. The density is high; the towers lean toward one another, sometimes touching at the tops to form natural bridges, filtering the light down into a perpetual, shadowy twilight.

Vegetation is sparse (1.0/3), struggling to find purchase on the perpetually wet, vertical stone faces. There is no soil. Life here is limited to tenacious, microscopic crusts of lichen that stain the highest points in neon rust-orange and pale mint-green, and thick, hanging drapes of moss that curtain the north-facing shadows like rotting velvet. There are no trees, no grass, no swaying fields. The green is merely a veneer on the rock.

Fauna is rare (1.1/3). There are no birds in the towers, perhaps because the thermals are too erratic around the stone forest. Occasionally, a silent, pale shape—a high-altitude glider or a solitary climber—will flit between the shadows, but the dominant presence is the stone itself. The silence of the place is profound (0.2/3). The wind is broken up by the millions of stone pillars, so it never howls; it merely shushes through the cracks, a constant, low-pressure white noise.

Yet, the ecosystem is active (2.1/3) in a way that requires patience to understand. The silence belies the motion of the water. Every surface is in the process of dissolving or growing. The water dissolves the calcite from the upper spires and deposits it as flowstone and travertine curtains on the floors. Stalactites hang from the "bridges" overhead like stone chandeliers, dripping onto stalagmites that slowly reach upward.

In the lower depressions, where the water finally pools, the silence is broken by the faint, periodic *plink* of water hitting the surface of black, mirror-still tarns. These pools are hyper-saline, closed systems where minerals concentrate into colorful, briny crusts.

To stand in the High Plinth is to stand in a city built by water and time. It is a place where geology mimics urbanism, where the "infrastructure" is simply the plumbing of the earth laid bare. It feels vast, empty, and entirely crowded all at once—a quiet, wet, stone metropolis that nobody built, and that will likely never be finished, growing and dissolving by the millimeter, long after anything that might have witnessed it is gone.
