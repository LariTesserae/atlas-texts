The space is less a valley and more a cavity—a hollowed-out pocket in the lowlands, defined not by geographical borders but by the sheer density of the life that fills it. The scale is intimate yet oppressive, roughly the size of a cathedral, but where a cathedral inspires awe with vertical height, this place inspires claustrophobia with total saturation. There are no open skies here, only a dense, interlocking lattice of canopy that creates a ceiling of vibrant, suffocating green.

The ground does not exist. What would be soil is a thick, spongy mat of root systems, binding together the few remaining artifacts of a bygone industrial age. This is a total plant ecosystem; the distinction between "plant" and "structure" has dissolved. The architecture is biological. Massive, woody pillars have fused with the rusted ribs of old gantries, forming vertical supports that pulse with slow, invisible sap. Walls of the space are comprised of layers of broad, rubbery leaves and thick vines, compressed over decades until they have the density of wood.

It is warm here, a humid, tropical heat that hangs heavy in the lungs. The air is visibly thick, a white mist that clings to everything, constantly condensing. The water level is high, not as a flood, but as a saturation. The rocks—few that remain—are weeping, constantly slick with trails of moisture that drip, drip, drip into the root-floor. Every surface is glistening. It feels like the interior of a lung, humid and breathing.

The sound is a constant, loud drone. It is not the noise of wind, nor the chatter of animals—fauna here is rare, perhaps nonexistent, the ecosystem having optimized them out. Instead, the noise is mechanical and biological combined. Somewhere deep within the tangle of vegetation, an engine is running. It is a low-frequency thrumming, a rhythmic chugging that vibrates through the soles of your feet. It sounds like a massive pump fighting a blockage, overlaid with the wet, squelching sounds of vegetation shifting and settling. The metal pipes that protrude from the green walls—scattered remnants of a factory that once stood here—vibrate with the pressure of steam or fluid coursing through them, adding a high-pitched hiss to the heavy bass of the room.

This is an established, weathered place, long settled into its own grotesque equilibrium. Erosion has not worn things down; it has fused them together. Iron pipes are no longer separate from the strangler figs that encase them; the rust provides nutrients for the bark, and the bark holds the rusting metal in a permanent, damp embrace. The industrial is not fighting the natural; it has been digested by it.

Despite the loud noise and the constant movement of fluids, the dynamic is slow. You cannot watch the plants move, but if you turn your head for an hour and look back, a branch has inched closer to a gear. The changes happen across long durations. The "machines" here are not processing goods; they are processing the environment, pumping the condensing atmosphere, circulating the water, perhaps keeping the temperature at that precise, incubating warmth.

Light is normal, filtered through miles of overhead canopy into a diffuse, pale emerald glow. It illuminates the weirdness of the space: flowers that do not bloom but rather vent steam, clusters of leaves that are shaped like gears, perfectly geometric and spinning slowly in the humidity. The tech is industrial, heavy, and iron-based, but it has been colonized. Where there once might have been control panels, there are now dense nodules of bright purple fungus. Where there were conveyor belts, there are now slow-moving rivers of moss.

It is a place of high saturation, packed with presence. To stand here is to be pressed upon from all sides—by the humidity, the noise, the smell of ozone and rotting verdure, and the sheer physical mass of the vegetation that fills every available cubic inch of space. It is a room in the world that is slowly, mechanically, turning itself into compost.
