The sky here is too white. It is not the white of clouds, but the white of a frequency turned up until it hurts, a relentless, unfiltered brilliance that sits heavy on the retina. At this extreme elevation, the atmosphere is thin, stripping away the protection of distance, making everything appear knife-edge close.

This is the High Scarp, a regional sprawl of jagged tablelands and spires that jut upward like broken bones from the crust of the world. The scale is vast—miles of verticality—but the space feels crushed, claustrophobic. The density here is not of clutter, but of verticality; thousands of stone pillars, some the size of skyscrapers, others mere needles, stand packed together in a chaotic forest of gray mineral.

There is no soil. The ground is composed of a dense, interlocking hexagonal basalt that has settled into a precarious equilibrium over eons. The erosion is established, a settled state of "ruined perfection." Every edge is rounded, every face smoothed by the perpetual sliding of wet air. It is a mature landscape; it looks exactly as it is meant to look, even if that look is one of catastrophic collapse frozen in time.

Water is the constant, oppressive presence. It does not fall as rain often, but the air is saturated to the point of liquidity (1.7/3). It is a cool, wet cold (1.2/3) that penetrates clothing and skin instantly. Water seeps from the fractured rock faces, weeping from every crevice. The entire plateau is alive with the sound of dripping—a thousand thousand distinct drips that coalesce into a rhythmic, white-noise roar. Pools of clear, ultra-cold water sit in depressions on the stone, so still they reflect the blinding sky with terrifying fidelity.

There are no plants (0.2/3). No moss, no lichen, no brave alpine flowers. The biological kingdom has been arrested. Instead, the mineral world has proliferated to fill the void. The rock faces are adorned with crystalline growths—delicate, branching structures of quartz and feldspar that mimic the shapes of trees and neural networks. They grow slowly, visibly shifting over the span of days, extruding from the living rock. They are cold and silent, shattering underfoot with a sound like glass breaking.

This is a place of primitive but persistent intervention (1.1/3 Built / Tech). Scattered among the natural pillars are the Works. They are crude, hand-fabricated structures made of stacked stone and rough-hewn timber that looks petrified. They are not shelters; they look like propitiation. Gigantic, crude stone wedges driven into cracks in the earth, as if to hold the mountains together. Levees made of piled boulders, redirecting the weeping water into artificial channels. Stone circles, single monoliths with chiseled, meaningless geometrical patterns. The builders are long gone, or perhaps they never existed—perhaps the rocks arranged themselves in these crude, desperate architectures.

And the air is loud (1.5/3 Sound). The wind howling through the dense forest of stone pillars creates a harmonic, a constant low-frequency thrumming that vibrates in the teeth and bones. It is the sound of a giant instrument being played by the weather. Added to this is the cracking of the expanding crystal-trees, the sudden thunderous release of accumulated water from a saturated ledge, and the grinding of the tectonic plates deep below.

The true nature of the place, however, lies in its instability (2.7/3 Weirdness / 1.6/3 Dynamic). This is a world where the erosion has not just worn things down, but worn through the fabric of reality. The "primitive" tools left behind—hammers of rough iron, chisels of flint—are often found embedded in solid rock where no seam existed, as if the stone solidified around them in an instant.

Sometimes, when the light hits the wet basalt at a certain angle, the depth perception falters. A spire fifty yards away might suddenly appear to be inches from your face. The water dripping from a ledge might freeze in mid-air for a heartbeat before resuming its fall, or flow upwards in thin, hair-like rivulets defying gravity for a moment before snapping back down. It is an active landscape; the geometry of the valley is in motion. The horizon line sometimes curves even when you know it is flat.

The "rules" here are simple but punishing. You do not climb high without a tether to the real; the air thins, and the visual glitches increase. You do not drink the still water, for it reflects things that have not yet happened. You keep moving. The density of the stone presses in, and the bright, white sky offers no mercy. It is a wet, loud, frozen museum of a world that is slowly forgetting how to be solid.
