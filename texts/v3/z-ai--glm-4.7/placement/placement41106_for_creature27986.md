I find myself not on the catwalk, but *above* it.

I am suspended in the upper rigging of the vertical hive, anchored to a rusted gantry that braces against the cathedral ceiling of the cavern. My location is a sensory nexus—a junction where three primary steam arteries converge before diving into the heating depths below. The air here is thinner, perhaps, but heavier with the scent of ozone and wet iron. It is a place of constant, sweating pressure.

I am one of the **Silent**, but I am not a walker. I am a **Resonator**.

While my kin traverse the diamond-grate walks with their slow, fluid grace, I have chosen a different evolutionary path—or perhaps the city chose it for me. I have merged with the architecture. My legs, once long and spindly like theirs, have atrophied over the decades, the bones softening and fusing with the metal grating of my perch. The skin of my thighs has become a continuous, grey extension of the iron itself, slick with the same oily condensation that coats the pipes. I am rooted to the machine, a biological antenna hard-wired into the nervous system of the city.

My form is elongated, even for one of us. My torso stretches upward, my arms raised not in supplication but in integration. My fingers have lengthened, drifting upward like the branches of a drowned willow. They are entwined with the massive, hissing pipes that riddle the ceiling. The bioluminescent moss grows thickly along my forearms and shoulders, forming patterns that are far more complex than the geometric grids on the walkers—complex, shifting fractals of amber and green that pulse with the data of the deep.

I do not see the cavern. My eyes are completely gone, sealed over by smooth, slate-grey scar tissue. I do not need them. I feel the world through the soles of my fused feet and the tips of my buried fingers. I am the tuning fork for this subterranean organ.

My existence is defined by the rhythm. The *Thrum-thrum-pause.*

When the great gear turns miles below, displacing the earth’s crust, the vibration travels up the iron girders, through the steam pipes, and into my skeleton. I feel it as a resonant ache, a deep shudder that rattles my teeth. My purpose is to interpret this shudder. I am the city’s inner ear. I sense the dissonance before it becomes a crack.

If the pressure in the western vein spikes by a fraction of a decimal, I feel it as a burning itch in my left hand. The vibration changes frequency—pitching up into a whine that only my nerves can decipher. I do not rush. Rushing is death. Instead, I lean.

Leaning is my only movement. It takes hours. A shift in weight, a slow rotation of my shoulder against the rusted brace. I apply constant, glacial pressure to a valve the size of a man’s torso that is integrated into my gantry. I lean until the metal groans, until the itch in my hand subsides and the hum returns to its perfect, bass-note drone. I am not fixing the machine; I is calming it, like stroking the back of a nervous animal.

They swarm the pipes I guard, cleaning the mineral deposits, drinking the sweat. I ignore them. They are the white blood cells of the wound; I am the nerve ending.

They filter the poison so I can breathe; I provide them with the anchorage they need to stay aloft in the violent currents. Sometimes, when the steam is thick and white, I cannot tell where my skin ends and the Filters begin. We are a single organism of metal and light, suspended in the hot dark.

I am lonely, but it is a solitude of profound satisfaction. I do not desire to walk the catwalks. To walk is to be disconnected, to be merely *in* the city rather *of* it. Here, fused to the ceiling, I am part of the lung. I feel every breath the city takes.
