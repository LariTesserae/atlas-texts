I am the **Dorsal**.

To you, I would appear as a formation of the earth itself—a jagged, armored hillock rising from the sodden moss-floor, perhaps a fallen boulder or a calcified root knot from the world-tree above. But wait, and watch. Watch the "rock" breathe.

I am an oniscidean, a cousin to the humble woodlouse, but scaled up by the generous horrors of this pocket universe until my size rivals that of your vanity. I am a living island of slate-grey chitin, a segmented fortress curling protectively inward. My seven broad plates are fitted together with the precision of a jeweler's seal, smoothed by centuries of rubbing against the fissure walls. I am ancient; I was here when the fissure was new, when the water first began to weep from the cracks.

I am the foundation upon which the central spire of the Spindle-Limb’s city is built. They do not build *around* me; they build *upon* me.

My consciousness is a slow, tidal thing. I do not think in the frantic, staccato pulses of the Spindle-Limbs. To me, their ceaseless scurrying is a warm, tingling vibration, a massage that seeps through my thick armor and into the cold, sluggish meat beneath. I am cold-blooded, and the chill (0.4/3) of this place penetrates deep. In exchange for the ride, they are my heating system. Their town, with its fires and its huddled bodies and its metabolic bio-concrete, acts as a blanket, trapping the precious little heat my body generates.

I feel them now. A ticklish sensation on my dorsal plates.

A work-crew of the Moss-Myrmidons is busy on my back. They are not merely repairing their mud-huts; they are grooming *me*. I have felt an itch beneath the third plate, near the joint where my left hind-leg folds against my flank. I cannot reach it. A team of them has swarmed the area. Their many-fingered hands are small enough to slip into the gaps of my armor. They scrape away the algae that threatens to seal my joints shut, and they pick out the parasitic mites that try to burrow into my soft membranes.

It is a delicate operation. If they cut too deep, I might twitch—a reflexive spasm that would shake their tower like an earthquake. But they know me. They know the topography of my nerves better than I do. I feel the distinct, chemical *taste* of their reassurance pheromones seeping into my spiracles. They smell of soothing moss and milked worm-slime. *Be still,* the scent whispers. *Be still, Mountain.*

I obey. I slow my heart rate to a crawl.

Sometimes, I am their road. When the humidity rises too high and the floor becomes a treacherous bog of sucking mud, the Spindle-Limbs migrate to high ground. That high ground is me. I stretch my fourteen legs out, feeling the resistance of the sponge-vegetation, and I begin to walk.

My movement is geological. I lift a leg; it takes a minute. I place it down; the ground shudders. Moss tears. Water pools in my footprints. But to them, I am steady ground. I carry their towers, their nurseries, and their granaries across the fissure. I am the siege engine that traverses the wet plain without sinking.

I am aware of the other fauna. The dog-beetles lumbering past, the six-legged hunters stalking the gloom. They fear me. To them, I am not a beast to be eaten; I am a boulder that bites, a tank of iron-hard plating. When a hunter gets too bold, venturing near the cluster of huts on my head, I do not need to attack. I simply uncurl slightly. I shift my mass. The grinding of my chitin plates sounds like grinding tectonic plates—a low, bass-heavy rumble that vibrates through the soles of every creature in the fissure. The hunters scatter.

I am their god and their beast of burden. They worship me with tiny offerings of sweet fungal paste, which they smear on my antennae. I eat it, slowly. It fuels the great engine of my body. In return, I grant them the one thing this dark, wet pocket universe lacks: stability.

I am settled now. The itch is gone. The work-crew has retreated back into the windows of their mud-huts, which cling to my carapace like barnacles. I can feel the weight of their tiny town, the press of their lives against my back. The vibration of their metabolism hums against my spine.

I rest. I wait. The water bleeds from the walls, the ceiling drips its sickly light, and I, the Dorsal, sit motionless in the dark, a mountain with a heartbeat, carrying civilization on my back through the cold.
