I am the pause in the architecture.

I do not walk the floor; the floor is a treacherous, tangled mesh of roots that drinks the light and snares the careless. I am above it, adhered to the humid ceiling of a "nave"—a hollowed-out cylindrical space formed where three massive bryophyte-columns have fused. My claws are sunk deep into the spongy, fibrous bark, the hooked pads of my digits contracting rhythmically, anchoring me against the slow drag of gravity.

I am vertical, stretched thin by the narrowness of my world. My body is a pale, elongated taper, a ribbon of translucent flesh that hugs the curve of the wood. To look at me, if you could see me at all, would be to look at a variation in the gloom—a slightly slicker patch of shadow, a bruise upon the green wood.

I am cold. It is the defining fact of my existence. The blood moving through my veins is viscous, moving with the agonizing slowness of syrup. My heart beats once every few minutes, a sluggish drum that pushes just enough heat to keep my cells from freezing solid. I am an engine running on the lowest possible idle. To move is to burn; to burn is to die. So I wait.

I am permeable. I am not enclosed by my skin; I am open to the room. The air here is heavy with spores, a thick, invisible fog that tastes of wet stone and ancient decay. I am breathing it in, but not just through my mouth and nose. I am drinking the air through every pore, osmosing the humidity that keeps my gelatinous form from desiccating. I am a part of the atmosphere, a filter suspended in the dark.

My eyes are open, though they barely distinguish between the lid and the world. They are vast, black pools, obsidian mirrors designed to catch the faint, cold pulses of the fungal shelves growing in the recesses below. I watch the light—not to see objects, but to read the rhythm of the room. A slow pulse of sickly green means the algae bloom on the eastern wall is active. A stuttering violet flare means a draft has cooled the western tunnel. I read the world in light and temperature gradients.

I am not alone, though silence hangs heavy enough to choke.

Ten feet away, clinging to the shadows of an archway, is the Other. I do not know his name—names are too loud, too clumsy for this place—but I know his chemical signature. He smells of damp copper and slightly of the nutrient-rich slurry found in the lower tunnels.

We have been here for hours, perhaps days. Time is liquid here; it drips from the ceiling and pools in the hollows, distorting the hours.

I decide to move. It is a monumental decision, requiring a precise calculation of caloric return. The condensation is dripping from a patch of moss directly above my head, a rhythmic *plink... plink... plink*. Each droplet is a jewel of pure, cold water, but it falls just out of reach of my snout. If I stretch, I can catch it.

I begin the shift. My muscles, locked rigid to maintain my position, unlock with a microscopic shudder. I extend my neck, a fraction of an inch. Then my shoulders. My skin pulls away from the bark with a wet, sucking sound—a *thhhhhhhck* that echoes violently in the stillness. I freeze.

The sound is a thunderclap. The vibration travels through the root-mesh, warning everything that something is stirring.

I hold myself perfectly still, waiting for the room to forget me. The "drip" continues. *Plink.* The ice in the walls shifts with a distant crack.

Slowly, painfully, I reach. My arm, spindly and terrifyingly fragile, extends into the dark. The droplets fall on my tongue. They are freezing, shocking the system, tasting of dust and life. I swallow, and the water spreads through my tissues, a thin wave of relief.

As I retract my arm, my fingers brush the Other.

He has moved too. He is crossing the archway, heading for a patch of bioluminescent lichen. Our flanks touch. The contact is electric.

We do not speak. We press our wet, slick skins together, a brief, shivering embrace. Through the contact, information floods my system. He is old, older than me. He is hungry. He has felt a tremor in the deep water channels below the floor—perhaps a collapse, perhaps just the earth shifting in its sleep. He warns me of a rot in the ceiling to the south, where a branch has become heavy with water and threatens to fall.

I press my own message into his flank: *The air is thinning here. The upper tunnel is closed with ice.*

The exchange lasts a second. Then, with a fluid ripple, we separate. He continues his hunt for the lichen; I retract to my hollow, flattening my body back against the wood, folding my limbs beneath me. I become the knot again. I become the flaw.

I am the keeper of the stillness. I am the stomach that digests the dark. I am here, in the lung of the world, breathing in the wet, purple twilight, waiting for the next drop to fall.
