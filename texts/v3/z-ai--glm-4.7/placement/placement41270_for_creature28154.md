I am the valve-turner of the Western Intake.

I do not have a name, for names require separation, and I am not separate. I am a component of the ventilation, a biological fuse in a circuit of iron. To look at me—if you could see through the gloom and the steam, if you had eyes to find me where I cling to the underside of the horizontal pipe run—you would see nothing but a protrusion of the machine itself. I am the color of wet slate, segmented and sleek, indistinguishable from the flange upon which I brace my hind legs.

My world is vibration. It is not heard; it is felt through the claws that grip the cold metal, resonating in the hollow chambers of my carapace. The universe speaks to me in the language of frequency. The high-pitched whine of the steam pressure is a scream that itches at the tips of my sensory stalks; the deep, guttural thrum of the piston below is a lullaby that vibrates my very marrow.

I am currently inverted. The dampness of the place clings to me in heavy beads, the oily sweat of the engine mixing with the condensation of the air. I taste the air constantly. It is thick, laden with the metallic tang of oxidized copper and the acrid bite of carbon. It is the taste of work. It is the taste of survival.

My primary claw—my heavy, crushing vice—is locked around the stem of the pressure valve. It is a massive thing, rusted tight by centuries of neglect and moisture, resisting the flow that must pass through. My smaller, hooking pick-claw is wedged into the collar of the valve, seeking leverage.

I am waiting for the beat.

The machine does not vary. It breathes in a rhythm older than the erosion of the stone basin. *Thrum-thrum-click. Thrum-thrum-click.* The piston drives, the steam expands, the pressure gauges twitch. I feel the wave of pressure traveling through the pipe like a snake beneath the skin.

There. The dip in the resonance. The micro-second of relaxation in the iron.

My muscles—bundles of fiber cables beneath the chitin—fire. I do not decide to move; the decision moves me. It is a memory etched into the ganglia of my nervous system, a pathway worn smooth by a trillion ancestors. I torque my body. The heavy claw grinds against the valve. The rust fights back, screaming in a high-frequency shriek that my stalks register as pain.

I do not stop. I pull until my shell creaks, until the friction burns my joints. And then, the movement.

*Scrape.*

The valve turns a fraction of an inch. The flow equalizes. The dangerous vibration that was building in the pipe walls flattens out into a harmonious hum. The pressure is released. The system survives.

I release my grip. My body sags for a fraction of a second, the tension draining out of my limbs. In that brief pause, I am aware of the swarm around me.

To my left, clinging to the vertical riser, is my brother. He is smaller, his shell still translucent in the joints, a nymph hatched only cycles ago. He struggles with the smaller auxiliary valve. I feel his frantic vibration through the pipe—a messy, jagged rhythm of inefficient movement. He is slow. He is learning.

But I do not help him. I do not pity him. There is only the work.

Above me, on the catwalk grid, I feel the heavy impact of footsteps. A leviathan of our kind, an elder whose shell is pitted and scarred by a thousand shifts, is dragging himself across the mesh. He stops directly above my perch. His shadow blocks out the little gray light that filters down.

He is dying. I taste the change in his thermal signature; he is cooling, his internal fires fading. His vibration is becoming erratic, skipping beats like a damaged cog. He has locked his joints. He is becoming part of the architecture, a calcified monument to a specific task he can no longer perform.

He does not fall. He simply stops. He becomes a foothold for the others scrambling over the railing.

I turn my attention back to the intake pipe. The cycle is beginning anew. The piston retracts, sucking air into the lungs of the basin. The vacuum pulls at my antennae. The grease is weeping from the joint I just turned, a thick black sludge of decay and heat.

I am ravenous.

I lower my head—or rather, the armored dome that serves me for a head—toward the weeping metal. I extend my hooking claw and scrape a globule of the black mixture from the threads of the valve. It is foul, gritty, burning hot against my mouthparts. I swallow it down.

It tastes of time. It tastes of the heat that was lost, the friction that was wasted. As it slides into my gut, it rekindles the fire in my abdomen. I am eating the entropy of the world. I am consuming the machine's death to fuel my own life.

The piston slams forward. *BOOM.*

The shockwave rattles my grip. The steam shrieks. The swarm on the catwalks shifts like a landslide of gravel, a thousand claws clicking in unison.

*Click-click-click.*

The sound is deafening, a wall of noise that drowns out thought. We are all clicking, all signaling, a Morse code of submission to the task. We are the jailers of this moment.

The cycle is closed. The valve is turned. The pressure is stable. The machine has not moved forward, but it has not fallen apart. I have succeeded.

I pull my legs in tight against the pipe, locking my joints to become a seamless part of the surface. The damp mist settles over me. I feel the next wave of pressure building in the line, faint and distant, but growing. It will arrive in exactly twelve seconds.

I am ready. I have always been ready. I will be here when the universe ends, turning the valve that goes nowhere, eating the grease of the unchanging gears. I am the Clicker. I am the pause that never ends.
