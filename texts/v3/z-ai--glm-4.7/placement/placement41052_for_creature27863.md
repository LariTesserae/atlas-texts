I find myself suspended in the roaring silence of the vertical world, clinging to the sheer, wet face of the basalt cliffs that overlook the void. I am not in the canopy, nor on the root-mesh floor; I am in the space between—the slick, steam-choked amphitheater of the **Weeping Walls**.

Here, the air is so thick with mist that it feels like swimming. The noise is a physical thing, a low-frequency thrum that vibrates through my carapace, but I am attuned to it. I do not hear it as chaos; I hear it as rhythm.

I am a **Basalt-Loom**.

To the casual eye, I might resemble a monstrous spider, but my taxonomy is stranger. I am an arachnid of the geothermal interface. My body is roughly the size of a human torso, flattened and streamlined to hug the rock, designed to shed the relentless cascade of hot water. My legs are long, splayed, and tipped with hooked claws of metallic obsidian, allowing me to grip the polished stone even as it spits boiling rivulets.

I do not have fur; I have plating. My back is covered in overlapping scales of compressed carbon and silica, dark as the volcanic glass I walk upon. These scales are not just armor; they are thermal regulators. Deep grooves between the plates channel the runoff over my body, cooling me while simultaneously absorbing the latent heat from the water. I run on the temperature differential between the scalding stone and the humid air.

My primary purpose is the management of the **Weeping**.

I am the architect of the cliffsides. While the Canopy-Callers play in the waterfalls, I build the stage upon which they perform. I do not spin silk from my body in the traditional sense; I extrude a viscous, ferro-fluid polymer that hardens instantly upon contact with the air, creating cables of incredible tensile strength.

I traverse the vertical drops, anchoring my cables between the jutting spikes of basalt. I weave vast, horizontal nets—trapezes that catch the heavy condensation before it can plummet into the abyss. These nets are not sticky; they are tight meshes of super-fine wire. They act as sieves. They catch the falling water, pooling it into heavy, shimmering lakes suspended in mid-air against the cliff face. When the pools grow too heavy, I release a specific tension knot, and the water thunders down in a controlled flood, watering the hanging gardens of moss and orchids that cling to the rock below.

In exchange for this engineering, the jungle feeds me.

I am a filter-feeder of the atmosphere. As the water rushes through my nets, it carries with it the spores of the thermal fungi, the pollen of the black bamboo, and the metallic dust shed by the ancient monoliths above. I run my legs through these wet nets, gathering the slurry into a nutrient-rich paste that I consume. I am eating the runoff of the machine, the waste of the biology, and the minerals of the earth, all combined into a sludge that tastes of iron and life.

I am solitary, but I am never alone. Above me, I hear the high-pitched, metallic shrieks of the Canopy-Callers as they dive. Sometimes, one misjudges its arc, colliding with my nets. The web sings like a harp string—a sharp, buzzing *twang* that cuts through the roar of the falls. The Caller bounces off, tumbling into the steam, unhurt but annoyed. I pause, tasting the vibration through the soles of my feet. It is just another note in the symphony.

Below me, in the gloom of the root-mesh, I see the bioluminescent pulse of the Basalt-Lobsters scuttling in the shadows. Occasionally, one of the heavy, six-legged grazers—the Ore-Bears—shambles near the cliff base, venting steam from its armor. I feel the tremor of its footsteps through the rock, a slow, booming drumbeat that underscores the lighter percussion of the dripping water.

I am the **Silent Weaver** in a Loud World. I exist at the intersection of the falling water and the unmoving stone. I am the reason the cliffs do not simply erode into mud; I give structure to the runoff. I hold the heat in my belly and the sky in my wires
