I exist in the space between the suspension and the stone.

I am not high up in the pristine, sterile observation domes where the light is artificial and the silence is absolute. Nor am I down in the mist, among the fleeing herds of seal-goats. I am stationed in the "Underbelly"—the maintenance gantries that encircle the base of the Obsidian Monoliths, just a few feet above the fracturing crust.

Here, the air is thickest, heavy with the metallic taste of the planet’s birthing pangs. The smart-glass of the city above me is opaque here, darkened by the soot of the geothermal vents, but it hums. It is a low-frequency bass note that sits constantly in my chest, vibrating my very ribs.

I am a **Tuner**.

While others of my kind cultivate the silver lichen or calculate the orbital mechanics of our hovering homes, my task is tactile and visceral. I am the interface between the rigid mathematics of our architecture and the chaotic, wet emotion of the mountain.

My form is like that of my kin—tall, elongated, possessing the sleek, wet sheen of smoothed obsidian. My skin, glandular and tough, glistens with the constant condensation that drips from the city above. But my hands are different. They are scarred. Where my people possess the long, elegant webbing of scholars, my fingertips are roughened, callused by centuries of direct contact with the raw, abrasive granite of the ledge.

I spend my "days" (a meaningless concept here in the twilight) suspended by a safety tether, floating in the low-gravity field generated by the city's base. My feet do not touch the ground; they dangle in the void over the abyss. I hover horizontally, my body parallel to the cliff face, like a swimmer hovering over a coral reef.

My work is to listen.

I press the palms of my hands against the wet, black basalt. Through the thin vertical slits of my nose, I taste the sulfur. But it is through my palms that I *hear*. I feel the tectonic plates grinding deep below, the stress fractures opening in the stone like veins filling with blood.

The planet is an adolescent. It is moody. It throws tantrums. When a tremor begins—a "shudder"—it starts as a tickle in the stone. It feels like a shiver passing through a feverish patient. My job is to catch that shiver before it becomes a scream.

I do not use controls. I do not use buttons. I use my own internal resonance.

When the stone vibrates with anxiety, I tense my dense musculature and I push a counter-frequency back into the rock. I hum. It is a sub-vocal sound, generated deep in my throat, too low for the air to carry, but potent enough to travel through my flesh and into the mountain. I match the chaotic frequency of the earth and invert it, canceling the violence. It is a conversation of vibration.

*Be still,* I hum. *Not yet. Wait.*

Sometimes, the mountain ignores me. The stone heaves, and the city above bucks in the air. I am thrown against my tether, slamming into the cliff face, my slick skin scraping against the jagged teeth of the world. But I do not let go. I wrap my long, splayed fingers into the crevices of the rock, anchoring myself, and I hum louder, forcing the stone to accept the gravity anchor's discipline.

In the quiet moments, when the mountain is merely breathing rather than thrashing, I am a voyeur of the wild.

I hang suspended in the mist, watching the great avians. They are massive, their leathery wings catching the updrafts that rise from the void. Sometimes, one glides close, its eye—a pool of ancient, golden intelligence—locking onto mine. It sees no difference between my black, sleek shape and the floating city above. To it, I am just another part of the strange, silent landscape.

I watch the silver waterfalls threading past me, the water so pure it looks like liquid mercury in the dim light. It runs off the glass of the city, cascades down the tether lines, and rejoins the planet. I sometimes cup my hands and let the water pool in my palms, drinking the iron-rich cold before returning my hands to the stone.

I am a creature of the damp and the hum. I have no voice that speaks to the air. I have no name that can be spoken aloud. I am simply the pressure that keeps the sky from falling.

I am the silence between the notes of the planetary song. I am the finger pressed against the trembling lip of the world, whispering to the stone:

*Hush. Grow slowly. We are here.*
