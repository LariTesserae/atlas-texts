I find myself in the Drip-Edge, the liminal zone where the braided webs of the city fray and dissolve into the murk.

I am a **Listener**, though in the tongue of the Architects, my name roughly translates to *One Who Hears the Stone Groan*. I do not live in the buoyant, swaying high-nets where the children play. My station is lower, closer to the waterline, where the humidity is so dense it feels like a physical weight against the chest.

My physical form is much like the others—elongated, pale as moonlight, with the double-jointed limbs that allow us to fold around the vertical world—but I am distinct. My skin is not just translucent; it is thinner, worn fragile by the perpetual damp. Through the membrane of my chest and stomach, you can see not just the dark pulse of my blood, but the slow, rhythmic filling and emptying of my air-bladders, internal modifications that allow me to stay submerged for longer periods.

I am the tension in the rope. I am the counterweight.

My existence is defined by the **Rising**. While the others weave and harvest in the twilight, I spend my days suspended upside down, my legs hooked around a jagged spur of obsidian that juts from the Finger’s side. I hang head-down toward the tea-colored water, monitoring the stress lines where our city anchors to the stone.

Because the land rises, the anchors are always screaming.

I feel the uprising through the soles of my feet before anyone above sees the shift. It is a vibration, a low-frequency grinding that travels up the basalt spine of the Finger and into my bones. When the tension becomes too great—when the rock tries to lift our homes into the cold, thin air above the mist—it falls to me to perform the micro-adjustments. I do not cut the thick bindings of the city—that is the communal ritual of the Severance—but I tend to the individual fibers. I untie the knots that have pulled too tight, I splice in new lengths of elastic liana to give the city the "slack" it needs to breathe.

My world is quiet, muffled by the heavy air, but it is loud in vibration.

Here, at the Drip-Edge, I live with the dead.

The Under-City is directly below me. When I crane my neck, peering through the oily swirl of the surface, I can see them. They hang in the brine, suspended in their peat shrouds, looking as though they are merely waiting for a conversation to resume. My grandfather is there, his pale face turned upward, his elongated hands still clutching the bone-smoothing tool he died with three hundred years ago. He has not aged; he has merely drifted lower.

Because the erosion is zero, he looks exactly as he did the moment he was lowered.

I am the only one who visits them regularly.

Often, when the work of the tension is slow, I lower myself on a single strand of hair-moss until the cold brine closes over my head. I do not breathe the water—I cannot—but I open my mouth to taste the minerals, and I open my lateral lines to listen. The dead do not speak with voices, but they resonate. They are part of the geologic memory now. In the absolute stillness of the depths, I press my forehead against the glassy smoothness of the rock face, inches away from my grandfather’s preserved face, and I hum. It is a low, thrumming note that matches the groan of the earth.

I tell the stone—and the dead within it—that we are still here. I tell them we have not floated away into the upper dark.

There is a specific tool I use, a hooked needle made of basalt, polished to a mirror sheen. It belonged to my grandmother. It was lowered with her, lost to the deep. But last decade, the Finger rose enough that her shelf came within reach of the Drip-Edge. I dove down, my fingers slipping in the slick murk, and reclaimed the needle. Now, I use it to splice the vines that keep the living city from floating away. The dead provide the tools to hold the living to the earth. This is our cycle.

Sometimes, the shadows in the deep water swell.

I have seen the Leviathans—the gelatinous cathedrals—drift past my perch. They do not see me. I am too small, a mere speck of grit on the towering finger of stone. But I feel their passage. The water displaces, a gentle swell that lifts my dangling body, rocking me like a cradle. When they pass, the Riders—the nomads who hitch their fate to the beasts—sometimes toss lines toward the spires. I have seen them leap, dark silhouettes against the faint phosphorescence of the deep.

I watch them go with a strange envy. They are drifting into the absolute unknown, tethered to life itself. But I do not follow.

I am the anchor. I am the knot that refuses to break.

I pull myself up, hand over hand, the mucous on my skin slicking the rough vines. I invert myself, righting my body on the narrow ledge. The city above sways gently, indifferent to the geologic violence beneath it. I check the tension of the main anchor line. It is tight, vibrating like a plucked cello string. The land has risen another fraction of an inch today.

I take my bone-needle, loop a fresh strand of moss around the chafing vine, and begin to weave. I am holding on. I am holding us to the past, even as the earth tries to thrust us into the future.
