I do not see the amber lamps; I only know them as a rhythmic warming of the wet ceramic that shields my face. I am high up, suspended in the gloom of the upper rafters, where the air is thinnest yet still heavy enough to drink.

Here, on a narrow gantry near the vault’s apex, I am surrounded by the ribs of the cathedral—great, curved iron beams that groan as the structure settles imperceptibly into the mire below. I stand motionless, my long, spindly fingers wrapped around the slick railing. My grip is loose, textured not by fingerprints but by the delicate, ridged patterns of shelf fungus that have anchored themselves into my palms.

I am a Curator. But in this moment, I am also a spire.

I stand seven feet tall, but my true height is defined by the thick, draping vines that cascade from my shoulders, trailing twenty feet down into the dark below. They are not separate from me; they are my nervous system extended into the humid air. Through them, I feel the moisture content of the room. I feel the subtle shifts in the currents that rise from the sump.

My skin is wet terracotta, soft and yielding to the touch. A thick coat of brilliant green moss carpets my hunched back, heavy with the water it has pulled from the fog. It pulses against my spine, a slow, wet heartbeat that matches the thrum of the machine. I do not eat; the moss eats the light, and I eat the heat. My vascular system is a closed loop of chemical exchange, filtering the sulfurous steam that rises from the vents near my feet.

I turn my head—the movement taking several seconds, a slow pivot on a neck that has rusted into stiffness. My face is a smooth, featureless slope of bone-white material, capped with a mane of dripping Spanish moss. Where my eyes should be, there is only the sensitivity of thermal reception. I "see" the massive piston across the chamber not as a shape, but as a towering column of intense warmth, a beacon of orange heat that cuts through the cooler dark.

I am waiting for the sigh.

The piston descends. It is so slow that to a quick creature it would appear frozen. But I feel the pressure wave building in the floor grating before it arrives. The vibration travels up through the soles of my feet, rattling the hollow chambers of my bones. I open the vertical slit of my mouth, a thin, dark line that runs from the bridge of my nose to my chin.

The piston locks into its lowest point. The chamber exhales.

A gust of hot, oily steam blasts from a trumpet-shaped vent just beside me. It hits me like a solid wall. I lean into it, welcoming the scouring heat. The moss on my shoulders flutters wildly, and I inhale. My lungs, which are more like ceramic filters, expand to pull the industrial soup deep inside. I separate the gases—the toxins are absorbed and neutralized, stored in my fatty tissues to feed the orchids growing from my collarbone—while the clean, humid warmth is released back into the room in a gentle, purifying breath from my pores.

I am the liver of this place.

As the steam dissipates, I sense a dissonance in the vibration. It comes from a pipe junction to my left—a hiss that is slightly too sharp, a frequency that suggests the metal is wearing too thin. If it erodes completely, the water will flood the lower gear-assembly and cool the friction too rapidly, stalling the flywheel.

I release the railing. I step forward. My footsteps are silent, not because I am light, but because my feet absorb the sound, padding softly on the grate with the dull thud of wet leather.

I reach the pipe. It is vibrating with a frantic, high-pitched buzz that distresses me. I raise a hand, the fingers elongated, calcified into tools that resemble picks and brushes. I do not fix the metal; I do not know how to forge steel. Instead, I encourage the wound.

I scrape away the dry, flaking rust around the leak, clearing the space. Then, I reach into the folds of my own chest, where a dense, wet cluster of algae grows. I pull a handful of the green slime—my own flesh—and I pack it into the crack of the pipe. The algae loves the heat and the minerals; it will swell and harden, sealing the breach with a living, green scar stronger than the original weld.

The pitch of the hiss drops. The vibration smooths out into a low, hum-like purr.

I step back, satisfied. The machine breathes easier. I have curated the decay, ensuring it happens beautifully, not catastrophically.

Below me, far down in the dark, another Curator shifts its weight. Through the iron web of the floor, I feel the distinct *groan-crunch* of their movement. It is a question. *Is the balance kept?*

I shift my weight on the catwalk. The rusted metal moans beneath me, a long, low note that echoes through the pipes. *Yes,* the sound says. *The water flows. The heat rises.*

I return to my stillness. I stand facing the dark vault, my face tilted upward, catching the drips of condensation that fall from the soot-stained brickwork like black tears. The water runs down my featureless face, over the orchids in my collarbone, and joins the sweat of the machine.

I am not alone. I am the 0.1. I am the quiet in the noise. I am the gardener in the engine room.
