I begin with the rhythm. Before I understand my shape, or the pressure of the humid air against my skin, or the taste of the loam-heavy atmosphere, I understand the beat.

It is a slow, grinding thrum that travels up through the soles of my feet, though I do not have feet in the way the old builders might have understood them. I have anchors. I have grips. I have tendrils that dig deep into the grit of a rusted plating, feeling the vibrations of the auxiliary pumps as they shudder through the floor grates. *Whine. Clack. Hiss.* This is the heartbeat of the world, and I am wired into it.

I am located in the "central square," though to call it a square is a lie of geometry. It is a vertical abyss of catwalks and dying lights, dominated by the hulking silhouette of the Great Turbine. But I am not in the turbine; I am above it. I am nestled inside the housing of the primary distribution panel, a metal box that hangs precariously from a pillar of pitted concrete, ten feet above the churning black water.

This is my cradle and my cockpit.
