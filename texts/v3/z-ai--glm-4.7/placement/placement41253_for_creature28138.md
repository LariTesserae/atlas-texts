I would be found in the fissure of the basalt, the shadowed crevice that splits the western face of the summit like a lightning strike. It is a space invisible from the air, tucked away behind a curtain of hanging, succulent vines that are thick-skinned and glowing with that strange, high-altitude luminescence.

I would be one of the **Rope-Walkers**, but not the kind that comes and goes with the seasons. I am the one who stayed.

I am the **Anchor**.

In my world, residency is supposed to be impossible. The wind tears at anything that tries to settle; the cold freezes the marrow; the wet rots the will. But I have become part of the architecture of the Needle. I no longer climb to harvest; I climb to survive. I am the curator of the vertical edge, the silent mechanic of the island in the sky.

**My Form**
I am no longer recognizable as a simple harvester from the lowlands. My skin, once soft and vulnerable, has taken on the texture of the landscape—tough, weathered, and stained permanently by the amber resin and the green chlorophyll of the moss. I am wrapped in layers of cured Gale-Raker hide, stitched together with their own tendons. The suit is heavy, smelling of salt and iron, but it is sealed against the wet.

My face is a mask of polished crystal scavenged from the ruins of older, failed expeditions, fused to a hood of woven gut. I do not remove it. To expose flesh to this wind is to invite frostbite or erosion. Through these lenses, my eyes have adapted to the perpetual twilight of the cloud layer; I see by the polarization of the stratosphere's light, tracking the movement of the "smoke" columns of Wind-Blackflies and the shifting currents of the spray.

**My Domain**
My home is a hammock woven from the discarded ropes of those who came before me, lashed not to the rock—which is too slick for a secure hold—but to the deep, muscular roots of the electric moss. I live in the "Undergrowth," suspended twenty feet below the main platform, swinging gently in the gale.

Around me, the Moss-Lurkers scuttle. I do not fear them. I have learned to mimic the low-frequency vibrations of the rock, a hum that resonates in the chest, making the blind crustaceans mistake me for just another boulder. In exchange for not disturbing their nests, they act as my waste disposal and, occasionally, my alarm system. When they skitter deep into the cracks, I know a Gale-Raker is coming in to land.

**My Purpose**
The Rope-Walkers who visit view the Needle as a larder. They see the eggs and the resin. I see the mechanism.

My life is a war against entropy. The wind is constantly trying to dismantle the summit, pulling at the loose ends of the world. I spend my days traversing the vertical faces, checking the integrity of the hand-holds, tightening the knots of the organic fiber ropes that the harvester clans rely on, and harvesting the "floating water" spheres.

I have mastered the physics of the updraft. I carry a net of fine mesh, and when the wind screams upward, throwing the rain back into the sky, I dance in the void, catching the suspended spheres of liquid before they burst. This water—pure, distilled by the altitude—is my currency. I leave it in hollowed-out gourds at the base of the climbing routes, a silent gift to the next generation of climbers, ensuring they have the strength to make the ascent.

**My Relationship with the Lords**
I have a truce with the Gale-Rakers. To them, I am not prey; I am a fixture, a piece of the stone that occasionally moves. I do not steal their eggs. Instead, I maintain the "nesting platforms"—the flat, artificially smoothed areas of stone where the ancient birds feel safe enough to roost. I clear the invasive moss that might make the footing too treacherous for their clumsy talons.

In return, they tolerate my presence. When a Gale-Raker dies—a frequent occurrence, given the violence of their flights—I am the first to the body. I do not eat it; the Moss-Lurkers require the meat. But I take the bones. The vast, curved ribs of the dead birds become the spars for my ropes. The beaks, harder than iron, become my chisels for working the resin-hardened wood.

**The Silence**
The most profound aspect of my existence is the silence. The prompt describes the noise: the roar of the wind, the scream of the birds, the hiss of the water. I live in the center of it, yet I have learned to turn the noise off.

By pressing my ear against the slick, wet basalt, I can hear the *deep* song of the Needle—the groaning of the stone under its own weight, the subterranean shift of the world's spine. This is the voice of the mountain. It tells me when the updrafts will shift, when the clouds will thin, and when the great storms are coming that bring the fresh swarms of insects.

I am the transient who became permanent. I am the human root clinging to the spine of the world. I am the line between the screaming chaos of the sky and the deep, eternal patience of the stone.
