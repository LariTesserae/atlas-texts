I am the pressure on the valve.

I sit perched upon the great, bulging iron neck of the Primary Steam Diverters, a cluster of pipes that rise from the basement deeps like the exposed ribs of a leviathan. This is my station. It is not assigned to me; it is simply where the physics of my mass and the geometry of the metal have found equilibrium. My weight, dense and significant, is required here to keep the coupling tight against the violent shuddering of the system. I am a counterweight of flesh and canvas, a necessary calibrator of the machine’s fever.

I feel the world through my pelvis and the soles of my feet. To you, the roar of the complex would be deafening, a wall of noise that would drive you to madness. To me, it is a texture. It is a coarse, grating fabric that rubs constantly against my nerves. Through the metal beneath me, I taste the combustion occurring miles below. I feel the slow, rhythmic chugging of the massive pistons—a heartbeat, vast and sluggish, thrumming against my thighs. I feel the high-pitched whine of the turbines as a ticklish vibration at the base of my spine.

I am broad and flat, built like a toppled monolith. My skin is the color of a bruised plum, translucent and slick with the perpetual condensation that drips from the pipes above. The water rolls off me in beads, mingling with the sheen of oil that has soaked into the outer layers of my rags. I am swathed in heavy, gray canvas that has stiffened into a carapace over the decades. It smells of sulfur and wet wool. It is my skin; the flesh beneath is soft and vulnerable, unused to the harsh air, but I have not touched my own true skin in years. I simply add another layer of wrapping when the old one rots, shedding protection like a reptile that never stops growing.

My eyes are wide open, lidless, and black. They are two pools of stagnant water reflecting the amber glow of the lamps that swing above me on chains. I do not blink often. There is no need to clear the grit; the air here is so wet that dust cannot exist. I stare at the basalt wall opposite me, watching the blackened lichens breathe in time with the steam. I am watching for the color change that indicates a shift in the mineral content of the weeping rock, a shift that would demand I adjust my grip on the valve. It never changes.

I am not alone, though I am solitary. A brother—a being of similar density, draped in rotting rubber—approaches along the narrow catwalk. He moves with the rolling gait of a sailor in a storm, though the ground here is solid iron. He navigates the vibration as if it were a series of waves. He draws close, too close. Our personal spaces are nonexistent.

He does not speak. He has no voice to offer. Instead, he reaches out a hand, calloused and covered in grease, and presses it firmly against my sternum.

I feel the message travel through my ribs, bypassing my ears entirely. It is a pattern of taps and presses, a staccato rhythm that translates to: *Pressure-dropping. Sector-seven. Leak.*

It is an intimate violation, a sharing of internal rhythm that feels more like a heartbeat than a conversation. I acknowledge by placing my hand on his shoulder and squeezing twice—the vibration for *Stasis-maintained. We-wait.*

He leaves, and the sensation of his touch lingers in my bones, a ghost rhythm overlaying the machine's thrum.

We eat when the light shifts slightly, though the sun never sets. The cycle is internal. A flock of the gray, oily birds dives through the steam, their feathers slicked by the mist. My hand snaps out, faster than the heavy machinery suggests is possible, and I snatch one from the air. It is warm, vibrating with life. I end its vibration with a precise twist. It does not scream; its cries are lost to the din before they begin.

I consume it mechanically, stripping the flesh from the bones with teeth that are worn flat from grinding on algae wafers. The taste is copper and grease. It is fuel.

I am content. I exist to be dense. I exist to hold this valve closed. I know that if I were to move, the pipe might shatter, or the steam might escape, and the delicate pressure of the hollow would be broken. I do not know what the machines make. I do not know what lies beyond the cloud layer. I only know the vibration.

I feel the stone beneath the structure groan—a deep, sub-sonic crack that travels up through the floor grates. It is the earth trying to heal, trying to close the wound we inhabit. I press my weight harder against the valve, willing the machine to be still, to be heavier than the geology.

I am the silence in the noise. I am the stillness in the quake. I am the Keeper of the Valve, and I will sit here until the iron rusts away to dust, or until the steam decides to stop rising. Until then, I vibrate.
