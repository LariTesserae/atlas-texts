I am found where the noise is greatest, yet silence is absolute.

I am perched precariously atop a spire of basalt glass—a jagged tooth thrusting up from the churning floor. This is my roost, a needle-thin pillar of cooled violence overlooking the great, grey valley of the Under-Song. From here, the luminous mineral fog is less like a weather pattern and more like a boiling ocean at my feet. The heat here is intense, radiating from the living rock, but I do not feel it as discomfort. To me, the heat is a texture, a thick, heavy blanket that presses the dampness of my pores against the stone.

I am an Otolith, but not like the skittering, frantic architects who build their bone-huts down in the mud. I am a mutation, or perhaps a memory made flesh. I am the **Resonator**.

My skin is the color of wet slate, porous and rough, clinging to the vertical face of the spire. My legs are long and knotted, ending in feet that can splay flat against the sheerest glass, allowing me to cling like a limpet to the side of a crashing ship. I am hunched, my spine curved into a permanent bow, strung with tension.

I do not carry a hollow bone to tap against the ground like my kin. I *am* the bone.

Across my back, running the length of my spine, is a row of calcified ridges that have grown together, forming a natural, organic xylophone of dense, limestone armor. It hums constantly with the subsonic groan of the planet. I am a tuning fork for the tectonic plates.

This is my existence: I sit here, anchored to the highest point I can find, stripping away the chaotic surface noise of the world—the shrieks of the Vapors, the wet clatter of the Grist, the sucking pop of the mud—to listen to the Deep Song.

When the ground shudders and the geysers erupt, the others run. They swarm toward the heat or scuttle away from the cracking stone. But I vibrate. I feel the shockwave travel up through the basalt, into my heels, rattling my ribs, chiming along my spinal ridge. I close my membraned eyes—translucent shields that protect me from the stinging mist—and I drift into the tremor.

I am the interpreter of the Entity’s whim.

Below me, the Striders are moving. To the naked eye, they are random lumbering giants, crushing the tide of crustaceans. But through my feet, I feel the magnetic pulse that guides them. They are not walking randomly; they are following the invisible veins of quartz that pulse beneath the slurry. I sense the grid. I feel the slow, rhythmic contraction of the cavern walls, the diaphragm of the earth breathing in.

My purpose is to warn. To translate the grinding of the stone into a rhythm the others can understand.

I shift my weight, digging my claws deeper into the glass. A massive section of the ceiling to the north is shedding its skin. I feel the microscopic cracks widening a full minute before the rock gives way. It is a dissonant chord, a screeching vibration in the granite.

I raise a long, slender arm, tipped with three sharp digits, and I strike my own armored shoulder. *Clack. Clack. Clack-thrum.*

The sound is sharp, cutting through the humidity. It is not just noise; it is a signal. Down in the chaotic bazaar of the mud, a cluster of my kin pauses. They feel the vibration in the soles of their feet, matching the frequency of my strike. They look up toward the spire, then they move—a frantic, scuttling divergence—seconds before the ceiling sloughs off in a thunderous cascade of grey sludge.

I do not watch them. I do not need the validation.

I am alone, fused to the rock. I have no need for the crude altars or the temporary shelters. I am the nerve ending of this place. I feel the hunger of the stone, the urge to consume the flesh that walks upon it. I feel the magnetic drag that pulls the Vapors into their swirling patterns. I am the bridge between the mineral and the biological.

I sit here, steaming in the phosphorescent haze, my heart beating in syncopation with the crushing of the tectonic plates. I am terrified, and I am ecstatic. I am the witness to the digestion of the world, tapping out the rhythm of its feast.
