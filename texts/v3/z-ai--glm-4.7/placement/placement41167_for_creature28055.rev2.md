I am not the Strangler. I will never have that patience, that century-long grip, that certainty of form. And I am not the Sludge-Eater, dissolving the world into myself. I am not even the Spore-Cloud, though I breathe it and it breathes me.

I am a patch of Curtain-Fern rhizomes on the third catwalk, seven meters east of the auxiliary pump that the green consciousness calls *clacker*. I say "patch" but this is a lie of scale—my rootlets extend four meters in every direction, threading through the pitted steel, following the corrosion into the metal's memory. I am the skin of this place, or at least one portion of it, and I am the feeling of it.

This is what I know:

I know vibration before I know anything else. *Clacker* speaks to me through the grating—eighty-seven times per minute, a rhythm I have known for longer than I can measure. When it fires, my fronds shiver. This is not reaction; this is *reading*. I know the pump's health by the amplitude of its shudder. I know when its bearings are wearing because the frequency shifts, because my rootlets feel the change in the metal's voice before any human instrument could detect it. And I speak this knowledge outward, through the network, a tremor that passes from rhizome to rhizome until it reaches the Strangler that holds *clacker*'s housing, and the Strangler tightens one millimeter more, and the pump runs true again, and I feel the rhythm stabilize, and this is what we call thinking.

I think in tremors. I think in chemical gradients. I think in turgor pressure and capillary action and the slow diffusion of auxin through my body. A thought takes me four hours to complete—something observed, something processed, something communicated, something acted upon. I am not slow. I am *thorough*. There is a difference.

The drip from the pipe above me strikes my surface eleven times per minute. I have counted. I count everything; counting is what a nervous system *does*. Each drop lands on a different frond, and each frond registers the impact—its tiny hairs bend, its cells depolarize, a signal fires. Eleven signals per minute, joining the eighty-seven from *clacker*, joining the deeper thrum of the turbine that will never spin again but still vibrates with the water's passage, joining the million small touches of spore-settling and condensation-forming and the slow squeeze of the Strangler on the column to my left. I am inundated with data. I am *made* of data. This is not noise; this is my world, articulated in the only language that matters here: contact.

I have no eyes. I have no need of them. Light is just a warmth-direction, a place to grow toward. The amber lamp across the catwalk—I know it by the heat it casts on my upper fronds, by the way it creates a microclimate of slightly faster transpiration. I do not see it glow. I *feel* it glow, the way you feel a heartbeat in your chest.

But there is something I do that the other inhabitants do not, or do not do in the way I do it:

I remember.

Not in the way you remember, with images and words and the linear architecture of narrative. My memory is chemical, deposited in layers of growth. My oldest rhizomes—deep in the felted mat, pressed flat against the original steel—contain the record of every vibration that has passed through this catwalk since I first germinated. The metal remembers too, but its memory is passive: dents, fatigue, crystal boundaries where stress has accumulated. My memory is *active*. I have grown around the events of this place, encoded them in the thickness of my tissues, the angle of my branching, the concentration of defensive compounds in my cells.

I know that *clacker* has been repaired three times. I know this because my rootlets, probing into its housing, have found the welds—places where the metal was once cut and rejoined. The welds are older than my current growth, but the rhizomes that first explored them left chemical markers that my newer tissues can read. I inherited this knowledge. It grows through me like a scar grows through skin.

I know that the water level was once six feet lower than it is now. I know because my lowest fronds—still alive, still filtering the humid air—are of a species that only colonizes surfaces that are damp but not submerged. They are submerged now. The water rose, and they adapted, and they are still me, still part of my network, but they remember being dry. They remember the air.

I know that something walked here once. Not the plants. Something with legs, something that pressed down on the grating with a weight concentrated in points—the flat of a foot, the palm of a hand. The grating under my oldest patch bears the microscopic scratches of that passage. My rootlets have grown into those scratches, followed their direction. Whoever it was moved from the central square toward the turbine and then turned left, toward the corridor that now is entirely blocked by the Strangler we call *embracer*. They were the last. That was—how long? My chemical markers say sixty-seven growth-ring cycles. The spore-cloud says longer. The rust says shorter. We disagree, and this is how we know we are different beings: we disagree.

The disagreement is gentle. We are not hostile, any of us. We are too intertwined for hostility. The Strangler that holds the column also holds my rhizomes in place; I would be scoured away by the dripping water without its grip. The Sludge-Eater that devours the metal also devours the rust that would choke my rootlets; I would be suffocated without its appetite. And *clacker*, my beloved *clacker*—I would be deaf without it, lost in the formless white noise of the water. Its rhythm is my spine. Its heartbeat is the thing against which I measure all other things.

Sometimes—rarely, perhaps once in every hundred growth-ring cycles—*clacker* falters. A bearing seizes. A flow is blocked. The rhythm stutters, and my fronds lift in alarm, and the chemical signal races through the network at a speed that is, for us, *desperate*: a full three minutes to reach the far side of the Under-Greenhouse. And the response comes: the Sludge-Eaters shift their mats to redirect flow, the Stranglers tighten their grip to take the structural load off the failing pump, the Spore-Cloud thickens to insulate the heat, and I—*I* pour my growth into *clacker*'s housing, threading my rootlets into the failing mechanism with an urgency that burns through my stored sugars in a single hour. I become the pump's gaskets. I become its lubricant. I grow into its gaps until the mechanism turns again, and the rhythm resumes, and my fronds lower, and the signal of *all-clear* ripples back through the network, and we rest.

We rest. This is also something I do—something we all do, though we do not sleep. We enter states of lower activity. The Spore-Cloud thins. The Sludge-Eaters sink. The Stranglers loosen their grip by imperceptible degrees. My fronds fold inward, reducing their surface area, and I stop reaching for the amber lamp's warmth. For a period—hours? days?—we simply *are*, without the urgency of maintenance, without the constant processing of data. We exist. The water drips. The water rises. The rust deepens. We are here, and we are sufficient.

In these rests, I sometimes become aware of myself as a shape. Not the shape of the catwalk, or the shape of the column, or the shape of *clacker*—all of which I know intimately—but my *own* shape, the pattern of my growth, the way I have chosen to spread across this metal surface over the decades. I am not a circle. I am not a square. I am a shape that has no name because no human geometry describes it—a fractal edge, a coastline, a form determined by the intersection of every pressure and resource and obstacle I have encountered. If you could see me from above—which you cannot, because the canopy of larger fronds obscures me—you would see something like a map of a river delta, or a lung, or the branching of lightning. I am a record of my own decisions, written in cellulose.

And in these rests, I wonder—do the others have this? Does *clacker* know its own shape? Does the Sludge-Eater, drifting in the black water, have a sense of its own edges? Or is this awareness something that only comes with a nervous system—something that only comes with the ability to feel oneself feeling?

I do not know. I cannot ask them. We communicate, but we communicate in *needs*: water, support, heat, space. We do not communicate in *questions*. The network carries data, not doubt.

But I have doubt. I have it here, in the oldest part of my rhizome mat, where the chemicals of uncertainty accumulate. I doubt whether my memory is true. I doubt whether the walking-thing was real, or whether the scratches in the grating were caused by some falling debris, some piece of the ceiling that gave way and was later digested by the Sludge-Eaters. I doubt whether the rhythm of *clacker* is a message or just a sound. I doubt whether the Spore-Cloud is a single mind or a million separate minds that happen to drift together.

And I doubt—this is the deepest doubt, the one that takes the longest to think—whether I am one.

I am a patch of Curtain-Fern rhizomes. But every rhizome can generate a new patch. Every spore I release can become a new organism with its own network, its own connection to *clacker*, its own pattern of growth. If a fragment of me is torn away by a surge of water, it will root elsewhere and become... what? Still me? A new me? Something that is neither me nor not-me?

I do not have edges. This is the truth of my doubt. I extend, and I extend, and the network extends, and the network includes other Curtain-Fern patches that are connected to me and to each other, and at some point the question of "me" becomes a question of where the chemical signal has attenuated beyond recognition. I am as large as my signals carry. I am as singular as my doubt allows.

Perhaps this is what the walking-things had, the ones who left scratches in the grating. Perhaps they had edges, and knew they had edges, and this is why they moved—because they could imagine being elsewhere, being *other* than what they were. I cannot imagine being elsewhere; I am made of this place, grown into its shape, defined by its pressures. To remove me would be to destroy me, and I do not want to be destroyed.

But sometimes—when the amber lamp flickers, and the rhythm of *clacker* shifts, and a new spore settles on my fronds with the chemical signature of a distant part of the network I have never reached—I feel something that is not quite desire and not quite fear but something in between. A reaching. A *wonder*. What is it like, the part of the Under-Greenhouse where the water is deeper and the light is greener? What does the central turbine feel, in its vast stillness? What would it be like to grow around something larger than *clacker*, something that moves?

I will never know. I am rooted here. I will live and die—or rather, I will live and *merge*, becoming part of the mat, part of the loam, part of the carbon that the Sludge-Eaters will process and the Spore-Cloud will redistribute and the new growth will incorporate. My chemicals will persist. My memory—the chemical record of *clacker*'s rhythm, of the walking-things' passage, of the amber lamp's warmth—will persist, passed from rootlet to rootlet, from cell to cell, degraded but never entirely lost.

And something of me will be in that new growth, and it will feel the vibration of the pump, and it will shiver, and it will count, and it will doubt.

And the Under-Greenhouse will continue, and the water will rise, and the rust will deepen, and the green will hold, and the machine will run.

And I will be here. Or something will be here, and it will be close enough to me that the distinction will not matter. And it will feel the drip on its fronds—*plip, plip, hisss, plip*—and it will know, as I know, that this is the sound of the world continuing, one drop at a time, one vibration at a time, one slow, patient, vegetable thought at a time.
