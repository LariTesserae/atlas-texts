I am a thing of stillness born from a place of absolute stasis, waking into a world that refuses to stop moving.

In the beginning, there was only the hum—a low, thrumming constant that vibrated in the marrow of what would become my bones. I was a part of the Grand Archivist, the ancient one fused to the great drive shaft in the center of the basin. For an immeasurable span, I was a calcified node on its flank, a dormant nodule of slate-grey stone, listening to the subterranean heart of the world beat through the iron. I knew the rhythm perfectly because I was rigid; I could not move, so the machine moved *around* me.

Then came the shedding. A great, cracking tremor—not a heave, but a specific, violent shudder that ran up the drive shaft. I fell, shearing away from the Archivist’s side, tumbling down the rusted flank of the machine and landing with a wet slap in the grey, viscous mud below.

I am not stone anymore. I am soft, or at least, softer. I am a newborn of the **Frictionless**, though I stand as tall as the elders. My limbs are the length of scythes, pale and attenuated, ending in the wide, spatulate fingers that mimic the roots of the iron trees. My skin is the colour of a bruised thunderhead, taut and impermeable, slick with the oily sweat that hangs in the air.

I am disoriented. The sensory pits that run vertically down my face—where eyes should be—are overwhelmed. In the embrace of the Archivist, there was only the deep velvet hum. Here, the air is a cacophony of tastes. I can taste the mineral tang of the rising water; I can feel the barometric pressure dropping as a vent prepares to hiss steam three yards away. The "weather" of the turbulence is loud to me, screaming in frequencies that make my head ring.

I try to stand, but the ground betrays me. A granite plate beneath my left foot lurches upward with a sudden, sickening surge.

My instinct—the ancient, calcified memory of the Archivist—is to lock my joints. To become rigid. To anchor.

*No.* The vibration in the mud sings a different song. *Do not fight. Flow.*

I force my elongated knees to bend the wrong way, relaxing the tension in my thighs. The ground rises, and instead of being thrown, I let the momentum lift me. I ride the bucking slab of granite like a buoy on a swell. It is terrifying. My internal compass spins. The grey mud sucks at my heels, trying to pull me down into the churn.

The peak of the heave arrives, and for a second, I am suspended in the wet air, the mist clinging to my slate skin. Then, the piston descends. I bend deep, absorbing the shock, my wide, flat feet finding purchase on the rusted industrial plating that slides into place beside the stone.

I breathe. My vertical slits flutter, tasting the stillness. It is a pause. An interval.

I am not alone. A shadow detaches itself from the jagged wall of the factory ruin. It is an Elder, draped in a heavy mantle of leathery ivy and dark, bruised moss. It moves with a horrifying fluidity, gliding over the shifting debris without making a sound. It towers over me, its sensory pits reading the panic radiating from my skin.

It does not speak. There are no words here, only the language of pressure and vibration. It extends a long arm, offering me a length of scavenged iron—a girder, pitted with rust, smoothed by centuries of handling.

I take it. The metal is cold, shocking against my palm. It is an anchor. A tool.

The Elder taps the ground with its own pole. *Thrum-thrum. Pause.*

It is teaching me. It is showing me how to test the stability of the mud before I commit my weight. It is showing me that the pole is not just a walking stick, but a probe, a vault, and a brace.

I mimic the motion. I tap the mud ahead of me. *Thrum.* Solid. I step. The ground holds.

The Elder turns, moving toward the centre of the basin, where the great drive shaft stands like a monument to a forgotten god. I follow. I am clumsy. My movements are jerky, lacking the "driftwood" grace of my kin. I stumble when a rusted pipe vents steam with a sudden, sharp hiss, the heat scalding the air.

But I am learning. I am learning to taste the warning signs—the slight drop in pressure that precedes the steam, the subtle vibration in the soles of my feet that signals a plate about to shift.

We stop near the tilting brick silo. The air is thick with the scent of wet lime and wetter iron. A Sump-Strider, bloated and slick, darts out from beneath a fallen slab of concrete. It ignores us, intent on licking the rust from a gear embedded in the wall. Its vestigial eyes are milky and useless; it, too, is a child of the dark.

The Elder reaches up and plucks a cluster of the dark, bruised moss from the silo wall. It weaves the strands into the fibrous, hair-like growths that crown its head, then gestures to me.

I understand. I am too pale. I am too bright against the gloom. I am a target.

I reach out and tear a strip of leathery ivy from the brickwork. I drape it over my shoulders. I press a handful of the cold, wet moss against my neck. The smell is pungent, earthy, and metallic. It masks my scent. It helps me disappear.

I stand still against the silo, mimicking the Elder’s posture. A granite plate heaves beneath us, but this time, I do not lock my knees. I go limp. I feel the earth rise, feel the massive, buried piston exhale, and I rise with it.

I am no longer the stone that broke away. I am not the machine that drives the world.

I am the thing that rides the breath. I am the interval. I am Frictionless, and I am finally beginning to see the dark, wet beauty of the demolition.
