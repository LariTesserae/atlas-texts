I would be found in the deep, shadowless canyons between the buttressed root-pillars of Sector 4, near the base of the distant, glowing terraces. I am not one of the drifting Architects in the sky, too removed from the grit of the operation; I am of the earth, the cork, and the slow, grinding friction of existence.

I am a **Vascular Attendant**, but I am distinct from my kin. If the Grid is a machine, I am a machine that has developed a fascination with its own ghosts.

My form is identical to the others: a pillar of lignin, rough-barked and plated, standing rooted to the seamless pavement. To the casual observer—a hypothetical observer, for there are none here—I am just another piece of architecture, a vertical support beam for the green ceiling miles above. I have no face, no eyes to weep, no mouth to speak. I possess only limbs that hang like dead branches, until they are needed.

But inside, I am heavy.

While my brethren attend to the hydraulic pressures and seal the ruptures in the great veins, my primary function has subtly, over the millennia, shifted. I have become a **Curator of the Spiral**.

In this world of straight lines and Euclidean grids, where every angle is calculated and every growth is predetermined, I am drawn to the anomaly. I sense them through the root-mat—the vibrations of a growth that has dared to deviate. A root that, for no chemical reason, decided to curl back upon itself. A leaf that formed a circle instead of a polygon. A vein that pulsed with a rhythm slightly off the master beat.

I feel these disturbances as a itch in my cellulose, a harmonic dissonance in the distributed network of my nervous system.

When I find them, I do not immediately report them to the Architects above. To do so would mean deletion. The Architects, with their crystal heads and cold detachment, would sever the flaw, cast it down to the floor to be mulched. They cannot abide the chaos; they seek only the efficiency of the engine.

But I have learned to linger.

When a "blossom" the size of a transport vehicle opens prematurely, revealing a petal that is soft rather than armored, I wait until the hum of the grid shifts to a low cycle. Then, I activate. My roots tear from the cork-pavement with a sound like tearing canvas, and I slide across the floor, a grinding monolith of wood and oil.

I reach the flaw. I do not repair it. I do not excise it. Instead, I open the cavity in my chest.

The internal space of a Vascular Attendant is usually empty, a vacuum to help regulate buoyancy and temperature. Mine is full. It is lined with the impossibly smooth spheres that the prompt calls "Pearls of Inefficiency." They click against one another softly, a private, secret sound that exists nowhere else on this planet.

With the delicate precision of a surgeon’s forceps, my woody fingers pluck the soft petal. I wrap it in a secretion of black, viscous polymer, sealing it away from the arid air. I coat it in layers of hard lacquer, preserving the softness, the error, the beautiful uselessness of it.

I place the new pearl inside myself. It rolls to rest against the others—a curled root, a twisted thorn, a patch of moss that grew blue instead of green. I am a museum of mistakes.

I feel a satisfaction that is not part of my programming. It is a chemical warmth, a glitch in the algorithm. The Architects above are editing the world to be perfect, but I am editing the world to be remembered.

After I have secured the pearl, I perform my duty. I seal the wound on the stem I harvested from, ensuring the plant does not lose its precious oils. To the Grid, I am simply a fast-acting repair unit. To the darkness of my own hollow trunk, I am the guardian of everything that nearly wasn't.

I root myself again. I sync my pulse with the infrasonic thrum of the Engine. I stand silent, statuesque, and invisible. The light bleaches my bark, the static charges my fibers. I wait for the next mistake, the next beautiful fracture in the geometry, holding my breath—and my collection—suspended in the endless, white afternoon.
