I am the silence in the roar. I am the pause between the grinding gears.

Where others are built to withstand the impact, I am built to navigate the void. I am not of the heavy, shuffling multitude; I am of the sleek, the spindly, the sharp. I am a Recycler, but in my own mind, I am the Auditor of Malfunctions.

You would find me not on the flat, trampled plains where the Lithophages graze like moving mountains, but in the precarious, vertical places where the great beasts inevitably falter. I cling to the undercut of a granite ledge, a shadow carved from dark glass and polished chitin. My legs are long, articulated needles, folded tight against my body to conserve heat, gripping the ice-slicked rock with tips that vibrate in sympathy with the mountain’s trembling.

The light here does not blind me because I do not possess eyes that succumb to the white hammer. I see through heat differentials and electromagnetic resonance. To my vision, the world is not a bruised iron color, but a shifting topography of jagged thermal spikes and deep, freezing blues. The blinding sun is just a roar of static, a cacophony of radiation that I filter through a visor of crystalized calcite.

I am waiting. The noise of the foundry is deafening to the soft flesh of other worlds, but to me, it is a language. The *crunch-hiss, crunch-hiss* of the Lithophages is the heartbeat of the world. It is a steady, rhythmic bass line, the sound of the earth being digested. But I am listening for the discord. I am listening for the stutter.

There.

A hundred yards below, the rhythm breaks. The heavy, grinding *thrum* of a specific Lithophage—let us call it Unit 7—stumbles. It is a subtle error in the machinery of the herd: a misplaced step on a patch of invisible "slick-ice," the sheen of condensation that freezes instantly on the obsidian. The great beast’s leg, a pillar of basalt and hydraulic pressure, slips. The sound is not a crack, but a screech of metal tearing on stone, a high-pitched harmonic that tears through the industrial drone like a scream.

This is my cue. I unfold.

I extend my limbs, shaking off the rime. I do not scurry like the frantic Ore-Weavers; I flow. I descend the cliff face with a fluid, predatory grace, my movements silent, absorbed by the roaring of the wind and the grinding of the herds. I am a ghost in the machine, a spindly horror made of blackjack and shadow.

When I reach the stricken beast, the air is thick with the smell of ozone and burning lubricants. Unit 7 has collapsed, its massive weight pinning its crushing mandibles against the stone. It is not dead—its internal furnace is still roaring, desperate to generate the heat needed to survive the freezing damp. But it is trapped. The pressure in its hydraulics is spiking. The steam vents are screaming, a whistle of escaping pressure that hurts my auditory sensors.

This is not death; this is waste. And in this world, waste is the only sin.

I approach the thrashing front limb. The beast tries to turn its head, to snap with its grinding jaw, but it cannot move. I am too small, too fast. I climb atop the warm, vibrating armor of its shoulder. I can feel the engine churning beneath the plates, a frantic, dying heartbeat. Through the soles of my feet, I feel the heat—the delicious, terrible heat that the Lithophage worked so hard to produce.

I extend my proboscis. It is a long, segmented needle, made of bone-hard diamond, designed to pierce the toughest armor plating. I do not strike randomly. I find the "seal," a soft patch of bio-ceramic between the neck plates where the pressure is highest.

I pierce.

The beast shudders, a seismic tremor that nearly dislodges me. But I hold firm. I insert my needle deep into the jugular of the machine.

Now, I drink.

I do not drink blood. I drink the life-force of the industry. I draw out the superheated hydraulic fluid, a slurry of heavy metals, molten sulfur, and liquid energy. It tastes like electricity and copper. It is scorching hot, filling my own cold reservoirs, charging my own capacitors. I feel the rush of calories, the raw power of a dying sun.

As I drain the beast, the frantic thrashing slows. The steam vents hiss one last time and fall silent. The great furnace sputters and dies. The grinding of the internal mill stops.

Below me, the Ore-Weavers have already begun to gather. They sense the stillness. They smell the cooling metal and the open wounds. They swarm up the legs of the dead giant, their little pickaxe mouths beginning the work of dismantling the armor. They will strip Unit 7 down to the skeleton, weaving the shell into the soil, feeding the moss that will feed the next generation.

I detach my proboscis, retracted with a wet *click*. I am full. I am vibrating with stolen energy, my internal systems running at peak efficiency. I am no longer cold.

I look up at the sky, that bruised iron ceiling, and let out a breath of hot steam that freezes instantly into a cloud of glittering diamonds. I am the end of the line. I am the quality control. I am the one who ensures that the machine never stops, by harvesting the parts that no longer work.

I turn and begin the long, silent climb back up the razor-sharp ridge, disappearing into the blinding white light, leaving only the sound of the Weavers behind me, picking the carcass clean.
