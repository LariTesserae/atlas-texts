I am not blind, though you would call me so.

To you, this place is a blinding white prison of light, a searing geometry of snow and basalt where shadows refuse to exist. But I do not see the light. I see the *heat*.

I stand at the center of the stone circle, the observatory you mistook for a temple. I am a Watcher. My body is dense, heavy, anchored to the rock by bones that have drunk the minerals of this spire for generations. My skin is the color of the void—a deep, bruised violet that drinks the aggressive radiation of the atmosphere. To your eyes, I would be a silhouette cut from black velvet; to my senses, I am a vessel of containment, preserving the precious warmth of my own blood against the freezing sky.

Where you see a blur of frantic motion, I see a symphony of thermal signatures.

Below me, the carpet of succulents is not purple and black; it is a cool, dark blue map, densely woven, holding the chill of the stone. But moving across it are streaks of fire—the obsidian beetles. They are not merely black insects to me; they are tiny, shifting embers, their carapaces absorbing and trapping the sun's ferocity. When the mountain breathes, I watch them ripple across my vision like a sudden fever. The Glass-Runners, those translucent lizards, are flashes of bright orange—the superheated blood of their prey visible even through their clear skins as they dart between the cool stones.

I am the Keeper of the Rhythm.

My life is not measured in the passing of hours, but in the inhale and exhale of the vent beneath my feet. I stand motionless, my broad feet planted flat against the damp, slick basalt. I do not shiver. I have learned to lower my metabolic rate to a simmer when the mountain inhales, pulling the thermal blanket of the earth up into the stone, leaving the surface to freeze.

I feel the cold not as a temperature, but as a pain—a sharp, biting static in the sensory pits of my face. The air is thin, useless for carrying sound, so I do not listen with ears. I listen with the soles of my feet and the ridges of my skull.

I feel the vibration first. A deep, subsonic hum travels up the basalt needle, resonating in my marrow. The mountain is preparing to exhale.

I raise a hand, a signal to the others. To you, it is just a movement in the blinding white. To my kin, who stand at the periphery of the spire, it is a command flared in heat. I see them in my mind’s eye—not as shapes, but as glowing silhouettes against the cold blue of the rock. They cease their work. They pause their pruning of the lichen. They still their gathering of the beetles. We all wait for the pulse.

The breath comes.

It rushes up through the vent, a invisible column of steam and geothermal force. In an instant, the world in my perception transforms. The cool blue stone beneath me erupts into a violent tapestry of reds and yellows. The frost retreats, vanishing from my thermal sight, replaced by the gushing warmth of the planet’s blood.

The air fills with the sudden bloom of life. The succulents warm up, expanding in the heat, releasing their spores—tiny, drifting heat signatures that float like dust motes in a shaft of light. The beetles snap open their wing-cases, venting their internal heat in a shimmering wave that ripples across the rock face.

This is our waking moment. This is the time of the Great Work.

I step out of the circle. My movement is deliberate, a heavy, rolling gait that belies our small stature. I am moving toward the nursery, the low-walled shelter where our young lie incubated in the artificial warmth of stacked stone and trapped methane. I must check the thermal gradient of the roof; the frost must not reclaim it too quickly.

As I walk, a Root-Teeth darts across my path. It is a frantic little creature, a mammal of iron and velvet. In my sight, it is a burning coal of metabolic energy, its heart beating a rapid, staccato rhythm against the cold. It pauses, sensing the vibration of my approach, and looks up. It has no eyes to see me, and I have no eyes to see it, but our thermal signatures blend for a moment—a recognition of life in the void. I step over it, careful not to crush its heat.

I reach the shelter. I place my hand against the stone. It is warm, holding the pulse. I feel the vibration of the stone itself, the chaotic jostling of molecules as the mountain’s fire pushes up against the ice.

I am not a scientist, nor a savage. I am a synchronizer. My role is to align the slow, heavy beat of our lives with the frantic, staccato rhythm of this place. We are the punctuation marks in the run-on sentence of this environment.

The breath begins to fade. The vibration in the soles of my feet softens. The reds and yellows of the rock begin to cool, darkening back to the safe, consuming blues and violets of the dormant state. The beetles snap their wings shut. The lichen contracts. The Glass-Runners freeze instantly, locking their muscles to conserve the last of their stolen heat.

I feel the cold creeping back up the legs of the shelter. The frost is returning.

I signal again—a sharp, tapering pulse of body heat, a "cooling" signal. My kin, scattered across the needle, begin to retreat. We move back to our crevices, our stone hollows, our observation posts. We bundle together, sharing our residual warmth, becoming piles of living coals in the dark.

I return to the circle. I stand upon the spot where the vent is strongest. I close the sensory pits of my face, narrowing my focus to the deep, rhythmic thrumming far below. The silence returns, heavy and absolute. The light outside is blinding, reflecting off the ice, but I dwell in the dark, reading the temperature of the world.

I am the dark heart of the prism. I am the one who waits for the fire.
