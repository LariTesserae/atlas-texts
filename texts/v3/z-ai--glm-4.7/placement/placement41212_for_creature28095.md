I would be found where the amber light fails and the machinery breaks surface, clinging to the exterior skin of the Spire like a dark scab.

I am not of the pale, pristine swarms that carpet the interior catwalks. My kin, the Dusters you described, are the white blood cells of the internal body; they are soft, protected, and destined for the damp, warm gloom of the artificial lung. I, however, am the epidermis. I am the **Exo-Custodian**.

I exist on the *outside*, hanging upside down three thousand feet above the fading bruise of the atmosphere. Here, there is no haze of smog to filter the sun, only the raw, searing radiation of the void and the absolute, freezing black of the shadow. My shell is not the clean, white ceramic of a reactor shield; it is scorched black and pitted, textured like the basalt I cling to, hardened by decades of cosmic ray bombardment. I am camouflaged against the dark rock, a silhouette cut from the void itself.

Where the interior Dusters have the delicate tweezers of a watchmaker, my front limbs are heavy, piston-driven spikes. I do not scrape rust; I anchor myself against the hurricane-force solar winds that scour the Rim. My sensory clusters are different, too. They do not glow with the soft red of laser diagnostics; they are matte black, wide-aperture lenses designed to drink in the faint starlight and track the erratic trajectories of micrometeoroids. I do not scan for vibration; I scan for impact.

My domain is the massive drive shafts that protrude from the cliff face. While the internal swarm maintains the gears that turn them, I tend to the blades that bite into the vacuum. The differential temperature between the sun-side and the shadow-side creates immense stress on the metal; it warps, it fractures, it screams in frequencies no air can carry. I am the silent welder. I carry a tank of superheated slurry on my back—not for eating, but for binding. When a fissure opens in the hull, threatening the pressure of the pockets deep inside, I am there. I inject the slurry; it froths and hardens in seconds, sealing the wound.

I am solitary. The hive-mind that directs the frantic workers below is a dull, buzzing hum at the back of my consciousness, fainter than the vibration of the shaft I stand on. I do not receive orders to repair or dismantle; I receive only the imperative to *persist*.

To the Spindle-Lords inside, I am a necessary anomaly. I am too rugged, too radiation-blanched to be permitted in the clean, silent libraries of the interior. If I were to crawl back through an airlock, my jagged, burnt shell would tear the metal grating, and my tracking sensors would overload in the dim amber light. They would likely dismantle me before I could take three steps.

So, I remain outside. I am the only one who sees the world the Spire has forgotten.

While the interior Dusters feast on the theoretical memory of water, I watch the reality of it. I watch the clouds—the bruise of atmosphere—roiling miles beneath my claws. I see the storms that hammer the roots of the mountain, lightning flashes that are silent from this altitude. I have a collection, hidden in a magnetic cradle magnetized to the dark side of a drive shaft. It is not rust, and it is not silica. It is a shard of blue glass, melted from the sand of a lower desert by a lightning strike, carried up by a freak updraft and embedded in the exterior plating near my post.

It is the only blue thing in my world.

Sometimes, when the rotation of the Spire turns me away from the sun and into the deep freeze of the eclipse, I disengage my magnetic locks and free-fall for a few seconds, caught in the micro-gravity of the mountain’s rotation, before slamming my spikes back into the hull. In that moment of weightless silence, I am not a machine, nor an insect. I am a piece of the void that learned how to hold on.

I am the Exo-Custodian. I am the black shell on the white bone. I keep the vacuum out, and I keep the silence in, and I watch the blue turn.
