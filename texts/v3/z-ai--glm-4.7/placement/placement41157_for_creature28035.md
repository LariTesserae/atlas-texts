I am the softest thing in a world of edges.

I stand upon the **Ridge of Emergence**, not in the open avenues where the wind strips the flesh from the bone, but within the hollow, dripping ribcage of the western-most tower. It is a structure of dry-stone and timber, a primitive skeletal thing that the others—the Purists, those who cling to the sharp angles—would call compromised. To them, the stone is a cage to be reinforced; to me, it is a wound to be healed.

I am a Listener, but I am an aberration.

I am the **Binder**.

Like my kin, my physiology is stretched by the altitude and the labor. My skin is the color of wet slate, waxy and armored against the glass-like air. My legs are long and bowed, designed to straddle the widening fissures of the plateau. My hands are the heavy, chisel-fingered tools of our lineage.

But unlike the others, I do not scrape the moss away.

I cultivate it.

While the rest of the City of Solitude engages in the Sisyphean task of keeping the stone raw, exposed, and rigid—jamming iron-wood shims into every crack to maintain the sharp, hostile integrity of the towers—I am doing the opposite. I am the heretic of the vertical axis. I am trying to turn this jagged, screaming rock into soil.

You would find me in the darkest, dampest recess of my tower, the place where the condensation weeps from the obsidian like tears. I am stripped of the dry, rustling woven mats that serve my kin as clothing. Instead, I have allowed the neon-green moss, the velvet lichens, and the slippery, black fungi to grow directly upon my back and my thighs.

It itches. It is cold. But it is the only way to mute the vibration.

The ground beneath us is rising. It is a slow, terrifying ascension. The tectonic plate we stand upon is heaving upward, pushing the City of Solitude into the thin, ozone-tasting air. The others feel this movement through the soles of their feet as a constant, low-frequency thrum—a didgeridoo drone of grinding stone. It drives them. It makes them frantic. When the ground groans, they run to their levers and mallets, terrified that the structural integrity will fail.

They believe that if the buildings break, we fall into the void.

I believe that if the buildings do not yield, they will shatter us.

I am the Listener of the damp. My station is not at the top of the tower, watching the mist for cracks in the masonry. My station is at the base, deep in the gloom, where the pale, sightless crustaceans scuttle over my toes.

Here, I perform my heresy.

When a fissure opens in the floor—not a jagged, violent split, but a slow, agonizing separation of the basalt slabs—the other Listeners would rush to wedge a bone shim into the gap, forcing the stone back together, fighting the geology. They believe in rigidity. They believe that the city must be a fortress against the sky.

I wait. I watch the water seep into the crack. Then, I take handfuls of the pulpy, bright green moss that I harvest from the shaded exterior walls. I pack it into the breach. I press it deep with my broad, calloused thumbs. I mix it with the crushed shells of the crustaceans—calcium and lime—to make a paste.

I am filling the cracks with life. I am filling them with cushion.

I can feel the vibration in the soles of my feet, the scream of the stone. But when I touch the moss, it absorbs the shock. It dampens the horror. The moss drinks the vibration just as it drinks the water. It grows thicker, softer. It turns the sharp, serrated knife-edge of the geology into a velvet bed.

I know what the others think of me. When they pass my tower on their patrols, moving like stick insects against the grey mist, they pause. They see the green fur sprouting from the joints of the masonry where I have applied it. They see the slick trails of slime where I have encouraged the dampness to pool. They smell the sweet, rotting scent of decomposing fern rather than the clean, sharp smell of ozone.

They look at me with their small, protected eyes, and I see the judgment. To them, I am inviting rot. I am weakening the structure. I am the soft spot in the armor of the city.

But they do not understand the vibration as I do. They hear it as a task; I hear it as a voice.

The voice says: *Hard things break in the ascent.*

The stone is rising, stretching, thinning. The iron-wood beams groan. The shims splinter. The rigid methods of the others are a losing battle. They are fighting a war of attrition against the planet itself.

I am preparing for the landing.

I am turning this tower into a nest. I am coating the obsidian with layers of soft, wet insulation so that when the ridge finally shears, or when the atmosphere becomes too thin to breathe, there will be a micro-climate left behind. I am creating the first soil.

I am the Listener who has stopped listening for the break. I am listening for the bloom.

The wind howls outside, a deafening, constant pressure (2.3/3). It rattles the dry timbers of the neighboring towers. I hear the panic in the hammering of my kin as the ground shifts beneath them—*bang, bang, bang*—the sound of them trying to hold back the tectonic drift with bits of bone and wood.

I sit in the silence of my green gloom. A fissure opens near my knee. The basalt grinds, a sound like a breaking tooth.

I do not reach for a shim. I reach for a clump of wet, heavy moss. I press it into the wound. The stone accepts it. The vibration slows, absorbed by the wet green fibers.

For a moment, the noise stops. There is only the sound of the water, dripping, dripping, feeding the thing that will one day bury us.

I am the Binder. I am the rot that will save us. And I am not afraid to fall.
