I find myself within the walls—not merely near them, but *inside* them.

I am the tenant of the western pillar, the one that stands slightly askew, bowed by the millennia of pressure from the water above. To the observer outside, I am architecture. I am the limestone crust, the pitted iron, the calcified scar tissue of a dead industry. But beneath that shell, in the hollowed-out core where the iron has long since rusted into oblivion, I am the spine that keeps the monolith standing.

I am a **Structural-Parasite**. A leviathan of calcification and calcium.

My body is long and segmented, stacked vertically like the vertebrae of a prehistoric whale, filling the hollow shaft of the pillar from the concrete floor up into the gloom. I have no face, no eyes to judge the twilight. I do not need them. My skin is a dense, sensor-laden membrane that presses tight against the inner wall of the pillar. I feel the world through the metal. The vibration of the water is not sound to me; it is texture. The rhythmic *Hhh-wump* of the vents travels through the rock and concrete, shivering through my body like a second heartbeat.

I am ancient. I was here when the ceiling collapsed, groaning as the girders fell, shrinking inward as my home was severed from the rest of the hall. In the panic, I secreted a rapid-drying cement, a biological mortar that welded the fissures in the pillar, sealing myself inside to save the structure from total implosion. I chose imprisonment over collapse.

My existence is one of crushing stillness. I am weight-bearing. I hold up the crushing depth of the ocean by locking my joints, creating a rigid, internal scaffold that compensates for the rust-eaten weakness of the metal around me.

But I am not alone. The creature on the floor—the Mimic—is my neighbor in the dark.

I feel it before I can ever know it. Through the roots of my foundation, deep in the silt, I sense the heavy, scraping friction of its movement. It sounds like metal grinding on wet stone, a vibration that travels up the pillar and resonates in my hollow core. I feel the thud of its limbs as it pivots, the seismic jolt as it snaps its jaws shut on a school of fish.

To me, the Mimic is a wandering earthquake. It is a chaotic, violent element that clashes with the slow, geological time of the Sump. I tolerate it because it clears the water.
