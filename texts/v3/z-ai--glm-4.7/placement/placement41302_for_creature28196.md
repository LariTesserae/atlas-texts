I am anchored in the suspension, held in the thick, mineral throat of the world.

I do not swim. Swimming implies a frantic, directional energy, a rebellion against the medium. Here, in the heavy brine of the Low-Pressure Sump, rebellion is inefficient. I drift.

I am situated in a place I think of as the Tri-Valve, a junction where three massive intake pipes converge like the fingers of a drowning giant. The water here is forced through a narrow aperture, creating a steady, artificial current. It is a river of pressure, invisible to the eye, but felt as a constant, vibrating hum against my skin.

At first, I am only the sensation of the cold. It is not a sharp, biting cold, but a deep, pervasive chill that slows the firing of my thoughts, dragging them down to the tempo of the silt settling on the floor below. The water is dense, heavier than air, heavier than memory. It stings slightly where it touches the softer membranes beneath my armor—the taste of dissolved copper and ancient oil.

Hunger is a slow thing here. It does not gnaw; it grinds.

I am the city eating itself.

A disturbance in the pressure field.

I freeze. My filaments retract instantly. I flatten myself against the curved wall of a silo, spreading my body to maximize surface area, vanishing into the shadows. My internal light dims, extinguishing completely.

Something is moving against the current, struggling slightly. A Glass-Crab.

It is ten yards away. I do not see it; I feel the displacement of the water, the chaotic turbulence of its legs gripping the silt. It is hunting the plankton, oblivious to the statue on the wall.

The crab drifts closer. It is grazing on the soft moss growing near a valve stem. It is directly below me now.

The ride is beginning.

I release my hold on the silo wall. The surge catches me, pulling me into the main artery of the Sump. I glide effortlessly now, riding the pressure wave generated by the machine. I pass towering structures of riveted steel, past coral-like calcifications that seal leaks in the pipe network.
