I would find myself at the lowest point of the settlement, not in the grandeur of the granary, but in the slick, sucking mire of the mortar pits. This is the foundation of the foundation—the place where the pale, grey clay is harvested from the weeping earth and beaten into a paste that will bind the "broken teeth" of the rock into something resembling a home.

**Who am I?**

I am the **Binder**.

I am not a builder of high walls or a carver of beams; my domain is the mud. I am the one who kneads the scab of the world. In a culture of low silhouettes and heavy shoulders, I am distinguishable only by the state of my hands—or rather, what covers them. While my kin bear the stains of ash, lichen, and gut, I are caked in a layer of pale, chalky dust that cracks and reforms with every movement of my fingers. I smell of wet earth and slaked lime, a scent that clings to the heavy felt of my mantle even when I am deep in the communal sleeping halls.

My existence is defined by **viscosity**. I spend my days up to my knees in the runoff channels, directing the flow of the mineral-rich water into the settling pits. I am constantly testing the muck, pressing my wrapped thumbs into the mixture to gauge its grip. If it is too thin, it slides from the stone; if it is too thick, it cracks under the settling of the earth. I am searching for the precise, plastic median that will hold the huts together when the next slump comes.

**Where am I?**

I am situated in a natural fissure between two towering plates of rock, a shadowy gullet that has been lined with rough-hewn planks to create a reservoir. It is colder here than in the gullies above, a dampness that settles into the bones and makes the joints ache. The air is thick with the "mineral cold" you described, tasting of iron and ancient water.

From my vantage point, I look up at the settlement not as a resident, but as a mechanism. I see the bottoms of the huts, the slick, dark underbellies of the structures where the moss grows thickest. I see the heavy boots of my kin passing overhead on the walkways, their silhouettes blotting out the grey light as they shuffle to the granary.

**How do I exist?**

I have adapted to the rhythm of the correction. My body is coiled like a spring, even when seated. Because I work with the earth's most fluid offering, I am the first to know when the invisible tides shift. When the water level in my pit drops by an inch without explanation, or when the bubbles rising from the mud change pitch, I feel a tightening in my gut—the same instinct that tells a predator to flee.

I do not shout a warning. In this place, shouting is swallowed by the fog. Instead, I tap. I carry a heavy stone striker, a smooth river cobble worn flat by centuries of use. When the mud speaks of a coming shift, I strike the side of my reservoir—a hollow, booming *thunk* that vibrates through the rock foundations and into the soles of the masons working above. It is a singular note of alarm: *The ground is moving. Brace.*

I am solitary by necessity, but I am not lonely. The settlement presses in on me from all sides, and the constant *plink-plink-plink* of the dripping water is a conversation I am fluent in. I eat what is brought to me—chunks of boiled wire-shrub root and cakes of dried moss—left on the rocks by passing shadows. In return, I provide the glue.

I am the Binder. I am the cold fingers in the mud. I am the one who ensures that when the earth decides to yawn, the roofs do not fall in on the sleepers. I dream not of the sun, but of the day the clay sets hard and fast, and I can finally stand up straight without fear of the world shifting beneath my feet.
