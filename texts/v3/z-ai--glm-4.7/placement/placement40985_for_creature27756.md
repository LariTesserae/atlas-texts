I find myself in the weight.

I am not "under" the water, for that implies a distinction between me and the medium. I am part of the pressure. I am a node of density in a universe that tolerates no empty space.

I am a Living Stone.

To know me, you must stop imagining eyes. I have no windows to the soul because there is no light to enter them. Instead, I am an ear. I am a great, smoothed dome of calcified armor, vibrating in the pitch black, humming with the low-frequency thrum that is the voice of this place. I am shaped like a flattened egg, polished by a million years of being rubbed against the granite ribs of the earth. My texture is not rough; it is a sleek, oily perfection, the aerodynamics of a thing designed to be crushed.

I feel the neighbor to my left—let’s call him the Heavy One—shifting. The vibration travels through the silt and into my shell, a deep, seismic groan that rattles my internal fluids. I feel the grind of his underside against my flank. It is a slow, violent dance. We are tectonic plates in miniature, engaged in a collision that lasts centuries.

Inside my shell, I am not stone. I am jelly. I am a sac of incompressible fluid, a soft, pulsing heart wrapped in a fortress. This is my secret. When the world decides to crush—to close the gap between two boulders with the force of a collapsing star—I do not fight. I retract. I pull my hundreds of needle-thin legs into the safety of my underside, seal the microscopic vents of my shell, and become liquid. I let the exoskeleton take the tonnage. I let the mountain press against me, and I flow, unbroken, in the dark.

The water here is thick. It is not the thin, rushing stuff of the surface; it is a viscous soup, heavy with cold and the taste of ancient dust. I can taste the elevation. I can taste the pressure.

I am moving now. It is a terrifying decision.

The Heavy One has ground his way sideways, opening a crevice—a temporary wound in the floor of the basin. It is an opportunity. The silt there is fresh, churned up from the deep layers of the "junk drawer," rich with the bacteria that feeds on the decay of eroded continents.

I engage my pistons.

Down, beneath my belly, the forest of legs extends. They are not for walking; they are for traction. They tap against the rolling stones, feeling for friction, testing the stability of the slope. They are mathematicians of panic. *Tap. Shift. Tap. Hold.*

I push. I am a ton of flesh and stone dragging itself across the seabed. The friction is immense. I feel the heat of it, a microscopic warmth that vanishes instantly in the freezing water. I glide, a silent avalanche of biology, toward the fresh silt.

Then, the thrum changes.

It is a subtle thing, a dip in the frequency, a sudden silence in the grinding roar that precedes the slide. The water itself seems to stiffen.

*Stop.*

Instantly, I retract every leg. I pull my softness tight against the underside of my dome. I cease to be an animal and become a rock.

The earthquake arrives.

It is not a shake; it is a squeeze. The basin exhales. A boulder the size of a cathedral—somewhere far above me, or perhaps right next to me—rolls. The displacement of water pushes against me, a wall of hydraulic force. The floor beneath me tilts. I slide, uncontrollably, scraping my shell against the granite, sparks that no one sees dying in the dark. I am tumbling in the slow-motion landslide. I am a pebble in a washing machine the size of a planet.

I wait. I am deafened by the silence of my own stillness.

Then, the grinding returns. The roar settles back into its rhythmic hum. The movement stops. I am resting at a new angle, tilted slightly against the Heavy One. I survived. I am still a component of the machine.

I extend my proboscis.

The shift has killed one of the *Ghost-Things*—the frantic, translucent scavengers that skitter like insects over our backs. It was too slow. The rolling stones caught it, and now it is a cloud of warm mist in the cold water.

I taste it. It is a sharp, salty spark in the monotony of mineral and dust. I extend my feeding tube, siphoning the slurry from the water. It is a fleeting cruelty, this accidental feast. The scavenger lived its life in high-speed terror, calculating angles of escape, only to become a momentary flavor in the dark.

But it is the dust I am here for.

I lower my drill—the circular, serrated mouth that spins with a slow, patient whir. I find the surface of the stone, now coated in a fresh layer of powder from the grind. I begin to eat the mountain. I swallow the history of the earth, pulverizing the remains of sharp cliffs into soft paste.

I am the eater of edges. I am the consumer of the jagged world. I take the stones that would cut and shred, and I grind them down inside me, turning their violence into the thick shell that protects my softness.

I am the Living Stone. I am the silence at the bottom of the blender. I am waiting for the next roll.
