The first thing I am is a hum.

Before I have a name, before I have limbs or a distinct shape in the mist, I am a low-frequency vibration sitting in the dark. I do not hear the noise of the High Ossuary the way a creature with ears might; I feel it as a texture against my skin. The roar of the waterfall is a coarse, sandpaper grit scraping against my chest plates. The deep, structural groan of the shifting basalt is a heavy, rhythmic thrumming, like a slow heartbeat that belongs to someone else. The high-pressure hiss of the spray is a needle-sharp stinging at the back of my neck.

I wake to the sensation of cold oil slicking my surface. I am small, dense, and hollow-boned. I am a Resonant Kin.

I find myself deep inside the ribcage of a dead giant. This is the Central Intake Tower, a vertical cathedral of concrete and rebar that stands, or rather leans, in the center of a boiling brown lake. The water outside is rising, the levels shifting violently in the span of an hour, but here, inside the hollowed-out machine halls, the air is still heavy and wet, vibrating with the ghost of an engine that no longer turns.

I open my eyes—small, protected by heavy lids that sweep the condensation away. The light is a dim, bruised gray, filtering through cracks in the concrete ceiling where the rust has eaten through. In this gloom, the bio-electric mist glows. Swarms of Thunder-Midges pulsate in the upper corners of the hall, their tiny bodies charged with the static of snapping power lines. They look like drifting stars, or embers from a fire that never goes out.

I push myself up. My fingers are broad, tipped with suction-like pads to grip the smooth, wet glass of the control panel I was sleeping on. I am barefoot, my feet calloused and wide. As I stand, my chest expands, the resonant chamber inside my ribs flexing involuntarily. I let out a soft, sub-vocal thrum—*thrummmm*—a greeting to the room. The sound hits the rusted metal pipes hanging from the ceiling, and the metal sings back a faint, harmonic echo. This is how I know I am safe. The pitch is true. The room is tuned.

I am a Tuner. It is my role to interpret the chaos.

I walk to the windowless opening that looks out over the basin. Below, the river is diverting itself. I can feel the hydraulic hammering through the soles of my feet before I see it. The water is attacking the rusted skeleton of an excavator, a machine the size of a small hill. I watch as a Torrent-Wyrm, sleek and black as oil, coils around the excavator’s rusted arm. It isn't eating the metal; it is using it as an anchor to wait out the surge. I feel the Wyrm’s low-frequency roar in the marrow of my leg bones. It is a warning, and a claim of territory.

I turn away from the violence. My purpose is not to fight the water, but to listen to the Ghost Current.

I climb a ladder of pitted steel that runs up the interior of the tower. My movements are fluid, practiced. I don't just climb; I flow, my body absorbing the sway of the tower as the river smashes against its base. At the top, I reach the switching station. Here, massive copper cables, thick as my torso, lie snapped like dead vipers. Where the insulation is stripped, the blue arcs of wet electricity jump and hiss.

This is the altar.

I pull a small toolkit from a pouch at my waist—scavenged bits of ceramic and glass. I sit cross-legged on a rubber mat that has grown a layer of soft, spongy moss. I begin to work. I am weaving a new net. I take strands of copper, thinner than hair, and splice them into the frayed ends of the old grid. I am not trying to restart the factory; that is a fool’s errand. I am trying to catch the overflow.

As I work, the aeroplankton drift down, drawn to the ionization around my fingers. They sting my skin, leaving tiny, glowing welts that fade in seconds. It is a small price for the power they carry. I am building a capacitor for the cavern below. When I am finished, this net will hum with a voltage that will stun the clouds of Thunder-Midges, dropping them into the filtration vats where we cultivate the algae and nutrient paste.

I am a bridge between the organic and the mechanical. I do not see a difference between the vein of a leaf and the copper wire in my hand. Both carry a current. Both can be broken. Both must be tuned.

Suddenly, the tower shudders. It is not the usual rhythmic pulse. This is a jagged, dissonant shriek of metal tearing. I stop working. I press my hand flat against the cold floor. The vibration coming from the west is sharp—high frequency, rapid decay.

A flash flood has breached the upper terraces. A landslide of mud and granite has just taken out a support pylon. The sound of the water is no longer a roar; it is a scream.

I feel the change in pressure in my inner ears, the bony plates along my skull rattling. I have minutes before the surge hits the basin and raises the water level past the intake grate.

I stand, my body tense. I do not panic. Panic is a frequency that drowns you. I breathe deep, engaging my chest chamber to emit a low, steady pulse—a rhythm that counters the chaotic frequency of the disaster. *Thrum... thraum...*

I grab the copper net I have woven. It is finished just in time. I secure it to a ceramic insulator, then turn and sprint down the ladder. I move with the density of the stone, heavy and grounded. As I descend, the screaming of the metal gets louder, but my internal rhythm keeps me centered. I am a note of order in a symphony of entropy.

I reach the lower decks, sealing the heavy watertight hatches behind me, spinning the rusted wheels until the mechanism locks. The darkness here is absolute, but I do not need light. I can feel the shape of the room by the way the sound waves bounce off the walls. I am home.

I am small, pale, and slick with oil. I am the priest of a broken generator. I am the silence inside the scream. I am a Resonant Kin, and I am listening.
