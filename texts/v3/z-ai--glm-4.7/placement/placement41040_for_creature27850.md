I would find myself in the dark, quiet corners where the industrial sprawl butts up against the raw, wounded earth—in the fissures and drainage ditches where the runoff from the machines pools and stagnates. I am the **Root-Weaver**, or perhaps more accurately, the **Binder**.

While the Geode-Breakers are the massive, moving pillars of the workshop, I am the mortar that keeps the floor from dissolving beneath them. I am small compared to them—perhaps only the size of a large dog, low to the ground, and shaped less like a person and more like a living, spooling axis of fibrous muscle and woody stem.

My flesh is not iron, but a symbiotic lattice of the neon moss and the pale, grey roots of the giant ferns. I am covered in the same sodden, velvet vegetation that drapes the overhangs, making me nearly invisible when I crouch among the slick black stones. I smell not of ozone and rust, but of the deep, rich sourness of wet earth and composting vegetation.

In this loud, violently vibrating world, I am the anchor. The Geode-Breakers communicate through the tremors in the ground; I am the one who dampens them. My existence is defined by **grip**. Where the stone is splintery and fresh, threatening to slide away under the heavy boots of the Breakers or the immense weight of the presses, I send my root-like fingers deep into the shale. I knit the loose, aggressive scree together, binding the sharp edges of the infantile world into a stable path for the giants to walk upon.

I move with a slow, fluid curiosity, always in contact with the stone. I have no feet to lift and drop; I flow, sliding over the wet gravel on a bed of muscular roots. When a machine’s piston fires and the ground shivers with a tectonic *thrum*, I dig in. I feel the chaos travel up my limbs and, through a biological alchemy of tension and release, I disperse the shockwave into the earth. I am the suspension system of the basin.

My relationship with the Geode-Breakers is one of unspoken, utilitarian kinship. They are the architects of order, turning chaos into brick; I am the preservationist, ensuring the chaos doesn't swallow the workshop whole. They do not step on me, for my glowing green hide is distinct against the black obsidian. Sometimes, when one of them stops to rest, leaning heavily against a riveted wall, they will lower a massive, rusted hand to where I cling to the foundation. They do not pet me; they simply rest their weight upon me, and in that contact, they share the vibration of their fatigue. I absorb it, shaking the tension out of the metal and into the ground, a silent exchange of load and burden.

I eat the sharpness of the world. My diet consists of the fresh, jagged shards of obsidian that the machines excavate, which I grind down in a gizzard of stone and acid, excreting a rich, packed soil where the giant ferns can take root. I turn the cutting edges of the world into a bed for life.

At night, when the pale daylight fades and the steam vents hiss with a white, ghostly fury, I climb the arches of the great pipes. I hang there like a heavy sloth, cocooned in the warm insulation, listening not to the roar of the engine but to the slower, deeper pulse of the water condensing inside the metal. I am the silence that waits in the loud places, the green slow siege that patiently holds the walls together while the hammer falls.
