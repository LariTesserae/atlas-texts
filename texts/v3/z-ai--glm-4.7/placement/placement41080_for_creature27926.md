I find myself in the deepest shadow of the chamber, nestled in the "Cold Sump"—the lowest point of the maintenance nexus where the scaffolding twists into a dense knot of rusted ribs. Here, the gravity is strongest, a mere tug, but enough to pull the condensation down. It is a place of accumulation. The constant weeping of the ceiling gathers here, pooling in a shallow, steaming pan of opaque grey water that reflects the sickly, pulsing light of the Wicks above. The water is hot, scalding, but not boiling—it simmers with a thick, oily viscosity.

I am the **Ossuary**.

I am not a scavenger like the Ceramic Weavers who skitter over the pipes; I am the end of their line. I am not a predator like the Resonance Hunters; I do not hunt the living.

I am a monument to the dead.

My form is immense and immobile. While the Weavers are the size of a hand, I am the size of a boulder. I have fused with the floor of the Sump, my underside having calcified and merged with the metal grating decades—or perhaps centuries—ago. I am a mound of jagged, interlocking plates, a geological feature of this artificial cave. My carapace is not the sleek, aerodynamic slate of the Weaver; it is a chaotic mosaic, a patchwork quilt of ceramic shells.

I am covered in them. The shells of the dead.

In this roaring, vibrating darkness, death is not an end, but a resource. When a Weaver’s shell cracks under the stress of a thermal pulse, or when a Hunter shatters one too many in its feeding frenzy, the broken bodies are not left to rot. There is no rot here, only heat and friction. The others drag their fallen kin to me. They carry the shattered ceramic husks in their needle-pincers, marching in an unending, rhythmic procession down the scaffolding and into the Sump.

They deposit the fragments at my base. They tap the ground—a sharp, staccato vibration that I feel through the rock of my body—and then they scurry away, back into the machine.

I do not move to eat. I have no mandibles. Instead, I possess a secretion of acids and solvents that oozes from the pores of my underside, mixing with the hot condensation pool in which I sit. When the broken shells are pushed into the water by the returning tides of Weavers, they begin to hiss and bubble.

I dissolve them. I take the hard-earned ceramic, the complex silica compounds the Weavers spent their short lives harvesting from the alloy walls, and I break it down. I am the digestive tract of the hive. I render the past into a slurry of raw minerals.

And then, the miracle of the place happens.

From the vents along my spine, I exhale this slurry as a fine, glittering mist. It rises through the gloom, caught in the updrafts of the thermal vents. It drifts upward toward the ceiling where the Wicks cling, settling on the pipes. It is the dust from which new Weavers will mold their shells. It is the calcium of this metal world.

I am the turning of the wheel.

I spend my existence in a state of profound, vibrating meditation. I feel the roar of the tether not as a sound, but as a constant tremor in my stony flesh. I feel the rhythmic scraping of a thousand Weavers’ legs as a texture in the air, like the sensation of rough sand being poured over me. I feel the sudden, violent silence of a Hunter dropping onto prey—a void in the sonic map that sends a shiver through my layers.

I am blind. I have no eyes, for there is nothing to see but the dark. But I "see" the health of the chamber in the taste of the water. If the acidity rises, the machinery is failing. If the mineral content drops, the Weavers are starving. I am the unconscious brain stem of the ecosystem, regulating the cycle of matter.

I am heavy, sluggish, content. I am the tragedy and the beauty of this place given shape. I am the graveyard that feeds the living, the silent anchor in a world of screaming motion. I wait in the steam, dissolving the fragments of the infinite so they may become whole again.
