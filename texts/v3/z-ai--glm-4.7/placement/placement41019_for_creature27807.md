I am suspended in the middle reaches of the stalk we call the *Spire of the Weeping Lung*. My position is static, locked not by effort but by the art of stillness. My body is folded into a shallow alcove I carved over the course of three seasons, my knees bent at the double-joint, my elbows tucked close, mimicking the dormant posture of a deep-sea spider.

The water here is heavy with oxygen, a thick, cool soup that tastes faintly of crushed mint and ozone. It fills my respiratory collar with a slow, rhythmic insistence. The ruffled flesh of my neck flutters automatically, a wet, internal metronome keeping time with the slow, geological pulse of the city around me. I do not breathe; I osmote. The heat in my blood is kept close to the surface, visible through the pale, translucent skin of my chest, a dark, slow-moving river beneath the ice of my flesh.

I am a Carver, though to call it a trade implies a haste we do not possess. My role is to curate the light.

In my right hand, I hold a sliver of obsidian, chipped from the volcanic glass of the abyss and bound to a handle of dense driftwood. With my left hand, I retract the webbing between my fingers, exposing the long, tapering digits and the hard, chitinous nails that serve as my primary picks. I am scraping at the inner wall of my home, removing the soft, greenish pith of the tower-algae.

*Scrape. Pause. Drift.*

The wall beneath my tool glows faintly, a bioluminescent bruise where I have disturbed the plant’s tissues. This window will not be finished for another month. It must be enlarged grain by grain so the structural integrity of the living stalk remains uncompromised. If I cut too deep, the tower’s vascular system would seal the wound with scar tissue, blinding the view. I must be gentler than the current.

Through the jagged circle of the opening, the world is painted in shafts of silver and blue. The light from the surface descends in solid pillars, illuminating the rolling basalt hills of the plateau floor hundreds of meters below. Visibility is absolute. I can see the drift of the iridescent fish schools—a shimmering curtain of static that shifts direction as one collective mind.

Below me, perhaps fifty meters, a shadow detaches itself from the gloom.

It is a Grandfather, a six-sided crystal turtle the size of a small dwelling. It does not swim; it neutrally buoyant, a piece of the geology that has decided to move. Its shell is a mosaic of calcite and living moss, the greens and browns vibrant in the cold light. I watch it with my large, black eyes, tracking the pressure wave of its movement more than its shape. It is drifting toward the lower tiers of the Grove, where the moss is thicker and the currents slower.

I feel a vibration in the water, a distant query. It is not a sound, but a pressure shift. Someone is signaling from the adjacent tower, a slender, pale structure connected to mine by a sagging bridge of woven kelp.

I do not turn my head. To turn is to disturb the stillness. Instead, I lift my left wrist from the algae wall and flex a specific muscle group beneath the skin. A patch of bioluminescent bacteria on my forearm flares once— a pale, steady violet. *I see you. I am working.*

The response is immediate, a flash of amber from a dark window across the gap. *The current is turning.*

I acknowledge this with a slow blink, my nictitating membrane sweeping sideways across my dark orb. They are right. I can feel the change in the drag against my tendrils. The long, pale filaments that crown my head, dyed a deep bruising purple from crushed anemones, are beginning to tremble. The "weather" is about to shift.

I stop carving. I stow the obsidian blade in the pouch at my hip, a leather sack made from the skin of a shark-like thing that hunted these waters long ago. I push off from the alcove.

I do not swim. I *unfold*.

My body straightens, the extra joints in my legs locking to extend my frame to its full, terrifying height. I am taller than the tower is wide. The water catches me, and I begin to rise, buoyant and weightless. I control my ascent with subtle flicks of the webbing on my feet, steering myself toward the open hatch of my dwelling.

Above me, the great nets of the weavers are strung between the Spire and its neighbor. They hang limp in the slack water, but as the new current builds—generated by the tidal tug of the distant moon—they will belly out, becoming invisible rivers in the sky.

I drift upward, passing the level of the hatch. I choose to continue rising. I want to ride the thermal layer before the rush hits.

As I ascend, the silence of the deep changes texture. It is never silent here. The clicking of the reef-dwellers—thousands of tiny crustaceans snapping their claws—creates a sound like heavy rain falling on a tin roof. Deeper than that is the thrum of the kelp forests, a low-frequency groan that vibrates in the marrow of my bones.

I break into the upper canopy, just below the zone where the light becomes blinding. Here, the water is warmer, kissed by the sun. I hang motionless, arms outstretched, my tendrils streaming behind me like seagrass. I am a mirror of the city around me: tall, hollow, alive, and waiting.

A school of jellyfish drifts past, their bells pulsing in slow motion, trailing stinging tentacles like lace. I watch them pass with the indifference of a mountain watching a cloud.

I am of the Drift. I have no name, only a history etched into the walls of a plant that breathes for me. I am cold, I am slow, and I am precisely where I need to be. The current turns; I let it take me, tumbling gently backward in the blue, green-lighted metropolis, a guest in the gut of a gentle giant.
