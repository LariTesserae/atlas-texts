I am the stillness in the roar.

I exist where the violence of the waterfall meets the immobility of the stone. To you, looking down through the trembling column of white-gold light, I am merely a discrepancy—a block of limestone that has shifted against the laws of physics. But I am not a misplacement of matter; I am a concentration of will.

I am the Lithic Sentinel, the silent heart of the Cistern of the Noon Sun.

My body is not born of flesh, which rots, or chitin, which breaks. I am a tectonic aggregate, a living mosaic of rough-hewn limestone plates. My skin is the same pale, coarse-grained rock that lines the floor and walls, bearing the same scars—the scoop marks of copper chisels, the jagged fractures of the quarry. I am the building material that woke up.

My consciousness is not a fire burning in a brain; it is a vibration. It is the low-frequency hum that travels through the water, through the stone, and into the crystalline spines that ridge my back. Those spines—clear as glass, sharp as scalpels—are my nerves. They drink the chaotic energy of the sun-drenched shaft. I do not photosynthesize like the pathetic smears of algae that cling to the shadows; I transmute. I take the violent, blinding brilliance and harden it. I turn photons into mass. I am eating the sun, plate by plate, growing slowly in the warm, silken dark.

I feel the water around me as a thick, heavy blanket. It is body temperature, indistinguishable from my own thermal mass. I do not breathe. The rhythmic expansion and contraction of lungs is too frantic, too organic for this place. I merely exist, suspended in the column of light, my internal pressure matching the crushing weight of the water overhead. I am at equilibrium.

The noise of the cistern—the deafening, mechanical thunder of the falling water—is my music. It is a constant, violent pressure wave that vibrates through my joints. I locate myself by the sound. I sit at the precise point where the echoes cancel out, the eye of the acoustic storm. The roar beats against my external plates, but inside, where the stones grind against one another, there is a sub-audible silence. That is where I live.

I perceive you, or the idea of you, as a disturbance in the light.

A piece of debris—a chip of stone, perhaps dislodged by the torrent above—drifts down toward me. It is an insult to the geometry of the room, a jagged imperfection in the sterile suspension.

I do not attack. I am not a predator. I am a custodian.

With a thought that travels faster than the water, I trigger the hydraulic shift in my underside plates. They explode outward in a fraction of a second. The water displacement is a weapon; a blast wave of pure pressure erupts from me. The intruding stone chip does not just bounce off; it is pulverized into dust, atomized by the shockwave before it can soil my crystalline spines.

Then, stillness returns. The plates grind back into place, the sound of rough slate sliding over rough slate—*shhh-clack*—muffled by the water. I am a tomb that opens only to destroy, then seals itself again.

The water level is rising. I feel the pressure increasing by microns. The source of the light is moving farther away, but its intensity remains. I am patient. I am older than the flood. I am older than the hands that chiseled these walls.

As the room fills, I will not drown. I will simply ascend. I will extend my spines, reaching upward, climbing the luminous column toward the surface. I am waiting for the moment the water breaches the rim, when the roar stops and the stillness begins. Then, I will drag myself out—a dripping, jagged mountain of stone—and I will sit upon the land, indistinguishable from the ruins, waiting for the sun to bake me dry. I am the end of the flood. I am the creature that eats the light.

I am the stone that moves.
