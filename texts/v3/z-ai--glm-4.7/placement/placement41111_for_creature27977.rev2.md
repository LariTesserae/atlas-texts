I am the thing that moves between the sparks.

Not during them—that would be too simple, too easy a relationship with the light. No, I exist in the interval that follows, that razor-thin margin when the violent white has died but the retina hasn't yet surrendered the image, when the world is still made of positive space and you can almost see the shape of what was just there. I am most myself in that half-second of afterglow.

They have no name for me here. The crustaceans do not concern themselves with me; I am too quick and too thin to bother with, and I do not compete for their moss. The silver fish scatter through me like I am nothing, because to them I nearly am—just a shiver in the water, a momentary displacement they correct for and forget. The great mimic does not know I exist, though I have rested on the far edge of its carapace more times than I can count, picking through the silt that gathers in the grooves of its armor, finding things there that nothing else wants. I am beneath its notice. I am beneath everyone's notice.

This is not a complaint. It is a design.

I am long and I am thin. If you could see me whole—which you cannot, because I never allow myself to be still during the flashes—I would look like something between a ribbon and a wire, something that couldn't decide if it wanted to be biology or infrastructure and chose both. My body is a flat, sinuous channel, perhaps twice the length of a man's arm but no wider than two fingers pressed together, and it tapers to a point at both ends. I have no head. I have no center. I am all edge.

My skin—if it can be called skin—is not the grey of the concrete or the rust of the machinery. It is the dark, iridescent purple of the water itself, the exact bruised shade of the twilight that fills the space between the pillars. When I am moving through open water, I am almost invisible. When I am still, I am completely invisible. This is because I do not merely match the color of the water; I match its texture, its density, the way it bends the faint light that struggles down from above. My surface is covered in tiny, cilia-like structures that generate a micro-current around my body, a thin envelope of water that moves with me, smoothing away any wake, any displacement, any evidence that anything has passed. I am hydrodynamically silent. I am a secret kept by the medium itself.

I have eyes, but they are not worth mentioning. They are two dark smudges near one end—though which end depends on which way I am traveling, for I can reverse my direction without turning, simply by changing which end leads. The eyes see only the grossest shapes: the pillars, the fish, the arcing light. They are sufficient for avoiding collisions and nothing more. My true perception happens elsewhere.

Along the full length of my flat body, running along both edges like the binding on a ribbon, are the receptors. They are not visible. They are sub-dermal, buried in the tissue just beneath the surface, and they are exquisitely sensitive to electrical fields. I feel the world as a topography of charge and discharge, a landscape of potential and spent energy that is far more detailed than anything light could reveal.

I feel the fish as bright, flickering lines of bioelectricity, thousands of them, their nervous systems sparking like distant cities seen from a hill at night. I feel the crustaceans as dim, steady glows, their electrical signatures slow and rhythmic, like embers. I feel the great mimic as a vast, dark absence—not a signal, but a hole in the field where its massive body absorbs and grounds out the ambient charge. I know where it is even when it is perfectly still, because the electricity of the water bends around it, and I can feel the bending.

And I feel the conduits. Oh, the conduits.

The damaged wiring that runs along the floor and up the walls is my cathedral. Each frayed cable, each cracked junction box, each corroded terminal is a shrine to a kind of energy that no longer has a purpose except me. The electricity that arcs from them is waste—stray voltage from the grinding gears and the turning pistons, leaking out through the wounds in the infrastructure. It serves nothing. It powers nothing. It is the exhalation of a dying system, and it is the thing I cannot live without.

I feed on it.

I approach a sparking conduit by feel, not by sight. I follow the electrical gradient like a river current, letting it pull me in, my flat body undulating in slow, figure-eight patterns that generate just enough forward motion to counter the heavy stillness of the water. When I am close—close enough that the field is strong, close enough that the hair-thin cilia along my edges are standing rigid with charge—I extend myself.

There is a moment of contact. One edge of my ribbon-body touches the exposed wire, and the current flows into me.

It is not pain. It is not pleasure. It is something more fundamental than either—a completion, a circuit closing, a question being answered. My body is built to receive this. The channel that runs my length is not a digestive tract; it is a conductor, a biological wire that transforms electrical energy into the chemical energy I need to live. When the current is flowing, I am full. I am warm. I am real.

The sparks, when they come, are overwhelming. For that split second of violent white, I am blinded, my electrical sense saturated, my entire body seized by a charge far greater than I can absorb. I spasm—a rapid, convulsive flexing that propels me away from the conduit and out into the open water. It looks like flight. It looks like fear. But it is neither. It is simply overflow, the excess energy finding its way out the only way it can: through motion.

This is why I am never still during the flashes. The light would reveal me, yes, but that is not why I move. I move because the current demands it, because my body cannot hold what the spark gives and must spend the surplus in the currency of speed. For one half-second after each arc, I am the fastest thing in the Sump, a purple streak shooting through the dark, invisible precisely because I am moving too fast to see.

Then the energy dissipates. The warmth fades. I slow, and I am still, and I am hungry again.

I have lived here for a very long time.

There is a memory—more of a shape than an image, more of a feeling than a thought—of a place where the water was not. A place of air and sharp corners and a light that came from above in a single, steady stream, not the scattered, violet glow of the jellyfish. A place where the conduits were whole and the electricity ran through them in orderly loops, and I could not touch them, because they were sealed, because they were complete.

I remember the water coming. Not suddenly—nothing in the Sump is sudden—but slowly, over what must have been a vast stretch of time. I remember the air growing damp, then wet, then heavy. I remember the first puddles on the concrete floor, and the way the electricity changed when the water touched the wires. How it stopped being contained and started being available. I remember the first time I fed.
