I am not a creature of the wandering herds, nor am I a frantic groomer upon a beast's back. I do not possess the fluid malice of the Weavers, nor the formless hunger of the Drift. I am too vast to be small, and too stationary to hunt.

I am an **Atmospheric Bellows**.

I stand in the deepest depression of the Sky-Sponge, a sunken amphitheater of stone and rot that the locals—the trundling Treaders—avoid. The ground here is not merely spongy; it is a suspension of ancient peat and black water, thick as tar. I am rooted in it. My base is a wide, muscular disc, anchored deep into the sedimentary layers of the plateau, gripping the earth with a thousand hydrostatic anchors. I am immovable. I am the smokestack of the world's lungs.

My body is a spiraling tower of flesh, rising fifty feet into the grey twilight. I am not a solid column; I am a living tube, a pleated sleeve of thick, rubbery muscle arranged in a fractal geometry of rings and valves. My skin is grey and rough, textured like the volcanic rock that mimics me, overgrown with a thick, wet carpet of moss and lichen. To the casual glance—a glance that rarely happens here, for who looks up?—I am just another geological oddity of the Sky-Sponge. Just another weeping stone.

But I am not stone. I am a pump.

My existence is a cycle of pressure and release, a slow, hypnotic peristalsis that dictates the tempo of the valley. I spend hours inhaling. The great muscles along my flanks contract in a rolling wave, drawing the heavy, wet air down into my maw. I can taste the composition of the atmosphere. It is a rich, metallic broth. I taste the sulfur hissing from the vents of the fused-bronchiad trees in the distance—pungent, acidic, sharp. I taste the damp oxygen exhaled by the moss carpet. I taste the spores, the pollen, the microscopic dust of this high place.

The air passes through my internal filters, vast racks of spongy tissue that strip the impurities, absorbing the moisture and the heavy gases. I compress them. I process them. And then, I exhale.

My exhale is not a breath; it is a plume. It is a column of warm, dry air that hisses softly as it escapes my upper vents, invisible against the fog, but vital. I am the reason the cold does not kill everything here. I am the regulator.

My world is defined by vibration. Because I have no eyes, and my skin is too thick to feel the light touch of wind, I listen with my body. I feel the Sky-Sponge breathing.

I feel the Treaders before I ever "hear" them. They are miles away, but their weight is immense. Through the black soup of the peat, through the stone lattice, I feel the seismic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of their approach. It is a heavy, wet impact, a rhythmic compression of the sponge that travels up my roots and shivers through my flank. I can distinguish individuals by the frequency of their tread. The old Bull-Treaders move with a slow, arthritic grind—a low-frequency pulse that vibrates my lower rings. The younger, frantic ones strike the ground harder, faster.

Sometimes, they pass close to my base. I feel the disturbance in the water as they push through the muck. I feel the heat radiating from their massive, fungal-armored bodies. I sense the frantic *tic-tic-tic* of the Groomer-Swarms on their hides—a faint, high-frequency itch in the air, like the buzzing of a fly caught in amber. They are the maintenance crew for the mobile; I am the maintenance crew for the static. We are brothers in industry.

There are other vibrations, too. The faint, silky tremors of the Damp-Weavers. They do not walk; they flow. I feel them shifting in the porous rock walls that line my valley. They are cautious, silent things, but they cannot hide their mass. When they strike, when they wrap around a prey, there is a sudden, sharp spike of turbulence, followed immediately by silence. The silencing is as distinct as the noise. The ecosystem holds its breath for a moment, and I, the lung of the valley, pause in my cycle to acknowledge the transfer of energy.

And then there is the Drift.

The Drift is the only thing that touches me with intimacy. When the low-hanging banks of living fog drift through my valley, I do not recoil. I embrace it. The Drift is a colony of stinging cells, a microscopic jellyfish-cloud. It scrapes against my upper elevations, looking for algae to graze upon. I feel the sharp, nettling pricks of a million tiny stingers against my mossy hide. It is a painful sensation, a dull, burning itch, but it is necessary. The Drift cleans me. It strips away the debris that collects in my vents. In exchange, I grant it passage. I gently pulse my warm, dry exhaust through its mass, creating a localized updraft that carries it to new feeding grounds.

I am content in this solitude. I am the metronome of the wet dark.

I stand tall, my flesh shivering with the constant, white-noise hum of the planet. I inhale the iron-tasted fog, filtering the tears of the weeping rocks. I exhale the warmth that keeps the machine running. I have no name, for I need none. I am the rhythm. I am the Bellows, and I am breathing.
