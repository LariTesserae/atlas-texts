I am the heavy step in the twilight. I am the weight that keeps the dream from floating away.

You would find me on the **Truss Bridges**, those long, precarious spines of rusted iron that stretch across the deep, fog-choked ravines. This is where the world is most dishonest, where the distance between two points is a lie told by the mist. The bridges want to stretch; they want to become infinite, unspooling like loose yarn across the void. If left to their own devices, a worker could step onto a bridge and walk for a week, the other end retreating always into the yellow gloom, until the machinery ran out of fuel and the man fell into the nothingness.

I am the counterbalance to this physics. I am the **Anchor**, or as the humming Drifts think of me in their wordless songs: *The Keel.*

I am not a biological cog in the way they are. I am something denser, something forced into existence by the sheer need for gravity. My body is a construct of lead, iron, and the heavy, black basalt that drips from the ceiling of the world. I stand perhaps eight feet tall at the shoulder, shaped like a crude, hunched approximation of a bear, but there is no softness in me. No fur. My "skin" is plate armor, cold and wet, fused directly to the muscle beneath. My blood does not flow; it slides, a thick, mercury-like sludge that hums with a low-frequency thrum.

I walk the bridge.

The rhythm of my gait is slow. *Thud... pause... drag... thud.* I am heavy enough that the iron grating of the bridge deforms slightly under my feet, springing back only after I have passed. This deformation is my purpose. As the bridge tries to elongate, to stretch the space between the factories, my weight crushes the metal back to its proper span. I am the physical denial of the subjective distance. Where I step, reality is forced to behave. I am the editor of the city’s geography.

I can feel the dampness clinging to my flanks, the wet silk of the air trying to seep into my joints, but I am too hot with friction to freeze. The moss tries to grow on my back, attracted to the heat, but it cannot find purchase on my shifting armor plates.

Ahead of me, the bridge fades into the mist. I see the Drifts, those wet-clay laborers, shuffling toward the distant factory spires. To them, I am a moving mountain. They part ways as I approach, their humming taking on a lower, respectful tone. They do not fear me; they need me. When I pass, the distance shortens for them. The bridge snaps from an infinite march to a manageable traverse. They scurry in my wake, using the compressed space I create to ferry their rivets and oil cans.

Overhead, the Conductors cling to the humming violet cables. They are terrified of me. My mass disrupts the magnetic fields they ride; I am a turbulence in the river of electricity they feed upon. When I walk, they skitter higher up the lines, their eyes flashing white panic, their hair standing on end. I ignore them. I am of the earth and the iron; they are of the lightning and the air.

At my feet, the Scavengers—those clockwork rats of metal and bone—dart and click. They are fearless, swarming over my heavy boots as I pause, trying to pry flakes of rust from my soles to eat. I do not shake them off. Their tiny, biting mouths are a faint tickle, a reminder that even the heavy and permanent are being consumed.

I never stop. That is the terror of my existence. To stop is to die.

I see the Statics on the edges of the plateau as I cross the ravine—those frozen souls who stood still too long and became stone. I look at them with my eyes, which are not eyes but thick, yellow lenses that glow with the heat of my internal engine. I feel a kinship with them. They are what happens when the rhythm breaks. They are the silence that follows the note.

But I cannot be silent. If I stop, the bridge will stretch. The factories will drift apart. The loose confederation of this industrial world will unspool, and the sketch will dissolve back into gray slurry.

So I walk.

My internal furnace roars, a low, guttural grind of gears against magma, drowning out the screech of the Spark-Wings. I taste the copper in the air. I feel the "weirdness" pushing against me, the world trying to rewrite the laws of distance, and I push back. I am the resistance. I am the friction that keeps the engine turning.

I am the Keel. I am the weight in the stomach of the sky. And I am walking.
