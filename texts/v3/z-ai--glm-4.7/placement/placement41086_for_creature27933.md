I find myself drawn to the verticality, to the sheer, sweating faces of the basalt towers. In this world of held breath and razor-sharp edges, I would not be one of the grand, swimming leviathans in the sky, nor the bloated, glowing ghosts in the moss. I am smaller than that, and more necessary to the preservation of this pristine, angry stasis.

I am a **Gloss-Ribbon**.

I inhabit the invisible trails of water that streak the black obsidian like tears. To the casual glance, I do not exist. I am a variant of the flatworm, evolved to extreme specialization in this zero-erosion world. My body is perhaps two feet long, but barely wider than a human finger, and impossibly thin—compressed to a living, muscular ribbon of translucent tissue.

My flesh is not one color, but a chameleon of the stone. Within my gelatinous form, I possess chromatophores that can mimic the exact sheen of wet basalt. When I am stretched across a dry patch, I look like a crack in the rock; when I am moving through a bead of condensation, I look like the water itself, dark and fluid. Only my eyes betray me—two tiny, pinprick dots of obsidian hardness at the leading edge of my form, scanning the texture of the wall.

I live my life entirely on the vertical faces, defying gravity through a combination of surface tension and microscopic suction pads located along my underbelly. I do not walk; I glide. I leave no trail, for my slime is not mucous but a clear, frictionless oil that actually polishes the stone as I pass, erasing the footprints of beetles and the scuffs of other climbers.

I am the guardian of the edge.

My niche in this ecosystem is the maintenance of the "newness." The world is defined by its lack of decay, by the fact that the edges remain razor-sharp. But biology is messy; spores land, bacteria begin to feast, lichens try to gain a foothold. This is where I come in. I am a grazer of the microscopic. I slide endlessly along the hexagonal joints and the sheer vertical drops, rasping with a tongue so fine it feels like a velvet cloth. I scrape away the microscopic spores, the invisible films of algae, and the dust that dares to settle on the glass face of the city.

I am the reason the stone still possesses the fresh, angry sheen of its creation. I am the geological immune system.

My existence is one of intense, quiet focus. I feel the world through my belly. I sense the deep, low-frequency thrum of the earth not as a sound, but as a vibration that travels up the rock and into my suction pads. When the ground settles, I freeze, flattening myself against the stone so completely that I become a two-dimensional shadow, indistinguishable from the wall itself. I wait for the silence to return, for the "held breath" to exhale.

I have a complex relationship with the other citizens of the mist.

I fear the **Green-Ghosts**, the translucent frogs. They are clumsy to me; they disturb the water I travel in. To them, I am a fleeting dark streak—a "shadow-snake" that vanishes before they can strike. I slide into the hairline cracks where their bloated bodies cannot follow.

I avoid the **Obsidian Skins**. They are competitors for the condensation, brutish and dry. They scrape the wall with their squeegee tongues, drinking the sweat of the stone. I follow them. Where they drink, they leave the stone dry and clean, and I feast on the microscopic organisms they expose in the process. I am the remora to their shark.

But my true purpose, my contribution to the silence, happens at the edges.

Occasionally, I will find a place where the "sweat" of the stone has pooled into a droplet heavy enough to fall. Before it can detach, before it can crash to the floor and break the silence with a splash, I intercept it. I slide underneath the heavy bead, arching my body to catch it. I absorb the water into my own tissues, hydrating myself, and preventing the noise. I drink the silence.

In this way, I am a creature of the boundaries. I exist where the liquid meets the solid, where the living meets the inert. I am the polish on the black glass, the movement in the stillness. I am the invisible ink writing the history of a city that refuses to decay.

I am currently high up a thirty-foot tower, hidden in the shadow of a leaning monolith. The mist is warm against my back. I am motionless, waiting for a single, heavy drop of condensation to gather weight above me. My body is tense, a coiled spring of dark fluid. I am watching. I am waiting. I am keeping the edge sharp.
