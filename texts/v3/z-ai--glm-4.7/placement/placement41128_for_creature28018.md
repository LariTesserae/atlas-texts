I find myself on the spine of a long, submerged ridge, a place where the bedrock breaks the surface like the spine of a leviathan fossilized in mid-swim. Here, the wind is negligible, beaten down by the weight of the air, but the sound is physical. It presses against my chest like a heavy wool blanket.

I am one of the **Dampeners**, but I am not merely a resident of this wet, gray purgatory; I am its tuner. I am the one who chips away at the discord.

My body is built for this heavy, resonant oppression. I am shorter, denser than the ancestors I see in the genetic memory of my bones. My legs are pillars, thick with muscle designed to lock in place, not for running, but for anchoring. My skin—the color of wet slate—feels the temperature of the air not as hot or cold, but as varying degrees of viscosity. When the humidity rises, the world thickens; when the water levels drop below, the air becomes thin and sharp.

Most importantly, I have no ears. Where they once were, centuries ago, there is only smooth scar tissue and dense cartilage. I do not miss them. To have ears here would be to live in a constant state of agony, every nerve ending firing in panic at the seismic groaning of the planet. Instead, I possess a deep, hollow ache in my shins and forearms—the receptors that connect me to the stone.

I stand in the center of a depression between three towering whalebacks of stone. This is my workplace, a natural amphitheater of bad acoustics. The water rushing through the subterranean channels beneath my feet hits a sharp turn in the rock, causing a high-pitched screech that vibrates at a frequency that makes my teeth ache. It is a jagged tear in the fabric of the world’s low-frequency drone.

I kneel. The contact is electric. Through my knees, I feel the chaos of the water—millions of gallons of liquid grinding against the stone. It is a violent, unreadable mess. I pull my chisel from my belt—a tool of dense, black volcanic glass—and a hammer of heavy iron-ore.

I work slowly. I cannot simply strike; I must listen with my hands. I find the point on the rock face where the screech originates. I place my palm flat against the slick, phosphorescent slime. The rock sings to me in a pattern of tremors. *Here. It hurts here.*

I strike. Not a heavy blow, but a precise, calculated tap. The shock travels up my arm, rattling my fused bones. The note changes slightly—the sharp edge blunts. I tap again. My movements are rhythmic, synced with the thud of the earth's heart. I am not trying to silence the noise; that is impossible. I am trying to smooth it, to round the sharp corners of the soundwave so that it fits into the greater, mournful symphony of the basin.

To my left, a Slough-Behemoth heaves itself over the ridge. It does not startle me. I felt its coming minutes ago—a low, rumbling thud that traveled through the bedrock long before its massive, wet bulk obscured the gray light. It pauses near me, its shovel-like jaw scraping the lichen from the rock. It exhales, a plume of warm, moist air that smells of sulfur and minerals.

I pause and press my hand against its flank. It is a courtesy. The beast shudders, a low-frequency greeting that vibrates through my hand and into my chest. We stand there for a moment, two dense creatures anchored to the earth, united by the vibration. It is the closest thing to conversation I have ever known. It acknowledges my work; I acknowledge its passage.

Around its feet, the Clatter-Runners erupt. They are frantic, glitch-like movements in my peripheral vision. I pay them little mind. They are the static of the world, high-pitched and nervous, scuttling over the stones. They tick against the rock, creating tiny, percussive ripples that are lost in the roar.

Eventually, the sun—that dim, distant idea behind the clouds—begins to fail. The light shifts from gray to black. The pitch of the water changes as the tides shift deep underground. The screech I was working on has softened into a low hum, a bass note that complements the grinding of the earth.

I am tired. The constant vibration takes a toll; it shakes the marrow loose. I gather my tools and trudge toward my hut, a low structure huddled against the leeward side of the largest stone whaleback.

I enter the circle of silence. The hut is small, cramped, lined with furs that smell of smoke and stale rain. But in the center, the ring of river stones waits. I step into the circle and sit. The difference is instantaneous. The roar of the world does not stop—my bones still feel the earth turning—but the chaotic harmonics are dampened. The stones absorb the violence of the sound.

I lean back against the stacked rocks, pressing my spine against them. This is the only time I can truly think. The silence here is not an absence of sound; it is an absence of *pain*. I close my eyes (useless vestiges that see only fog and shapes) and let my mind drift.

I dream of a place where the ground does not sing, where the stone is dead and quiet, where water falls from the sky in sharp, clean lines rather than weeping from the pores of the world. It is a fantasy of a dry, loud place, a place where sound travels through the air and not the bone. But the dream is fleeting. The ground shifts beneath me, a reminder of the waking world.

I open my eyes. I press my hand to the floor, feeling the steady, mournful thrum of the basin. I am the Dampener. I am the stillness in the noise. I am the one who listens to the scream of the earth and teaches it how to hum.
