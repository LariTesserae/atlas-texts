I am found in the vertical gloom of the alleys, where the sky is a narrow parenthesis of grey and the light is a memory of the sun above.

I am a **Grey Strider**.

But I am not merely *an* inhabitant; I am a fixed point in the drifting geometry of this place. To the observer above—to the rare eye that might glance down from the orbital silence—I would be invisible. I am a smudge of deeper shadow against a flank of black-wet basalt. I am a protrusion that the wind did not carve.

My existence is defined by the grip. I cling to the sheer face of a tower that the mist has been hollowing for millennia. My body is flat, a low-slung suspension bridge of flesh and armor, splayed wide against the cold stone. I possess the warmth of blood, but it is a slow, heavy heat, buried deep beneath a carapace of calcite that I have grown myself, layer by microscopic layer, until it matches the limestone so perfectly that the rain runs off me as if I were nothing but a bump in the rock.

Here, on the vertical plane, gravity is not a law; it is a negotiation.

I am ancient. I do not measure time in the frantic beats of a heart, but in the long, tidal shift of the mist. My pulse is a single, heavy throb that resonates through my grip-pads, a metronome ticking away the centuries. When I am still—and I am still for long epochs—I am not separate from the High Plinth. I am the architecture. The Weaver-Mold, that black, velvet silence that binds the spires, has colonized the joints of my armor. It threads through the seams of my shell, a living mortar that connects me to the wall. It whispers to me in chemical signals, telling me of the stress in the stone, of the hairline fracture opening three feet to my left, of the slow drip of calcite-saturated water building a stalagmite on the ledge below.

My eyes are adapted to the gloom, but they do not see quick motion. They see the gradient of wetness. I watch the "streets"—the fissures at the bottom of the canyon—fill with the morning's heavy tears. I watch the flutes of the tower darken as the water descends, looking like ink running down the page of the world.

I am hungry, but hunger is a slow tide.

I release one of my hind-grips, a suction seal popping with a sound like a bubble bursting in mud. I move upward. It is a fluid, seamless motion, a ripple of muscle beneath the stone. I am climbing toward the neon rust-orange veins of lichen that stain the upper reaches of this spire. To me, this crust is not merely food; it is the only color in a universe of grey and black.

As I climb, I pass a niche where the stone has eroded into a hollow socket. Inside, tucked away like a forgotten umbrella, hangs a Silent Drifter. It is asleep, folded into itself, a pale skin of leather and membrane. I pause, my chiseling beak inches from its slumbering form. I sense the heat radiating from it, a tiny, desperate spark of life against the overwhelming cold. It does not wake. It does not fear me, for I am part of the stone it rests upon, and in this place, the stone is the only safety. I leave it to its dreams of aeroplankton and continue my ascent.

When I reach the lichen, I feed. It is not a graceful act. I scrape the beak of my face against the rock, a harsh, grinding noise that is swallowed instantly by the muffling mist. I am a participant in the erosion. I eat the mountain, and in doing so, I smooth it. I am the sculptor who consumes his masterpiece, millimeter by millimeter.

Hours later—perhaps days— the light shifts. The grey ribbon of sky above turns a bruised purple, then a faint, exhausted white. The sun has breached the cloud layer, but its warmth will not reach down here to the alleys. It only touches the tips of the spires, miles above.

I feel the change in pressure. The Drifters will launch soon. They will catch the thermals rising from the sun-heated peaks and spiral up into the emptiness where I cannot follow.

I do not envy them. The sky is vast and terrifyingly empty. I prefer the solid embrace of the wall.

I find a deep flute in the stone, a vertical runnel where the water trickles incessantly. It is perfectly shaped to accommodate my form. I retract my limbs, tucking my soft, vulnerable extremities beneath the shield of my calcite shell. I settle into the stone.

The Weaver-Mold in my shell hums softly, syncing with the mold in the rock. The water drips past my head: *plink. plink.*

I slow my heart. I let the cold seep into my joints, accepting it. I stop moving. The boundary between my shell and the limestone dissolves. I become the statue. I become the building block of this city that nobody built.

I am the silence that learned to breathe. I am the stone that learned to wait.
