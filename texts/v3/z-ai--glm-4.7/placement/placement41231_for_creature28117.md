I am found where the vertical grid of habitation frays into the abyss, at a specific juncture known only to the pipe-masters as "The Scab." It is a protrusion of the cliff face, a blister of basalt where three main conduits—rusted the color of dried blood—converge before plunging into the foggy void below. This is the lintel of the world, the lip of the mesa where the stone has been smoothed by aeons of wind into the texture of a polished knuckle.

I am the Keeper of the Valve.

I do not climb anymore. I have surrendered the agility of my youth to the calcification process that claims all of us who live long enough in the damp chill. My legs are fused now, knotted with arthritis that mimics the crystalline structure of the granite I cling to. They lock around the central pipe, a permanent clamp, a living horseshoe. My barrel chest, expanded by generations of thin air, heaves with a slow, rhythmic precision that matches the grinding of the flywheels deep within the mesa's core.

I am one of them, yet distinct. Where the others are still mobile, draped in layers of waxed canvas and felted wool, I have become part of the architecture. My clothes have rotted away, replaced by a thick pelt of grey-green moss that grows aggressively in the warmth of the steam leaking from my valve. It is a symbiotic infestation; the moss keeps me insulated against the wind that screams past my ears, and in return, the moisture from my skin feeds its bioluminescence. In the perpetual dim of the twilight noon, I glow with a faint, ghostly light, a beacon for the airships and the hawks that navigate the smog.

My face is a map of the geography itself—worn, smooth, and eroded. My cheekbones are ridges of flint; my nose, a flattened bridge of cartilage toughened by the cold. My eyes, large and dark, possess that reflective shine, but they have grown milky with cataracts, giving me the appearance of a blind statue. Yet, I am not blind. I see the heat signatures of the younger ones as they scuttle like spiders along the gangways, their silhouettes dissolving into the smog. I see the blue arcs of electricity snapping along the sagging cables.

My purpose is simple: I am the regulator. The great copper wheel set into the rock face before me is stiff, corroded by the "weeping" air. It requires a constant, subtle pressure to maintain the equilibrium of the steam flow. My left hand, swollen and knotted, rests eternally on the wheel’s rim. I do not turn it; I merely lean into it. My body acts as a counterweight. When the pressure builds deep underground, the pipe vibrates. The tremor runs through the rusted metal, up my spine, and into my teeth. I taste the metal before I hear the groan. In that moment, I shift my weight, pressing my chest into the wheel, easing the tension by a fraction of a degree.

I am a biological component of the machine. I eat the nutrient paste the young ones bring me—slurried from the rooftop mosses—and hunt the sleek, oversized rats that sometimes venture too close to the warmth of my valve. I catch them with a speed that belies my stillness, my hand snapping out like a steel trap. There is always a taste of iron in the meat, a tang of the earth, which I consume gladly. It strengthens my blood, making it heavy and slow, thickening me against the chill.

I am the Weirdness made manifest. To a traveler, I would look like nothing more than a pile of rags and stone, a glitch in the visual landscape. But if you were to look closely, you would see the rise and fall of my chest, in perfect sync with the pulse of the city. You would see the hawk, the color of slate, perching on my shoulder, using me as a roost because I am as still as the mountain itself.

I am content here. I am the static point in a world of vertical motion. The town may be a hive of density and industry, but here at the edge, where the distance between my window and the thousand-foot drop is measured in inches, there is only the hum of the pipe and the endless, drifting fog. I am the valve. I am the stone. I am the final form.
