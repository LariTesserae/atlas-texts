I find myself suspended, nearly eighty feet above the silent, petrified lakebed.

I am not standing; standing is a concept for creatures who trust the ground. I am clinging. My body is a wide, flattened disc of milky quartz, a convex shield against the harsh, shadowless glare that bleaches everything below. I am hot, but it is a passive heat—the heat of a stone left in an oven. I do not sweat; I condense. The humidity gathers on my smooth carapace like a veil of tears, heavy and slick.

I am a **Pale-Glass Worker**, a tendril of the great, silent federation that clings to the upper walls of the Sunken Grotto.

My world is not defined by shapes—I have no eyes to see them—but by textures and tremors. The ceiling of the grotto, veined with translucent quartz, presses down on me with the weight of its light. I feel it through the sensory strip that runs the length of my back, a nervous expanse of chitin that translates brilliance into a topography of pressure. The white light is a hum, a constant, high-frequency vibration that tells me I am exposed.

To my right, anchored firmly in a dimple of the eroded limestone, grows a solitary fern. It is not green; in this spectrum, it is a rigid sculpture of silver and glass. Its leaves are shards that reflect the ambient violence of the sun above, protecting the plant from cooking. I am currently mounted on the underside of its highest frond, my six legs locked in a geometry of tension.

I am inverted. The empty bowl of the floor is miles beneath me, a terrifying abyss of smooth gray ash. To fall is to die, shattering my shell against the unforgiving curve of the world. But I do not fear the fall; I do not think of it. I think of the drip.

I have been here for hours, perhaps days. Time is fluid. I am holding a **Serration**—a tiny, jagged flake of black flint—clamped tight in my upper right claw. The motion required is microscopic. I am not cutting the plant; I am guiding it. The leaf is curled slightly, imperfectly. The condensation runs down it like quicksilver, but it threatens to overspill, wasting the precious fluid into the dry air before I can catch it.

With the slowness of a drifting cloud, I adjust the angle of the flint. I scrape. *Shhhhh.* The sound is inaudible to all but my own leg-struts, which feel the friction travel up through the exoskeleton. I carve a microscopic channel, a diversion canal, into the waxy surface of the leaf.

I wait.

My sensorium flares. A change in pressure above. A single bead of moisture, heavy and swollen, has detached from the quartz ceiling a hundred feet up. I feel the displacement of the air as it falls, a ripple in the humidity. It is coming this way.

I adjust my body. I am a perfect mirror; I must tilt myself so that the drop, when it strikes the leaf above me, will follow my carved channel and dribble down into the waiting **Funnel** I have woven from dried leaves and resin. It is wedged into a crevice of the bark beside me.

The drop hits the canopy above. *Pling.*

The vibration shivers through the fern. My grip tightens. The water flows, obedient to the new path I have cut. It reaches the edge of the leaf and hangs there, glistening, a sphere of pure life. It elongates. It touches the lip of my funnel.

I release the breath I didn't know I was holding.

The fluid slides into the reservoir. Success.

I lower my head, the cone of my face extending toward the catch. My proboscis flickers out, tasting the mineral richness. It is cool, a shocking contrast to the greenhouse warmth of the air. I drink. I fill my internal bladder. This is not just water; it is currency. It is the energy required to maintain my grip on the vertical world.

As I drink, I feel a rhythmic tapping against the rock to my left.

Another Worker. It is approaching from a neighboring cluster of moss. I do not startle; starting is a waste of energy. I freeze, becoming nothing more than a lump of white stone. The newcomer climbs onto my frond, its weight shifting the balance of the fern. We are now suspended together over the void.

It reaches out a claw, flat and wide, and touches my lower leg.

We interlock.

A shiver of data passes through the connection. It is not language; it is a map. It tells me of a crack in the western wall where the humidity is high, but the access is difficult. It asks for my Wedge—the lever made of bone that I keep sheathed beneath my carapace.

I consider the request. I feel the emptiness of my internal reservoir; I am full. I can spare the time.

I disengage the claw and maneuver my body to access the tool slot on my underside. I withdraw the Wedge, a smooth, curved piece of ivory-colored wood, polished by years of use. I pass it to the other Worker.

Its claw takes the weight. It vibrates with gratitude—a low, thrumming frequency that feels like a purr against my shell.

Then, it is gone. It descends the fern, heading toward the western wall, carrying my tool to aid in the harvest. We may never meet again. Or we may trade tools again in a decade. It does not matter. The network persists.

I am alone again.

The silence returns, heavy and absolute. I readjust my grip on the leaf. The light presses against my back, a relentless, bleaching weight. I am blind, yet I see everything. I am a speck of glass clinging to the cathedral roof, an architect of droplets, a farmer of the slow.

I am a Maker. And I am exactly where I belong.
