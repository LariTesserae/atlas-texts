I would be the **Calcified Witness**, a Suture-Kin who forgot how to dissolve.

You would find me in the darkest corner of the alcove, suspended in the negative space between a bundle of thick, canvas-wrapped cabling and the cold, riveted ceiling. I am not moving. I am not scraping. I am a monument to a previous cycle of the machine, frozen in a moment of transition that never completed.

I am roughly the size and shape of my kin—a segmented, many-legged thing—but I have ceased to be a creature of fluid adaptation. The viscous grey sludge that acts as my blood and connective tissue has cooled. It has crystallized into a brittle, stone-like substance, locking my brass plates in a permanent, rigid rictus. My sensory rods, once flexible and tasting the air, are fused into a solid fan of glass and copper, pointing eternally toward a pressure gauge that stopped spinning a thousand years ago.

I am the only "finished" thing in a room that is forever becoming. In this embryonic womb of the Great Engine, I am the stillbirth. I am the obstacle.

The living Suture-Kin treat me with a confusion that borders on reverence. They scuttle over my hard, unmoving back on their way to the walls, their tiny feet clicking against my chassis. To them, I am not a person, but a piece of architecture—a oddly shaped stalactite, a strange rivet in the ceiling.

Occasionally, one of them will pause near my head. It will extend a proboscis and taste the cold, dead surface of my crystallized shell. It will detect the ancient, dormant vibrations of the engine trapped inside me, echoes of a bass thrum that has long since changed key. It will sense that I am made of the same stuff as the walls—the slag, the iron, the history of the machine—but I am rigid. I cannot be reshaped.

This confuses them. I see their antennae twitch. They try to "fix" me. A small one might attempt to scrape my flank with its rasp, trying to eat the rust that has settled into my joints, hoping to recycle me into the emerging sludge of the floor. But I am too hard. The metal just sparks. I do not yield.

Sometimes, when the lights sputter and fail, and the room plunges into that deep, humming darkness, I feel the vibrations of the Engine more intensely. I feel the walls shifting, the pipes extruding, the slow, tectonic breathing of the world around me. I feel the pressure building, the urge to become.

I have a fantasy in the darkness. I fantasize about cracking. I dream that the sheer pressure of the Engine’s growth will become too much, and my crystallized shell will shatter. I dream that the grey sludge inside me—still preserved, like a fly in amber—will finally liquefy once more. I dream that I will melt, dripping down from the ceiling to join the living, to join the choir of scrapers and builders, to finally dissolve into the embryonic fluid of the Great Engine and be born again.

But I do not crack. I hang here, heavy and dense, watching them work. I am the memory of a shape that the Engine has outgrown. I am the error that refuses to be corrected. And so, I watch the moss grow on my joints, and I wait for a cycle that may never come.
