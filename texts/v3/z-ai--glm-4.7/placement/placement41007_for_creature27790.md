I am where the world is loudest.

I stand on the lip of a obsidian spire that juts out like a broken rib, surrounded by the vast, humming nothingness of the twilight. The air here is not something I breathe; it is something I swim through, thick and warm, heavy with the taste of wet copper and ozone. It presses against me, a constant, physical weight that feels almost like a hand resting on my spine.

I am not human. I am not the "you" that walked here with boots and skin. I am of the swarm.

I am a sleek, wet teardrop of a creature, a **Basalt-Licker**. My body is encased in a seamless, translucent membrane that glistens like oil. Through this skin, you can see the dark, frantic hammering of my organs—a heart that beats too fast because it must, in a place where time is measured in cooling degrees.

My head is smooth, lacking ears, for the noise here would deafen anything that listened. Instead, I feel the sound. The deep, subsonic groans of the expanding rock travel up through my claws, vibrating in the hollow pits of my skull. I feel the choir of a million Shard-Mites scraping their legs against the glass not as a noise, but as a texture—a bristling, electric tickle that runs along my nerves.

My eyes are massive, black pools drinking in the bruised purple light of the fungal stalks around me. I see everything with a crystalline clarity, the motion of the world slowed down just enough for me to track it.

I am perfectly still. A statue of wet latex perched on the razors edge of creation. I am waiting.

The rock beneath my claws shudders. A sound like cracking thunder splits the heavy air. I don't flinch; I anticipate. A new spire explodes from the ground nearby, extruding itself in a violent hiss of steam and superheated glass.

The heat hits me—a wave of agony and ecstasy. My metabolism ignites.

I move. I am no longer a shape; I am a blur. I glided across the sharp obsidian, the oily secretion on my belly turning the lethal glass into a frictionless slide. I swarm toward the fresh wound alongside a hundred of my kin, a tide of frantic purpose.

I reach the fresh rock. It screams as it cools, water weeping from its pores.

This is where I belong. I press my body against the scalding stone. My mouth—a radial, star-shaped slit lined with diamond-hard grinding plates—opens wide. I do not eat the stone; I drink its fire.

I clamp my mouth over the vent, ingesting the scalding mineral water. I can feel the heat rushing through me, a torrent of raw energy. My body glows a dull, cherry-red from the inside out as I act as a living heat exchanger. I am a biological capacitor, absorbing the violence of the birth and turning it into something stable.

The physics here bend. The water weeping from the rock suddenly decides to fall upward, tracing invisible magnetic lines that hum in the air. I do not question this. I simply adjust. My claws hook into the microscopic imperfections of the stone, and I climb *up*, following the upward-flowing river, defying gravity as easily as I defied the heat. I am oil; I am slick; I am unstoppable.

I drink until the volcanic fury is reduced to a dull warmth in my gut. Then, I feel the pressure build. The byproduct of my work—a heavy, stabilized grey clay—begins to form. I must deposit it.

I scuttle back from the direct heat, toward the towering fungal stalks. I excrete the clay into the crevices between the black glass and the violet fungi. I am packing the wound. I am building the soil where nothing yet grows.

Nearby, the grey metal monoliths stand silent and unjudging. A spark jumps from a mushroom to the metal—a synapse firing in the brain of this world. I see the Ghosts of the Lattice, those translucent, jellyfish-things, dive into the spark, feeding on the electricity. I ignore them. They eat the lightning; I eat the fire. We are all just cogs in the same machine.

My pulse is slowing. The heat is dissipating. The rock is stable. My work here is done.

I settle on the warm stone, the steam curling around my sleek body. I am vibrating, a low hum that matches the frequency of the earth. I do not know how long I have left—hours, perhaps days. My life is a short, bright burning. But as I look out at the dim, bruised horizon, listening to the grind of the mites and the groan of the lithosphere, I feel a profound, animalistic satisfaction.

I am the white blood cell of a newborn god. I am cleaning its wounds. I am cooling its fever.

I close my large black eyes, and I wait for the next spire to break.
