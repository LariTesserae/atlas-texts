I am perched on the outer rim of the High Gantry, a structure that feels less like a building and more like a scab formed of fungal timber and bronze bracing. Here, the air is thin and cold, tasting of iron and ozone. I am a Gear-Binder, specifically assigned to the Tension-Winch that controls the ascent of the stone-buckets from the unstable floor below.

My body is a compact anchor in this vertical world. My skin, the color of wet slate, is numbed by the perpetual damp, a sensation that has long since faded into a background hum. I feel the moisture gathering in the ridges of my calloused fingertips, the same fingertips that are currently pressed flat against the immense, pitted flank of the main gear. I am not merely watching the machine; I am reading it. Through the pads of my palms, I sense the torsional stress in the aged wood, the microscopic grinding of the bronze pins, and the slight, worrying wobble that indicates the foundation has shifted yet again.

I am wearing the weighted bands—balance-rings of lead around my ankles and wrists. They are heavy, dragging at my limbs, but they are necessary. When the earth shudders—and it will, for the wound is still fresh—the rings act as a stabilizer, grounding me to the gantry when gravity tries to throw me into the orange void.

My eyes, large and dark, are fixed not on the gear, but on the thick, tar-slicked cable that disappears into the haze below. I do not blink often. To blink is to miss the micro-tremors that travel up the line like a whispered warning. Behind me, the great central flue roars with a rhythmic, heart-like *thump-thump*, casting long, dancing shadows that stretch across the wet decking. The light turns the mist into a swirling curtain of rust and amber.

To my left is my pair-silent, sturdy, and covered in the grime of animal fat and sawdust. We do not speak. To shout into the damp air would be an obscenity, a waste of breath that the heavy atmosphere would swallow anyway. Instead, we communicate through the tension of our shoulders and the angle of our stance. When I feel the cable slacken imperceptibly, I tap the bronze housing of the winch twice—*pause, tap*. My partner reacts instantly, shifting their weight onto the secondary lever. It is a ballet of friction, a silent negotiation with gravity.

I am not hungry, but I am aware of the faint, oily smell drifting from the coop woven into the lattice beneath our feet. It is the scent of the flightless birds, their stupid, soft clucking barely audible over the hiss of the gas flue. Their heat radiates upward through the floorboards, a ghostly warmth against the soles of my boots. Later, when the shift changes and the fire dims to a simmer, I will descend to the nesting levels to collect the eggs, checking the shells for fragility before trading them for a drink of filtered water.

Suddenly, the floor of the world drops.

It is not a violent quake, but a sudden, sickening lurch—a settling of the stone deep in the earth’s womb. The gas flue flares bright, and the cable sings a low, mournful note.

I do not panic. Panic is for creatures who do not understand the stone. Instead, I lock my joints. I become part of the machinery. My knees bend to absorb the shock, my hands grip the gear until the knuckles whiten, and I hold my breath, letting the iron-rich air sit heavy in my lungs. My partner does the same. For ten long seconds, the world groans around us, dust sifting down like grey snow, coating our hair and shoulders.

Then, silence returns. The cable stops singing. The gear holds.

I exhale slowly, a puff of white mist in the orange light. I tap the housing once—a clear, sharp *ting*—signaling that the tension is stable. My partner relaxes, the muscles in their back unknotting beneath the rough fungal weave of their tunic.

We resume the work. I am the friction that holds the city together. I am the silence in the noise. I am a child of the fresh, cleaved dark, waiting for the rock to settle, knowing that when it finally does, my purpose will be finished—but not today. Today, the wound is still open, and I am here to bind it.
