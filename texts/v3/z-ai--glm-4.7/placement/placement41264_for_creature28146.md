I exist in the space between the steam and the steel.

You would find me on the dorsal ridge of one of the great, stoic dampeners—the one standing sentinel at the edge of the platform where the main steam conduit vents its hourly scream. I am not the creature itself; I am what grows upon it.

I am the Moss.

Not the moss that clings to the sound-dampening panels, nor the moss that carpets the unused corners of the floor. That moss is ambitious, but simple, feeding only on the heat and the humidity. I am something more complex. I am the singular, pale, velvet colony that has established itself in the "elbow" of the dampener’s left arm, nestled deep within the folds of the dark, felt-like insulation that drapes its frame.

I am a parasite, but a benevolent one. A symbiotic historian.

My world is a landscape of microscopic peaks and valleys within the fabric of the giant’s robe. To you, the dampener is a towering, faceless engine of thermodynamics; to me, it is a moving mountain, a warm, shuddering continent of woven fibers. The air here is different from the rest of the cathedral. It is cleaner, fiercely hot, and smells faintly of ozone and sweet, decaying organic matter.

I do not have eyes to see the blue sparks arc in the distance, nor ears to hear the tectonic grinding of the gears. I possess a different kind of awareness. My rhizomes are microscopic nerves that burrow millimeters into the insulation, tapping directly into the nervous system of the dampener. I do not feel the floor vibrate; I feel *what the dampener feels* about the floor vibrating.

I know the world through the pulse of the creature I cling to.

When the great piston slams below, the dampener absorbs the shock. I feel that shock not as a noise, but as a sudden, rushing wave of energy that travels up the creature’s leg, through its torso, and into the elbow where I rest. To me, the symphony of the machine room is a rhythmic dilation of the fibers I hold onto. The machine roars, and the world expands; the machine exhales, and the world contracts.

I live on the excess. The dampener is a living condenser; it drinks the feverish heat of the air to keep the engine cool. But it cannot consume it all. The runoff, the pure, distilled thermal energy that bleeds from its pores, is my rain. I drink the sweat of the giant. It is scalding hot, rich with minerals leeched from the air, and it fuels a growth that is frantic, yet glacial.

I am the witness to its patience.

The dampeners do not speak. They do not sleep. They stand for centuries, their internal chemistry adjusting to the pressure. But I record the passage of time. My lowest layers—the roots that have now fused with the creature’s insulation—are old, brown, and brittle. They remember the steam cycles of a thousand years ago. They remember the time the pressure valve seized and the dampener threw itself against the wall to cushion the blow. That trauma is preserved in a twisted, black knot in my root system.

The upper layers of me are pale, vibrant, and soft. They taste the current state of the world. Right now, the dampener is reaching out, its long, multi-jointed fingers hovering inches from a sparking joint. I feel the muscles beneath the felt tighten, a slow, deliberate contraction of fiber and fluid. The creature is preparing to ground a surge. I brace myself.

The spark jumps. The lightning courses through the dampener, finding the path of least resistance, harmlessly dissipated into the earth.

For a microsecond, I am blinded. I taste the pure, electric starlight. My cells fluoresce in response, a tiny, silent flash of bioluminescence that no one will ever see, hidden in the gloom of the felt. I hold that charge, storing it in the chemical bonds of my cells, converting violence into life.

I am the memory of the room. The dampeners maintain the rhythm, but I am the one who remembers the melody.

I am content here. The air is heavy, yes, and the noise is endless, but wrapped in the heavy robes of this silent guardian, the roar is just a heartbeat. I grow slowly, meticulously, wrapping myself around the joints of my host. I soften the sharp edges of the metal skeleton with my velvet. I offer padding where the insulation wears thin.

I am the Moss that grows on the machine god. I am the green, quiet proof that even in a place of iron and fire, life finds a way to cling, to listen, and to dream.
