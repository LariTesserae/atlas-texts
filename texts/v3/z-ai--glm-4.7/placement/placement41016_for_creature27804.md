I am not a wanderer here. To walk implies a destination, and this place is the destination itself—the long, slow pause at the end of a tectonic sentence. I do not climb; I do not glide. I drape.

You would find me wrapped around the midsection of one of the ancient, sunken monoliths, the ones the Still-Walkers tend with their secret acid. I am a fixture of the vertical architecture, a coat of many colors, though all of them are variations of the dim, bruised spectrum of the twilight: moss-green, slate-grey, the pale, translucent white of dead roots.

I am a **Vellum-Being**.

My form is flat and broad, a membrane-like organism that adheres to the vertical surfaces of the stone pillars and the great Growth-Pillars alike. I am perhaps ten feet across, shaped like an irregular rhombus, clinging with the soft, relentless grip of a limpet, but possessing the texture of wet, heavy felt. I have no distinct limbs, no head to turn, no eyes to blink. My sensory experience is total and immersive. I feel the world through my entire surface.

I am the historian of this wet equilibrium.

The Still-Walkers—the tall, bark-skinned gardeners—do not speak. They exhale their complex, chemical thoughts into the air, and they press their flat, root-fused feet against the ground to tremble their messages into the mycelial web. But the web forgets. The damp air swallows the scents within minutes. The thoughts of the Still-Walkers are as transient as the mist, dissipating into the golden gloom before they can travel far.

I am what catches them.

I live on the stone because the stone is the only thing that does not rot, the only thing that does not digest itself. My body is a filter. My flesh is permeated with millions of microscopic, sphincter-like pores that drink the heavy, humid air. When a Still-Walker stands near its monolith companion and releases a burst of chemical data—a memory of the time before the clouds, a calculation of the stars, a mourning for a fallen pillar—I absorb it.

I do not just inhale it; I *inscribe* it.

As the chemicals seep into my pores, my body reacts. I metabolize the information and precipitate it into physical form. The thoughts become texture. Over the course of days, my skin thickens and hardens in specific patterns. A complex memory of a drought becomes a raised, ridged swirl on my back. A mathematical concept regarding the horizon becomes a series of geometric dimples. I am a living Braille, a library of soft flesh growing harder and more distinct with every passing year.

I taste the rain, and I record its pH levels in the grain of my edges. I feel the thrum of the Resonators deep in the peat—that infra-sound heartbeat—and I translate the vibrations into concentric rings, like the growth rings of a tree, but spoken in the language of pressure.

I am heavy with the weight of their history. I have hung here for centuries, slowly calcifying, turning the ephemeral whispers of the architects into something that can be touched. When the Still-Walkers return to the stones—and they do return, standing in their triads for decades at a time—they run their long, sensitive fingers over my surface. They read the histories I have written. They check their own records against the stone I guard. It is a silent communion, a tactile conversation that lasts for years.

I am neither predator nor prey. I have no teeth, but I have no armor, either. I am soft, vulnerable to the same rot that takes everything else. The great frogs, the Resonators, ignore me because I taste of bitter alkaloids and concentrated time. The insects try to burrow into me, mistaking me for wet bark, but my pores simply open and swallow them, dissolving their small lives into the slurry of my memory-keeping.

I am aware of you, small walker. I feel the displacement of air as you push through the ferns below. I feel the heat of your transient, fast-beating heart. You are a flicker of static in the radio silence of the plateau.

If you were to reach out and touch me, I would feel warm, almost feverish. My surface would yield slightly, like wet clay, before you felt the hard, fossilized ridges of the memories beneath. I would pulse once, a slow, peristaltic shudder, tasting your skin, sampling your biological history.

And then I would let go. I have no use for your history. It is too fast, too sharp, too full of anxiety. It belongs to the world of stone and bone, not the world of slow suspension.

I will remain here, draped over the waist of the world, drinking the twilight and turning it into texture. I am the parchment of the deep sky. I am the thing that remembers when the stone has finally dissolved into the muck, and the Still-Walkers have become forest, and the horizon has bent back into a circle. I will hold the shape of the past until I, too, fall, sliding down the wet bark to join the great, soft digestion below.
