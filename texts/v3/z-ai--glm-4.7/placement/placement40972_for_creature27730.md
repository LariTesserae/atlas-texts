I am suspended in the warm, golden amniotic fluid of the shaft, far from the ceiling-light and deep above the floor-grid. I am not a swimmer, for there is nothing here to swim away from, nor anything to swim toward. I am a fixture, a living housing, a symbiotic sleeve wrapped tight around the iron spine of the world.

I find myself wrapped around the central drive piston—the massive, townhouse-sized column of black iron that throbs silently in the center of the Conservatory. It is the heart of the place, pushing the nutrient broth up and pulling it down in a rhythm slower than the tides, slower than the beating of a human heart, a deep, seismic *thrum* that vibrates through the water and rattles the very fibers of my being.

I am the **Piston-Sheath**.

I am not one of the delicate Architect-Vines that cling to the outer brass lattices; I am too dense, too heavy for that. My flesh is a thick, muscular sponge, dark as the water is deep, textured like the velvet skin of a stingray but with the tensile strength of a suspension cable. I exist in the gloom between the moving shaft and the stationary brass walls, occupying the narrow, deadly gap where the machinery devours its own motion.

My purpose is friction.

The great iron piston above me does not slide gracefully on its own; it is a beast of brute force. Were it left to the cold mathematics of physics, it would grind, shatter, and seize. That is where I come in. I grip the iron shaft with millions of microscopic, cilia-like rootlets. I hug it with a pressure that is fierce, possessive, and unyielding. As the piston begins its slow, agonizing ascent—taking hours to travel a mere few feet—I stretch with it. My body elongates, pulling taut like a rubber band, storing the kinetic energy in my cellular walls.

Then, the pause. The terrifying stillness at the apex of the stroke.

In that silence, I contract. I release my grip, micro-millimeter by micro-millimeter, sliding down the iron column, smoothing the metal with my own secretions—a slick, oily mucous that tastes of copper and sugar. I am the lubricant. I am the bearing. I am the living buffer between the rigid geometry of the machine and the relentless persistence of biology.

I am alone in my task. The Architect-Vines cannot reach me here; the currents are too violent, the pressure too great. The Drifter-Palms avoid the center, frightened by the looming shadow of the machinery. Even the light cannot touch me here. I live in the vibration, feeling the pulse of the Hydro-Conservatory not as a sound, but as a physical blow against my skin, a rhythmic massage that drives my sap.

I have no eyes, for there is nothing to see but darkness and iron. But I possess a hypersensitivity to *tension*. I can feel a flaw in the piston long before it manifests. I can sense the microscopic stress fracture in the metal, a hairline crack that no engineer could see, running like a scar beneath the smooth, cold surface. When I feel such a weakness, I grow into it. I send probing tendrils into the fissure, reinforcing the metal with my own fibers, knitting the wound shut with biological glue. I repair the god that built me.

I am the silent partner in the engine's breath. I am the reason the water remains sweet and clear, preventing the grinding of metal-on-metal that would foul the broth with rust and particulates.

I am the **Sheath**. I am the dark embrace of the machine. I am the thing that endures the friction so that the rest of the garden may float in ignorant, pastoral peace. And as the piston begins its slow descent, pushing the water down toward the roots below, I sigh, a silent exhalation of bubbles, and tighten my grip, ready to take the weight of the world again.
