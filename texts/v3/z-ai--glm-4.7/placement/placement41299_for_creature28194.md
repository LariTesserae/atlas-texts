I would not be found in the shanties that cling to the verticality of the pistons, nor in the lowest deeps where the Keepers hold their silent vigil over the holy water. I exist in the negative space between—a niche carved into the "Elbow" of a great, hydraulic arm that stands frozen in a permanent gesture of lifting.

I am a **Silt-Stalker**, but not one of the Scavengers who flicker with restless youth, nor a Keeper who has succumbed to the meditative paralysis of age. I am an **Echo-Tuner**.

My skin is the color of wet slate, smooth and impermeable, slick with the sheen of the constant damp. I am dressed in scavenged canvas, stitched tight with copper wire to minimize drag against the wet iron. I am suspended in a harness of my own making, woven from the shed wings of dead Wraith-Weavers, anchored to the rust-encrusted elbow of the machine.

From this vantage point, I am level with the gliding paths of the avian mechanics, but I do not move. I do not scuttle. I drape.

My purpose is singular: to listen to the iron, not with my ears, but with the hollow cavities of my bones.

In the Drip-Quarter, silence is not empty; it is heavy with information. The machine is not dead; it is dreaming a dream that lasts centuries. My body acts as a living sounding board. When a Rust-Mite disturbs the sludge three decks below, the vibration travels up the pipe, through my boots, and hums in my marrow. When the temperature shifts in the ventilation shafts, the expansion of the metal sings a low note against my spine.

I spend my cycles—my "days"—tuning the resonance of the Quarter. The community of Stalkers relies on a shared frequency, a low, thrumming vibration that passes through the gratings to convey complex messages regarding the sludge levels or the migration of the Weavers. If the frequency is off—if a rust-buckled pipe creates a dissonant hum—the message garbles. Panic can ensue. Energy can be wasted.

So, I wait.

I position my hands against the cold flank of the hydraulic piston. I feel the texture of the conglomerate slag, rough and ancient. Through my fingertips, I sense the microscopic grinding of tectonic plates shifting against the outer hull of the world. I feel the infinitesimal expansion of the metal as a reaction from the surface world sends a shiver down through the miles of rock.

A Weaver descends from the dark above, its canvas wings catching the oily air with a soft *hiss*. It lands on a conduit nearby, steam puffing from its joints. I do not turn my head. I merely shift my weight, allowing the harness to sway. The Weaver watches me with unblinking, lens-like eyes. It senses that I am part of the furniture, part of the machine’s pause.

In return, I offer a vibration. I engage the secondary sack in my throat and emit a low, rolling thrum that travels down the arm of the piston and into the puddle below. It is a reassurance: *The pressure holds. The cycle is true.*

The Weaver accepts this. It releases a plume of warm steam that washes over my face. I taste the ozone—a sharp, clean tang that cuts through the wet dust. It is my sustenance for this shift, a small gift from the sky of the deep.

To look at me, an outsider would see a gaunt, spindly creature sleeping in the rust. But inside, I am counting. I am tracking the imperceptible shift of the great turbine in the center of the room. I know that in three years, the blade will align with the intake grate, causing a resonance that will shatter the brittle glass of the lucifer-panels unless we dampen the floor with specific sludge.

I am the clock-watcher of the geological clock.

When the dampness becomes too heavy, seeping into the joints of my limbs, I will flex my fingers, feeling the cool oil on my skin. I will remember that I am not iron. I am soft. I am temporary. But for now, I am the nerve ending of this great, rusted lung.

I close my eyes, or what serve as eyes—black voids that see only heat—and I dissolve into the vibration. I become the pause. I become the metal, waiting for the turn of the world.
