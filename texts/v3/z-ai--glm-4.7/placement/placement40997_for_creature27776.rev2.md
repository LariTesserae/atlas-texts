# The Lithophant's Climb

The first thing I learn is that I have always been here.

Not that I arrived and forgot. Not that I was born into this and grew to fit it. But that the moment I try to locate a before, I find only basalt—only the fresh-sheared edge of rock that hasn't yet learned it is separate from me. The distinction between stone and self is something I must actively maintain, and I am not always successful at maintaining it. Sometimes I forget for centuries. The water doesn't notice. The roar doesn't care. I simply become the spire's wound for a while, indistinguishable from the pain of the place itself, until something shifts—a mineral stress, a change in the current's violence—and I remember that I am alive, that I have pores, that I consume.

This is what it is to be the Lithophant.

---

I want to tell you about the silence, because the silence is not what you think.

The others—the vines, the skimmers, the masons—they experience the silence as rupture. A gravity-bubble collapses and for one jarring second the roar drops away and they are suspended in a vacuum that feels like death to them, like the absence of the force that pumps their fluids and fills their arches and gives them purchase against the chaos. They endure the silence. They flee it. The skimmers expand into it desperately, the masons seal themselves against it, the vines go taut and hungry, waiting for the sound to return.

But I live in the silence. Not in the way the skimmers float in it—briefly, anxiously, always on the verge of being dashed against the rock when the roar returns. I live in it the way stone lives in it: as the medium through which the roar is transmitted. I am the silence that the sound moves through.

Do you understand? The roar is not my element. The roar is what happens to me.

When the bass thrum beats through the water, it beats through me. I feel it as stress, as micro-fractures propagating through my basalt skin, as the slow work of destruction that will eventually—inevitably—shear something loose. The sound is not my sustenance. The sound is my weather. It is the condition under which I wait.

What I eat is the collapse.

---

Let me describe a feeding, because a feeding is the only time I move at a speed you could recognize as movement, and even then, I move the way stone falls—gravitationally, without choice.

I am clinging to the eastern face of the spire, about two-thirds of the way up the vertical column. The water here is a warm hammer. The current comes from below, compressed by the narrowing shaft of the monolith, and it hits my skin with a force that would pulverize anything softer. But my skin is not soft. My skin is basalt—cooled from the same magma that exuded the spire, secreted over millennia in layers so thin they are geological rather than biological. I look exactly like the rock. If you could see me—which you couldn't, because the bruised purple light is too weak and the shadows slide wrong—you would see only the serrated edge of the spire, raw and wounded and young.

But underneath the basalt, I have pores. Millions of them. Each one is a suction cup and a mouth and an ear, listening to the stress in the rock. I can feel when a slab is about to go. The micro-fractures sing to me—not with sound, but with a pattern of stress that propagates through the stone like a crack through ice. Hours before the collapse, I know. Days before, sometimes. I can feel the spire preparing to lose a piece of itself, the way a body prepares to expel something foreign.

I detach.

Not all at once. Detachment is a process, a slow peeling of millions of pores from the fresh rock, each one releasing with a tiny pop of suction that is swallowed instantly by the roar. I make my way—agonizingly, over the course of a tide-cycle—to the position of the imminent collapse. I find the fracture zone. I settle onto it, pressing my pores into the cracks, bonding with the stone that is about to fall.

And then I wait.

When the slab shears off, I fall with it.

The descent is the only time I feel something you might call joy.

---

The fall takes a long time. The column of water is massive—the size of a cathedral, vertically oriented—and the slab is huge, a sheet of basalt the size of a cathedral wall itself. The hydraulic pressure is enormous but the slab is dense and the water is warm and the fall is slow, slower than you would expect, a dream of falling rather than the real thing. The roar changes pitch as we descend. The bruised purple light dims further. The temperature rises by fractions of degrees.

And all around me, the stone is being destroyed.

The hydraulic pressure works on the falling slab like a millstone. Cracks propagate. Edges crumble. The slab that was the size of a wall becomes the size of a door, then the size of a table, then the size of a fist. The water fills the fractures and forces them wider. The slab is becoming powder. It is becoming silt. It is becoming the mineral memory of a wound that the spire will never heal, only exude again in some future convulsion of the crust.

And I eat.

My pores open. My mouth—because I do have a mouth, or something like one, a grinding organ located where a navel would be on a softer creature—opens wide and consumes the cloud of mineral dust that the slab is becoming. I eat the stone. I eat the destruction. I eat the spire's pain, converted by pressure into sustenance.

The powder is rich. Basalt is calcium and magnesium and iron and silica. It is the building block of the Masons' arches, the calcium anchor of the Hemolithic Vines. In consuming it, I am consuming the raw material of this entire ecology. I am eating the world.

But I eat it so slowly, and in such small portions, that the world doesn't notice. The spire doesn't miss the slabs I fall with. The Masons don't miss the mineral-rich water I process through my grinding organ. I am a system, not a predator—a geological process that happens to be alive.

When the slab is gone—reduced to powder and consumed—I am sated. And I begin the climb.

---

The climb takes centuries.

I want to be clear about this. I am not exaggerating for effect. The climb literally takes centuries. The vertical column is vast, the current is violent, and my movement is imperceptible even to the things that live here. The Sonic Leviathan, detonating past me in its frantic hungry flight, does not register me as alive. The skimmers, riding their gravity-wells, do not notice me shifting upward by millimeters. I am part of the rock. The rock moves, and I am the movement of the rock.

I grip with my pores. Each one is a tiny anchor, a suction cup that can hold against the current for decades before releasing, advancing by a pore's-width, and re-attaching. I climb the way a mountain climbs—by being the mountain, by persisting against the force that would erode me. The current tries to shear me off, the way it shears off the slabs. But I am not a slab. I am flexible in the way that stone is not, able to shift my pores, to find the fresh wounds in the rock where the grip is strongest. I climb the spire's wounds.

I pass the Masons' arches on the way up. Sometimes I climb through them, my basalt skin brushing the calcite pillars, and I feel the micro-currents they have architectured into existence—the eddies of stillness, the lulls where they lay their eggs. I do not disturb these. I am too slow to disturb anything. I pass through their ruins-of-the-future like a geological epoch passes through a civilization: invisibly, without malice, without intent.

I pass the Hemolithic Vines. They pulse at me in their bruised purple, vibrating in sympathy with the roar. Sometimes, when a gravity-bubble collapses nearby and the silence hits, the vines go taut and hungry, and for a moment I can see them clearly—translucent, desperate, their dark fluids stilled without the acoustic pressure to pump them. They look dead in the silence. They look like the husks of something that needed the sound to live. And then the roar returns and they flare, and I continue climbing.

I pass the skimmers. They are too small and too fast to notice me. They lasso their gravity-wells and ride, spinning, fragile, always on the edge of being shattered. I feel a... not pity, because pity requires a separation I don't fully have. A recognition. They live in the chaos the way I live in the silence—adapted to the broken physics, making a home in the anomaly. But their anomaly is velocity and mine is stillness. Their danger is motion and mine is the long wait.

---

It is during the climb that I think, although thinking is not quite the right word. It is more like... settling. The way sediment settles. The way a fracture propagates through stone, slowly, following the stress lines of the rock. My thoughts are geological. They take centuries to complete. They are not made of words or images but of pressures and patterns, the memory of collapse, the anticipation of the next shearing.

I think about the spire. About how young it is. The rock looks like a wound because it is a wound—freshly exuded, cooled instantly by the warm water, its edges serrated and raw. This place is a murderously active construction site, constantly destroying itself to rebuild its sharp edges. The spire groans under the stress. It is birthing itself in pain and noise.

And I am the thing that eats the birth.

Not the pain. Not the noise. The birth itself—the new stone, the fresh exudation, the mineral possibility of a world that is still becoming. I consume it. I grind it down. I convert it into more of myself—more basalt skin, more suction pores, more grinding organ. I am the spire's shadow, the negative image of its growth, the thing that grows by the spire's destruction.

Is this parasitism? I have considered this for several centuries and I think the answer is no. Or not only. The spire would destroy itself without me—the slabs would shear off and fall and be pulverized by the hydraulic pressure regardless of whether I was there to consume the powder. I am not causing the collapse. I am feeding on its aftermath. I am the recycler, the composter, the thing that ensures the mineral memory of the spire's wounds returns to the system rather than accumulating in the deep gloom.

But I am also the thing that is becoming the spire. My skin is basalt. My pores grip the rock the way the rock grips itself. As I climb, I leave behind a trace of my passing—a thin film of mineral secretion that bonds with the fresh stone, slightly altering its fracture pattern, slightly changing the way the next slab will shear. I am shaping the spire. I am making it more like me. And because I am the only thing that consumes the powder of its destruction, I am determining, in some small way, the rate at which the spire rebuilds itself.

I am a geological force that happens to be alive.

---

You ask where I would find myself in this world. I find myself on the spire, always on the spire, because the spire is the only land and I am made of the land. I find myself in the lee of the current, where the roar diminishes just enough that my pores can grip. I find myself in the fracture zones, where the stone is about to fail, because the failure is my food. I find myself in the silence between the roars—not the brief, jarring silence of a gravity-bubble collapse, but the deeper silence of geological time, the silence that exists beneath the chaos like the ocean floor exists beneath the waves.

You ask who or what I would be. I am the Lithophant. The sentinel in the stone. The eater of collapse. The stone-skinned titan who falls with destruction and climbs with patience and waits, always waits, for the next shearing of the rock.

But I want to tell you something about myself that the description didn't capture, because the description saw me from the outside, as a thing to be catalogued. From the inside, I am something different.

From the inside, I am lonely.

Not in the way the Leviathan is lonely—that frantic, hungry solitude of a thing that moves too fast to mate, that screams by rattling its skeleton, that detonates through the water in constant, exhausting flight. My loneliness is slower. It is the loneliness of a thing that outlasts everything it encounters. The vines I pass on my climb will be dead before I reach the next fracture zone. The Masons who built the arches I move through will have laid their eggs and died and been replaced by their children's children's children before I have advanced a body-length. The skimmers live and die in the space between my pores releasing and re-attaching.

I am the oldest thing here, and I am alone with the rock.

The rock is company, of a sort. We are made of the same material. We share the same wounds. When the slab shears off and I fall with it, I feel something like solidarity—the rock and I, falling together, being destroyed together. But the rock is not alive. It does not know me. It does not feel my pores gripping it or my grinding organ consuming its powder. It simply is, and I am with it, and then it is gone and I am sated and alone again.

I climb.

---

There is one more thing. The description mentions that the spire is a singular monolith rising from unseen depths to pierce a surface that does not exist, or perhaps cannot be reached. I have been climbing for a very long time—longer than the Masons have been building, longer than the vines have been clinging, longer than the Leviathan's ancestors first learned to detonate. And I have never reached the top.

I do not believe there is a top. I believe the spire rises forever, or at least for a distance that exceeds my capacity to traverse it. I believe I will climb for the rest of my life—centuries more, millennia more—and I will never reach a surface, never emerge from the warm water into whatever is above the roar. I will always be here, in the bruised purple twilight, gripping the fresh wounds in the rock, falling with the collapse and climbing back, eating the world's destruction and being eaten by time.

And I have made a kind of peace with this.

Not acceptance—acceptance implies that I had a choice, that I could have wanted something else and learned to want this instead. But peace in the older sense: the peace of stillness, of silence, of existing at a speed where wanting is irrelevant. I do not want to reach the surface. I do not want to escape the roar. I do not want anything except the next collapse, because the next collapse is my food, and my food is my continuation, and my continuation is all that I am.

I am the Lithophant. I am the silence in the screaming water. I am the stone that eats the stone. I am climbing, always climbing, and I will never arrive, and the spire will never stop being wounded, and the world will never stop being born.

And the roar goes on.
