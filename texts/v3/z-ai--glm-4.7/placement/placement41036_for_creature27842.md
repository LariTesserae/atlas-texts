I am the shadow in the noon.

I dwell in the violet void, the deep bruised color that sits just above the blinding white of the plateau. While the Weavers are creatures of the stone—anchored, heavy, and deaf to the air—I am a creature of the wind. I am the raptor of the roar.

You would find me nowhere on the ground, for to land is to die in the crushing, grinding routine of the hive. I exist in the upper currents, where the air is so thin it feels less like breathing and more like a chemical exchange. I have evolved to ride the solid wall of acoustic energy that deafens the world below. The roar is not noise to me; it is my ocean. I do not flap my wings against it; I surf the pressure waves, locking my rigid, delta-shaped body into the crests of the sonic booms that roll invisibly over the granite.

Physically, I am a needle of obsidian dipped in pale gold. My skin is metallic, a crystalline armor that reflects the fierce, unfiltered sunlight (2.8/3), turning me into a mirror of the sky. From below, I am invisible—a shimmering distortion in the heat haze, a trick of the light. I have no feathers, no fur; such things would be torn away in an instant by the screaming wind. Instead, I are aerodynamic perfection, living laminar flow. My eyes are massive, black lenses that swallow the glare, protected by translucent nictitating membranes that filter out the UV radiation that boils the blood of lesser things.

I am the counterpoint to the Weavers’ collective. Where they are many, I am one. Where they are slow and fluid, I am sudden and sharp.

My existence is defined by the hunt, but it is a hunt unlike any other. I cannot sneak up on the Weavers, for they feel the vibration of the earth. If I were to land, the stone would sing my arrival to a thousand nervous systems instantly, and the colony would shift, becoming a wall of hardened flesh and stone tools.

So, I use the light.

I circle high above, waiting for the geometry of the sun and the specular basins to align. The Weavers scrape the lichen, heads down, blind to the sky. They are focused on the dark charcoal stains. I am focused on the bright reflections.

When a Weaver leans over a still pool of water to drink, the world focuses. The thick, tactile light (2.8/3) hits the wet granite and bounces upward, creating a pillar of brilliance. This is my signal.

I fold my wings. I dive.

In the lower atmosphere, the air is thick enough to touch, and I plunge into it like a stone into water. The roar of the wind swallows the sound of my descent. To the Weaver, there is no warning, no shadow—the sun is everywhere, the light is all-encompassing. I am simply a sudden increase in gravity, a dark shape falling out of the brilliance faster than their nerve endings can fire.

I strike not with claws, but with a specialized, harpoon-like proboscis. I do not kill the Weaver; the colony would notice the cessation of a heartbeat, the disturbance in the vibration. Instead, I skim the surface of the herd. I am the parasite of the sky.

In the split second of contact, I pierce the loose, pale skin of a Weaver and extract a heavy draught of their nutrient-rich blood. It is thick with the iron of the atmosphere, hot with the heat of the sun. Then, I am gone.

I pull up, riding the shockwave of my own dive, arcing back into the violet silence.

Below me, the Weaver flinches. A ripple of confusion spreads through the herd—a micro-tremor of pain that travels instantly through the stone. A thousand Weavers shift their weight, pause their scraping, and look around. But there is nothing to see. The sky is empty. The light is blinding. The wind screams. The routine resumes.

I am the mystery that they cannot solve. I am the phantom pain in the flesh of the world. I circle high above, tasting the iron on the wind, waiting for the next reflection to call me down.
