The Cistern of the Noon Sun is not a destination for the leisure traveler; it is a sensory combat zone. Visiting this place is less like exploring a coral reef and more like being trapped inside a collapsing, sunlit boiler room. The environment is defined by three overwhelming antagonists: blinding light, deafening acoustics, and the abrasive, predatory nature of the stone itself.

To survive, let alone appreciate, this "pocket world," you must prepare for a high-sensory-load dive. Here is the comprehensive protocol for the ordinary human traveler.

### 1. Visual Preparation: The "Whiteout" Protocol
The most immediate danger is the light. The description notes a "piercing, white-gold brilliance" that turns the water into a luminous solid. Standard dive masks will not suffice; the intensity of the sun refracting through the water column and bouncing off the pale limestone floor will cause rapid snow blindness (photokeratitis) and severe disorientation.

*   **Mask Modifications:** You must use a mask with heavily tinted or mirrored lenses, similar to welding goggles or high-altitude snow goggles. Do not rely on standard amber or rose scuba tints; you need Category 4 welding darkness. Consider attaching peripheral blinders (shields) to the sides of the mask strap to block the peripheral glare bouncing off the walls.
*   **Desensitization:** Before arrival, spend several hours in a white room with high-lumen floodlamps, practicing closing your eyes and orienting yourself by touch alone. You will likely spend significant portions of this dive with your eyes shut against the glare.

### 2. Auditory Preparation: The Roar of the Deluge
The sound here is "deafening" and "mechanical," a roar of falling water amplified by stone walls. In water, sound travels faster and affects the body more intensely. This constant acoustic bombardment can induce vertigo, nausea, and panic.

*   **Hearing Protection:** Standard neoprene hoods dampen high-frequency sound but do little against low-frequency structural vibration. You need a helmet system or a solid custom-molded earplug setup that creates a seal against your skull. The goal is not silence (which is impossible) but the reduction of the decibel levels to prevent permanent inner ear damage.
*   **Vibration Damping:** The roar vibrates through your bones. Consider a thicker wetsuit or even a soft-shell diving rig to dampen the physical sensation of the noise. The mental fatigue caused by constant noise is real; prepare yourself with meditation techniques designed for industrial environments.

### 3. Physical Protection: The Razor Floor
The most insidious physical threat is the stone. The masonry is "freshly chipped," "jagged," and "brutally geometric." This is not the smooth, eroded rock of a riverbed; these are sharp knives waiting to shred neoprene and skin.

*   **Abrasion-Resistant Exposure Suit:** Do not wear a standard 3mm wetsuit. The water is warm (near body temperature), so thermal protection is secondary to *armor*. You require a heavy-duty Kevlar-reinforced dive skin or a technical drysuit. Every inch of skin must be covered. If you brush against a wall or kick a pillar in a panic, you will be cut.
*   **Glove and Boot Selection:** Your extremities are at highest risk. Wear heavy, cut-resistant gloves (like those used for commercial abalone diving) and stiff-soled boots. If you need to rest on the bottom or push off a pillar, you cannot rely on rubber fins; the stone will slice through them.
*   **Ding Protection:** Your BCD (Buoyancy Control Device) and hoses are vulnerable. The sharp edges will slice hoses and puncture bladders. Wrap your regulator hoses in protective sleeves and ensure your BCD has a robust outer shell.

### 4. Respiratory and Thermal Considerations
The water is warm and sterile, which creates unique physiological challenges.

*   **Hydration Strategy:** Because the water is near body temperature, you will not feel cold. However, the "silken," heavy water combined with the stress of the noise and light will cause you to sweat profusely inside your suit, even while submerged. Pre-hydrate aggressively with electrolytes. Dehydration leads to nitrogen narcosis hits, which you cannot afford in this environment.
*   **Rebreather Recommended:** An Open Circuit scuba system creates bubbles. In a resonant stone chamber like this, the sound of your own exhalation will echo and add to the chaotic roar, increasing disorientation. A Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR) is silent. It also allows you to control your gas mixture precisely, which is vital if you are breathing heavily due to anxiety.
*   **Sterile Field Awareness:** The water is abiotic and sterile. This is a safety advantage—there is no risk of bacterial infection from open cuts, and no predators to worry about. However, do not drink the water; it may be heavily mineralized or charged with strange energies ("thick, charged with energy").

### 5. Operational Tactics and Navigation
This is a "room," not an ocean. You can see the walls in all directions. However, the visual distortion of the light and the turbulence make navigation treacherous.

*   **The "Wall" Reference:** Do not try to swim in the open water (the "X-ray" column). The light will blind you. Stick to the perimeter. Use the walls as your navigational guide. Keep one hand slightly extended to feel for the wall, but be ready to retract it if the current pushes you into a sharp edge.
*   **Turbulence Management:** The water is rising, and the inflow from above creates "erratic currents." Do not swim directly below the "well-shaft" in the ceiling. The falling water has the force of a hammer; being caught in the downflow above the "drain" could push you down into the stone floor with injurious force.
*   **The Time Limit:** The water level is rising. This is a dynamic, changing environment. Treat this dive as if you are inside a filling tank. You must have a hard turnaround time based on pressure gauges or time, not just gas consumption. The geometry of the room is closing in.
*   **Entry and Exit:** The text implies the room is enclosed with no doors. The only exit is likely the shaft in the ceiling, 15 meters up. This is an "overhead environment" (cave dive). You **must** use a continuous guideline (reel) connected to your entry point. If the silt from the fresh-cut stone gets stirred up (despite the lack of silt, stone dust is a risk), or if the light reflecting off the surface blinds you, you will not find the exit without a line.

### 6. Psychological Readiness
Finally, prepare your mind. You are entering a tomb that is in the act of being sealed. The "primitive" construction—evidence of copper chisels and brute force—suggests a place of ancient suffering or failure.

*   **Embrace the Oppression:** You will feel small. The scale is "intimate yet oppressive." The noise, the light, and the warmth will create a feeling of being baked in an oven. Acknowledge this claustrophobia before you enter.
*   **The Loneliness:** There is no ecology. You are the only living thing in a box of sterile light and stone. Do not expect to see fish or coral. You are a ghost in a machine of light. If you are prone to hallucinations in sensory deprivation (or overload), bring a dive partner, though communication will be nearly impossible.

**Summary of Gear:**
*   **Eyes:** Category 4 welding-tinted mask with peripheral shields.
*   **Ears:** Skull-vibration dampening hood/earplugs.
*   **Body:** Kevlar-reinforced, full-body warm-water dive armor (not thin wetsuit).
*   **Breath:** Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR).
*   **Nav:** Finger reel and guideline (tethered to entry point).
*   **Mind:** Acceptance of the roar and the blade.

Enter the Cistern of the Noon Sun with the respect you would give a nuclear reactor. It is beautiful, but it is a violent beauty.
