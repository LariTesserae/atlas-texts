Life here does not just inhabit the Iron Deck; it mimics it, mimics the metal, the heat, and the relentless, grinding patience of the machine. In a place where the organic and the industrial have merged into a single, sweating organism, the inhabitants are evolved to navigate a world that is simultaneously vertical, deafening, and wet.

At the microscopic level, the rust itself is alive. It is not merely oxidation, but a complex colony of iron-consuming bacteria and litho-mosses that digest the metal lattice, excreting a fine, orange dust that becomes the soil for the next generation. Feeding on this foundation are the **Rust-Ticks**, small, armored arachnids with bodies shaped like rivet heads. They scuttle in the millions across the flaked terrain, their legs designed to hook into the pitted metal. They are blind, the harsh glare of the sun being irrelevant to their existence in the shadows of the flaking steel. Instead, they navigate through the vibration, sensing the tremors of the deck through the soles of their feet, clustering around the warm spots where grease has seeped up through the cracks.

Above them, in the vertical space of the gantries and smokestacks, dwell the **Slick-Skinned Climbers**. These are the lizard-like creatures mentioned in the passing, but upon closer inspection, they are marvels of hydrodynamic engineering. They possess no claws; their grip is derived from the microscopic suction cups on their bellies and the oily secretion of their skin, allowing them to adhere to the sheer, wet walls of the machinery. They are neon-green and vibrant yellow, flashing warnings to each other amidst the grey iron. They bask on the warm brass vents, their scales spreading wide to catch the thermal updrafts. When the great steam vents hiss, they do not flee; they flatten themselves against the metal, letting the scalding vapor wash over them, killing the parasites that cling to their undersides. They eat the thicker algae mats that grow in the perpetual twilight of the deep maintenance shafts, their tongues rough enough to scrape the slime clean off a piston.

Sharing the vertical realm are the **Avian Mimics**. These are not birds in the traditional sense, but creatures of hollow bones and metallic plumage. Their feathers are modified keratin plates, shiny and chrome-like, reflecting the blinding sun to blind predators or signal mates. They nest in the high rafters, dangerously close to the massive, rotating fans. Evolution has hard-wired their brains to the rhythm of the *whump-whump-whump*; they dive through the blades in the split-second intervals of the blades' passing, a dance of fatal precision. They do not sing—the roar of the turbines would drown out a melody—instead, they communicate by clacking their beaks against the metal struts, a rhythmic percussion that carries through the vibration of the structure itself.

Descending to the floor of the deck, one encounters the grazers. These are the **Gear-Walkers**, massive, hulking beasts the size of bison, but with a physiology evolved for the unstable ground. Their hooves are wide, splayed discs of tough, rubbery tissue that conform to the shape of the grating, distributing their massive weight so they do not crash through the rust-weakened panels. They are covered in shaggy, damp fur that is constantly matted with grease and moss, acting as a natural insulation against the searing heat of the exposed pipes. They move in herds, slowly migrating across the regional expanse of the deck, following the seasonal weeping of the condensation pipes. They use their tusks, which are shaped like pry-bars, to rip open the casings of insulated wires to get at the nutritious, salty copper within, or to uproot clusters of nutrient-dense fungi growing in the shadows of the I-beams.

But the true masters of this environment, the ones for whom the distinction between builder and dweller has fully dissolved, are the **Resonants**.

They are humanoid, or perhaps they were once, but they have been shaped by eons of life on the deck. They are short and dense, their musculature designed for climbing and leverage rather than speed. Their skin is a pale, translucent grey, slick with a constant sheen of protective oil that their pores excrete to prevent chemical burns from the acidic air. They possess no outer ears; the noise here would shatter the auditory organs of a lesser creature. Instead, they hear through their bones. Their jawbones and skulls are directly connected to specialized vibration sensors in their inner ears, allowing them to "hear" the hum of the turbines as clearly as a whisper. They can diagnose a failing valve or a loose rivet three miles away by feeling the change in the deck’s frequency through the soles of their calloused feet.

The Resonants are a people of profound stillness. Because the machinery moves glacially, they have learned to move with it. They do not walk against the vibration; they synchronize their steps with the shudder of the deck. When they speak, they do not shout. They press their foreheads against the metal pillars or rest their hands on the railings, transmitting deep, thrumming vocalizations through the iron itself. This "metal-speak" allows them to communicate across the roar, carrying complex messages over miles of conduit.

Their society is nomadic, dictated by the "Cycle of the Turn." They live on the massive, slow-moving conveyor belts that crawl across the horizon. A family might build a temporary shelter of scrap metal and living vines on a moving platform, inhabiting it for weeks as it travels a mile, then dismantling it before the belt drops them into the smelters or the crushing gears at the end of the line. They harvest the aggressive vines, weaving the fibrous, steel-strength plant matter into armor and rope. They farm the moss, cultivating patches of it in the rust-hollows to harvest as a potent source of antibiotics.

They are the caretakers by necessity. When a steam vent threatens to rupture, the Resonants are there, tightening the bolts with massive, muscle-wrenching wrenches, their bodies absorbing the heat. When a bird is grounded by a broken wing, they may carry it to the updrafts of the fans to give it flight again. They do not worship the machine, nor do they fear it. They are simply a component of it, the biological grease that keeps the Iron Deck from seizing up, living in the spaces between the gears, waiting in the warm, wet dark, forever listening to the song of the iron.
