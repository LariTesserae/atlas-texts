At first, you do not see them. In this dim, bruised twilight where the air is thick with spray and the roar of the falling water drowns out the mind, vision is a secondary sense. To see a resident of the Ossuary, you must first learn to stop looking for movement and start looking for *stillness*—a stillness that feels wrong, a density in the mist that has weight and intention.

The primary inhabitants of this place are the **Lithic-Drifters**.

They are massive, low-slung beings that cling to the obsidian spires like gargoyles frozen mid-descent. At rest, a Drifter is almost indistinguishable from the basalt columns it calls home. Their carapaces are not biological in the way we understand flesh; they are constructed of dense, overlapping plates of mineral—slate-grey, iron-black, and occasionally the bruised purple of hematite. These plates are wet, perpetually sheened by the weeping rock, and they fit together with the terrifying, geometric precision of a stonemason’s puzzle, leaving no soft tissue exposed to the relentless cascade.

You might mistake one for a boulder that has settled improbably high on a ledge, until it shifts.

When a Drifter moves, it does not scurry. It flows with the grinding, glacial slowness of tectonic plates. Beneath the armored shell, they possess a hydraulic musculature that relies on the immense water pressure of the Ossuary. They do not breathe air; they draw water in through vents in their flanks, filtering the rich mineral soup—silica, iron, salt—and expelling the waste through ports in their rear, creating small, localized jets of steam that add to the valley’s fog.

They have no eyes. In a world of perpetual twilight and visual obstructions, sight is a useless liability. Instead, their "faces"—if the smooth, vertical cliff of a head plate can be called that—are dominated by a series of deep, lateral grooves. These are vibrational pits, exquisitely tuned to the specific frequencies of the Ossuary. They do not hear the roar; they *read* it. The constant thunder of the water acts like sonar for them, painting a vibrating, tactile map of their surroundings. They know the exact distance of a falling droplet, the structural integrity of the stone beneath their claws, and the presence of intruders by the way the sound waves bounce off the basin walls.

Their diet is the geology itself. The "vegetation" of this place—the strange, hard tubes of salt and silica—are their pasture. A Drifter will position itself against a vertical face of obsidian, extending a specialized, rasp-like appendage from its underbelly. With a sound like sandpaper on stone—inaudible over the roar of the falls—they scrape the mineral accretions away, consuming the crystalline growth. Over millennia, the Drifters are the architects of the valley's smoothness, their grazing the sandpaper that wears down the sharp edges of the world.

Smaller cousins, the **Salt-Skitterers**, live in the shadows of the giants. These are pale, crab-like creatures, no larger than a human hand, with shells translucent as white marble. They are the cleanup crew. Where a Drifter moves, it leaves scratches in the rock and dislodged debris; the Skitterers swarm these areas, feeding on the algae and bacterial mats that grow in the microscopic fissures of fresh stone. They are fast and frantic, ticking across the wet rocks like erratic raindrops, constantly fleeing the crushing, oblivious passage of the giants.

But the true terror and wonder of the Ossuary’s inhabitants lies in their reproduction.

The Lithic-Drifters do not lay eggs or give live birth. They *build* their young. In the deepest, most sheltered crevices of the basin—where the water sprays but does not crash—an elder Drifter will begin a construction project. Using secreted binders from its glands and gathered sediment from the basin floor, it meticulously assembles a small, geometric form: a perfect, multi-faceted cube of stone.

It looks, for all the world, like one of the "artifacts" of the ruined architecture—the scattered cubes of stone that litter the ground. The Drifter leaves this stone seed in a pool of mineral-rich water. There, it soaks. Over the course of decades—perhaps centuries—the chemistry of the water permeates the stone seed. Silicates replace the binders. A lattice of crystalline intelligence grows within.

Eventually, the shell cracks. Not from biological pressure, but from the expansion of a new, internal architecture of life. A new Drifter pulls itself from the stone casing, wet, dark, and heavy. Its first act is to consume the casing that housed it, recycling the minerals to thicken its own plating.

Because of this, the Drifters treat the "scattered built" quality of the landscape with reverence. They can hear the difference between a rock that is merely a rock and a rock that holds the dormant vibration of a potential kin. When you walk through the basin and kick a loose stone that looks suspiciously like a cut block, you are not desecrating a temple; you are threatening a cradle. The Drifters sense the disturbance in the roar, the sudden, sharp change in acoustics, and they turn their sightless faces toward you, vibrating with a low, infrasonic hum that you feel in your teeth.

They are not hostile. They are simply the patience of the valley given form. They are the slow eaters of the stone, the listeners to the thunder, and the architects of the Ossuary’s endless, wet, geometric nightmare. They endure as the water endures—hard, cold, and carving their place in the world one drop at a time.
