In the suffocating dimness of the Riven Spire, where the air is thick enough to taste like copper and old electricity, the inhabitants do not announce themselves. To see them is to witness a trick of the eye—a shadow detaching itself from a cliff face, a patch of condensation suddenly deciding to flow uphill.

They are tall—grotesquely so—and impossibly thin. If a human were to stand beside them, the inhabitant would look like a wire coat hanger unspooled and teased into a mockery of a humanoid shape. Their limbs are elongated, possessing far too many joints, allowing them to fold in ways that suggest broken bones rather than flexibility. This hyper-articulation is necessary; in the Riven Spire, where "down" is a matter of opinion and walkways spiral into non-Euclidean knots, a creature must be able to navigate walls as easily as floors.

Their skin is not biological in the way we understand flesh. It appears to be a matte, porous ceramic, the same non-reflective alloy that comprises the buildings themselves. It absorbs the pale-blue bioluminescence of the environment, rendering them nearly invisible until they move. However, this carapace is not whole. It is cracked—a network of hairline fractures that run across their bodies like jagged lightning bolts. From these cracks, a faint, rhythmic pulse of warmer light escapes, the color of a cooling ember, revealing that they are hollow, filled not with organs but with the same humming, dense machinery that drives the city.

They have no faces. Where a head should be, there is only a smooth, tapering curve of the ceramic shell, marked by a single, vertical slit that opens and closes like the gill of a deep-sea fish. This slit is not a mouth for eating, for there is no food here. It is an intake. When the "breath" of the city occurs and the humidity spikes, these slits gape wide, and the inhabitants inhale the heavy, mineral-laden fog. They process the moisture, extracting the trace metals and the raw energy suspended in the vapor, and then exhale a perfectly silent, dry gas. They are the filtration system of the Spire given ambulatory form.

Their movement is silent and unsettlingly fluid. They do not walk; they flow. Because gravity is a suggestion regulated by local fields, they often detach from the walkways entirely. They drift in the slow, heavy currents of the air, their long, spindled fingers trailing against the slick, sweating basalt. They possess an affinity for the stone. You will often see them pressed flat against the vertical walls, their multi-jointed limbs splayed out to maximize contact, seemingly listening to the groans of the cooling earth deep within the bedrock. They are tuning the resonance of the Spire, adjusting their own internal magnetic fields to counteract the stress fractures forming in the rock.

There is no gender among them, and no discernible individuality. They operate with a singular, hive-minded focus, though they do not communicate with one another. There are no gestures, no nods, no clicks of language. They seem to know each other’s positions intuitively, like scattered components of a single brain reassembling. If two pass each other on a helix ramp, they might pause, their vertical gill-slits flaring in unison, exchanging a burst of electromagnetic data—invisible to the eye but felt as a sudden spike of static in the teeth—before continuing their separate tasks.

They are the custodians of a tomb that has not yet claimed a corpse, or the midwives of a birth that refuses to happen. They spend their "lives" maintaining the siphons that pull the water upward into the sky, cleaning the mineral deposits from the energy-portals with delicate, scraper-like fingernails, and standing motionless for hours at the edge of the abyss, staring down into the obscurity below.

And when the silent, heatless lightning cracks the artificial ceiling above, they do not flinch. They simply tilt their elongated forms toward the light, their cracked shells glowing brighter for a fleeting second, drinking in the discharge. In those moments, they look less like creatures and more like captured lightning trying to find a way back to the storm. They are the sleepless shepherds of the Riven Spire, waiting in the damp, sterile heat for the day the machine finally wakes up—or dies—and they are no longer required to hold its breath.
