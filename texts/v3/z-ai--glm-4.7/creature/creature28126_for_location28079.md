Here, life is not a delicate struggle against the elements; it is a conquest. The inhabitants of this frozen, blinding foundry are not merely surviving the violence of the light and the razor-edge of the stone—they have internalized it. They are a biology that has ceased to be organic in the way we understand it, evolving instead into a living, breathing industry.

The apex of movement in this screaming landscape are the **Great Lithophages**. They are the bulldozers of this high-altitude hell, massive, tortoise-like monstrosities the size of earth-moving equipment. Their shells are not grown as simple calcium shields; they are forged over centuries of mineral deposition, creating layered armor of basalt and iron. They look like hills that have decided to move.

Their anatomy is a miracle of hydraulic pressure. The Lithophage does not have muscle in the soft, fleshy sense; it has tensile fibers capable of contracting with the force of a piledriver. Its legs are articulated pillars, jointed not with cartilage, but with sockets of polished granite and lubricating slimes of extreme-pressure grease secreted from glands. When they walk, the sound is a rhythmic, earthquaking *crunch-hiss, crunch-hiss*—the impact of tonnage on obsidian, followed by the venting of superheated gas from their flank-vents.

The head of a Lithophage is a nightmare of ingestion. It possesses no distinct face, only a radial array of grinding mandibles that spin slowly, powered by the creature’s internal body heat. These mandibles are tipped with diamond-hard spurs, capable of biting into the granite. They do not chew; they pulverize. They scrape the mountainsides, leaving behind smooth, terrified scars on the rock face.

Inside the Lithophage, there is a furnace. Because the air is freezing, their metabolism runs at a combustion level that would boil a human alive. They are, in essence, steam engines made of meat. They intake the freezing air, superheat it in internal reaction chambers using mineral catalysts, and vent the scalding steam to keep their extremities from freezing in the damp cold. This constant exhaust creates a local fog bank around each beast, a personal microclimate of sweat and sulfur.

Beneath the feet of the giants, scuttling in the millions through the steam and the razor-dust, are the **Ore-Weavers**. These are the isopod-like scavengers, the size of dogs but flatter, possessing segmented bodies that gleam like wet mercury. They are the clean-up crew and the soil-makers. They move with the frantic, jittery energy of insects, their legs a blur of metallic tapping.

Their purpose is twofold. First, they swarm over the fresh waste droppings of the Lithophages—piles of gravel and pulverized stone mixed with caustic slag. Using mouthparts that resemble tiny pickaxes and centrifuges, they sort the debris. They extract trace silicates and heavy metals, which they digest to reinforce their own exoskeletons. The rest—the fine, useless dust—they weave.

The Ore-Weavers possess spinnerets that extrude not silk, but a conductive, metallic mesh. They crawl over the sharp, naked rocks, binding the dust into intricate, felt-like blankets. This is the "soil" of the mountain. Over time, these metallic mats thicken, softening the jagged edges of the world, creating a substrate upon which the **Armored Moss** can take hold.

And then there is the moss itself: the **Chain-Mail Flora**. It is not passive greenery; it is a colony organism. Under a microscope, it is a city of tiny interlocking plates, each one a living silicon cell. They are gray and black, absorbing the punishing white light with near-perfect efficiency. They do not photosynthesize sugar; they photosynthesize energy. They strip electrons from the solar radiation, feeding directly into the electrical currents that run through the metallic soil created by the Weavers. The moss hums with a low-voltage charge, a static field that tastes of ozone and copper. It is the primary battery of the ecosystem, turning the violent sun into raw power for the grazers.

Connecting them all is the **Sky-Drift**, the atmospheric life. The air is thick with spores, but not the soft, floating kind of a forest. These are heavy, crystalline spores, shaped like micro-shards. They float in the glittering haze, inhaled by the vent stacks of the Lithophages and filtered through the gill-like rasps of the Weavers. They carry the genetic blueprint for the production of new enzymes—biological catalysts needed to digest new types of rock as the mountains shift.

Finally, moving silently through the cacophony, are the **Recyclers**. If the Lithophages are the heart of the ecosystem, the Recyclers are the immune system. They are sleek, spindly horrors, resembling a cross between a heron and a hydraulic crane. They have no mouth, only a long, needle-like proboscis made of bone-chitin. They patrol the edges of the herds, looking for the wounded, the stuck, or the broken.

When a Lithophone cracks a leg on a hidden crevice, or freezes solid because its furnace has failed, the Recyclers descend. They do not eat the flesh; they drain it. They insert their proboscises into the internal hydraulics of the dying beast, sucking out the superheated fluids and the dissolved minerals. They strip the creature of its metallic shell while it is still alive, dismantling the machine piece by piece. It is a gruesome, efficient process. Nothing is wasted. The armor is carried away to reinforce the nests of the Weavers; the fluids are used to jumpstart the metabolic engines of the next generation.

In this place, there is no death, only "malfunction" and "reclamation." The inhabitants are a singular, sprawling factory of flesh and metal. They do not live in the world; they process it. They are the teeth of the planet, grinding the sharp stones into dust, turning the blinding light into heat, and the freezing air into steam, endlessly manufacturing the noise that fills the bruised iron sky.
