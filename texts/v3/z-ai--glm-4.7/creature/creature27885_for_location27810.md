Here, the "citizens" do not walk; they haul themselves through the viscous gloom, their existence defined by the heavy, gelatinous drag of the world. To observe them is to watch a silent, frantic industry played out in slow motion.

The primary architects are the **Pale Masons**. Resembling translucent, decapitated shrimp, they are no larger than a fingernail, yet their bodies possess a dense, muscular integrity suited for pushing against the thick brine. Their exteriors are slick and glassy, allowing them to slide over the raw bone of the towers without friction. They move in ticking, rhythmic pulses, their myriad legs—a fringe of fine, hair-like cilia—vibrating against the water to propel them forward. You can see the machinery working inside them: a pulsing, clear sac of a stomach, a heart beating with a sluggish, cold rhythm. They are the masons of this world, and their task is endless. They congregate around the fractures and breaks in the calcified spires, their mandibles clicking open and shut in a vacuum of silence. From specialized glands in their underbellies, they excrete a milky, luminous resin—a rapid-drying secretion that hardens into the chalk-white stone of the city. They do not build homes for themselves; they build the very substrate upon which they stand, smearing the slime over the jagged edges of the geode walls, smoothing the rough architecture into eerie, organic perfection.

Occupying the "windows" and hollows of these towers are the **Glass-Shell Dwellers**. These are the soft-bodied crabs mentioned in the old lore, creatures that have evolved not to hide, but to fuse. Their carapaces are not made of chitin, but of silicate—clear, brittle shells that look like blown glass or frozen crystal. Inside these fragile bubbles, their organs are visible, suspended in pale fluid. They cling to the interior walls of the bone-towers, their legs tucked inward, appearing less like animals and more like decorative lamps embedded in the architecture. They are passive collectors. They extend feathery, bioluminescent tendrils into the slow currents, snagging the drifting "snow" of organic matter. When a current shifts, they do not scuttle away; they simply let go, drifting weightlessly through the heavy water like falling leaves, their glass bodies catching the dim radioactive light, before catching onto a new perch with hooked claws. They are the ornaments of the city, beautiful and infinitely breakable.

In the labyrinthine channels where the water is forced into rushing streams, the **Velvet Wolves** patrol. These are the slug-like predators, elongated and rippling with muscle. They lack the hard shells of the dwellers, their skin instead a textured, dark velvet that absorbs the scant light, rendering them nearly invisible until they are upon their prey. They hunt by ambush, folding their soft bodies into the crevices of the coral-bone. When a Glass-Shell Dweller drifts too close, the Wolf unravels—not with a strike, but with a bloom. It extrudes a net of sticky, anemone-like filaments that engulf the prey in a silent, suffocating embrace. There is no struggle, no thrashing in the thick brine. The Wolf simply enfolds the crab, dissolving its glass shell with internal acids until only the clear silica remains, which is then regurgitated to patch the wall nearby. The predator is also a builder.

But the most populous, and perhaps the most tragic, inhabitants are the **Drift-Swarms**. These are the larvae, the "children" of the city, birthed in the absolute darkness of the lower depths. They rise toward the dim light in a massive, writhing column of life—millions of tiny, eyeless noodles, propelling themselves upward in a silent migration. They possess rudimentary forms, looking like grains of rice with beating tails. They are driven by a biological imperative to reach the peaks of the towers. As they ascend, they age rapidly in the cold. The lucky ones reach the upper spires and metamorphose into Masons or Dwellers, taking their place in the bone-city. The unlucky, however, are swept into the currents or caught by the Velvet Wolves. But even in death, they serve. Their tiny bodies do not rot; they harden. The calcite in their systems crystalizes upon death, turning their corpses into tiny pellets of limestone that rain down upon the city, settling into the foundations to build the floor for the next generation.

Everything here is a cog in a biological machine. The Masons build, the Wolves hunt and polish, the Dwellers filter, and the children die to become the mortar. It is a civilization of slavery, where the slave, the master, and the building are all the same entity, locked in a silent, freezing loop of construction and consumption.
