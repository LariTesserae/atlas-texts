In a place defined by the anxious pause between conception and realization, life does not evolve; it is debugged. The inhabitants of this gray, embryonic basin are not biological in the way a lungs-and-heart creature understands biology. They are emergent properties of a system that is trying to decide if it wants to be a rock or a processor. Consequently, the fauna here behaves with a terrifying mix of mechanical precision and organic confusion.

The most common inhabitants are the **Seam-Walkers**.

They are the scavengers of the architectural errors. If you stare closely at the junction where the raw, precipitated stone meets the hyper-advanced alloy, you will see movement—tiny, frantic disturbances. Up close, the Seam-Walkers resemble a cross between a silverfish and a circuit breaker. They have bodies of flattened, geometric chitin, segmented like an insect, but each segment glows with a faint, rhythmic pulse of LCD light. They possess too many legs; some legs end in sharp tarsal claws for gripping stone, while others terminate in frayed bundles of copper wire that frantically search for electrical current.

They do not eat. Instead, they feed on the "friction" of the environment. They congregate around the rendering errors—the pixelated grids shifting beneath the rock face—pressing their wire-legs into the static as if siphoning energy. When a brick detaches from a wall and hangs suspended, unsure whether to fall or fly, the Seam-Walkers swarm it. They crawl over its surface, tasting the uncertainty, and in doing so, they seem to stabilize the object, their collective mass convincing the reality algorithm that the brick exists enough to be interacted with. When they finish, the brick usually falls, and the creatures scatter into the cracks, sounding like marbles dropped on a linoleum floor.

Higher up in the spectral hierarchy are the **Lag-Hounds**.

You do not see them so much as experience the absence of them. They are predators, but they hunt in the temporal gaps. They appear as large, gaunt shapes, vaguely canine or ursine, composed of a wireframe mesh that struggles to hold its texture. The outer layer of a Lag-Hound is constantly flickering, cycling through random assets—fur, steel scales, wet tar, exposed muscle—before settling on nothing at all.

They move with a horrifying, jerky gait. A Lag-Hound will lunge forward, freeze in mid-air for a heartbeat as the environment struggles to process its momentum, and then snap instantly to a new position several yards away. They hunt the wireframe sparrows and the more solid-seeming creatures by attacking the *processing* of their prey. When a Lag-Hound bites, it does not tear flesh; it creates a collision error. It clamps its jaws around a victim, and for a split second, the victim’s geometry overlaps with the predator’s. The result is a violent explosion of graphical noise—a scrambled collision of limbs and light—leaving behind nothing but a fading cloud of digital confetti and the lingering smell of ozone.

There are also the **Architects**, though they are less creatures and more autonomous organs of the city itself.

They resemble human figures, but they are towering, perhaps twenty feet tall, fused at the waist to the dense clusters of potential buildings. They stand half-embedded in the masonry, their torsos rising from the spiraling, knotted towers like statues trying to pull themselves free of the stone. Their skin is the same grey, slick material as the ground, cold and damp to the touch. Their faces are featureless, smooth save for a single, horizontal slit that acts as an eye, glowing with a flat, uninterested white light.

The Architects are in a state of perpetual, agonized labor. Their arms are raised above their heads, fingers splayed wide, manipulating the air around them. They do not touch the bridges that connect windows to empty air; they *will* them into existence. You can watch an Architect strain, its shoulders grinding with the sound of tectonic plates shifting, as a staircase slowly elongates from a wall. They are the physical interface between the algorithm’s dream and the city’s matter. They do not acknowledge observers. To them, you are merely a variable in the equation, a texture to be rendered later. If you stand too close to one, you risk being incorporated into the structure; the air around them is heavy with a magnetic gravity that pulls loose screws, pebbles, and fingers toward the masonry, to be calcified into the wall.

Beneath it all, in the oily, iridescent water that pools in impossible shallow depressions, live the **Sub-Drifters**.

The water here violates gravity, clinging to the undersides of overhangs and forming vertical lakes. Within these translucent, oily depths live things that look like umbrellas made of stained glass. They are translucent, displaying internal organs that look suspiciously like clockwork gears and bundles of fiber-optic cables. They drift through the heavy liquid with silent grace, trailing long, filamentous tentacles that probe the cracks in the pavement.

The Sub-Drifters are filter-feeders, but they filter the "noise" of the environment. They absorb the ambient uncertainty, the metallic taste of the air, and the low, infrasonic vibration of the stone. As they consume this psychic detritus, they glow brighter, their glass bodies shifting from dull greys to vibrant, impossible neons that hurt the eyes to look at directly. They are the only things in this place that seem to enjoy the embryonic state. When a tower re-calibrates its coordinates with a screech of tortured metal, the Sub-Drifters in the nearby pools ripple with excitement, their internal gears spinning faster, feasting on the chaos of the update.

Finally, there are the **Echoes**.

These are the rarest, and perhaps the saddest, inhabitants. They are what happens when the algorithm attempts to render a human consciousness but lacks the reference data. They look like people from a distance—shadows in the mist, standing on the hovering platforms. But as you approach, you realize that the details are wrong. A coat might have no sleeves, just the suggestion of sleeves painted onto the torso. A face might be a smooth oval, waiting for features to be drawn in. The legs might be absent entirely, the torso simply floating a few inches off the ground.

They do not speak. They stand on the edges of the basin, staring out at the horizon that curves upward into the void. They seem to be waiting for the "signal" the city is waiting for. Occasionally, an Echo will try to move, but the animation cycle is incomplete; a leg might move forward without the body following, or the head might turn 360 degrees while the shoulders remain locked. They are the citizens of this ghost city, trapped in the moment before the first breath is drawn. They are not alive, but they are not dead; they are merely buffered, existing in a holding pattern that may last until the algorithm finishes dreaming, or until the universe runs out of memory.
