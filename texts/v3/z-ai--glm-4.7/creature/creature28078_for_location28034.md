To look into this hollow is to see a world paused in a deep inhalation, and for a long time, the eye slides over the inhabitants, mistaking them for the very furniture of the earth. They are there, pressed into the tight, chaotic jigsaw of the room, but they possess a mastery of stillness that makes them invisible to the casual glance. They are the things that wait.

They are roughly the size and shape of the fallen slabs of shale, low-slung and flat, designed to slot into the negative spaces between boulders. Their backs are not covered in fur or scales, but in a ceramic-like plating, hard and dull, colored the exact slate-grey of the limestone walls. Over this, a crust of mineral patina has formed—greens of copper and rusts of iron—so that when they sit motionless, they are indistinguishable from the eroded pillars and the debris of the floor.

They have no need for eyes here. In the flat, shadowless twilight, vision is a useless sense. Instead, their faces are smooth, sloping domes, interrupted only by a series of shallow, vertical slits where a nose or a brow might be on a human. These slits pulse with the agonizingly slow rhythm of the air, tasting the heavy, humid soup of the hollow, sensing the chemical composition of the wet dust. They are breathing the minerals, tasting the geology of the place.

Beneath the heavy, stone-like shell, their bodies are soft and pale, translucent like the blind slugs that share the crevices, but vastly larger. They are aquatic in their physiology, though they live on the boundary between water and air. They cling to the wet stone with a broad, muscular foot that secretes a thin, viscous mucus, allowing them to adhere to the vertical walls of the hollow or the slick sides of boulders without ever slipping.

Their movement is a geological event. When they decide to shift—to slide from one dry patch of stone to a shallow pool of black water—it happens over the course of hours or even days. A casual observer would see nothing; only by fixing one's gaze on a specific ridge of shell and returning an hour later would one notice that the ridge has inched forward. They slide with the friction of tectonic plates, a silent, grinding displacement of mass.

They are solitary, spaced out across the cavern like anchors in a net of stone. Each one claims a territory—a specific cluster of rocks, a stagnant pool, the ruins of a crude wall—and they sit amidst them, absorbing the radiating heat. They are ectothermic in the extreme, their internal clocks tuned to the glacial pace of the dripping water. The *plink… plink… plink* seems to regulate their metabolism; one might see a flank shudder slightly in response to a heavy drop, a reflex that takes minutes to propagate through their dense tissues.

They are the inheritors of the primitive pottery and the stacked walls. Occasionally, one will be found draped over the remnants of the low wall, its grey body conforming to the shape of the ruins, as if trying to merge with the history of the place. They do not build; they only occupy. They do not eat the crabs or the slugs; they are filter-feeders of the finest order. They run the warm, mineral-rich water through their internal systems, straining out microscopic bacteria and dissolved organic matter, essentially drinking the soup of the earth and excreting clean water.

There is a sense of profound loneliness about them, but it is not a human loneliness. It is the loneliness of a stone that remembers the ocean, or a pot that remembers the fire. They are the final sentence in the paragraph of this place, living beings that have simplified themselves down to the bare minimum: heat, water, and stone. They sit in the warm, wet dark, their shells slowly eroding, their soft bodies pulsing in the gloom, waiting for a change that will never come.
