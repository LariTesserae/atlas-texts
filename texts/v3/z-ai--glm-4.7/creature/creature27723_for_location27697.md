The inhabitants of this wet, unfinished forge are not people as you might define them—they are components. They are biological cogs in a machine that is still trying to decide if it wants to exist.

First, there are the **Drifts**. They are the labor force, though they do not labor in the traditional sense; they *maintain*. They move through the steam and the damp in loose, shambling clusters, their silhouettes indistinct against the yellow fog. If you were to look closely, you would see that their skin is the texture of wet clay—pale, malleable, and constantly weeping a thin, gray fluid. They wear clothing that is less fabric and more architecture: aprons made of chainmail that has rusted into a solid sheet, boots with soles three inches thick to insulate against the vibrating ground, and goggles with lenses made of thick, colored glass that distort their eyes into wide, unblinking orbs.

The Drifts communicate not through speech, but through a low, resonant humming that vibrates in their chests. They hum in harmony with the steam turbines, matching the frequency of the factory’s roar. To watch them work on a truss bridge is to watch a hive mind; they do not gesture or shout orders. One Drift will hand a heavy iron rivet to another, and the transfer happens without a glance, guided purely by the shared rhythm of their song. They seem immune to the vertigo of the extreme elevation, moving with the confident, swaying gait of things that belong in the sky.

Then there are the **Conductors**. These are taller, spindlier creatures that seem to be caught in the act of being pulled apart by the gravity of the place. They have an extra set of arms, long and skeletal, that end in sharp, hook-like claws. Their primary role is the management of the electricity. They climb the humming violet cables like spiders, their bodies crackling with static discharge. Their hair stands perpetually on end, floating around their heads like a halo of lightning. They do not wear insulation; rather, they *feed* on the voltage. When a cable sparks, a Conductor will be there instantly, cupping the blue arc in its hands as if catching an insect, absorbing the energy before it can damage the machinery. Their eyes are not solid orbs but shifting storms of white light, mirroring the chaotic weather above.

Living in the gutters and the massive, dripping ventilation shafts are the **Scavengers**, the things that scuttle. These are the rat-sized creatures mentioned, but to call them rats is an insult to the complexity of their wretchedness. They are singular organisms composed of scrap metal and biology. A Scavenger might have a skull made of a bent iron bolt, or a spine that is a discarded gear. They are the ultimate recyclers. They eat the moss that grows in the brick, they eat the rust flakes that fall from the girders, and they eat the softer, softer slurry of the emerging stone. They move with a jerky, clockwork motion, their legs clicking on the wet stone. They are fearless, swarming over the boots of the Drifts, knowing that as long as the machines run, there will be refuse for them to consume.

Most disturbing, perhaps, are the **Statics**. These are the ones who stood still for too long.

In a place where reality is a sketch and distance is subjective, stopping is dangerous. The Statics are former inhabitants—perhaps once Drifts, perhaps something else entirely—who lost the rhythm of the city. They have begun to merge with the environment. You will see them standing on the edges of the precipices or leaning against the smokestacks, frozen in mid-stride. They are not statues; they are still alive, but their bodies have calcified. Moss grows thick in the crevices of their skin. Vines have wrapped around their limbs, anchoring them to the iron. Their faces are masks of terror or bliss, etched into the stone of their flesh. Occasionally, if the steam whistle blows loud enough, a Static might twitch, or their lips might move, whispering secrets into the damp air that no one can hear over the roar of the turbines. They are the milestones of the city, living road markers showing the cost of hesitation.

Finally, flitting through the sallow twilight are the **Spark-Wings**. These are the bat-like entities, metallic and chitinous. They are the pollinators of this industrial nightmare. They do not seek nectar; they seek heat. They swarm the cooling vents of the massive factories, darting into the blasts of hot steam to warm their metal bodies. Their wings are thin sheets of copper, beaten translucent by centuries of flapping. When they swarm, the sound is a rain of tinfoil, a chattering cacophony that adds to the industrial din. They nest in the high rafters, hanging upside down from the power lines, sleeping while the electricity hums through their claws, charging them for the next night's flight.

They are all caught in the act of becoming. The Drifts are becoming machinery; the Scavengers are becoming garbage; the Statics are becoming geology. And the city watches them with its unblinking, foggy eyes, waiting to see which of them will finally solidify into something permanent, and which will dissolve back into the wet, gray slurry of the dream.
