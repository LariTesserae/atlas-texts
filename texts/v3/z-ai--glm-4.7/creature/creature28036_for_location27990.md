At first, you would swear that nothing could survive here, let alone thrive. The overwhelming roar of the unseen water acts as a shroud, a sonic barrier that seems to repel life itself. But if you were to stand perfectly still on that slick, rounded basalt—if you were to slow your own heartbeat until it matched the rhythm of the dripping stone—you would begin to see them.

They are not distinct creatures in the way surface animals are; they are extensions of the rock itself. They call the *Basalt-Born* by no name, for they have no need of language, and they possess no vocal cords that could ever hope to pierce the thunder of the river. They are a species of troglodyte that has surrendered the chaos of the surface for the ordered, humming stasis of the deep.

Physically, they are shaped by the chamber that holds them. They are low-slung and elongated, their spines flexible to the point of fluidity, allowing them to drape over the smooth, curved protrusions of the cave walls like discarded wet rags. Their skin is not the pink or brown of surface dwellers, but a pale, translucent gray, speckled with the same bruised purple-gray as the twilight that filters from above. It is tight against their bodies, slick and hairless, minimizing drag in the damp air and offering no purchase for the chill.

They are blind. The useless orbs of their eyes are sealed over with layers of thick, calcium-like film, protecting them from the constant, abrasive spray. Instead, they perceive the world through a network of hyper-sensitive lateral lines running down their flanks and across their hairless scalps. They do not "see" the light; they feel the pressure waves of the water's roar. To them, the deafening noise is not a chaotic assault, but a complex, three-dimensional map. The vibration of the water rushing through the hidden veins of the earth tells them where the walls are, where the floor drops off, and where the moss grows thickest.

They are the reason the moss persists. Hanging from the ceiling in the purple gloom, suspended by hooked claws that are perfectly adapted to the polished glass-stone, they are slow-motion grazers. Their primary diet is the lush, velvet mats of liverwort and lichen that carpet the high reaches. They move with agonizing deliberation, scraping the damp greenery from the rock with flat, spatulate teeth. Their metabolic rate is a fraction of a human’s; a single mouthful of moss sustains them for days. This slowness is their camouflage. To the fleeting glance of a visitor, they are just another shadow or a trick of the light reflecting off the wet stone.

Down on the floor, near the scattered primitive cairns and the knee-high wall of the "camp," live the elders. These are massive, swollen things, their heavy bodies resting on the mud-covered ledge, absorbing the seeping cold through their broad, flattened bellies. They are the architects of the silence here. The cairns are not graves, but acoustic dampeners—piles of stone placed with mathematical precision over centuries to break up the reverberation of the chamber, creating a pocket of relative quiet where the young can gestate. The "dry-stone wall" serves no defensive purpose; it is a windbreak for the clutch of soft, leathery eggs that are buried in the warm mud beneath the fire pit, utilizing the faint geothermal heat that rises from the earth’s wound.

The artifacts—the flint knapping debris, the bone needles—are indeed theirs, but they are not tools of survival in the traditional sense. They are tools of *maintenance*. The Basalt-Born possess an innate, instinctual drive to smooth. Their fingers are long and tapered, tipped with claws made of a material harder than the surrounding rock. They spend their geological lives sitting in the roar, gently scraping the basalt walls, polishing the rough edges that time creates, ensuring the chamber remains a perfect, frictionless vessel for the sound. They do not build; they merely refine what the water has begun.

They are not aggressive; they are barely aware of your existence. If you were to reach out and touch one hanging from the wall, it would not flinch. It would simply press itself tighter against the stone, merging with the shadows, waiting for the vibration of your intrusion to fade so it could return to the eternal, roaring song of its home. They are the caretakers of the scream, living fossils in a dark, cold lung of the world, forever tuning the acoustics of the invisible river.
