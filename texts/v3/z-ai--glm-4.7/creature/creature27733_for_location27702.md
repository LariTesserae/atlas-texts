To look for them is to miss them. The inhabitants of the Sunken Ward do not stand against the backdrop; they are the texture of the backdrop itself. They are a people—or perhaps a single, organismic colony of beings—that have evolved to embody the very principle of the place: absolute, luminous stillness.

They are tall and impossibly thin, their limbs attenuated like the dangling roots of the ferns, possessing a cartilaginous flexibility that allows them to fold themselves into the negative spaces of the architecture. Their skin is the most striking adaptation; it is not flesh as we understand it, but a porous, pale membrane, translucent and slick. It lacks the warmth of blood, appearing instead as a living porcelain that absorbs the relentless white light and diffuses it, giving their bodies a faint, ghostly inner glow. This sheen is perpetually wet, coated in the same viscous slime that sheathes the stone walls, making them silent when they move.

There is no hair upon them, only the faintest sensory filaments that tremble in the humid air, tasting the chemical composition of the mist. Their faces are smooth and oval, dominated by eyes that have sacrificed depth for clarity. Because the light here banishes shadows, their eyes have no pupils to dilate; they are vast, dark orbs of liquid obsidian, reflecting the clinical brightness with zero resistance. They never blink. To blink would be to admit a passage of time, to introduce a rhythm that does not exist. They stare, unblinking and unweeping, their gaze as fixed and cold as the air itself.

They are a silent species. Their vocal cords have atrophied, or perhaps they were never needed. In the acoustic vacuum of the Ward, a shout is a blasphemy that dies instantly. Instead, they communicate through a language of pressure and gesture. They move their long, spindly fingers with agonizing slowness—a curl of a digit, a tilt of the wrist—signaling to one another across the bridges. When they must touch, which is rare, they press their damp foreheads together, exchanging thoughts through the conductivity of the mucus that coats them. It is an intimacy of absolute sufficiency, requiring no words.

Their society is a reflection of the verticality. They do not walk on the "floors" of the huts, which are treacherous with stagnant black water. They are climbers. Their fingers and toes end in soft, suckered pads that adhere to the slick, moss-covered stone and the waterlogged wood of the bridges. They hang from the underside of the walkways like bats, or cling vertically to the walls, perfectly motionless for hours, indistinguishable from the bruised-purple mosses that surround them. They are the "urban" parameter made flesh: they are the structure that grows inward.

They do not hunt. To hunt implies pursuit, noise, and the chaotic struggle of predator and prey. The Ward is a closed loop, a solved equation, and they feed on the surplus of the solution. They harvest the succulent, fleshy vines and the water-rich ferns. They tend to the invisible ecosystem, gently pruning the aggressive vegetation to ensure the structural integrity of their bone-scaffolding homes. They are the gardeners of the stasis, ensuring that nothing grows enough to change the balance, yet nothing dies enough to decay.

Sleep is unknown to them. Without a sun to set or a moon to rise, their consciousness is a singular, unbroken stream. They enter into trances—states of suspended animation where they cling to the high arches of the Ward, their metabolism slowing to match the freezing temperature of the air. In these trances, they become statues, part of the diorama, indistinguishable from the primitive masonry.

Occasionally, the small, sleek rodents—the only other mobile life—will dart over a resting inhabitant. The being does not stir. There is no aggression here. The rodent is part of the equation; the being is part of the equation. They exist in a harmony of indifference. The rat cleans the moss; the being tends the vine; the vine feeds the being. It is a perfect circle.

They are, ultimately, a haunting sight to an outsider—not because they are monstrous, but because of their terrifying peace. They possess a calm that is predatory in its absolute lack of urgency. They are waiting for nothing, because nothing ever changes. They are the eternal, silent witnesses of the white light, frozen in a dynamic arrest, living in the center of a diamond, beautiful, cold, and utterly hollow.
