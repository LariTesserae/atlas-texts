At first, the plateau seems empty, a sterile desert of gray geometry and weeping stone. But as your eyes adjust to the blinding, reflection-scattered glare, you begin to notice that the absolute stillness is actually a form of camouflage. The life here does not scuttle, flutter, or graze; it does not breathe in the way you understand breath. The inhabitants of this place are inextricable from the architecture itself—they are the custodians of the flow, the unblinking eyes of the mountain.

They are a caste of living lithoids, beings that appear less like animals and more like self-assembled masonry. You might mistake the smallest of them for a pile of discarded chisel debris left behind by the vanished builders—a low, flat cluster of angular shards maybe six inches across. But then the "debris" shifts. It slides forward with a sound like two wet stones being rubbed together, a rough, grinding friction that is barely audible over the constant lapping of the water.

These are the **Scourers**.

They possess no distinct head or limbs, only a flattened, segmented body composed of the same dense, fine-grained gray material as the floor. Their edges are sharp, flaked like obsidian but tougher than granite. They move with agonizing slowness, creeping along the labyrinthine channels. Their purpose is singular and obsessive: maintenance. They slide into the microscopic grooves of the stone, using their geometric bodies to scoop out the slightest accumulation of silt or mineral deposit. They are the reason the water is perfectly clear. They are the immune system of the plateau, consuming entropy—dust, grit, imperfection—and excreting nothing but fine, silvery powder that dissolves instantly into the flow. They do not eat the water; they polish the path the water travels.

You have to stand very still to see them. There are thousands of them. In every square yard of the "empty" expanse, three or four Scourers are at work, gliding over the slick surface, pausing, vibrating as they scrape a microscopic imperfection from the trench wall, then moving on. They are silent, mindless mechanisms of stone, driven by a biological imperative to keep the circuit clean.

Higher up, near the vertical monoliths, live the **Overseers**.

These are larger, towering figures that stand taller than a man, yet so thin and narrow that from a distance, they look like shadows cast by the obelisks. They are bipedal, though their legs are fused pillars of stone, and their upper bodies taper into multiple long, spindly arms that splay outward like the ribs of a broken umbrella.

They do not move. They stand waist-deep in the catch-basins, rooted to the floor by a slow, chemical dissolution of their foot substance into the stone. They are frozen in postures of intense, surgical attention. Their many arms hover just millimeters above the water’s surface, sensing the tension, the viscosity, and the temperature. They are the regulators.

If a channel becomes blocked, or if the gradient shifts due to some infinitesimal settling of the mountain, an Overseer will detach one of its spindly arms. The arm will drop into the water, shattering the surface tension, and unspool a length of fibrous, cable-like muscle from within the stone torso. The Overseer will reach down into the trench and physically rearrange the Scourers, herding them like sheep toward the blockage. It is a terrifying display of strength; the Overseers can crack the bedrock with a simple contraction of their torsos, shattering a wall to redirect the flow, then simply stand there as the water rushes over them, their bodies healing and calcifying within minutes.

But the true horror and beauty of this place’s population is not found in the workers, but in the water itself.

The water is not merely water. It is a colonial organism, a **Vasticulate Hydrozoan** that has adapted to this high-altitude, mineral-rich environment. The "tears" that condense on the stone are the spawn of this entity. The water flows so slowly not just because of the shallow gradient, but because the water *wants* to stay. It is a gelatinous, intelligent slime that has chosen the form of clear, pure water to exist.

It coats the skin of the Scourers, lubricating their movement. It pools in the reservoirs of the Overseers, providing them with the chemical signals they need to direct the labor. The intricate carving of the plateau—the millions of chisel marks—is not just plumbing; it is a neural map. The water flows through the grooves like electricity through a circuit board, carrying information from the center of the plateau to the edges.

At the center, where the land is highest and the air thinnest, lives the **Heart**.

It is not a creature you can see in one glance. It is a massive, sub-saturated conglomerate of the Hydrozoan, trapped in a deep, circular depression carved into the mountain's peak. Here, the water is thick, almost viscous, moving in a slow, clockwise churn. The Heart pulses. Once every hour, the level of the water in the central basin drops by a fraction of an inch, sending a pressure wave rippling outward across the entire plateau.

This wave is the heartbeat of the civilization.

When the pulse hits the distant Scourers, they stop scraping and turn, unerringly, toward the center, bowing slightly in a gesture of submission or synchronization. When the pulse hits the Overseers, they shudder, their long arms vibrating against the air, transmitting the pulse into the stone monoliths, which hum with a low, infrasonic frequency that keeps the atmosphere thick and charged.

This is the ecosystem: a tripartite symbiosis of the stone-carvers (Scourers), the stone-gods (Overseers), and the living water (The Heart). They are not distinct species fighting for survival; they are components of a single machine. The Scourers clean the body; the Overseers guard the nervous system; the water is the blood.

The civilization that built this did not vanish. They evolved. They stripped away the softness of flesh, the noise of voice, and the chaos of emotion, and they calcified. They turned themselves into the floor and the flow, choosing a existence of static, eternal maintenance over the messy entropy of biological life.

They are still here. If you were to place your hand on the wet stone, the water would ceaselessly bead against your skin, trying to parse your geometry, trying to understand if you are a new component to be filed down, or an imperfection to be scoured away. The silence of the plateau isn't empty; it is the held breath of a billion stone creatures, waiting for the next pulse from the Heart to tell them what to do.
