To live in the hollow is to exist in a state of perpetual vibration, and the people here have evolved—or perhaps devolved—to match the frequency of the machinery. They are a stocky, dense breed, human in shape but seemingly carved from the same raw materials as their surroundings: iron, shale, and damp clay.

They do not speak. The roaring drone that fills the basin makes vocalization futile, and generations of life in the noise have physiologically rewired them. Their vocal cords have atrophied into vestigial bands of tissue, and their ears are heavily caulked with scar tissue and a natural buildup of waxy protective deposits, rendering them functionally deaf to the outside world. They do not suffer in the silence, however; rather, they have become masters of interpretation. They "hear" through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. They are acutely attuned to the subtlest variances in the grinding thrum of the pistons, able to discern a dropped wrench from a shifting fault line miles away simply by feeling the change in the tremor running through the floor grates.

Visually, they are draped in layers of loose, oil-soaked canvas and rubber that hangs off their frames like the skin of a shedding reptile. This clothing is not changed but simply added to; it acts as a sponge for the omnipresent moisture and a barrier against the scalding bursts of steam that jet unpredictably from the bowels of the complex. Underneath these heavy rags, their skin is pallid and translucent, almost blue-tinted from the lack of true sunlight, taking on the sheen of wet marble. They are hairless, the humidity having stripped them of fur millennia ago, leaving only smooth, slick scalps that glisten under the amber lamps.

Their faces are dominated by the eyes. Large, lidless, and dark, adapted to the perpetual twilight, they reflect the dim amber glow of the lamps like pools of stagnant oil. Because they do not shout or call out to one another, their faces are remarkably still, devoid of the animation of conversation. They communicate through a complex, tactile sign language performed with the hands pressed firmly against the recipient's body—usually the back or the chest—so that the rhythm of the message is transferred physically rather than audibly. It is an intimate, claustrophobic form of connection, necessitating that the people of the basin are rarely out of physical contact with one another. They move in clumps, tangled masses of limbs and canvas, navigating the slick basalt with a coordinated, rolling gait that mimics the sway of the reeds in the ditches.

They are functionally immortal in their stagnation. There are no children here, or rather, no distinction of age. The population does not grow; it maintains. New generations are not born in the traditional sense but seem to emerge from the mists of the pools or the recesses of the machinery, small, slick creatures that grow slowly into the heavy, laboring adults. They do not mourn death, for when a worker falls into the gears or succumbs to the sulfur sickness, the body is simply left where it lies, becoming part of the lubrication or the structure, a seamless return to the industrial cycle.

Diet consists of the gray, oily birds and the bloated amphibians that cling to the pipes, hunted with silent, darting speed. The inhabitants view these vermin not as pests but as wandering livestock. They harvest the thick, pulsing algae from the puddles, pressing it into cakes that dry into hard, black wafers. They eat these meals in the open, sitting on the jagged edges of the rock, staring vacantly at the static cloud layer, their jaws moving in a slow, rhythmic grinding that mirrors the machinery above them.

There is no hierarchy among them, only a shared, communal consciousness of duty. They are the keepers of the roar. They spend their lives tending to the pipes, tightening rivets that never loosen, and clearing valves that never clog. They do not know what the machines produce, nor do they care. The process is the purpose. They are the biological components of a vast, mechanical organism, lubricating its joints and feeding on its parasites. To watch them is to watch a colony of ants inside a clock: frantic yet motionless, terrified of silence, and perfectly, tragically adapted to a purgatory that asks nothing of them but to endure the loop.
