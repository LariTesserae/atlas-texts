The inhabitants of this dim, roaring crucible are shaped by the press of stone and the relentless biology of the thicket. They are not merely people living *in* a place; they are a component of the dense, metabolic engine that drives it, as integral to the ecosystem as the massive cacti or the swarming bats.

**The People of the Ribs**

The human figures here are short and dense, possessing a wrestler’s compact strength. Their skin is the color of the pale dust that coats everything, a greyish-ochre that seems to absorb the weak twilight rather than reflect it. They move with a fluid, rolling grace, born of a lifetime spent navigating uneven surfaces and squeezing through tight gaps. Because the light is filtered and shadowless, their eyes are large and dark, adapted to strain clarity out of the gloom, with heavy lids that blink against the constant drifting of organic particulate matter.

They dress in layers of coarse, woven fiber stripped from the tough agave plants. Their garments are heavy and armored, thick leather tunics and skirts reinforced with dried spines and wooden splints to protect against the constant thorn-prick of the city walls. They wear wraps around their lower legs and arms, leaving only hands and faces exposed. These clothes are not washed; they are seasoned by the air, smelling permanently of dried spice, sweat, and the sweet, fermenting tang of cactus nectar.

To survive the noise—the deafening wall of screeches and wing-beats—they have developed a culture of touch and proximity. They do not shout to be heard; instead, they communicate through a complex sign language performed with quick, sharp flicks of the fingers, often while maintaining a grip on the other person’s shoulder. When they must speak, their voices are low-throated and powerful, designed to cut through the roar only at close range. They are tactile creatures, constantly bumping, leaning, and steering one another, a hive of humanity flowing through the narrow alleys.

Their hands are their primary tools. They are stained dark with plant juices and clay, callused into hardened pads. With stone axes and blades made from obsidian glass, they harvest the flesh of the columnar cacti. They are master weavers, lashing the dried ribs of dead giants together to reinforce their homes, constantly repairing the city as the living vegetation seeks to reclaim it.

**The Rooftop Lizards**

Sharing the urban space are the great lizards, the "ridge-runners." These creatures are the livestock and the scavengers combined, roughly the size of a large dog or a small wolf. They have evolved to blend perfectly with the architecture; their scales are slate-grey and textured like dried mud. They skitter across the domed roofs and along the dried-wood beams, their claws creating a rhythmic, clicking clatter that blends into the ambient noise.

They are semi-domesticated but fiercely independent. The people do not herd them; the lizards herd themselves, following the smell of ripening fruit or the scent of rot. They are the garbage disposals of the city, eating the pest insects that the humans cannot catch, and in turn, the humans harvest their eggs—which are laid in the warm, dark corners of the clay ovens—or occasionally cull the weak and sick for meat. There is an uneasy truce on the rooftops; a man sleeping on a terrace must sleep with one eye open, or a lizard may gnaw on the leather of his sandals or, if he is unlucky, a toe.

**The Winged Tide**

Above all, the airspace is owned by the flyers. These are not the delicate birds of the open sky, but heavy, primitive things. The birds here have dull, dusty plumage, feathers that feel more like dry leaves than down, with sharp, hooked beaks designed for tearing into tough cactus fruits or carrion. They navigate the twilight with a loud, thrashing confidence, landing heavily on the window ledges, screaming for scraps.

Below them, pouring out of every crevice in the rock, are the bats. They are the true rulers of the dark, immense leather-winged beasts that swarm in densities that darken the felt-filtered light. The people live under a constant rain of guano, which they scrape off the roofs and terraces to use as fertilizer for their small, hidden gardens of medicinal herbs. The bats are a danger to the unwary—fangs and claws striking from the gloom—but they are also the portents of the nectar flow. When the bats swarm thickest, the cactus flowers are open, and it is time for the harvest.

**The Burrowing Millions**

Beneath the feet of the people and the claws of the lizards lies the third layer of society: the soil of the city is alive. Rodents—blind, pale, and naked—live in colonies that undermine the streets. They are rarely seen, but the ground is constantly shifting as they excavate the waste of the world above. The inhabitants build their foundations on stone to avoid collapsing into the rodent tunnels, but the sounds of scratching and squeaking are ever-present, vibrating up through the soles of their feet.

Accompanying them are the insects. Black clouds of beetles and flies are drawn to the clay lamps, the open wounds of harvested cacti, and the sweat on the people’s skin. The humans wear veils of netting when working near the fermentation pits, where the smell of rotting cactus pulp attracts the thickest swarms. Here, the noise is a buzzing fury so loud it feels like pressure against the eardrums.

**The Metabolism of the City**

Ultimately, whoever lives here is defined by a lack of waste and a ferocity of survival. There is no room for sentimentality. The fallen are buried quickly, becoming part of the dust that feeds the next generation of succulents. The sick are tended to in the deepest, most silent hollows, away from the chaos, but if they do not rise, their belongings are stripped and repurposed before the body is cold.

They are a people who do not look at the horizon, for there is none. They look *up*, watching the dim light fade on the rock shoulders, or they look *in*, peering into the fleshy hearts of the plants that sustain them. They are stoic, high-strung, and intensely communal, bound together by the realization that in this claustrophobic, roaring room, isolation is a death sentence. They flow like blood through the veins of the city, indistinguishable from the heat, the dust, and the thorns.
