The ecology of the Sunken Atrium is defined by a single, overriding imperative: the conservation of momentum. In this warm, syrupy gloom where the machinery grinds on a geological timescale, speed is a vice, and stillness is the highest virtue. The inhabitants do not live; they settle. They do not hunt; they filter. They are not distinct entities so much as mobile organs of the room itself.

**The Cladding (The Living Moss)**
The velvet moss that coats the upper strata of the pipes is not a plant, but a colonial zooid—a "living lace" of millions of tiny, cupped organisms anchored to the iron. They are the primary harvesters of the dim, chemical light that permeates the water. Each zooid is no larger than a fingernail, translucent and pale, filtering the suspended minerals from the water and excreting a hard, white calcite.
This excretion is the mortar of the Atrium. Over centuries, the Cladding has built the smooth, bone-white casings that sheath the black iron pipes, entombing the machinery in a biological sarcophagus. When a shadow passes over them—a rare occurrence—the entire forest of tiny cups pulses, retracting into the calcite shell in a slow-motion wave that takes minutes to travel from one end of a pipe to another. They do not fear predators; they simply resent the disturbance of the current.

**The Rivet-Mimics**
Between the great, clogged pistons and along the spiraling ridges of the pipes dwell the Rivet-Mimics. These are crustaceans that have abandoned the exoskeleton in favor of a hydro-static skeleton, their bodies soft and gelatinous like the water they inhabit. To protect themselves, they sequester iron from the water and plate their skin with it, forming heavy, metallic shells that look exactly like the industrial heads of bolts or rivets.
They are sessile, cementing themselves to the machine hulls, indistinguishable from the hardware save for when they feed. A Rivet-Mimic will remain frozen for days, its "head" retracted, waiting for a drift of nutrient-rich silt. Then, with agonizing slowness, it extends a feathery, iron-plated limb—resembling a corrupted fern leaf—to sweep the detritus toward a mouth hidden beneath the metal plate. They are the janitors of the ruin, scraping the slow accumulation of slime from the gears, their lives ticking in rhythm with the machine's deep thrum.

**The Gear-Walkers**
 clinging to the massive, creeping flywheel are the Gear-Walkers. These are spindly, spider-like shapes, their limbs elongated and jointed with a fluid grace that mimics the pistons around them. They do not walk on the machinery; they synchronize with it.
The Gear-Walker positions itself within the teeth of a massive cog. As the wheel rotates—taking an hour to complete a fraction of a turn—the Walker is lifted. It drifts through the amber water, suspended in the machinery’s embrace, filtering the water through gills that line its underbelly. When the tooth dips back into the darkness, the Walkers scuttle with lightning speed—relative to this place—across the metal surface to the next rising tooth. They are nomads of the torque, migrating eternally around the circumference of the machine, riding the tides of industry.

**The Needle-Eels**
The only active swimmers in the Atrium are the Needle-Eels, described as translucent, languid shapes. They are the phantoms of the room, possessing bodies so clear they are visible only by the distortion of the bioluminescent moss behind them. They have no scales, only a slick, gelatinous skin that offers no resistance to the thick water.
They are creatures of pure impulse. Unlike the sessile Rivet-Mimics, the Eels patrol the corridors of rusted weed. They do not bite; they cannot. Their jaws have atrophied into useless ridges. Instead, they possess a specialized throat-sac that creates a localized vacuum. They drift over a patch of Cladding or a stationary Rivet-Mimic and simply inhale, sucking the soft, nutrient-rich bodies right out of their shells or calcite homes. They move in silence, entering the water column like a thought and vanishing just as quickly, their presence felt only by the sudden, mysterious absence of life in their wake.

**The Apex of Stillness: The Bell-Anchor**
Dominating the center of the Atrium, suspended in the open water above the slow-grinding flywheel, hangs the Bell-Anchor. It is massive—a bloated, balloon-shaped organism easily the size of a small whale, though it weighs little due to internal gas bladders filled with the methane of the deep earth.
Its skin is thick and scarred, patterned with the rust-colored rings of a thousand discarded pipe couplings. It is a living filter, a vacuum cleaner of the deep. A massive, funnel-shaped mouth hangs permanently open, trailing a curtain of sticky mucus that drifts in the current.
The Bell-Anchor does not swim. It adjusts its buoyancy slightly to rise or sink, hovering in the sweet spot where the nutrient density is highest. It is the heart of the ecosystem. Its body hosts a garden of parasites—smaller crustaceans and moss-like plants that burrow into its flanks—creating a mobile, living island. It pulsates once every ten minutes, a slow, muscular contraction that sends a ring of ripples through the viscous water, a gentle heartbeat that echoes the sub-sonic groan of the machine.

**The Atmospheric Harvester**
Finally, hidden in the darkest crevices where the calcification is thickest, lives a colony of creatures that resemble bioluminescent fungi. They are not fungi, but highly specialized, lung-like polyps. They exist to breathe the *sound* of the room. The thrumming vibration of the deep earth is conducted through the metal pipes and into the water, and these polyps vibrate in sympathy. They feed on the kinetic energy of the sound, using the microscopic vibrations to agitate tiny cilia inside their bodies, generating a electrical charge that they use to fix carbon from the mineral-rich water. They are the eaters of the thrum, transforming the machine's agony into the sugar that feeds the moss, which feeds the mimics, which feed the eels.

In the Sunken Atrium, the food chain is a circle, and the energy is drawn not from the sun, but from the slow, dying turn of the world. The life here is a persistence, a biological pause button held down in the warm, amber dark.
