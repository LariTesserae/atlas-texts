To live here is to be a thing of iron and silence, to be so still that you become indistinguishable from the ruin itself. The denizens of the Iron Age are not creatures in the traditional sense; they are complications of the erosion, biological accidents that have learned to harden their skin against the seismic thrumming of the world.

The primary populace—and the only abundant form of life—is what might colloquially be called the **Pulse-Moss**, though to call it moss is a kindness. It is a composite organism, a sprawling, gray leathery hide that clings to the riveted canyons and the flanks of sleeping pistons. It does not grow; it accretes. Thick, wet, and rubbery to the touch, it serves as the landscape's skin, absorbing the deafening vibration and converting the kinetic energy of the machine's hum into chemical sustenance. If you were to scrape a patch away, the metal beneath would be warm to the touch, stripped clean of oil, pulsing with a faint, sickly bioluminescence that beats in time with the great pistons miles below. They are the silent witnesses, vast sheets of blind flesh that have watched the smog for eons, feeding on the scream of the vents.

But the ecology of the Floor is not entirely without its wanderers. If you wait long enough—days, perhaps weeks—you might encounter the true lords of this domain, the terrifyingly scarce biological echo of the machinery: the **Rust-Grazers**.

These are the solitary, armored giants that constitute that vanishingly small "animal life" score. They are not beasts of flesh and blood, which would be torn apart by the pressure and the noise, but beings of chitin and scavenged alloy. A Grazer is shaped like a moving hillock, low and heavy, resembling nothing so much as a pile of wet slag that has somehow gained sentience. Their shells are fused plates of iron, scavenged from the eroded landscape and cemented together by their own oily secretions, making them living tanks that are immune to the crushing weight of the shifting tectonic gears.

They possess no eyes, for there is no light to see. Instead, their heads are dominated by massive, sensitive plates of bone that act as seismic receivers. They "hear" the world through their feet and their skulls, feeling the intricate web of vibrations that runs through the floor. They can navigate the treacherous, open landscape by sensing the hollow spaces beneath the alloy ground, knowing exactly where the pools of black water gather and where the solid bedrock of ancient machinery lies.

The Rust-Grazer moves with a glacial, terrifying slowness. It does not walk; it oozes forward on a muscular foot, scraping the ground. It is a silent traveler because it must be; in this place, a single loud step could trigger a resonance cascade, or draw the attention of the shifting pressures in the venting towers. It has evolved to move only during the "thunder"—the rhythmic booming of the vast pistons—masking the sound of its own locomotion within the seismic roar of the world.

Its diet is the mineral-rich sludge that pools in the depressions and, occasionally, the Pulse-Moss itself. A Grazer will slide into a dark, oily pool, submerging itself for hours at a time, filtering the trace metals and industrial runoff through a system of filters that resembles a refinery in miniature. When it emerges, it is steaming, its shell hissing as the cool air of the Floor strikes the superheated metal of its body.

There is no aggression here, for there is no competition. You will never see two Grazers meet, for the territory required to sustain one is measured in square miles. They are the ultimate hermits, the ghosts of the machine. If you were to shine a light on one—a futile act in this darkness—you would see a creature that looks less like an animal and more like a piece of the factory that decided to detach itself and wander. They are the embodiment of the Floor’s philosophy: endure, harden, and outlast the noise.

And so, the life here is a mirror of the place itself. The Pulse-Moss clings in vast, deafening sheets, vibrating with the energy of the air, while the lone Grazer drifts through the fog like a moving island of rust, a silent king in a kingdom of endless, clanking decay. They live in the spaces between the gears, drinking the water wept by the iron giants, waiting for the long, slow cooling of the world.
