To exist in a place of absolute exposure requires a form that has abandoned the vanity of distinct outlines. Here, biology does not bloom; it submits. The life that inhabits this blinding, hexagonal hive is barely discernible from the architecture itself—a "fauna" of 0.4 implies not just scarcity, but a near-total withdrawal from the biological imperative.

You would not see them at first. In the wash of actinic white, the eye searches for the contrast of shadow or the movement of limb, but the inhabitants possess neither. They are the color of the grout—a pale, dry beige. They have evolved to be the negative space of the room, the living embodiment of the interstices.

They are low, flat creatures, possessing an exoskeleton that mimics the very material of the bricks. Their carapaces are not smooth, but faceted, etched with microscopic geometric ridges that catch the light in the same chaotic, blinding way as the walls. They are lithomimics, stone-crabs that have forgotten how to be soft. If you were to place one on the floor, it would be indistinguishable from a fallen hexagon tile until it moved, and even then, its movement is not a scramble or a scuttle, but a glide.

They move with a horrifying silence, their six or eight limbs—jointed like masonry tools rather than flesh—extended to grip the textured walls. They do not walk; they interlock. Their limbs terminate in pads that can adjust their density, creating a temporary suction seal against the microscopic imperfections of the stone. They can cling to the vertical drop of the pit or crawl across the ceiling with the ease of a beetle across a leaf, insulated from the vertigo by their own chitinous gravity.

These are the "Polishers."

The wear at hip height, the smoothness of the relief carving in that specific band, is their ancient doing. For thousands of years, perhaps millions, they have traversed the walls in a slow, migratory rhythm. Their underside—the only soft part of them, a pale, leathery membrane like a dry lizard’s belly—is coated in a fine, abrasive dust. As they drag their heavy bodies along the geometric swirls, they act as living sandpaper, slowly burnishing the ancient carvings into oblivion. They are the entropy of the perfect room, the slow-motion decay that wears down the "scream" into a whisper.

They are eyeless. In a world where to look up is to go blind, eyes are a liability. Instead, they perceive the world through the vibration that permeates the stone. The constant 2.5 drone is their sensory input; it is their sight, their smell, and their touch. They possess a series of vibration-sensitive pits along their lateral lines, resonating chambers that allow them to "hear" the texture of the wall meters ahead of them. They can feel the stress fractures in the hexagonal bricks, the density of the rock, and the presence of the sparse silica "plants" that cling to the ceiling seams.

They feed on these lithophytes. This is the only movement that betrays urgency. When a Polisher senses the crystalline "leaf" of a silica fern, it detaches from its slow patrol and scuttles with surprising, jerky speed toward the vertical seam. It does not chew; shearing the crystal would create noise, a violation of the room’s acoustic dominance. Instead, it secretes a mild acid from its mandibles— a solvent that tastes of lemon and chalk—dissolving the silica sheet into a gel which it laps up. This is their only source of water; they are miners of hydration, extracting the bound moisture from the crystal lattice of the "plants."

Their social structure is nonexistent, fitting the 0.4 density. You might walk for an hour and see nothing, then suddenly realize the shape in the corner thirty feet away is not a trick of the light but a motionless guardian, flattened against the wall, waiting for the vibration to shift. They are solitary, territorial only in the sense that they require a certain mass of wall to sustain their grazing. They do not communicate; the room communicates for them. The drone is the collective consciousness of the space, and they are merely its antibodies, cleaning, feeding, and maintaining the density.

To encounter one is to feel a deep, sympathetic vibration in your own chest. As they cling to the wall, their bodies absorb the thrumming of the masonry and re-radiate it at a slightly offset frequency. If you place your hand near them, you feel a hum that makes your bones ache. They are warm to the touch, despite the 10-degree chill, a byproduct of the immense metabolic energy required to process stone and silence.

They are the keepers of the stillness. They remove the dust that does not settle, consume the growth that threatens to crack the mortar, and slowly, over eons, planing down the sharp edges of the architect’s intent into a smooth, featureless wear. They are the reason the room feels finished; they are the living proof that even a place of absolute density and zero decay is slowly, relentlessly being devoured.
