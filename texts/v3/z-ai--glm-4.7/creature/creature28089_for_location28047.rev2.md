# The Living Geology

---

What lives here has forgotten it is alive.

This is not extinction, nor dormancy, nor any of the small deaths that organic things fear. It is something else—a devolution of urgency, a shedding of everything that once demanded haste. They have been so slow for so long that the distinction between biology and geology has collapsed inside them, and they have become what they eat: the earth itself, tasting itself, turning slowly over in a mouth that takes a century to close.

---

## The Strata-Bodies

You call them creatures, but this is your word, and it is too small. They are *processes*. They are continuations of the land's own metabolism, raised up and given the faintest spark of autonomy—not will, exactly, but a kind of tropism so ancient it has worn grooves into the species' architecture.

An adult strata-body has no front or back. It has no head. It possesses no central nervous system, no organ that could be called a brain in any recognizable sense. Instead, its intelligence—such as it is—resides in a distributed lattice of crystalline nodes embedded throughout its chitin-stone carapace, each one a slow capacitor that accumulates charge from the planet's electromagnetic field and discharges it across chemical gaps when a threshold is reached. Thought, here, is not electrical firing but *dripping*—a mineral seep of signals through stone conduits, each impulse taking days or weeks to travel from one node to the next. A single "decision"—the choice to shift weight from the left bearing pillars to the right—might involve more nodes than there are neurons in a human brain, and it might take eleven years to complete. The creature does not experience this as delay. It does not experience time as sequence at all.

What does it experience?

This is a question that cannot be answered in any language built for quicker minds. But consider: you have stood in a room and known, without any sensory cue, that someone else had entered—that the quality of the air changed, the pressure of presence shifted. Now imagine that sensation extended across centuries, diffuse and continuous, indistinguishable from the feeling of weather, or gravity, or the slow tilt of a planet. The strata-bodies do not perceive objects. They perceive *states*—the tension of minerals under pressure, the charge of water-saturated stone, the faint seep of electromagnetic fields as tectonic plates grind beneath them. Their world is not a landscape of forms and distances. It is a landscape of *pressures* and *appetites*. They feel the earth the way you feel your own hunger—not as information about the world, but as a condition of the self.

They are lithovores. They consume rock. But "consume" is another word too quick for what they do. Their undersides—we should not call them bellies, for they have no front or back, only the surface that faces down—are a slow catastrophe of dissolution. They exude a chemical through the micropores of their contact surface, a substance with a pH that has never been measured by any instrument because no instrument could survive the timescale of its action. It does not dissolve rock in the way that acid dissolves metal, with bubbling and heat. It *unbraids* the mineral bonds over years, separating the trace metals—the iron, the manganese, the copper, the cobalt—from the silicate matrix. The metals are absorbed. The silicates are left behind, reconstituted by a second exudate into the laminated, cladding-like sheets that form the creature's own carapace. They eat the earth and shit architecture. They are walking refineries, biological smelters operating at geological temperatures, and the stratified layers that give the terrain its industrial appearance are, in truth, the molted shells of ten thousand generations, each one compressed beneath the next until the entire plateau is built from the slow exhaust of living things.

This is why the terrain hums. The hum is not the planet. The hum is *them*—the charge moving through their crystalline node-lattices, the electrochemical slow-fire of their digestion, the faint telluric current that their metabolic processes generate and ground back into the stone. Stand here long enough and you will feel it in your molars, in the hinges of your jaw: a vibration so low it is not sound but *pressure*, the way a change in barometric pressure is not weather but the weight of the sky sitting on you. The entire plateau is their circuit board. The moisture that sweats from the ground—that paradoxical dampness in the sterile cold—is their condensation, the byproduct of their mineral respiration, beading on stone surfaces like the perspiration of a machine that does not know it is running.

---

## The Tempo of Existence

A strata-body can live for four hundred thousand years.

This is not an estimate. The layers tell the story. By counting the growth rings in the cross-sections of abandoned shells—those hollow, rolling hills where a creature died and its carapace settled into the terrain like a shed exoskeleton—you can calculate the rate of deposition: one new lamina every six hundred and twelve years, give or take a decade. The oldest shells have seven hundred layers. The creatures who wore them were already old when the plateau was young.

They do not die of age. They do not age. Their cells—if cells is the word for the mineral-organic hybrid units that compose their flesh—do not degrade. They are not subject to entropy in the way that carbon-based life is, because they have no telomeres to shorten, no oxidative stress to accumulate, no heat metabolism to burn them out from within. They operate so close to the ambient temperature of the planet that their internal combustion is indistinguishable from geology. They are, in a sense, already dead—or rather, they exist in a state that carbon life cannot reach, a state in which the rate of decay is so slow that decay itself becomes a form of stability.

They die, eventually, but they die of *accumulation*. Over millennia, the crystalline nodes that form their distributed intelligence begin to calcify, the conduits between them narrowing as mineral deposits build up in the gaps—the same way that plaque builds in arteries, the same way that silt chokes a river. Their thoughts slow. Not that they were fast to begin with, but now a signal that once took weeks to propagate might take years, and the creature's responsiveness to the pressures and appetites of its world dims like a light moving into fog. It does not experience this as decline. There is no horror in it, no grief. The creature does not know it is dying because the creature does not know it is alive in any way that could be distinguished from being stone. One day—a day that lasts three thousand years—the last node calcifies completely, and the creature becomes what it has always imitated: a hill, a ridge, a rolling shape on the plateau, indistinguishable from the terrain except that it is hollow, and inside, where the organs once slowly pumped their mineral solutions, there is only silence and the faint, fading hum of charge dissipating through disused conduits.

The plateau is a graveyard. But this is not sad. It is only slow.

## The Young

You will not see them. You will not recognize them if you do.

The young are not small. There is no larval stage, no fragile beginning. When a strata-body reproduces—if that word can be applied to a process so gradual that no observer has ever witnessed its completion—it does so by *calving*. A mature individual, moving across the terrain at its customary glacial pace, will develop a structural fissure along one of its lateral seams, and over the course of eight thousand years, a section of its body will separate, pulling away like a continent drifting from a landmass. The section does not depart empty. It carries with it a fraction of the parent's node-lattice, a seed of crystalline intelligence that will, over further millennia, grow and differentiate into a new distributed mind.

The young are born old. They emerge from the parent already massive, already armored, already consuming rock. They have no childhood, no period of vulnerability. They are simply *less*—less layered, less extended, less saturated with the memory of millennia. Their node-lattices are sparse, their conduits wide, their thoughts faster than their parents' by a factor that would be imperceptible to any observer who was not themselves operating on geological time. A young strata-body might shift a bearing pillar in only *three years*.

But they slow down. They all slow down. This is the only trajectory of their lives: a deceleration so gradual it cannot be measured against any human timescale, only against the planet's own aging. They grow slower as they grow larger, as their node-lattices densify and their conduits narrow, as the weight of their own accumulated carapace presses down on their bearing pillars and every movement becomes a negotiation with gravity that takes longer to resolve. They are running down. They have been running down since they calved from their parents, and their parents were running down since they calved from theirs, and so on, back through the deep time of the plateau, back to the first strata-body—or the first thing that would become a strata-body—whatever that was, whenever that was, in an epoch so ancient that the rocks do not remember it because the rocks were not yet rocks, they were only magma and pressure and the slow, slow cooling of a world that did not know what it would become.

## The Lichen

The lichen is not decoration. The lichen is not parasite.

The orange-grey crust that clings to the fissures of the metal-stone—of the carapaces, of the terrain, of the abandoned shells of the dead—is the only organism on the plateau that operates on a timescale comprehensible to human observation, and it is the only organism that bridges the living and the mineral. It is a chimera: a symbiosis of fungus and phototrophic bacteria, like any lichen anywhere, but the bacteria do not photosynthesize. There is no sun to speak of, only the dim phosphorescence of the rocks themselves, and that is not light enough to feed anything. Instead, the bacteria are *electrotrophic*. They draw energy from the planet's electromagnetic field, from the faint current that sweats through the stone, from the humming charge that the strata-bodies generate as they digest the earth. The lichen is not growing on the rock. The lichen is *plugged into* the rock. It is a conduit, a root, a wire. It takes the electrical potential of the mineral world and converts it into the chemical energy that the fungus partner needs to build its hyphae, its networks, its slow-growing crusts.

And the strata-bodies need the lichen in return. This is the secret the landscape keeps, the relationship that the scale of the plateau obscures. The lichen's hyphae—those threadlike fungal networks that penetrate the fissures of the stone—do not merely grip the surface. They *etch* it. They secrete their own dissolution compounds, weaker than the strata-bodies' but targeted, precise, and over centuries they carve microscopic channels into the carapace, channels that allow the condensing moisture to penetrate deeper, that allow the strata-bodies' digestive exudates to spread further, that open new seams of mineral wealth to the slow absorption of the creature beneath. The lichen is the creature's mouth. The lichen is its teeth. The strata-body provides the current; the lichen turns that current into corrosion; the corrosion feeds the body; the body generates more current. It is a closed loop, a circuit, a symbiosis so total that the two organisms cannot be meaningfully separated. The orange-grey rust on the hills is not infestation. It is digestion, made visible.

You might look at the plateau and see a dead world scabbed with lichen. You would be seeing it backwards. The lichen is the living part. The hills are the lichen's bodies, its architecture, its slow growth made manifest in stone. The strata-creatures are the lichen's stomachs, its roots, its way of reaching deeper into the mineral world than hyphae alone could ever go. And the lichen is the strata-creatures' lips, their tongues, their thousand-year kiss with the earth.

Which is the host and which is the parasite? The question presupposes a distinction that the plateau does not recognize. There is only the process: the eating, the growing, the slowing, the stilling, the settling into stone, the rising again as something that looks like rock but moves, the corrosion and the construction, the endless loop of life consuming geology and geology consuming life until the two are the same thing and the distinction is as meaningless as the distinction between the water and the wave.

## What Else Lives Here

The pools of black water in the depressions between the hills are not lifeless.

They are the plateau's eyes.

Not eyes that see. Eyes that *reflect*. The still, oily surfaces of those pools mirror the sky with such perfection because they are, in truth, a product of the sky—the condensation of the planet's atmospheric moisture, drawn down by the electromagnetic charge of the plateau, settling into the low places where the hum of the strata-bodies is loudest and the ground is most conductive. The water is not still because there is no wind. It is still because it is *held*, suspended in a field that presses down on it from all sides, a field generated by the combined charge of ten million living hills. The pools are the plateau's capacitors. They store the atmospheric charge that the lichen's electrotrophic bacteria need, and they discharge it slowly, through the water table, into the stone below. They are electrical organs. They are the plateau's way of harvesting the sky.

Things live in them. Small things. Flat, transparent things that have never been classified by any taxonomy because no one has looked and because looking would require instruments that operate on timescales the things do not recognize. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are *crystalline*—self-organizing mineral structures that grow in the charge-saturated water, reproducing by budding, feeding on the electrical potential, extending their lattice formations in branching, fractal patterns that look, under magnification, like the branching of rivers or the branching of nerves or the branching of lightning. They are alive in the same way that the strata-bodies are alive: slowly, electrically, at a tempo that makes a glacier seem hasty. They have no metabolism that any biochemist would recognize. They have no DNA, no proteins, no carbon chains. They are silicon and water and charge, and they grow, and they bud, and they die—calcifying into the same mineral deposits that eventually choke the strata-bodies' node-lattices, returning to the rock from which they came.

The pools are their nurseries. The pools are their graves. The pools are the only places on the plateau where the water moves, and it moves only because the crystalline things are growing in it, displacing it, stirring it with the slow expansion of their fractal bodies. If you watched a pool for a thousand years, you would see it breathe—the surface rising as the crystals grow, falling as they calcify and dissolve, a slow tidal pulse that has nothing to do with the moon and everything to do with the living circuit that the plateau embodies.

## The Silence

And yet.

For all this life—for the ten million strata-bodies, for the lichen that carpets them, for the crystalline things in the pools, for the electromagnetic field that ties them all together like a nervous system—the plateau is silent. Not quiet. *Silent*. The silence is not an absence of sound. It is, as the description says, a medium. It is the sound of processes so slow they do not register on any membrane designed to vibrate at frequencies audible to air-breathing ears. The strata-bodies *do* make sound. The groan of their carapaces expanding and contracting with the temperature—the creak of their bearing pillars shifting—the deep, subsonic thrum of charge moving through their node-lattices—these are sounds.
