They are a people of the wet dark, carved from the same grim stone as the city they inhabit. To look at them is to look at a creature that has surrendered entirely to the subterranean, evolving away from the sun so completely that they have become almost elemental—beings of mineral and mist rather than flesh and blood.

Physically, they are elongated and starkly pale, possessing a skeletal, almost lithographic grace. Their skin is not the soft, porous dermis of surface dwellers, but a thick, seamless sheath of smooth, pearlescent tissue that feels slick and cool to the touch, like wet glass. It fits tightly over their frames, eliminating the drag of loose cloth or hair; they possess no hair at all. Their heads are streamlined, the features compressed and swept back as if to better cut through the heavy air. There are no protruding ears to trap water, only small, sealed slits behind the jaw, and their noses are mere slits, evolved to inhale the thin, oxygen-poor air while expelling the excess moisture that constantly threatens to drown them.

They are blind in the way the deep ocean is blind. Their eyes have not vanished, but have receded, becoming shallow, milky cups covered by a transparent, nictating lid that protects them from the perpetual upward spray. They do not "see" the city; they inhabit a topographical map of pressure and texture. Their hands and feet are their primary sensory organs—oversized, spatulate, and tipped with broad, spatulate nails that act as both climbing implements and tactile receptors. Every step on the slick basalt is a reading of the stone; every grip on a hemp bridge is a conversation with the tension of the fibers.

The silence they inhabit is paradoxical, defined by a deafening roar they have learned to ignore. Their internal ears are desensitized to the thunder of the falls, but their bodies are hypersensitive to the low-frequency thrum that vibrates through the city’s bones. They "speak" not with vocal cords, which would be shredded by the volume of the cavern, but through a complex language of percussive touch. They press their long, prehensile fingers against the rock, the wooden gears, or the shoulders of their kin, transmitting vibrations that convey complex syntax. A sharp tap means danger; a slow, rolling caress of the palm means work; a rhythmic stomp against the chest signifies grief or belonging.

Clothing is nonexistent, replaced by a functional integument of harnesses and tools woven from the guts of the slick-runners they hunt. They move through the vertical hive with the terrifying confidence of spiders, their elongated limbs allowing them to span distances between the precarious walkways that would send a human plummeting. They are constantly in motion, yet their movement is fluid and glacial, conserving energy in a world where calories are scarce and the cold is a constant predator.

Their society is a rigid, silent clockwork, mirroring the water wheels that turn around them. There is no hierarchy of wealth, for there is no wealth to be had; there is only the hierarchy of function. One tends the grinding stones that pulverize the few fungi and amphibian carcasses into nutrient paste; another monitors the stress fractures in the ancient wooden gears, feeling the tremors of the wood with a cheek pressed against the timber. They are the maintenance crew for a machine they did not build, serving a mechanism whose purpose has been lost to time.

The young are not born; they are "hived." Females retreat into the deepest, dryest crevices high above the spray to lay soft, leathery eggs that adhere to the ceiling. When the young hatch, they are born with a full set of claws and an instinctive understanding of the vertical world. They are immediately part of the workforce, their lifespan measured not in years, but in the revolutions of the great wheels.

They possess a profound, unsettling stillness. When the machinery groans or a slick-runner darts past, they freeze, blending perfectly into the wet, obsidian backdrop, their pale skin reflecting the faint, ghostly blue of the fungi. In these moments, they look less like living beings and more like statues left behind by a forgotten civilization, waiting for the rain to stop.

But the rain never stops. And they never leave. They are the equilibrium made flesh—holding the chaos of the water at bay with their fragile bodies, suspended forever in the cool, dark throat of the world, vibrating in time with the roar that they can no longer hear.
