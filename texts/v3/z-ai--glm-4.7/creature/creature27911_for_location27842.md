In the high, suffocating gloom of the Cathedral, where the air is too thick to breathe easily and the light is merely a suggestion, the true citizens reveal themselves only by their movement against the dark. They are not incidental residents of this place; they are its consequence, molded by the geometry and the noise just as surely as the stone channels were carved by the water.

Dominating the vertical expanse are the **Rust-Wings**.

They are massive, things of leather and muscle that seem too heavy to fly, yet they own the air with a terrifying, effortless buoyancy. To call them bats is a disservice; they are more like manta rays that have traded the ocean for the storm. Their wingspans easily exceed ten feet, constructed of a taut, membrane-thin skin stretched over bone-like cartilage. This skin is not merely dark; it is the exact color and texture of the oxidized copper that stains the ceiling—a flaking, pitted rust-red and bruised purple that allows them to vanish the moment they clamp onto the basalt arches.

They do not hang upside down like common beasts. The condensation here is too violent, the walls too slick. Instead, they cling spider-like to the rough cyclopean blocks, their wings wrapped around their bodies like wet cloaks, using hooked, calcified talons to grip the natural imperfections in the stone. They sleep in huddled masses that look from below like patches of spreading damp on the ceiling.

But they are never truly at rest, because the noise never stops. The Rust-Wings have evolved to live inside the roar. Their screams are not the erratic chirps of ordinary cave dwellers, but focused, piercing beams of sound—staccato clicks that cut through the pneumatic thrumming of the falls. They possess no eyes; the gloom is too absolute for vision to be of use. Instead, their faces are dominated by sleek, hydrodynamic ocular cavities sealed shut by skin, and complex, flaring auditory channels around their mouths that ripple as they echolocate. To a Rust-Wing, the world is a wireframe sculpture of sound-vibrations, where every falling drop of water paints the air in shimmering ripples of feedback.

They ride the thermals—columns of slightly warmer air rising from the deep, friction-heated stone and the cold water below. They do not flap often. When they hunt, they dive. They drop from the ceiling in silent, stooping freefall, tucking their wings to become aerodynamic missiles, only snapping them open at the last second to skim the water channels. Their lower jaws are unhinged, extending into needle-filled rakes designed to scythe through the surface of the water.

And the water is far from empty.

Beneath the surface of the smooth, lipped stone channels thrives the prey: the **Blind-Runners**.

These are not fish in the traditional sense, but eel-like aberrations, pale and translucent as wax. They have evolved for a life of zero light and endless current. They lack pigmentation entirely, their long, ribbon-like bodies revealing the pulsing dark coils of their digestive tracts and the white, calcified vertebrae within. They have no fins for speed; the channels are too smooth and the flow too regulated. Instead, they are covered in thousands of microscopic, bristle-like hairs along their undersides, allowing them to cling to the stone bottom or crawl up the vertical sides of the channels against the current.

They are the scavengers of the machine. Their heads are little more than sensory nodes— wide, lipless mouths framed by sensitive tendrils that taste the water for chemical traces. They feed on the sludge that washes down from the upper levels, the algae that grows in the pipes, and the unfortunate insects that fall into the flow.

But their most defining trait is their synchronization. Because the rain above is rhythmic, artificial, the Blind-Runners have developed an internal clock that mimics the machine. When the flow is low, they scatter, hiding in the microscopic fissures of the stone floor. But when the "heartbeat" of the Cathedral quickens and the scheduled deluge begins, they enter a spawning frenzy. The rushing water vibrates their lateral lines violently, triggering a mass migration where they ball together in writhing, knot-like clusters of thousands, rolling downstream in a desperate, reproductive surge. It is this frenzy that the Rust-Wings are timing their dives to catch.

On the periphery of this violent cycle, clinging to the highest, most aggressive spray zones where the water hits the stone with the force of a hammer, lives the **Phosphor-Mold**.

It is the only "plant" life here, though it is more animal than vegetable. It is a colonial organism, a slick, velvety veneer of greenish-gray slime that seems to secrete directly out of the basalt itself. It thrives where the erosion is highest, feeding on the microscopic mineral particles shaved off the stone by the water. It is bioluminescent, glowing with a sickly, pulsing light—the only illumination in the twilight.

The mold is not passive. It exudes a thick, oily mucus that coats the rocks above the channels. This mucus is highly nutritious to the Blind-Runners, who risk the spray to graze on it. But it is also a trap. The oil is slick, and many eels are washed from the walls by the heavy droplets, falling stunned into the central current where the Rust-Wings wait.

The ecosystem is a closed loop of mechanics and biology. The machine creates the water and the rhythm; the water feeds the mold; the mold feeds the eels; the eels feed the bats. Even the waste of the bats, falling into the deep channels, creates the detritus that the lowest caste of eels filters from the silt.

When the deluge hits, the transformation is instant. The thrumming of the water becomes a physical blow. The Rust-Wings, huddled against the ceiling, scream in unison—a harmonic, resonant note that vibrates through the hollow bones of the observer. Below, the water turns white with the bodies of surfacing eels.

In this moment, the Cathedral is not empty. It is a single, pumping organism of stone, water, and flesh, driven by a false heart that beats in the dark.
