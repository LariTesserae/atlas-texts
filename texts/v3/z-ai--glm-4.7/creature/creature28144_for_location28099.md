They are a people carved from the same cleaved granite as their walls—short, stocky, and possessed of a dense, tensile strength that belies their silence. Their skin is the color of wet slate, thickened and calloused by generations of abrasion against the raw crystal faces of the cavern. They are not merely dwellers in this hollow; they are extensions of its geology, moving with a slow, deliberate friction that mimics the grinding of the earth itself.

Their eyes are large and adapted to the perpetual twilight, possessing a reflective quality that catches the dim orange pulse of the central flue. This creates an uncanny effect when they gather: a forest of faint, glowing points in the gloom, blinking in unison with the fire’s rhythm. They do not blink often, however, prioritizing the constant visual scanning of unstable surfaces and shifting loads. Because the air is thick with the metallic taste of iron and dust, their respirators are internal—a complex system of dense sinus cavities and heavily filtered nostrils that allow them to breathe the heavy air without choking, while their mouths remain perpetually shut or covered by dampened cloths to conserve moisture.

Physically, they are built for the vertical. Their legs are disproportionately powerful, bowed from a lifetime of bracing on slopes that refuse to settle. Their hands are their defining feature: broad, spatulate fingers with flattened nails and ridged fingertips, capable of finding purchase on the slickest, moss-slicked rock. They are tactile creatures, "reading" the vibrations of the cavern through the soles of their boots and the pads of their palms. When the earth groans and the floor heaves—a tremor shaking the dust from the ceiling—they do not panic. They freeze, locking their joints and becoming statues, waiting for the stone to tell them when it is safe to move again.

Their society is devoid of leaders in the traditional sense, organized instead around the great, wooden gears that dominate their architecture. The "Gear-Binders" are those who tend the massive winches and pulleys, their bodies stained dark with the animal fats and tars used to lubricate the machinery. These individuals are coated in a layer of sawdust and grime that protects them from the damp, giving them the appearance of living statues made of bronze and wood. They move in absolute synchronization, a silent ballet of leverage and friction, communicating through a complex language of tugs, taps, and weighted gestures. To speak aloud in the damp, absorbent air is considered wasteful; a shout is an act of aggression, reserved only for the moment a winch line snaps or a foundation gives way.

Clothing is minimal and strictly functional, woven from the fibrous, woody flesh of the subterranean fungi harvested in the lower dark. The fabric is coarse and water-repellent, dyed dull shades of rust and ochre to blend with the rock. They wear little jewelry, save for "balance-rings"—weighted bands of lead or bronze worn around the wrists and ankles to maintain equilibrium during the frequent tremors. These rings clink softly as they work, a gentle percussion that underscores the city’s quiet hum.

They are omnivorous scavengers of a harsh landscape. Their diet consists largely of the blind, white crustaceans fished from the gutters and the mosses that they cultivate in the shaded cracks of their towers. However, the center of their domestic life is the keeping of the flightless birds. These birds, heavy-feathered and stupid, are kept in cramped coops woven into the upper levels of the city. The people tend to them with a fierce, protective devotion, as the birds are the only reliable source of protein in the newborn world. The relationship is symbiotic: in exchange for food and shelter, the birds’ body heat helps to dry the fungal timber and warm the sleeping mats of their keepers.

At night, when the central fire dims to a low, simmering pulse, the people retreat into the interlocking warrens of their city. They sleep in shifts, never fully unconscious, always one ear tuned to the song of the stone. They dream of stability, of a day when the floor will stop rising and the walls will stop cracking, but they awake to the reality of creation. They are the architects of the temporary, the caretakers of the wound, living in the breath between the earth’s violent inhalation and its steady, burning exhalation.
