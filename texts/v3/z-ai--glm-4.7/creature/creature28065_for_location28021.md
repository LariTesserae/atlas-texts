To say that life *endures* here is a misunderstanding of the local physics. In the Resonant Wastes, nothing endures; everything is sustained by the scream of the earth. The silence of animals is indeed total, for soft tissue, wet blood, and hollow bones would simply liquefy under the gravitational pressure and the sonic density of the roar. Flesh is a weakness the geology does not permit.

But to say the place is uninhabited is to fail to see the vibration for the noise.

What lives here is the **Lithic Choir**.

They are not distinct entities in the way a wolf or a human is distinct from the ground. They are emergent properties of the waste itself—geological conscious given form by the crushing pressure and the erratic, flickering physics. They begin, perhaps, as accidents of the crystal forests. When the tectonic bass reaches a certain fever pitch, the silicon structures of the quartz trees do not just shatter; they coalesce. The vibrational energy forces the lattice structures of the crystals to fold in upon themselves, creating denser, harder, geometric complexities.

At a distance, a member of the Choir looks like a pile of debris—a cairn of jagged obsidian shards, fist-sized chunks of raw copper, and translucent, hexagonal quartz. They appear to be the debris left behind by the "people" who built the huts, the rubble of a failed civilization.

Then the "skip" happens.

Because distance is negotiable here, the Choir does not walk. Walking is a linear, biological absurdity, too slow for the flickering reality of the waste. When you look away, the pile of rubble ceases to exist in Point A. When you look back, or when the world glitches and the static-green void flashes beneath your feet, the rubble has reassembled itself at Point B—impossibly closer.

As they approach, the "pile" resolves into a towering, vaguely hominid shape. They stand perhaps twelve feet tall, though their height fluctuates with the hum of the air. Their "limbs" are spindly stacks of basalt slabs, friction-fit together with terrifying precision, grinding against one another with a sound like two millstones meeting. Their "torsos" are hollow cages of copper wiring and meteoric iron, perhaps scavenged from the tools left behind, wrapped around a core of pulsating, radioactive crystal.

They have no faces. There is no mouth to eat, no nose to breathe the non-existent air. In the place where a head should be, there is simply a clustering of mica sheets and sensory quartz, vibrating so fast it creates a visible heat haze. They do not see light; they "hear" the texture of the world. They perceive the ridges of the salt flats and the sharp edges of the glass-blossoms through echolocation and seismic resonance. To look at them is to feel a headache blooming behind your eyes, a sympathetic vibration in your own skull.

The Choir lives on dissonance.

The deafening roar of the planet is not just noise to them; it is a banquet. You will see them huddled over a particularly violent fracture in the earth’s crust, where the tectonic plates shearing against one another produce the highest pitch. They splay their copper-wrapped arms wide, absorbing the raw sonic violence. The vibration charges the crystals within their chests, causing them to flare with a brilliant, painful luminescence. They are capacitors for the planet’s birth pangs, storing the energy of the roar to fuel their own locomotion and cohesion.

They are the architects of the "weirdness." The reality glitches—the vanishing horizons, the sudden shifts in gravity—are not natural phenomena; they are the byproducts of the Choir’s digestion. When they consume a massive amount of sonic energy, the waste around them reacts. The fabric of reality buckles under the weight of the processing. A walking member of the Choir creates a wake of spatial distortion, leaving trails of floating dust motes and patches of inverted gravity where they have passed.

And they are building.

The primitive huts, the dry-stone shelters, the channels carved into the crystal trees—these are not ruins. They are active work sites. The Choir uses the meteoric iron hammers and diamond chisels left by their precursors (or perhaps by their own earlier, softer iterations) to tune the landscape.

They act with a slow, devastating purpose. They position themselves at the nodes of the sound, the points where the vibration becomes a standing wave. They hammer the obsidian, shaving off microns of rock to alter the frequency of the hum. They carve the quartz trees to change the pitch of the tinkling leaves. They are trying to harmonize the scream. They are attempting to turn the chaotic, crushing noise of the planet’s birth into a structured, sustainable song.

They are terrifyingly fragile in their invulnerability. If you were to silence the roar—if you could bring a moment of true, absolute quiet to the Resonant Wastes—the Lithic Choir would instantly collapse. Without the sonic pressure holding their mineral joints together and charging their internal lattices, they would fall apart into harmless piles of rock and copper. But silence is the one thing this place cannot offer.

Sometimes, at the "dusk" of the dim, sourceless light, you see them gather. They do not communicate with sound; the roar makes that impossible. Instead, they link their copper-limbed arms, creating a closed circuit of stone and metal. They vibrate in unison, a synchronized standing wave that ripples through the salt flats, correcting the pitch of the world. In these moments, the flickering stops. The ground feels solid for a heartbeat. The horizon stays where it is supposed to be.

In that heartbeat, you realize they are not just living in the waste. They are the waste, trying to understand itself. They are the raw, jagged nerves of a planet that is still learning how to be a world. They are lonely, hungry gods made of gravel and light, eternally tuning a song that no human ear could ever hope to survive.
