To look for life here is to misunderstand the nature of the Grid. The observer, conditioned by the chaotic biology of other worlds, scans the horizon for movement—for the darting of a bird, the scuttle of an insect, the distant shadow of a grazer. They see none. They conclude the place is sterile, a biological desert where only the mindless pumping of chlorophyll exists.

They are wrong. The silence is not empty; it is holding its breath.

The inhabitants of the Chlorophyll Grid do not live *on* the world, nor do they live *in* it. They *are* the components of it that have been granted the terrifying luxury of agency. If this place is a factory floor, then its citizens are not the workers, but the specialized tools—the wrenches that can tighten themselves, the levers that can decide when to pull.

They are called the **Vascular Attendants**.

At first glance, an Attendant is invisible. It mimics the geometry of its surroundings with such perfection that the human eye slides right past it. It stands upright, rooted to the cork-pavement by a mass of heavy, fibrous tendrils that look for all the world like the base of a trunk. Its "body" is a pillar of lignin, rough and plated with bark, indistinguishable from the pillars that form the buttresses of the great cathedrals. It possesses no discernible face, no eyes to capture the blinding light, no mouth to eat. It is a statue of wood and geometry, perfectly still, locked into the Euclidean grid.

It is only when the observer stops moving and waits—wait for the infrasonic thrum of the world to rise and fall—that the distinction becomes clear.

An Attendant does not move quickly. It moves with the tectonic patience of the world itself. To see one shift is to watch erosion happen in fast-forward. The bark along its "limbs"—which are long, jointed branches that hang suspended in the arid air—does not flex; it slides. The plates of wood expand and contract, releasing a fine, golden dust of dry lubricant. Slowly, over the course of an hour, the Attendant rotates its upper torso. It is realigning itself with the light, or perhaps with the magnetic resonance of the grid.

They are the immune system of the Engine. In a place where no animal exists to spread disease or pests, the threat is internal: blockage, structural fatigue, and the entropic creep of inefficiency.

When a leaf-vein ruptures, spraying a mist of pressurized hydraulic oil into the thin air, the Attendants nearest to the breach do not rush. They *activate*. You might see a patch of moss on a nearby pillar suddenly darken, the texture shifting from soft velvet to hardened, interlocking scales. This "moss" is actually a cluster of sensory tendrils. It tastes the oil in the static-charged air.

Then, the Attendant begins to walk.

It is a horrifying thing to behold. The roots that anchor it to the ground tear free with a sound like heavy canvas ripping. The Attendant hauls its massive, tree-like bulk forward, not by stepping, but by extending its root-legs and contracting its woody spine. It creates no rhythm, no footsteps—just a continuous, grinding slide across the root-mat.

It reaches the rupture. It does not heal it with sap; it *mechanics* it. The Attendant extrudes a ribbon of hard, black polymer from a slit in its torso—a substance similar to scab tissue, but dense as tar. It wraps the leaking vein, tightening the ribbon with the precision of a ratchet strap. The pressure equalizes. The leak stops. The Attendant then reabsorbs the spilled oil through pores in its lower trunk, recycling the energy, wasting nothing.

There is no brain in these creatures, not as we understand it. Their nervous system is distributed throughout their cellulose, a web of chemical logic gates. They do not "think." They *process*. They are algorithms made of flesh, executing a maintenance routine that has lasted for eons.

And yet, there is something else here. Something rarer.

If one were to climb the great living ziggurats, miles above the floor, where the air is thin enough to freeze water instantly and the bioluminescent glow turns the green structures a ghostly teal, one might find the **Architects**.

These are not the builders; the Grid built itself. The Architects are the *quality assurance*. They are spindly, impossibly tall entities that stand at the intersections of the grid, where the massive glass-leaves interlock to form the ceiling. They are almost translucent, their bodies composed of a stiff, gelatinous chitin that refracts the searing white light into rainbows. They have no legs; they are suspended in the mesh of the canopy by hooked claws that pierce the leaf-flesh.

The Architects do not move to repair. They move to *curate*.

Their heads are domes of sensory crystal, packed with photoreceptors that can see the spectrum of heat and radiation. They watch the "factory floor" below with an alien, cold detachment. When a section of the grid begins to grow wild—when the Euclidean perfection is threatened by a chaotic surge of new buds—the Architect intervenes.

It descends from the canopy on a strand of silk-strong vine. It does not prune the growth; it *selects* it. With surgical mandibles that click silently in the vacuum, it cuts away the mathematically imperfect branches, severing them from the main system. The discarded wood falls to the floor miles below, where the Attendants will dismantle it and feed it back into the root-matrix.

In this way, the Architects enforce the geometry. They are the editors of the world, ensuring that chaos never gains a foothold. They are the reason the horizon remains flat and the lines remain straight.

But the true strangeness of the inhabitants is not found in what they do, but in what they *collect*.

Because there are no animals here, there is no bone. There is no stone. The only hard materials are wood and thorn. Yet, if one were to break open the hollow trunk of a dormant Attendant, they would find a cavity lined with impossibly smooth spheres.

These are "Pearls of Inefficiency."

Occasionally, the Grid produces a mutation. A leaf grows that is too soft. A root curls in a non-linear spiral. A flower blooms that is the wrong shade of gold. These are errors in the code of the world. The Attendants do not destroy these errors. They encapsulate them. They wrap the defective tissue in layer after layer of hard lacquer, preserving the flaw like a gem.

The Attendants keep these pearls inside their bodies. When two Attendants meet—a rare occurrence, usually only to trade genetic material or share structural loads—they press their chests together. Internal cavities open, and they show each other their collections of imperfections.

It is the only thing in this perfect, mathematical, blinding engine that resembles art. It is the only act of tenderness in a universe of consumption. They hoard the mistakes, cherishing the tiny, chaotic absurdities of existence, keeping them safe in the dark, hollow spaces of their wooden bodies.

So, the inhabitants are not the beasts of the field. They are the gardeners and the ghosts, the mechanical limbs of a planet that decided long ago to be a machine. They are silent, they are slow, and they are watching you—even if you cannot see the eyes in the wood.
