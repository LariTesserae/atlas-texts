They are called the **Bark-Striders**, and they are not so much a civilization as they are a precarious infection upon the skin of a world that is constantly trying to heal over them.

Physically, they are shaped by the abrasive claustrophobia of the Hollow. They are small—rarely exceeding four feet in stature—a necessary adaptation for moving through the press of the "tangle-weed." Their bodies are ropy and dense, stripped of excess fat to survive the sweltering heat. Their skin is the texture of cured leather, calloused into a hardened armor that resists the slashing thorns and the desiccating air. They do not sweat visibly; any moisture they release is a precious resource, so they have evolved to radiate heat through the massive, thin ears that flatten against their skulls when the roar of the forest becomes too loud.

Their faces are almost entirely hidden behind **Beetle-Carapace Masks**. These are not mere ceremonial garments; they are respirators carved from the hollowed-out heads of the giant obsidian beetles that roam the floor. The mandibles of the mask are held shut by leather cords, forcing the Strider to breathe through a series of filtered siphons that trap the lung-choking dust. The eyes of the mask are polished lenses of chitin, allowing the Strider to see without risking blindness from the blowing grit or the blinding flatness of the Glow-Slate above.

They live in the **Vertical Scaffolding**, a network of fragile platforms woven from dried, dead vine-fiber and lashed with the sinew of the flying ray-things. These hovels cling to the sides of the giant plant-towers like barnacles on a whale’s belly. There is no "down" for a Bark-Strider once they leave the cradle; the floor is a place of death, where the beetle-rivers flow and the ground itself rises to crush the unwary. Their existence is a constant, athletic negotiation with gravity. They move with the desperate, jittery agility of spiders, using hooks made of bone to catch the ridges of the bark-scalloped towers.

Their society is defined by **The Fluid Economy**. There is no water, only the Resin. The Bark-Striders are addicted to the thick, viscous sap that pulses through the giant stalks. It is sterile, hyper-oxygenated, and painfully hot, but it is the only hydration that exists. Harvesting it is a communal act of violence. Using stone axes tipped with diamond-sharp glass, they score the bark of the towers, dodging the explosive sprays of sap. When the wound opens, the resin gushes out, scalding hot, and they must catch it in hollowed gourds before the air hardens it into amber. To drink it is to burn the throat, but the burn is the only proof of life.

They are a people perpetually on the verge of being **consumed**. The Calcified Hollow does not tolerate stagnation. A Strider cannot sleep for more than a few hours at a time. If a platform is left untended for half a day, the tangle-weed grows through the floorboards, weaving itself into the weave of the huts. If they stand still for too long on a branch, the bark will begin to swell around their boots, fusing their feet to the tower. They are constantly pruning, constantly cutting, constantly moving. Their clothing is stitched from the shed skins of the flying beasts, and it is tattered and constantly in need of repair, much like their sanity.

Perhaps the most defining aspect of their existence is **The Red Fear**. The Bark-Striders know that the plants are aware. They have seen what happens when one of their own falls. A Strider with a broken leg is abandoned instantly—not out of cruelty, but out of terrifying necessity. Because the moment blood is spilled, the chemistry of the Hollow changes. The green-grey tangle-weed turns a bruised crimson. The serpentine vines stop their random reaching and lash toward the scent of iron and salt. The beetles change direction, sensing the open wound. To bleed is to send out a dinner bell to the entire forest. Therefore, the Striders are obsessed with containment. They use cauterizing irons heated on the rocks for even the smallest scratch, and their healers are also their executioners, swift to end the life of anyone who compromises the safety of the perch.

They are a silent, twitchy people, even in the midst of the deafening roar. Their communication is a series of sharp, complex clicks and taps against their masks, amplified by the bone of the beetle-heads. They do not scream; the noise of the forest is too loud for screaming to matter. They endure the tectonic grinding, the blinding light, and the dry heat with a stoicism that borders on the catatonic, watching the floor rise beneath them, forever climbing, forever pruning, forever stealing just enough life from the towering giants to survive one more day in the oven.
