They are called the Suture-Kin, though they possess no kinship with anything living, and they are not so much born as they are minted from the engine’s excess.

At first glance, they are merely another part of the chaotic industrial collage—a trick of the failing amber light, a shadow cast by a swinging cable. But watch the drip of condensation closely as it runs down a blackened iron stanchion. You will see the shadow move against the flow of gravity.

The Suture-Kin are small, no larger than a house cat, yet they possess a density that suggests they are made of solid neutron matter or perhaps the compressed history of the machine itself. Their bodies are segmented, overlapping plates of russeted brass and verdigris copper, articulated not by pins, but by a viscous, grey sludge that oozes from their joints like heavy grease. This sludge is identical to the semi-liquid slag that comprises the emerging walls; they are mobile droplets of the Engine’s embryonic fluid, given independent will.

They have no distinct head. At the anterior end of their segmented bodies, there is a cluster of articulated sensory rods—antennae made of stripped wiring and glass tubing—that constantly taste the air. They do not see the space; they feel it. The vibration of the bass thrum is their language, and the taste of ozone is their sustenance. They move with a eerie, fluid grace, pulling themselves along the pipes and rivets using limbs that are too many to count easily—some are hooked pikes for gripping, others are delicate manipulation claws ending in flat-head screwdrivers or soldering irons.

They are the agents of the "emerging" quality of the Gut.

Where a wall transitions from solid iron into the oozing, pre-formational slag, a Suture-Kin will be there. They do not flee the light, nor do they seek it. They exist in a state of perpetual, obsessive labor. One hangs upside down from a steam vent, its many legs locked securely into the metal grating. It is "eating." Its primary mandible—a rotating, rasp-like file of hardened steel—scrapes away the layers of old, dead paint and corrosion from a pipe. It consumes the decay, internalizing it, the metal dissolving into its own grey sludge body.

Then, it scuttles a few inches to the left to where the wall is soft, half-formed like wet clay. The creature extends a proboscis, injecting the reclaimed material back into the structure, smoothing it with a spatula-like limb. It is tending the geology. It is knitting the machine together, speeding up the process of industrial renewal. They are the white blood cells of this vast, artificial womb, attacking the entropy of rust and repurposing it into new growth.

There is no hierarchy among them. They are a solitary phenomenon, though they often work in proximity. If two meet on a crowded pipe, they pause. Their sensory rods intertwine, sparking with tiny, blue arcs of static electricity. They exchange data—a schematic of a pressure leak, the location of a new vent shaft—and then dispassionately continue their work. They do not breed. They do not sleep. They simply function.

And they are changing.

Because the Engine is building itself, the Suture-Kin must adapt to the new blueprints. On a freshly extruded section of wall made of a strange, polished polymer not yet seen in the Gut, a Suture-Kin is stuck. Its old brass legs cannot grip the smooth surface. It struggles, the vibrations in its chassis rising from a hum to a whine. But the Engine provides. The creature begins to dissolve its own legs, the grey sludge retracting, bubbling. Moments later, it extrudes new appendages—cupped suction pads made of a translucent, silicone-like material. It tests the grip, finds purchase, and resumes its scraping. It evolves in real-time to match the architecture it serves.

They are indifferent to the observer. If you were to reach out to touch one, it would not attack. It would simply analyze your hand, tasting the salt and warmth. It would likely categorize you as "foreign debris"—an anomaly to be either ignored or incorporated. It might attempt to scrape a layer of skin away to see if the material is useful for patching a leak, or it might simply solder a loose rivet to your knuckle, trying to fix you into the grid.

They are the silent midwives of the compartment. They ensure the density remains absolute. When the lights sputter and die for a long second, and the darkness is total, you can hear them everywhere—a symphony of tiny metal clicks, the wet sound of sludge moving through valves, and the rhythmic *scrape-scrape-scrape* of their rasps against the iron. They are the ones who ensure the space never finishes, ensuring the gestation continues forever, maintaining the beautiful, terrifying, eternal becoming of the Great Engine.
