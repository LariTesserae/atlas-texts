To live here is to be a creature of intervals. You do not run in the basin; you wait. You exist in the pause between the heave and the settle.

The primary residents of this wet, grinding bowl are a species of tall, attenuated bipeds known only as the **Frictionless**. They are an evolutionary response to the crushing indifference of the hydraulic earth. They stand perhaps seven feet high, but they are terrifyingly thin, their limbs skeletal and elongated, ending in digits that are spatulate and splayed, resembling the roots of the grey trees that choke the silos.

Their skin is not the soft, porous hide of a mammal, but a smooth, taut membrane of pale, bruised-grey slate. It looks like wet clay that has been fired in a kiln but left cold. This integument is impermeable to the constant oily sweat of the air, and it provides no purchase for the clinging moss or the rust. As they move through the mist, they are nearly invisible, save for the disruption they cause in the hanging vapors—a ripple in the damp air, a shadow cutting across the rusted plating.

They are blind. The heavy, metallic humidity and the near-total lack of distinct light made vision a liability eons ago. Instead of eyes, their faces are dominated by wide, vertical slits that run from the crown of the skull to the bridge of the nose—complex sensory pits lined with cilia that taste the changes in barometric pressure. They can feel the "weather" of the turbulence before it happens. They know when a granite plate is about to buck, or when a rusted pipe is about to vent steam, reading the vibrations through the soles of their wide, flat feet.

Their movement is a haunting thing to witness. Because the floor is in a state of perpetual demolition, the Frictionless do not walk with the expectation of stable ground. They move with a fluid, rolling gait, their knees bending backward to absorb the shock of a rising floor tile. When the earth heaves beneath them, they go limp, riding the crest of the movement like a piece of driftwood on a swell, then snapping back to rigid stillness the moment the ground settles. They carry long poles of scavenged iron—girders from the ruins of the factory wall—which they use to vault over sudden gaps or to brace themselves against the walls when the basin "exhales."

They dress in nothing, for they have no need for warmth in the suffocating humidity, but they are rarely seen unadorned. They cultivate the leathery ivy and the dark, bruised-colored moss, weaving it into their hair and draping it over their shoulders. This serves a dual purpose: it masks their mineral scent from the predators of the deep mud, and it allows them to blend perfectly into the ruins when they stand motionless. A Frictionless standing against the tilted brick of the silo is indistinguishable from the architecture itself.

And then there are the **Sump-Striders**, the "livestock" of this sunken world, if such a hierarchical word can be applied to such a chaotic place. These are the slick, dark shapes glimpsed darting between the plates. They resemble salamanders, but bloated to the size of hounds, with oversized, flattened heads and vestigial, useless eyes. They are the garbage collectors of the basin. They possess a highly acidic saliva that allows them to dissolve the rust and the lime that fuses the bricks to the rock, licking the surfaces clean and excreting the fine, grey mud that coats the floor.

The Frictionless tolerate them, and occasionally, when a plate shifts and crushes a Strider, the Frictionless will harvest the carcass. But mostly, the relationship is one of mutual indifference. The Striders clean the ruins; the ruins provide the Striders with shelter.

At the very top of the sparse food chain, hidden in the darkest recesses of the inactive drive shaft in the center of the basin, lives the **Grand Archivists**. These are rarely seen, ancient specimens of the Frictionless who have grown too heavy to move quickly with the shifting plates. They have fused with the machinery. Their skin has calcified, merging with the iron of the drive shaft, their bodies becoming anchors for the others. They do not eat. They sit in a permanent state of meditative hibernation, their sensory pits wide open, processing the deep, velvety hum of the subterranean machine. They are the memory of the place, recording the cycle of the piston, the slow, wet breath of the world, keeping the rhythm of the chaos.

Life here is not about conquering the environment; it is about synchronizing with it. To survive the basin, you must become another component in the great, buried engine. You must become part of the rust, part of the heave, part of the wet, silent demolition.
