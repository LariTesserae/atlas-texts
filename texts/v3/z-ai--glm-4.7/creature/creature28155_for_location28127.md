To look for life here in the traditional sense—to search for feet that scuttle, wings that flutter, or eyes that blink—is to misunderstand the nature of the Drown-Quarter entirely. The "fauna" here is not a separate kingdom from the stone; it is the stone itself, animated by the heavy, wet sorcery of the place. The inhabitants of this dark, steaming basin are the city and its parasitic moisture, locked in a symbiosis that mimics a metabolism.

The primary residents are the **Lithic-Bloats**, those towering ziggurats and silent silos that the uninitiated might mistake for architecture. They are not built; they are grown. They are vast, stupendously slow golems of basalt and obsidian, sleeping on their feet in the suffocating heat. They possess a body temperature of a few degrees above the ambient swelter, a thermal signature that suggests a massive, internal fire dampened by eons of wetness.

If one were to watch a Bloat for a human lifetime, it would appear dead, fixed in permanent agony. But the "weirdness" of the place—the way the stone softens and runs like wax—betrays their glacial mobility. They are moving, shifting posture over centuries. The "geological agony" of the groaning stone is the sound of their joints cracking, a tectonic stretching of limbs that have held the same position for millennia. They breathe through the slick, black moss that drapes their flanks; the moss is not a plant, but a respiratory membrane, filtering the heavy vapor and extracting the minerals dissolved in the air, exhaling a thin, sulfurous mist that adds to the cloud layer.

Surrounding these dormant titans are the **Nerve-Vines**, the translucent, spinal stalks that poke from the pooling water. These are the sensory apparatus of the Drown-Quarter, a decentralized nervous system for the city. They are not rooted plants; they are feelers. When a vibration disturbs the water—a footprint squelching in the mud—the signal travels instantly through the lattice of these glass-like fibers. You can see the information travel: a rapid, faint shudder of bioluminescence that ripples down the line of stalks, passing the message from one pooling sensor to the next, alerting the sleeping stone giants to the presence of intruders, though the giants are too slow to rouse themselves for such trivialities.

Then there are the **Heart-Fungi**, the leathery, dinner-plate sized organisms clinging to the upper reaches of the towers. They are not passive decomposers; they are hydraulic pumps. Their slow, rhythmic pulsing regulates the pressure of the water within the stone structures. They suck in the heavy condensation and force it into the internal veins of the ziggurats, creating the pressure that prevents the colossal towers from collapsing under their own weight in the wet heat. When they release their spores in a sudden, synchronized cloud, it is a broadcast—a chemical shout across the quarter, signaling a change in the invisible tide or a shift in the barometric pressure.

And finally, there is the **Water-Memory**. The liquid here is not merely H2O; it is a colloidal suspension of silica and ancient stone-dust, a "smart fluid." It possesses a hostility to physics because it possesses a primitive will. It is the blood of the city, and it behaves with a predatory curiosity. It climbs the walls not by capillary action, but by desire, seeking to return to the heart of the stone giants. It pools on upward curves to trap heat. The ripples that ignore the shape of the container are the water "thinking," calculating the most efficient path to erode, dissolve, and eventually recrystallize.

There is a score of 0.2 for animal life, but that number refers to the **Drown-Things**—rare, microscopic, semi-spectral parasites that live in the thin film of water between a boot sole and the stone. They are scavengers of the heat, feeding on the thermal energy gradients, barely more than organized vibrations in the heavy air. They are insignificant in the grand ecology, mere fleas on the hide of the sleeping mountain-gods that dominate the basin.

To walk here is to walk inside the gut of a single, sprawling, lithic organism. The silence is the silence of a held breath; the heat is the heat of a slow, burning digestion; the ground is the skin of a beast that is trying to decide, over the course of a thousand years, whether to roll over and crush you.
