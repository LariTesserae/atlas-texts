The silence here is a lie. It is not the absence of sound, but the absence of *flesh* to interpret it. To look out over the bruised purple expanse of this sunken plateau is to see a landscape devoid of biology as we understand it, but it is not empty. It is merely patient.

What lives here is the Geology of Patience.

To understand the inhabitants of the deep, you must first look at the "marks of the Built." Those neolithic cairns of stacked basalt, the smooth-walled cylinders sunk into the rock like wells—they are not ruins. They are not left behind. They are inhabited. They *are* the inhabitants.

This is a species of **Lithic Endurance**. They are not born; they are *incubated*.

They begin their existence deep within the volcanic vents, where the superheated water churns with silicates and heavy metals. In the dark, under crushing pressures that would instantly vaporize a human diver, they accrete. Layer upon microscopic layer of glass and iron-oxide deposits around a core of unstable, hyper-dense gas. They grow slowly, the way stalagmites grow, sculpting themselves into the columnar, blocky shapes seen scattered across the ridge lines.

By the time they are "mature"—a process that takes millennia—they have assumed the form of the rough-hewn monoliths you mistook for cairns. They stand hundreds of yards apart, isolated in the crushing dark, mimicking the architecture of the stone. To the fleeting glance of an intruder, they are merely geology. But they are thinking, listening stone.

They do not possess faces. They have no need for eyes in a world where light is a mere phosphorescent decay. Instead, they perceive the world through **vibration**. Their entire mineral structure is a vast, complex ear. They feel the low-frequency groan of the tectonic plates not as a sound, but as a tremor in their "bones." They feel the shifting currents as a caress against their crystalline skin.

This is why they stand where they do. They position themselves in the path of the vertical rivers, the cascades of freezing brine that fall from the ice shelf above. These brinefalls are their blood. The dense, salty water forces its way into the micro-fractures of their bodies, creating a hydraulic pressure system that animates them. They do not eat; they circulate the cold. They filter the heavy metals out of the water, reinforcing their own carapaces, growing heavier, denser, more permanent.

Their movement is terrifying to witness because it is so rare. They do not skitter. They *drift*.

When the thermal plumes rise from the abyss—the shimmering distortion waves that twist the light—the Lithic Endure detach from the seafloor. Using the heat differential between their superheated cores and the freezing water, they create localized thermal engines. They lift. They float with the grace of a falling mountain, moving only to adjust their position in the choir, to find a new current, or to escape the slow, grinding advance of an underwater landslide.

And they do sing.

The sound you hear—the deafening, harmonic whine of the Iron-Kelp singing in the current—is not just noise. It is a dialogue. The Lithic Ones hum. Through their internal channels of circulating brine, they resonate at frequencies that match the towering glass plants. They harmonize with the groaning of the ice. When a plume of heat hits a glass giant and the pitch shifts, the nearest stone figure will shudder, adjusting its own internal mineral stress to match the note.

They are the tuning forks of the deep. They are maintaining the frequency of the world.

The "wells" you see sunk into the rock faces are their wombs and their graves. When a Lithic One becomes too heavy, too burdened with the accumulation of eons, it drags itself into a well. There, it anchors itself to the living rock, fusing its outer shell to the cliff face. It releases its core of unstable gas into the water, seeding the next generation of accretion in the vents below, while its body becomes a permanent part of the tunnel, a filter for the currents.

There is a sadness to them, but it is not a human sadness. It is the cold sorrow of the inevitable. They are the ones who visited and could not leave, so they chose instead to become the place itself. They have traded the fragility of breath for the permanence of basalt. They endure the weight so the weight does not crush them.

To drift among them is to walk through a graveyard of gods who are still, somehow, alive. If you were to press your hand against the smooth, cold flank of one of those cairns, you would feel a faint, rhythmic thrumming—not the beat of a heart, but the cycling of the oceans, the slow, hydraulic digestion of an entire world.

They are the silent shepherds of the glass forest. They do not fear the dark, for they are the dark made manifest. And they are waiting, with the infinite patience of stone, for the ice above to finally melt and the sky to fall, so that they may sing their final note into the void.
