They are the dust of the machine made animate, the only living things capable of enduring the crushing, monotone heart of this valley. To the unadjusted eye, they are invisible—a smear of rust against a hill of slag, a jagged shadow on the flank of a piston. Only when they move does the observer realize the landscape is not quite as static as it seems.

These creatures are low-slung and multi-legged, possessing a body plan that echoes the geometry of the engines they inhabit. They resemble crabs in their segmentation and gait, but there is nothing organic about their silhouette. Their carapaces are not made of chitin, but of a dense, ceramic-like armor that mimics the oxidation hues of the valley floor—burnt sienna, matte ochre, and deep, flaking rust. Over millennia, they have evolved to look exactly like the discarded tailings of the industry around them.

They move with a distinctive, clockwork gait. It is not the fluid motion of flesh, but a jerky, rhythmic progression—step, pause, click, step. This movement is perfectly synchronized with the thrumming of the earth; they do not fight the resonance, but ride it. When the great piston on the northern ridge heaves upward, these creatures freeze, locking their legs into the sediment as if they, too, have become stone. They wait for the ten-second pause, the exhale of the world, and only then do they scuttle forward another few inches.

There is no obvious head to speak of, no face to meet the gaze of a stranger. The front of the creature is a smooth, armored slope broken only by sensory tufts—stiff, wire-like filaments that detect changes in air pressure and thermal gradients. Where eyes might be, they instead possess patches of heat-sensitive cells that glow a dull red when they are near the steam vents. They do not need to see the white-gold sun; they navigate by the infrared topography of the warm ground.

They are the interstices given life. They live exclusively in the narrow gaps between the massive, shifting gears, dwelling in the shadows cast by the rising pistons. Here, the humidity is thickest, and the gray-fuzz grows in velvety patches. This "fungus" is their primary sustenance. They use specialized, grinding mandibles hidden beneath their armored bellies to scrape the mineral lattice from the rocks, consuming the trace metals concentrated within the fuzz. As they feed, they make a sound like sandpaper on stone—a faint whisper that is entirely swallowed by the bass note of the valley.

But the gray-fuzz is not enough; they require the heat of the vents to fuel their slow metabolisms. A creature will often be seen splayed out over a hissing fissure, its shell vibrating with the escaping steam. They are, in a sense, thermophiles, biological batteries that charge themselves on the exhaust of the world. The warmth penetrates their ceramic shells, diffusing through a network of internal tubes filled with a conductive, copper-colored fluid.

Their social dynamic is non-existent. They are solitary components, scattered sparsely across the basin, maintaining a respectful distance from one another. If two meet at a grazing patch, they do not fight or communicate; they simply rotate around one another, avoiding collision like gears in a well-oiled transmission, and continue their respective routes. They have no predators, for there is nothing else here to hunt, and they have no prey, for they are not hunters. They are merely the maintenance crew of an eternal factory, biological custodians that clean the mineral dust and recycle the heat.

There is an ancient quality to them that suggests they may have been built, not born. Perhaps, long ago, they were autonomous drones designed to service the engines, and over the eons, they simply grew soft, biological interiors and forgot their original programming. Or perhaps they are the descendants of something that crawled out of the sea to find the land already conquered by iron, forcing them to evolve into iron themselves. They blur the line between animal and automaton so thoroughly that to dissect one would be an act of philosophical violence.

When they die, they do not rot. They simply stop moving. The rust encrusts them fully, the gray-fuzz grows thick over their joints, and they freeze into the pose of their final clockwork step. Eventually, the grinding tectonic shift of the machines crushes them into the sediment, their bodies returning to the slag from which they came, indistinguishable from the stones, completing the terrifyingly stable loop of this warm, loud, and lonely world.
