They are the Children of the Condensation, beings shaped as much by the dripping iron and the basalt scree as by any biological imperative. To look at them is to look at the city itself made mobile; they do not merely live in the Sprawl, they are its migrating cells.

The dominant denizens are tall, gaunt figures that move with a fluid, boneless grace, defying the rigidity of the brick and mortar that surrounds them. Their skin is the colour of wet slate—pale, translucent grey that reveals the dark, sluggish pulse of veins beneath. These veins carry not red blood, but a thick, oily ichor that smells faintly of ozone and copper, reacting to the magnetic hum of the massive gears grinding beneath the floorplates. They are nearly hairless, save for patches of coarse, moss-like fur that sprouts along their spines and shoulders, a symbiotic colonization that serves to camouflage them against the neon-green slickery of the smokestacks.

Their faces are devoid of noses, mere smooth slits flanked by eyes that are enormous, black, and reflective, adapted to catch the feeble, diffuse light (2.2) that filters through the perpetual cloud cover. These eyes see in gradients of heat and moisture, allowing them to navigate the dense, choking fog where visual distinction is impossible. They do not speak with mouths; their jaws are fused, covered by a hard, beak-like carapace used for scraping nutrient-rich lichens and grease-moss from the pipes. Instead, they communicate through a series of subsonic thrums and clicks that vibrate through the soles of their feet and the metal decks they walk on, sensing the replies of others through the sensitive pads of their elongated, three-fingered hands.

Living alongside them are the "Pipe-Runners," smaller, swarming creatures that fill the ecological niche of the rodent, yet are something far more disturbing. They are sleek, wet-furred beasts with overly developed forelimbs and hooked claws that allow them to scale vertical iron with terrifying speed. Their skeletons are partially external, reinforced with plates of metal scavenged from the city’s refuse—iron filings fuse to their cartilage as they grow, creating a natural exoskeleton of rust and steel. They move in vast, silent tidal waves, pouring out of the ventilation shafts to feast on the softer fungal blooms that erupt around leaking steam valves. When they swarm, the sound is not the chitter of rats, but the wet slapping of a thousand wet objects sliding against metal, a sound that mimics the city’s own internal plumbing.

Higher up, nesting in the lattice of cranes and the skeletal ribs of unfinished towers, live the Avians. They are not birds in the gentle sense; they are scavengers of the thermals. Their feathers are sleek and coated in a natural oil that repels the acidic damp, giving them the sheen of beaten lead. Their wings are leathery and broad, designed to ride the turbulent updrafts created by the massive cooling vents. They possess sharp, serrated beaks capable of cracking the hardened shells of the industrial gourds that grow water towers, drinking the putrid, sweet water inside. Their eyes burn with a phosphorescent green gaze, piercing the grey mist, and their cries are piercing, metallic shrieks that echo against the cliff-faces of masonry, momentarily cutting through the muffled silence (2.6, 0.8) of the basin.

But the true strangeness of the population lies in the integration of the technology (2.5) with the biology. There are entities here that are neither fully machine nor fully flesh. They are the "Valve-Walkers," humans or near-humans who have undergone a terrible, voluntary adaptation to survive the verticality. They have replaced their atrophied legs with rusted, hydraulic pistons scavenged from the industrial floor; their nerves splice directly into copper wiring. They move with a jerky, mechanical gait, steam hissing from the valves implanted in their shoulders, releasing excess heat from the furnaces of their metabolisms. They tend to the high-pressure lines that no organic limb could withstand, their faces serene masks of acceptance, their eyes clouded over with cataracts formed by the constant stare into the white heart of industry.

And then there are the "Root-Weavers," keepers of the vegetation (1.7). These are shamans of the sprawl, draped in heavy, waterlogged coats made from the cured leaves of the giant ferns. They encourage the parasitic ivy to grow in specific patterns, training the aggressive creepers to bind cracks in the masonry or to throttle a broken piston that threatens to explode. Their skin is stained green from the chlorophyll-rich sap they drink, and their hands are swollen with thorns that have grown *into* their flesh, becoming permanent, living tools. They do not see the city as a machine to be fixed, but as a garden to be pruned, a living wound that must be kept wet and open.

All of them, from the highest Valve-Walker to the deepest Root-Weaver, share a common posture: a hunched, attentive curl. They are listeners. In a place of silent turbulence, they have evolved to feel the world. They feel the shift of tectonic plates through the soles of their feet; they taste the change in pressure on the air. They are a nervous, twitchy populace, vibrating with the same unstable energy as the city itself, forever on the verge of flight or repair, caught in the eternal, damp churn of creation.
