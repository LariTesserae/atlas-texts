At first glance, the rating holds true: the fauna is nonexistent. To a casual observer glancing down from the rim, or even one standing waist-deep in the steam on the basin floor, there is no movement of legs, no beating of wings, no chitter of claws. The industrial roar seems to have sterilized the air, a sonic scrubber that has scraped the world clean of anything that might dash or dart.

But the absence of the familiar does not mean an absence of life. It means that life here has had to adapt not just to the heat or the wet, but to the absolute dominance of the machine. What lives here does not live *on* the rock or *in* the water; it lives in the friction between them.

If you were to stare at the great, rusted piston jutting from the basalt wall—the one shuddering with the rhythmic thrum of the engine’s heart—you might eventually notice a discrepancy in the corrosion patterns. The rust is not uniform. It clumps in certain regular, geometric shapes that defy the chaotic run-off of water.

These are the **Lithic-Mimics**.

They are not animals in the sense that anything above ground would recognize them. They are solitary, sessile organisms that look less like biology and more like a mistake in the geology. They appear as flattened, asymmetrical slabs of dark, pitted stone, roughly the size of a dinner plate, clinging with terrifying adhesion to the vertical faces of the machinery or the slick basalt walls.

They possess no eyes. In a world where the light is stark and revealing, sight is a vulnerability. Instead, their "skin"—a tough, silica-based carapace that feels like rough sandpaper—is a continuous sensory organ. They do not hear the deafening noise of the machine; they *drink* it. The roar is their food. The vibrations that would drive a human mad are translated through their bodies into rhythmic pulses of energy. They are connoisseurs of the thrum, positioning themselves on the piston housings where the frequency is most potent, basking in the seismic warmth of the engine like lizards on a sunlit rock.

Their movement is invisible to the human timescale, rated strictly by the agonizing 0.6 of the basin’s dynamics. They do not crawl; they flow. Over the course of a day, a Lithic-Mimic might slide its way three millimeters along a gear shaft, following a particularly nutritious vibration. To watch them is to watch paint dry, but if one were to employ time-lapse, you would see a slow-motion migration, a flock of living stones drifting aimlessly along the bones of the dead factory.

Feeding is a grotesque, slow-motion process. They are opportunistic filter-feeders of the machine’s decay. Where the "weeping" water runs down the black iron, carrying microscopic traces of oil, heavy metals, and hydraulic fluid, the Lithic-Mimics unfurl microscopic feeding tubes from their underside. They press these tubes against the wet metal, scraping up the sheen of industrial grime as if it were algae. They internalize the toxicity, binding the heavy metals into their shells, which is why their carapaces shine with the dull, poisonous iridescence of spilled gasoline.

Reproduction is an event of supreme stillness. When the humidity peaks and the air is thick enough to choke a lung, a Mimic will release a cloud of spores—silicon-heavy, metallic dust—into the steam. These spores do not float far; they are heavy, dragged down by the wet air to settle on the sharp scree below. There, they must wait for a "lucky" accident: to be splashed onto the moving machinery by a falling drop of water, or to be picked up by the shifting of a tectonic plate and pressed against the warm, vibrating side of the engine. Most die, crusting into useless mineral deposits. But those that find purchase on the machine begin the slow calcification, growing their first shell, becoming part of the landscape of arrested violence.

There is a secondary, smaller inhabitant, too, barely worth mentioning but vital to this microscopic ecosystem. Look closely at the puddles gathering in the crevices of the basalt, atop the islands of bilious green algae. You will see tiny, metallic shards—spring-like creatures no larger than a fingernail. These are the **Gear-Mites**.

They are the clean-up crew, evolved from something ancient that has since shrunk and armored itself in plate-steel scales. They possess multiple hooked legs that allow them to navigate the knife-edge ridges of the broken rock without cutting themselves open. They are deaf, vibrating sensors on their legs alerting them to danger. They scavenge the waste of the Mimics—the metallic dust that falls—and feed on the neon algae, their tiny, mandible-like mouths moving with frantic speed, the only things in the entire basin that seem to operate in real-time.

But even they are slaves to the rhythm. When the great engine lets out a high-pitched hiss of steam, the Gear-Mites freeze instantly, locking their legs against the rock, flattening their bodies until the steam passes. They move in frantic bursts between the long, agonizing pauses of the machine.

Together, they form a closed loop of consumption and decay. The machine bleeds oil and heat; the rock provides the sharp sanctuary. The Mimics adhere to the machine, eating its vibration and its grime, shedding metallic dust. The Mites eat the dust and the algae, and in turn, are sometimes crushed by the slow, grinding movement of a Mimic, becoming a smear of organic paste that feeds the algae.

It is a kingdom of patience. No one sings here. No one runs. The Lithic-Mimics cling to their vibrating thrones, and the Gear-Mites tick like clockwork second-hands in a world where the hour hand never seems to move. They are the ghosts in the machine, not haunting it, but keeping its decay warm.
