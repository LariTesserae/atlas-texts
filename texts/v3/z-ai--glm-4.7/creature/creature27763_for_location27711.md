At first, they appear to be nothing more than extensions of the stone itself—silent, grey sentinels standing motionless against the backdrop of the dripping cliffs. But as the eye adjusts to the perpetual, unchanging twilight of the altitude, the distinction between architecture and anatomy begins to blur, revealing the beings who tend to this suspended world.

They are tall and elongated, their physiology seemingly dictated by the verticality of their home. Their limbs possess an impossible reach, spanning distances that would require a human to climb or leap, allowing them to navigate the tiered city with a slow, spider-like grace. Their skin is not the soft flesh of lowlanders, but a dense, parchment-like substance that mirrors the color of the wet slate. It glistens perpetually, slick with the same damp sheen that coats the mountains, as if they have absorbed the moisture of the air into their very pores.

They are draped in crude, heavy garments made from the pelts of the six-legged grazers, stitched together with gut and bound by the rawhide lashings that characterize their architecture. But these are not clothes worn for warmth alone; they are burdens. The furs are weighted with smooth, heavy stones sewn into the hems, pulling the shoulders down and grounding the wearer. Perhaps this is to combat the thin air, to anchor them to the precipices, or perhaps it is simply to match the gravity of the place. They do not walk so much as they glide, a shuffling friction of hide on stone, their heavy robes whispering against the granite like dry leaves.

Their faces are smooth and remarkably flat, almost mask-like. There are no protruding noses to catch the freezing wind, only two small, dark slits that serve as nostrils. Their mouths are narrow, lipless seams, rarely opened. It is their eyes that command the most attention: large, liquid, and devoid of the white sclera found in other creatures. They are pools of absolute black, reflecting the jagged peaks and the endless cloud sea below, absorbing the light without giving anything back. They do not blink often, adding to the illusion that they are carved statues rather than living things.

They are the Keepers of the Equilibrium.

Given the city’s strange stasis—the fixed water levels, the non-shifting shadows—these beings do not live in the linear progression of time. They exist in a looping present. A Keeper might spend an entire day standing on a narrow ledge, observing a single drop of water as it forms at the tip of a stalactite. They do not move to catch it; they simply watch. When the drop falls, striking the basin below with a chime that rings clearly through the thin air, the Keeper nods, a slow, deliberate tilt of the head. Then, they turn and begin the long, rhythmic climb to the next basin to witness the exact same phenomenon.

Their society is devoid of the bustle of construction or creation. They do not build new towers, for the old ones do not decay. They do not forge new tools, for the iron rivets of yesterday hold as firm as the iron of tomorrow. Their labor is entirely dedicated to *maintenance* and *observation*. They can be seen on the vast, empty boulevards, polishing the moss that grows on the rooftops—not removing it, but grooming it, ensuring it remains at the precise, fluorescent thickness that the equilibrium demands. They use primitive tools: smooth river stones used to rub the timber, cloths woven from fern fibers to wipe the condensation from the windows.

There is a profound loneliness to them, yet it is not a sadness. It is a solitude enforced by the physics of their world. Because they do not age visibly—their skin does not wrinkle, their backs do not bow with the years—they have no concept of an elder or a youth. A being who has stood on the highest rampart for a century looks identical to one who has just climbed from the cloud-forests below. They recognize one another by the patterns of scarring on their heavy robes or the specific arrangement of stone weights they wear.

Communication between them is rare and non-verbal. To speak would be to disturb the respiration-rhythm of the wind. Instead, they communicate through gesture and position. If a bridge needs securing, a Keeper will stand at its center, leaning slightly against the rawhide bindings. Another passing by will feel the vibration of the intent through the soles of their feet and stop to assist, tightening the lashings without a word exchanged. It is a hive mind of silence, a collective understanding that the city is a living clock, and they are the hands that must not move.

When they sleep, they do not lie down indoors. They retreat to the wide, open courtyards and strap themselves to the stone pillars using their belts, leaning back against the rock. They enter a state of hibernation so deep it mimics death, their heartbeats slowing to match the dripping of the distant water. In this state, they become part of the architecture, their grey skin blending into the granite, their stillness matching the grazing beasts nearby. When they wake, it is not with a start, but with a slow inhale, as if they are resuming a sentence they never finished.

They are terrifyingly patient. A human observer might scream in frustration at the lack of change, at the eternal now, but the Keepers are content. They do not hope for the clouds to part to reveal the world below, nor do they fear the clouds returning. They simply *are*. They are the custodians of a pause, the monks of a moment that refuses to pass, forever watching the water bead and fall, forever waiting for a change that will never come.
