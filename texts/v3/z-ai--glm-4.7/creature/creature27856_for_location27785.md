The life here begins with the sound—a surging, tidal roar of a million tiny impacts against metal. It is the sound of the world being eaten, and then, immediately, being worn down again.

Primary among the inhabitants are the **Chitter**, a collective term for the vast, omnipresent arthropod swarm that forms the baseline of the ecosystem. They are not merely insects; they are biological mining equipment. Their carapaces are composed of a dense, steel-blue chitin that mimics the very rivets and beams they cling to, providing them with natural camouflage against the grey lattice. The smallest of them, no larger than a human fist, possess mandibles designed not for cutting flesh, but for shearing metal. They feed on the "softness" of the world—the velvet curtains of oxidized iron that hang from the gantries. You can see them lining the pipes in columns thousands deep, their legs twitching in a rhythmic, synchronized dance as they scrape away the rust, consuming the iron oxides to fuel their own metallic bodies. They leave behind trails of polished, silver streaks on the pipes, the only shiny things in this dull place.

Above the scrappers are the **Strainers**, larger, cousins of the Chitter that have evolved to exploit the humidity. They perch atop the weeping vents and pressure valves, their bodies swollen and distended with water. They possess filaments resembling matted steel wool that they drag through the oily steam, filtering out the metallic particulates and the traces of algae that grow in the warm, wet dark. They are the water-bearers of the High Rust, and when the predators come, it is the Strainers that are the first to be harvested, bursting with hot, mineral-rich fluid.

But the true definition of this place lies in the giants, the things the locals simply call "The **Trundles**."

These are the "living tanks" mentioned in the lore of the rust, massive beasts that have given up flight and speed for invulnerability. They are the size of the ancient mining trucks, built low to the ground with shells that appear to be made of nothing more than layered slag and plate steel. In truth, their shells are biological ceramic composites, grown over decades of calcification. A Trundle does not walk; it grinds. Its legs are stubby, piston-like pillars that end in flattened, ridged pads, allowing it to distribute its immense weight over the uneven, treacherous flooring of the High Rust.

The Trundle is an eater of the swarm. It moves with a terrifying slowness, glacial and inevitable, plowing through the dense clusters of the Chitter. It does not chase them; it simply moves into their midst and waits. Its head is a shovel-shaped bone structure, lined with vents that exhale a thick, yellow fog—a pheromone signal that pacifies the smaller insects. As the Trundle trundles forward, the Chitter climb onto its back, mistaking the massive beast for a stationary geological feature. It is then that the Trundle engages its secondary apparatus: a massive, retractable tongue covered in bristles like a street sweeper, which it drags across its own back and the ground before it, scooping up thousands of the creatures in a single, slow swallow. The cycle is patient, measured in the slow turning of the gears beneath the floor.

Between these two extremes exists a menagerie of horrors adapted to the verticality of the city. The **Rail-Runners** are sleek, multi-limbed mutants that cling to the undersides of the tram-ways. They have evolved extreme visual acuity to pierce the pearlescent gloom, and their limbs are tipped with powerful magnetic pads that allow them to run inverted across the ceilings of the industrial canyons. They are the lone wolves of this world, snatching individual Strainers or unwary Chitter from the safety of the pipes.

Then there are the **Pipe-Eels**, elongated, sinuous creatures that have colonized the interior of the superstructure. They live inside the massive ducts, swimming through the rivers of condensed steam and lubricant. They are blind, navigating through sensitive vibration pits along their flanks that can detect the rhythmic *clank-hiss* of a piston from miles away. They breach the surface of the pipes only to lay their eggs in the warm puddles of the rust-hollows, wriggling masses of wet muscle that look alarmingly like discarded cables until they move.

And at night—or whatever passes for night in this shadowless grey—there are the **Mirrors**.

These are small, amphibious-like creatures that inhabit the stagnant billions of tiny still-pools created by the erosion. They are flat and disc-shaped, their skin a perfect, matte grey that reflects the diffused light. They lie motionless in the water, looking exactly like the rusted metal plates that surround them. But if a Chitter falls in, the Mirror snaps open, a radial of teeth erupting from its flat form to drag the prey under the oily surface. They are the trapdoors of the ecosystem, the final destination for anything that slips.

All of them, from the smallest scraper to the great Trundles, are coated in the dust of their world. They move with the viscosity of the air, their actions thick and deliberate. They are not fighting the machine; they are part of it. The Chitter cleans the rust that would otherwise choke the pipes; the Trundles keep the swarm in check; the corpses of the massive beasts eventually rot into the ferrous dust, becoming the soil for the next generation of rust-blooms. It is a closed loop of biology and industry, a nightmare symbiosis where the difference between a living creature and a mechanical component is merely a matter of time.
