In this place where sound is a physical blow and the air is a thick, metallic soup, life does not scurry; it adheres. If you were to stand on the grating and look past the rhythmic plunging of the skyscraper-sized pistons, past the stroboscopic flicker of the failing amber lights, you would see that the "ghostly movements" in the periphery are not tricks of the mist. They are the **Resonant Feeders**.

They are solitary creatures, rarely seen in pairs, evolved for the specific horror of the High Condensery. To look at them is to understand that nature has conceded the floor to the machine, but refuses to surrender the shadows.

They have no ears. Where a head would be, the sensory orifices are sealed over with thick, calloused plates of keratin and scar tissue. The roar that would liquefy the inner ear of a human being is simply ambient pressure to them; it is the ocean in which they swim. They do not hear the scream of the turbines; they feel the vibration of the metal in their bones, reading the complex symphony of the engine through the soles of their wide, splayed feet. They are deaf to the world, but intimately attuned to the machine’s pulse.

They have no lungs. The air here is too thin, too toxic, and too acidic for gas exchange. Instead, their bodies are covered in a lattice of wet, feathery frills that droop from their underbellies and the insides of their limbs, constantly glistening with the accumulated condensation. They breathe through their skin, filtering the ozone and iron particles directly into their bloodstream, excreting rust-red waste that drips from their elbows and knees. This is why they stay in the wet corners; to dry out is to suffocate.

Physically, they are spindly and tall, mimicking the verticality of the vault. Their limbs are elongated, possessing too many joints, allowing them to fold themselves into the narrow maintenance gaps between the riveted steel plates. Their skin is not the pink of flesh, but the mottled gray-blue of bruised iron, perfectly camouflaged against the sweating walls. When they press themselves flat against the machinery, they vanish, looking for all the world like a fresh weld or a patch of shadow.

They are blind in the traditional sense. The light is too poor, and the oily mist too thick, for eyes to be of use. Instead, their faces are dominated by wide, shallow pits lined with sensitive cilia that detect changes in air pressure. They can "see" the rush of the superheated steam seconds before a valve opens, allowing them to flatten themselves into safety niches before the scalding white fog obliterates the room.

They are scavengers of the "young" forces. They feed on the detritus of creation: the curlicues of copper wire left on the floor, the iron shavings, and the black, greasy moss that grows in the vibration-dead zones. Their primary source of sustenance, however, is the slurry that pools in the overflow channels—a mixture of lubricants, condensation, and dissolved metals. They lower their elongated, neck-less heads into the chemical stew and drink, filtering out the toxins that would kill a lesser beast.

They are the mourners of the Condensery. When the pistons plunge and the room shudders, the Feeders will often gather near the main pressure valves—not out of curiosity, but to bask in the heat radiating from the friction. They stand perfectly still, vibrating in sympathy with the engine, their feathery gills fluttering in the draft. In the amber gloom, they look like abandoned workers waiting for a shift that will never end, or perhaps the spirits of the place, given flesh by the damp and the rust. They are the only things that do not fear the noise, for without it, they would be hungry and cold in the dark.
