They are the Architects of the Slack, a people who do not walk so much as drift. To call them human is to accept a definition stretched thin by the environment; they are elongated beings, their physiology drafted by the relentless pull of gravity and the demands of the vertical.

At first glance, they seem brittle. Their limbs are disproportionately long, possessing an extra joint in the arm and leg that allows them to fold inward, hooking and clamping around the wet, glassy obsidian of the Fingers. They possess the tensile strength of a spider’s silk but the density of a dried reed. Their skin is not the opaque barrier of a terrestrial human, but a translucent membrane, pale as mist, through which one can see the slow, dark pulse of blood. It is perpetially slick, coated in a mucus that protects them from the sharp, un-eroded edges of the basalt—a world where a single slip against the raw stone means a fatal slice into the abyss below.

Their faces are dominated by sensory input evolved for the dim, tea-colored twilight. Their eyes are vast, black orbs that absorb what little phosphorescence trickles down from the fungal shelves, lacking pupils and seemingly adapted to detect movement through the murky density rather than detail. But it is the ears—or rather, the lateral lines running down the sides of their necks and continuing along their forearms—that define them. In the High Sump, where the thick air swallows sound, these people listen with their bones. They can feel the thrum of the deep-water leviathans through the soles of their feet, interpreting the groans of the geologic uplift as a language of pressure and vibration.

They are a people of the knot and the braid. Their technology is manual, yes, but it is also an extension of their own biology. They weave with the fervor of spiders. The "hair" on their heads is not dead keratin but living, muscular tendrils that they can voluntarily splice into the hanging vines. When they work the braided lianas of their hanging cities, they are not just tying ropes; they are grafting themselves to the architecture. An adult Sump-dweller often spends so long suspended in their hammock-like nests that their legs atrophy slightly, while their arms and gripping digits become powerful enough to crush the porous wood of the local flora.

Socially, they are solitary clusters. The physical strain of supporting a large population on the needle-thin spires is impossible, so they live in small, interdependent family units clinging to the sides of single Fingers. Communication is a private affair—a complex system of tugs on the web of bridges that connect the spires. A sharp jerk on a sinew cable signals danger; a rhythmic plucking announces a harvest of bloated air-vines.

Because the land rises, pushing them upward into the sky in a slow, geologic creep, their culture is obsessed with the ritual of "The Severance." As the spire rises and the waterline effectively lowers (or rather, the distance to the abyss increases), the settlements must cut their anchorings to drop back down toward the life-giving humidity. This is a solemn event. They gather with tools made of polished bone to slice through the thick, woven roots that bind their homes to the rock, lowering the entire platform inch by inch in a controlled, terrifying descent. It is a constant cycle of building and unbuilding, a rejection of permanence. To build something permanent here is to watch it rise into the cold, dry upper atmosphere where the air is too thin to breathe.

Their relationship with the dead is perhaps their most defining trait. The Sump preserves everything. Below the waterline, visible through the murky tea when the light is right, are the ancestors. They do not bury their dead; they lower them. They wrap the departed in a shroud of woven peat and weight them with heavy stones, sinking them into the mineral-rich brine. There, the bodies remain—perfect, suspended, and eerily present—in the "Under-City."

It is common practice for a grieving relative to descend on a rope of hair-moss to sit face-to-face with a preserved mother or father from a century prior. The living will press their forehead against the water, perhaps to share the vibrations of the living world with the silent dead, or perhaps to borrow tools that were lost to the deep. The past is not gone; it is simply ten feet below the surface, waiting for the water to drop or the land to rise another few inches to reclaim it.

And then there are the few—the drifters—who choose a different path. When a gelatinous leviathan, a filter-feeder the size of a cathedral, drifts near a spire, the bravest (or most weary) of the people will leap. They carry massive, barbed harpoons made of sharpened basidian, not to hunt, but to anchor. They embed these hooks into the gelatinous hide of the beast and secure their floating gourds to its back. These "Riders" abandon the static struggle of the rising Fingers for a nomadic life on the back of a living island, drifting through the twilight, trusting that the beast will not dive too deep, carrying their fragile civilization into the crushing dark. They are the true nomads of the Sump, moving at the agonizingly slow pace of the ocean itself, untethered from the rising rock, belonging only to the buoyant, heavy breath of the world.
