They are a people carved from the same silence as the stones, the High-Keepers, or simply the Spine’s Children. To look at them is to look at a creature designed by the unyielding logic of the vertical world.

They are short, but not in a way that suggests frailty. Rather, they are compressed—stocky, barrel-chested, and dense, with a center of gravity so low they could probably sleep hanging upside down from the rafters of their loftiest towers. Their limbs are thick and ropy, muscles wound tight under layers of insulating fat, evolved for the singular purpose of hauling their own weight—and the weight of the stone walls they live within—up sheer inclines where the air is too thin to support the ambitions of lowlanders. Their skin is the color of a bruise, a pale, ruddy purple that flushes dark in the cold, a permanent adaptation to the violet altitude and the blood-thinning rarity of the atmosphere.

Their faces are broad and shielded, dominated by immense nasal cavities—warmed passages for the razor-cold wind before it burns their lungs. Their eyes are black, liquid, and wide, possessing a tapetum lucidum that catches the dim starlight, allowing them to navigate the absolute darkness of the unlit tunnels. They lack the whites of eyes that other races possess; theirs are pools of obsidian that reflect the icy world around them, making it impossible to read emotion, granting them a perpetually stoic, unreadable demeanor.

Movement for them is a study in friction and patience. They do not walk; they flow with a slow, deliberate undulation. Because a slip means a fall of thousands of feet, every step is a calculated event. Their boots are masterpieces of crude engineering, soles carved from yak-horn and lined with the fur of the great white rats, spiked with iron scavenged from meteorites or the rare vein of ore. When they climb, it is less like a human scaling a wall and more like a spider returning to its web—their fingers are long, tipped with hard, calcified nails that can hook into the smallest cracks in the basalt.

They are swaddled in layers. Clothing is not fashion here; it is architecture. They wear robes of layered mountain-goat hide, treated with oils and rendered fat until they are waterproof and stiff as parchment. Underneath, next to the skin, they wear woven nettle-wool that smells faintly of woodsmoke and the herbs they tuck into the lining to repel the biting lice of the high peaks. Around their necks hang the tools of their trade: chisels made of flint, small leather pouches of precious salt, and bone-carved idols of the "Deep Below"—the underworld they view with superstitious terror.

The society is hive-like, mirroring the city they inhabit. Privacy is a foreign concept; families exist in "cells," small cubicles carved into the rock, stacked one atop the other. The inhabitants pass each other in the narrow vertical shafts with a tactile, silent language. A touch of the shoulder means "move aside," a grip of the forearm means "brother," and a hand over the eyes means "storm coming." They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are low rumblings, resonant in their broad chests, designed to travel through the solid rock of the walls rather than the thin air. Shouting is reserved for the rare, catastrophic events—a rockfall, a breach in the dam—because the wind carries sound too far, and noise is energy wasted in a world of scarcity.

Their relationship with the fauna is symbiotic and brutal. The yak-like beasts are not just livestock; they are partners. The High-Keepers share their hearths with these animals during the deepest freezes, the body heat of the beast saving the family from hypothermia. In return, the beasts are milked, sheared, and eventually, when their strength fails, eaten. The goats that graze the rooftops are tended by children who scamper across the slate like spiders, learning the balance that defines their lives. Even the white ravens are semi-domesticated; the Keepers hollow out spaces in the walls for them to nest, and in exchange, the birds bring back nesting materials and act as an early warning system for the shifting weather patterns of the jet stream.

They are a people of the long memory. Because everything moves slowly—the growth of a wall, the accumulation of peat, the carving of a new tunnel—they think in generations. A mason might begin a structure knowing his grandson will be the one to lay the capstone. This glacial pace has stripped them of urgency. They possess a calmness that borders on the comatose to an outsider. They can sit motionless for hours, watching the slow drip of condensation into a cistern, meditating on the cycle of water and stone.

They are not conquerors. They do not expand. They endure. They are the lichen on the rock, made flesh—stubborn, silent, and utterly unshakeable, clinging to the Spine while the world below turns and fades.
