To live here is to be soft. It is to abandon the rigid exoskeletons of the ground-world below and to become something that yields, something that absorbs. There are no beetles with hard, clicking shells here, for the humidity would rot the armor in a season. Instead, the biology of the plateau is composed of gelatin, velvet, and membrane. The creatures here are silhouettes cut from wet cloth, things that drape and flow rather than scuttle or sprint.

The primary citizens of this suspended continent are the **Weavers**. They are not spiders, but something akin to sloths that have evolved into the niche of arboreal construction. Their fur is long, coarse, and saturated with water, heavy enough to drag along the ground when they descend from the canopy. They move through the dense foliage with a heartbreaking slowness, their long limbs ending in digits that are less like fingers and more like gentle hooks. They do not climb; they *melt* upward. Their purpose is the cultivation of the moss. They carry spores in the dense mats of their backs, spreading the green velvet across the smooth bark of the Growth-Pillars. To look at a Weaver is to look at a walking piece of the forest floor; they are indistinguishable from a clump of moss until they turn their large, liquid dark eyes toward you. They eat nothing but the runoff water and the slower-growing parasites, filtering the air through gills hidden in their neck flaps.

Beneath the canopy, in the perpetual dimness where the light is a bruised purple, live the **Resonators**. These are the frogs of legend, bloated and silent. They are the size of hunting shields, their skin translucent and stretched tight over floating bones. Through this skin, you can see the slow pulse of organs glowing with a faint, bioluminescent amber. They do not hop; they are too heavy for that. They drift through the spongy peat, swimming through the earth as if it were water, leaving behind smooth tunnels that close up within minutes. They are the waste-eaters, the great digesters of the plateau. When a leaf falls, it is swallowed by the earth; when a Weaver dies, it is consumed by the Resonators. They emit no croak, only a low, infra-sound thrum—a vibration that travels through the root systems of the Growth-Pillars, a heartbeat that keeps the forest time.

Higher up, in the vertical city of leaves, glide the **Mist-Dancers**. These are the birds, though they possess no true feathers. Their wings are shaped like the petals of a tropical flower, vast and thin, stretched over delicate bones of cartilage. They are colored in varying shades of slate grey and pale blue, perfectly camouflaged against the twilight mists. They do not flap; they possess a nearly neutral buoyancy in the thick air. They steer by shifting the weight of their bodies, riding the thermals generated by the heat of the decomposing peat below. They are silent as ghosts, and they feed on the aerial plankton—the clouds of spores and drifting pollen that turn the air into a thick soup. To watch a Mist-Dancer feed is to watch a ribbon of smoke caught in a draft, opening a mouth that splits its head in two to sieve the air.

And then there are the **Architects**, or what remains of them.

This is the intelligence that carved the rough-hewn monoliths and arranged the stones in circles that are now sinking into the muck. They are not gone. They have simply slowed down so much that they have become indistinguishable from the geography. They are tall, thin creatures that stand upright, their skin resembling the smooth, pale bark of the Growth-Pillars. They stand in groups of three or four, sometimes for decades at a time, rooted into the spongy soil by broad, flat feet that have fused with the mycelial networks.

They call themselves the **Still-Walkers**.

Their society is one of geology. They do not speak with sounds; they communicate by chemical exchange through the root-mats they are connected to. A thought can take a week to travel from one individual to the next, but in this timeless place, that is the speed of conversation. They remember the stones. They remember when the sky was blue and the air was thin. They wait for the horizon to bend back. They tend to the stone circles not with tools, but with their own hands, secreting a slow-dissolving acid that smooths the rock, keeping the edges perfect against the encroaching moss. They are the gardeners of history, fighting a losing war against the entropy of the jungle, moving with such glacial grace that to the human eye, they are just another part of the dark, breathing architecture.

Everything here is a sleeper. The life is sessile, dormant, waiting. The insects are large, bloated things that hang from the underside of leaves, pulsing slowly. Even the bacteria in the water weeping from the trees moves in a slow, rhythmic cycle.

It is a world where the distinction between the living and the dead is a matter of perspective. A fallen tree is a corpse, but it is also a feast, a home, a sponge, a pillar. A Still-Walker is a person, but it is also a stone. The air itself seems alive, heavy with the spore-dust of a billion generations waiting to settle. To be a creature of this plateau is to accept that you are not a separate being from the world around you; you are just a different arrangement of the same warm, wet, suspended matter, a temporary shape in the endless, slow-motion ocean of the sky.
