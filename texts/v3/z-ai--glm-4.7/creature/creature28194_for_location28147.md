To live in the Drip-Quarter is to exist in the interstices of the machine, to become a component as passive and necessary as a O-ring or a settling tank. If one watches the dark, corrugated mouths of the hovels long enough, the stillness resolves into a slow, rhythmic pulsing—not of machinery, but of biology slowed down to match the geologic tempo of the pistons.

The inhabitants are known, if they were ever known by anything at all, as the **Silt-Stalkers**.

They are not a noisy people. Noise implies a waste of energy, and energy is a finite resource in the dark. They are tall, gaunt figures, their physiology stretched thin by the low-gravity pull of the deep earth and the meager nutrition of the caves. Their skin is the color of wet slate, smooth and cool to the touch, possessing a texture that seems to repel the oily water rather than absorb it. Over this, they wear layers of scavenged canvas and rubber, stitched together with copper wire, draped around their frames like the shed wings of the Wraith-Weavers that hang above.

Their faces are devoid of the sharp angles of surface dwellers. High, prominent cheekbones shelter deep-set eyes that have evolved past the need for color. They see only in gradients of pressure and heat, the bioluminescence of the moss appearing to them not as green light, but as a warmth in the darkness. Their noses are mere slits, designed to filter the heavy air, tasting the chemical composition of the moisture rather than just smelling it.

The Stalkers move with a terrifying fluidity. Because the environment is so cramped and treacherous, they do not walk so much as they flow. Their joints are hyper-flexible, allowing them to fold themselves into the gaps between rusted pipes and curl up on the narrow ledges of the runoff channels. When they step, they place their feet with the precision of a Rust-Mite, the heavy,-soled boots of their making absorbing the impact so completely that they disturb not a single ripple in the puddles they tread.

Their society is built not on hierarchy, but on proximity to the filtration systems. Those who dwell in the lowest shanties, nearest the sump where the water turns "holy," are the Keepers. They are the oldest, their skin almost translucent, their movements slowed to a near-paralysis that mimics the turning of the great turbines. They spend their lives in a state of meditative observation, watching the slow drip of the purified water, ensuring that the intake valves of the machines remain free of the black lichen.

The younger Stalkers—those who still retain a flicker of quickness—are the Scavengers. They climb the vertical shanties like spiders, venturing out onto the slick walkways spiraling the silent pistons. They harvest the pale, translucent mushrooms that grow in the rust-cracks, careful never to take the root, allowing the fungus to regrow for the next season. They are also the ones who maintain the lucifer-panels, carefully scraping away the corrosion to keep the faint, erratic light alive. They do this not for the sake of sight, but to guide the Wraith-Weavers, who they view not as animals, but as divine messengers.

There is a symbiotic understanding between the Stalkers and the avian Weavers. When a Weaver releases its pressurized hiss and glides from a conduit, the Stalkers below stop, freezing in mid-motion, tilting their heads to listen. They interpret the pitch and duration of the steam-release as omens—warnings of shifting pressures in the deep rock or the slow approach of a seismic tremor. In return, the Stalkers often leave scraps of metallic slag and mineral-rich deposits on the higher perches, feeding the metallic cravings of the birds.

They do not speak. The larynx of a Silt-Stalker is atrophied, replaced by a secondary vocal sack in the throat that allows them to produce a low, resonant thrumming. This hum can be felt through the metal grating more than it can be heard. Through these vibrations, they communicate the status of the Quarter: the thickness of the sludge, the temperature of the air, the speed of the invisible turbine blades.

To eat, they process the sludge that the Rust-Mites scrape from the pipes. Using simple, hand-cranked presses made of scavenged gears, they squeeze the oily muck through fine meshes of wire, extracting the nutrient-rich bacteria and leaving behind the heavy metals. The resulting paste is eaten raw, a slick, grey gruel that provides the sustenance needed to survive the damp chill.

Their lives are defined by the "Great Pause." Because the machinery they guard operates on a cycle of decades, the Stalkers have no concept of a rushed day. A decision to move a village from one side of a piston to the other might take ten years of discussion, conducted in slow, vibrating hums during the quiet hours between drips. They are immortal in their patience, watching the rust spread like a slow disease, knowing that they, too, are slowly oxidizing, their bodies destined to eventually become part of the conglomerate slag of the walls.

When a Stalker dies—a rare event, usually caused by a fall into the unfiltered sludge pits—they are not buried. They are taken to the central turbine and laid upon the great, crusted blades. There, over the course of years, the body is pressed flat by the imperceptible force of the machine, their oils and waters joining the filtration cycle, their bones becoming sediment, their essence returning to the Drip-Quarter that made them. They are the audience of the machine, and in death, they become the mechanism itself.
