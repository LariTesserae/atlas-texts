They are the shadows that move when the arc-sodium lights flicker. To the casual observer, they are not there at all—mere tricks of the heavy, wet fog, patches of deeper darkness detaching themselves from the gloom of the pipe-works. But if you were to stand on the rusted grating of a high gantry and wait, watching the dripping rivet heads with a predator’s patience, you would begin to notice the discrepancies in the architecture. You would see that a cluster of pipes sometimes breathes. You would see that the pile of discarded slag near the sluice gate has limbs.

They are the children of the Cold Iron, born not of biology, but of the slow, magical corruption of industry. They are bipeds, roughly, though their proportions are an affront to nature. Their legs are long and spindly, constructed of exposed, metallic struts that resemble the scaffolding around them, ending in feet that are multi-jointed pincers designed to grip the slick, rounded surfaces of the pipes. Their torsos are hunched masses of plating that looks for all the world like patched boiler-steel—grey, pitted, and scarred by centuries of abrasive water.

There is no flesh on them, only a hydraulic musculature of black hoses and cables, bound tight by a "skin" that feels like wet sharkskin to the touch. They do not generate their own heat; they steal it. They are cold to the bone, matching the ambient chill of the cavern perfectly, exuding only the faint warmth of the fluids they pump through their internal veins.

Their heads are featureless, smooth ovals of reinforced glass, clouded by the eternal condensation, through which one can sometimes see the dull, pulsing glow of a central core—amber, like a dying lightbulb. They have no mouths to speak of, and no eyes to see the artificial twilight. Instead, they possess a sprawling array of sensory filaments that writhe like the whiskers of a catfish from the base of their skulls, tasting the air for copper, listening for the stress-fractures in the concrete, and feeling the vibrations of the thrumming pumps.

They are the custodians of this unfinished tomb. They move with a slow, agonizing deliberation, their movements accompanied by the screech of metal on metal and the wet slap of their manipulators against the tank walls. They do not walk so much as they climb, navigating the vertical abyss of the vaulted cavern with terrifying ease. They cling to the sheer concrete faces, their magnetic limbs anchoring them to the "young" rock, picking their way through the dark ivy of the pipe complexes.

Their society, if it can be called that, is centered entirely on the maintenance of the decaying machine. They are the parasites that keep the host alive, not out of altruism, but because the machine is their environment. Where a seal fails and black oil begins to weep, they gather. They descend in clusters from the high gloom, their pincers clicking together in a rhythmic, industrial percussion that mimics the drip-drop of the condensation. They consume the leaked sludge—the black tears of the machine—converting the toxic hydrocarbons into the fuel that powers their internal hydraulics. They clean the stains, patch the leaks with crude, welded slabs of scrap metal, and clear the drainage grids of debris.

They are intelligent, but their intelligence is alien and singular. They do not dream of the sun or the surface. They do not wonder about the architects who built this place. Their world is defined by pressure, flow rate, and structural integrity. To them, the massive thrum of the pumps is a holy language, a continuous sermon that dictates the rhythm of their existence. When the great sluice gates groan open, they pause whatever task they are performing, turning their smooth, featureless heads toward the sound, rapt in a state of religious ecstasy.

Occasionally, the pale, sightless birds flutter too close to the heat-exchange vents, seeking warmth in the freezing dark. The inhabitants do not chase them. They do not hunt for sport. But if a bird falls, stunned by the rotating vent fans, crashing onto the grating below, the custodians will swarm. They do not eat the bird; they have no use for meat. Instead, they pick the bones clean, stripping the feathers away with terrifying precision, and incorporate the lightweight cartilage into their own patchwork armor. Nothing is wasted here. The bird becomes part of the machine.

At night—though there is no night here, only the dimming of the arc-sodium floods during the shift cycles—they retreat into the hollows of the unlit chasms. They hang suspended from the darkest pipe-works, folded into tight, dormant shapes, indistinguishable from the machinery itself. In sleep, their cores dim to a faint ember, and their sensory filaments retract, leaving them as just more statues in the dark, waiting for the temperature to drop, for the steam to hiss, for the great heart of the cavern to beat once again.

They are the priests of a dead god, tending to a body that refuses to die, slowly rusting in the dark, beautiful and hideous in their absolute loyalty to the cold and the wet.
