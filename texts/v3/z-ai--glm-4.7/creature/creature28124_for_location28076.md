To see them against the backdrop of the grey, wet rock is difficult at first; they wear the landscape as a camouflage. They are a people of low silhouettes and muted tones, moving through the mist like the shifting stones themselves. There is no splash of color in their dress, no dyed linens or bright embroidery to catch the eye. Their clothing is constructed from the same stuff as their huts: heavy, untreated felts scavenged from the sparse, wiry vegetation of the surrounding wastes, and thick, ungainly hides cured in the smoke of peat fires. They are wrapped in layers that add bulk to their frames, making them appear wider, squatter, and more solid than they perhaps are, covered in mantles of oily leather that repel the constant weeping of the sky.

Physically, they are built for the friction of this place. They are not a tall people. Generations of navigating the low-ceilinged interiors of their stone dens and the wind-scoured hollows of the valley have selected for a compact, dense stature. They possess a cervical heaviness—their necks are thick, sloping directly into powerful shoulders, as if their heads are hewn from the same block as their torsos. This is a body plan designed to conserve heat and endure the crushing claustrophobia of the gullies.

When they emerge from the huts, they move with a peculiar, rolling gait. It is not exactly a limp, but a constant micro-adjustment, a fluidity of the knees and hips that allows them to absorb the treacherous shifting of the gravel underfoot. To walk here is to constantly dance with gravity; a straight, stiff spine would result in a twisted ankle within minutes. They seem to flow over the sharp rocks, their heavy-soled boots—wrapped in rawhide and tied with intricate, slippery knots—finding purchase where there appears to be none.

Their faces are maps of the geology. The skin is pale, almost translucent in the rare instances when the clouds part, but it is weathered into a coarse, parchment texture by the acidic, mineral-laden air. Cheekbones are high and sharp, echoing the jagged horizon, and eyes are set deep beneath heavy brows, shaded to protect against the glare of wet slate. These eyes are striking: dark and liquid, possessing a reflective, prey-like quality that is always scanning the upper ridges or the slick ground. Unwrapping them would reveal skin that is less like flesh and more like cured leather—thick, scarred, and numb to the biting cold. They are artisans of the immediate. Their dexterity is not one of fine filigree but of brute-force manipulation: gripping heavy, rough-hewn beams; mudding cracks in freezing water; torqueing gut-lashings until the fibers sing.

Socially, they operate as a singular, dense organism. Privacy is a foreign concept, impossible in a maze where sound travels through the damp air and thin walls. They speak little, and when they do, their voices are low, rumbling things that seem to vibrate in the chest rather than project through the air. Shouting is useless; it would be swallowed by the fog and the dripping stone. Communication is a language of proximity—shoulder brushes, the tap of a boot, the meeting of gazes in the gloom. They pass each other in the narrow gullies with a practiced, intimate choreography, pressing flat against the slick walls to let another pass, exchanging a breath that mingles in the cold.

Their diet mirrors the scarcity of the valley. They are processors of the tough and the bitter. You might see them crouched on the rooftops or the low platforms, picking at the stringy moss or prying up lichen with flat, chisel-like scrapers. They prize the "wire-shrubs," uprooting them to boil the nutrient-poor roots into a gelatinous mash that sustains them. The central granary, the squat tower that overlooks the bowl, is their holiest site, not because it is full of golden wheat, but because it contains the dried, processed cakes of moss and fungus—the only insurance against the day the mist turns to freezing rain and nothing grows.

There is a tension in their existence, a coiled readiness. Because the land is "actively becoming," the people are perpetually braced for the "correction." They sleep in shifts, and even in sleep, they seem to rest lightly. A sudden drop in the water level or a distant groan of the settling earth brings them instantly to their feet, not in panic, but in a rhythmic, disciplined response. They know that the stone teeth that form their doorway today might be their tomb tomorrow, so they treat the architecture with a deference that borders on the religious. They patch the cracks not with annoyance, but with a solemn duty, understanding that they are not building on the earth, but *in* it, clinging to the scab of a wound that has not yet healed.

They are a people who do not dream of the sun. They dream of stability. They dream of a day when the rock stops sharping and the water stops weeping. But until that day comes, they are the damp, silent curators of the young, harsh world, as jagged and enduring as the stones they call home.
