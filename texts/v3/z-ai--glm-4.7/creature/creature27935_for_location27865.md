To look for them is to look for a flaw in the weave of the world. In this place, where the distinction between architecture and organism has dissolved, the inhabitants are the knots in the wood, the imperfections in the jade.

They are few, and they are quiet, but they are unmistakable once you understand the texture of the gloom.

They are vertical creatures, elongated by the constraints of their tunnel-city. Imagine a salamander, but one that has been stretched by the gravity of the high peaks and compressed by the tightness of the burrows. They possess no bulk; they are composed of spindly limbs and a flattened, torpedo-shaped torso, designed to slip through the wet, interlocking "alleys" where the fungal-roots press too tight for a human chest.

Their skin is not the armored hide of a mountain dweller, but something porous, pale, and translucent—a gelatinous membrane that blushes with the color of the bruised-purple light. It glistens perpetually, slick with the same viscous condensation that coats the walls. To touch them would be to touch a living slug, cold and yielding. This permeability is their survival; they do not merely breathe the spore-heavy air, they drink it through their skin, osmosing the humidity that hangs in the stillness.

They are the "gargoyles" of this living cathedral. When they rest—which they do for long, agonizing hours to conserve heat in the 0.1-degree chill—they clamp themselves onto the spongy bark of the bryophyte-trees. They tuck their limbs beneath them, folding their elongated bodies into the hollows and natural recesses of the trunk until they become invisible. Their skin perfectly mimics the texture of the wet, fibrous moss, their closed eyes resembling nothing more than lichen blotches. You could stand inches from one, staring at a dark recess, and mistake the creature for a shadow cast by a dripping frond.

But their eyes, when open, betray them. They are vast, liquid pools of obsidian, domed and unblinking, designed to catch the faintest photon of the bioluminescent fungal shelves. There is no whites to the eyes, no sclera, just a deep, absorbing black that reflects the green pulse of the walls. They do not see the world as we do; they see the gradients of heat and the rhythmic flashing of the slime molds.

They move with a heartbreaking slowness. This is not the ambush stillness of a predator, but the cautious, fluid economy of the starving. Every motion is a calculation of heat loss. They creep along the "ceilings" of the root-tunnels, their digits tipped with hooked, sucker-like pads that make no sound against the wet wood. They hang upside down over the dark, rushing subterranean channels that cut beneath the root-mesh, dipping their snouts into the freezing water to sip at the microscopic debris carried down from the peaks above.

They are not hunters. The density of the biomass leaves no room for predation. They are foragers of the infinite small. They scrape the edible algae from the insides of the leathery canopies with spoon-like teeth. They harvest the "sweat" of the room, licking the nutrient-rich condensation that weeps from the bark. Occasionally, if a fungal shelf becomes too heavy and rots, falling into a tunnel below, they congregate in a silent, writhing mass—a parliament of limbs and damp skin—to dissolve the soft wood with enzymes secreted from their undersides, drinking the resulting slurry.

There is a profound loneliness to them. They do not call to one another; the air is too thick with spores to carry a song, and the damp walls swallow sound. Their society is tactile. When they meet in the tight squeezes of the "streets," they press their flanks together, exchanging chemical signals through their damp skins, reading each other’s health and mood in the oils that slick their bodies. A flicker of bioluminescence passes beneath their translucent skin—a subtle, Morse code of pale light visible only in the absolute dark of the lower hollows.

They are the architects of the silence. When the ice expands in the hollow stalks with a sharp *pop*, they do not flinch. They are part of the furniture of this place, the living tenants of a city built by instinct. To see one is to see a pale, wet hand gripping the edge of a dark doorway, watching you with those black, infinite eyes, before slowly, painfully, retracting back into the wet green dark.
