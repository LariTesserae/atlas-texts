The silence here is not empty; it is occupied.

If one were to watch the polished, slick expanse of the processional ways for long enough, the "emptiness" would begin to resolve into figures. They are difficult to see at first, their forms blending so seamlessly with the slate-grey basalt and the perpetual mist that they seem more like shadows given mass than flesh and blood. These are the stewards of the high terrarium, the architects of the stillness.

They are tall, elongated beings, standing upright on two legs but possessing a stoop that suggests a constant reverence for the sky above. Their height is difficult to gauge against the verticality of the peaks, but they tower over the silver-barked trees, likely reaching seven or eight feet in stature. Their skin is the texture and color of the wet stone itself—smooth, cool, and slate-grey, smelling faintly of ozone and damp earth. There is no hair upon them; instead, their heads are crowned with a series of low, ridged plates that run down the spine, resembling the geological strata of the mountains.

They are biologically distinct from the noisy, frantic animal life of the lowlands. They possess no fur to hold the heat, and no sweat glands to cool the skin; rather, their thermoregulation is external, reliant entirely on the heavy, humid blanket of the atmosphere. They breathe slowly, a deep, rhythmic expansion of the chest that occurs only once every minute or so, making them appear like statues carved from living rock. Their eyes are large, dark pools devoid of whites, adapted to absorb the diffuse, shadowless light. They do not blink often.

Physically, they are immensely strong, though their movement betrays no effort. The cyclopean architecture—those massive, mortarless blocks stacked with impossible precision—was moved by hands alone. Their fingers are long, double-jointed, and equipped with flat, spatulate nails that serve as chisels and trowels. They do not use metal; they have no need for it. They understand the fracture points of basalt and granite intuitively, sensing the microscopic fissures in the stone. When a building requires repair, or a new terrace must be carved, a worker will simply place their hand against the rock, apply a specific, resonant pressure, and shear the stone along the desired plane. It is a quiet violence, a surgical detachment of earth from mountain.

Their society mirrors the landscape: sparse, isolated, and orderly. They do not cluster in crowded markets or loud plazas. The "city" is less a hive of activity and more a circuit of meditation. A single steward might inhabit a district the size of a lowland village, tending to the water channels and the great mirror-pools with the devotion of a monk.

They are sustained by the water and the light. While they possess mouths, they are small, thin-lipped slits used primarily for the inhalation of the mist. They do not hunt; there is no prey here, and the presence of slaughter would shatter the sanctity of the equilibrium. Instead, they are cultivators of the "perfect specimens." They tend the massive ferns and the domed trees with ceremonial care. It is believed they absorb nutrients through their skin, immersed in the mineral-rich runoff of the mountains, converting the geologic energy of the young peaks into biological fuel. They are, in essence, living filters, processing the raw violence of the earth into a state of static grace.

Because there is no bird song, no insect hum, they have evolved a silent language. Communication occurs through a complex combination of subtle hand gestures and the tapping of fingernails against stone. A single tap against a granite block can carry a vibration for miles through the connected architecture, relaying messages of structural integrity or weather changes. To a human observer, they are locked in eternal, solitary contemplation, but they are deeply connected in a web of geological empathy.

They are immortal, or close enough to it that the distinction is meaningless. The "eternal recurrence" is not just a feeling in the air; it is their lifecycle. They do not die of old age; they simply erode. After centuries, a steward’s skin begins to weather, cracking like a drying riverbed. When they can no longer move with the required fluidity, they walk to the highest promontory, sit cross-legged facing the pale, washed-out blue sky, and enter a state of terminal hibernation. There, they moss over. Vegetation claims them. They become the lush, green outcrops that decorate the ledges, returning to the stone from which they were born.

In this place, time is measured not in hours or years, but in the slow polishing of a rock face by a hand that has known it for a millennium. They are the silence given form, the ghosts in the machine of the terrarium, ensuring that the rain falls, the water weeps, and the sharp mountains remain forever young.
