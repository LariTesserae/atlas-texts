They are the great, still sculpture of this place—an architecture of biology jammed into an architecture of steel. To call them a mere population is a misunderstanding of their density; they are a biomass, a solid-state tide of chitin and alloy that fills the gutters and clings to the vertical faces of the cooling towers like a crustacean cement.

Physically, they are a paradox of the organic and the engineered. They possess the segmented bodies of arthropods, but their limbs terminate not in the delicate pincers of nature, but in blunted, flattened tips that seem molded to fit the hexagonal tiles of the floor. Their carapaces are not the smooth, glossy shells of a crab, but are instead pitted and ridged, resembling cast iron or rough-hewn ceramic. These are the **Caisson Dwellers**, though they possess no name for themselves, for they possess no language. They have no mouths in the traditional sense; they do not eat, nor do they hunt. The "vegetation" of grey leathery fungus that coats the lower strata is their lungs. They breathe through osmosis, their porous undersides pressed tight against the warm, damp metal, absorbing the thermal energy and the microscopic spores that drift on the heavy air. They are thermophiles, creatures of the heat gradient, feeding on the waste warmth of the sleeping engines.

The most striking feature of their existence is their total lack of urgency. Here, in the humid embrace of the factory-world, the concept of "speed" has died. They live in a state of glacial torpor. A single step might take a decade. The raising of a claw is a geological event. Because of this, they have grown together. The masses piled in the depressions are not merely resting upon one another; they have fused. Where the wet shell of one touches the wet shell of another, a slow, calcification process occurs, welding them into precarious, living towers. To disturb one would be to topple a column of them.

They are not dead, however. If one listens with the right instrumentation—or simply presses a hand against the damp, slick carapace of a truck-sized specimen—one can feel the thrum. It is the "dynamic zero," a vibration so deep and slow that it feels like a muscle twitching beneath the skin. It is a heartbeat measured in hours per beat, a circulation of thick, syrup-like ichor that moves at the pace of cooling lava.

Their sensory world is likely a dim, suffocating map of pressure and heat. Without eyes, they navigate the corrugated landscape of the factory floor through the vibration of the water droplets. They can feel the *drip-drip-plink* echoing through the metal plates, sensing the topography of their prison through the conductivity of the steel. They know where the pipe-valves are because the pipes hum with a slightly different frequency than the floor plates.

And they are waiting. This is the dominant emotion projected by their silent, interlocking forms. They are designed for purpose. The hydraulics that bulge beneath their plating suggest immense latent strength; claws that could shear through the hull of a tank hang limp and open, caught in a moment of suspended readiness. They look like a workforce that has just clocked in, or an army that has been frozen on the march.

They are the ultimate consumers of this stagnant perfection. The factory produces heat; the Caisson Dwellers consume the heat. The factory weeps water; the Dwellers filter the water. They are the biological filtration system for a machine that has stopped moving but cannot stop running. They are the keepers of the stasis, clinging to the sharp edges of the world, locking claws with their neighbors in a silent, eternal pact to hold still, to endure the simmer, and to wait for a command that will never come.
