They are there, though to call them "inhabitants" implies a separation from the room that does not truly exist. They are the third element in the binary ecology, the strange synthesis of the vegetable silence and the mineral roar. If one looks carefully at the "wide gaps" between the machines, where the steam curls and the light fails, the shadows there resolve not into empty corners, but into tall, stoic silhouettes that stand as still as the pillars themselves.

At first glance, they appear to be nothing more than auxiliary equipment—odd, anthropomorphic supports clad in the same heavy, riveted iron as the rest of the facility. They stand upright, elongated and spindle-limbed, their forms wrapped in thick, trailing drapery that looks like saturated insulation felt. This padding is dark, stained to the color of dried blood by the constant drip of the rusty water, and it hangs heavily from their shoulders, obscuring any distinct shape underneath.

But they are not machines. When a blast of superheated steam belches from a vent, they do not rattle with the vibration of the pipes; they absorb it. They are the dampeners.

They possess a skin that is not skin, but a porous, bark-like texture, capable of drinking the moisture that slicks the walls. Their bodies are incredibly hot to the touch, radiating a feverish warmth that matches the sweltering air. They do not sweat like animals; they transpire. The water that rolls off the gantries beads on their surfaces and is instantly drawn into their systems, cooling their internal workings in a perpetual cycle of evaporation. They are creatures of thermodynamics, living condensers that maintain the delicate heat balance of the sunken cathedral.

They have no faces. Where a head should be, there is only a smooth, curved helmet of organic carapace, sloping back into the hood of their insular robes. They do not need eyes in this gloom, nor ears in this cacophony. Instead, they possess a sensory web that runs through their limbs and the soles of their feet. They feel the world through the floor grating. They know the precise RPM of the massive tectonic gears by the trembling of the deck plates; they taste the ozone content of the air through the pores of their hands. They "see" the machinery by feeling its magnetic fields and the harmonic discordance of its friction.

Their movement is terrifyingly slow, a glacial motion that is easily mistaken for the shifting of settling metal. They do not walk so much as they flow in sync with the pistons. When a massive arm swings to weld a seam, one of these figures will be standing nearby, its long, multi-jointed fingers resting lightly on the conduit. As the spark flies, the creature sways, absorbing the excess current, grounding the surge. They are the immune system of the place. They prune the aggressive ferns with surgical precision, snapping the thick, waxy stems that grow too close to the rotating drives, yet gently guiding the root systems of the moss into the sound-dampening panels where the vibration is most nourishing.

They are solitary. There is no hierarchy, no communication between them. The silence of the room is absolute because they share a hive mind of purpose without the need for social contact. They are distributed nodes of a single consciousness, each one a custodian of a specific sector. One might spend an entire century standing on a suspended platform, its hand resting on a single pressure valve, perfectly still, its internal chemistry slowly adjusting to the shifting pressure of the deep earth.

Over the eons, they have become part of the erosion. The rust that patinas the walls has begun to crust over their own felt coverings, fusing them to the railings they lean against. Moss grows in the creases of their elbows. They are halfway to becoming stone, halfway to becoming plant, fully dedicated to the service of the engine. They do not eat, and they do not sleep; they simply exist in a state of constant, low-level maintenance, their vital signs synchronized with the thrumming heartbeat of the floor.

They are the keepers of the rhythm. If the great gears ever faltered, they would be the ones to intervene—not with tools, but with their own bodies, throwing themselves into the breach to bridge a gap or cushion a blow, sacrificing their physical form to keep the ancient song playing. They are the ghostly maintenance crew, silent, blind, faceless, and utterly, terrifyingly immortal.
