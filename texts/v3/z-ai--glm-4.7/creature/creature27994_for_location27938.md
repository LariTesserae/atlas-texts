To exist in the Hollow of the Dry Aeon is to be a master of tension and vibration. The fauna here is negligible—a statistical rounding error of 0.2—but what remains is not biological in the wet, squishy sense understood by most worlds. The inhabitants of this dark, resonant vault are **The Tympanics**.

They are spidery, lithoid things, evolved not from the soft flesh of animals, but from the hard, desiccated necessities of the Aeon. They stand no taller than a man’s torso, though their legs are long, spindle-thin, and composed of a material identical to the polished bark of the great pillars: a ceramic-like chitin that has been rubbed smooth by eons of friction. They possess no distinct head; their sensory organs are clustered in the center of their torso, a flattened, vertical oval that resembles the sound hole of a stringed instrument.

They are the color of the darkness—deep charcoal and matte ash—broken only by the faint, bioluminescent tracery of veins that pulse with the sluggish, amber mineral oil they pump instead of blood. This oil does not flow; it hydraulic-locks their joints, allowing them to freeze in place for decades at a time, indistinguishable from the knots in the ancient wood.

Because the water value is effectively zero, The Tympanics do not breathe. They have no lungs to moisten. Instead, they possess a series of spiracle-like filters along their backs that hum with a low, oscillating frequency, constantly cycling the dry, static-filled air to recharge their internal electric glands. They feed on the electricity that builds up in the atmosphere—ozone and static—licking the charged air with proboscises that unfurl like dried fern fronds. They are grazers of the storm, clinging to the underside of the woven walkways or the high branches of the tree-pillars during the worst of the acoustic gales, sipping the voltage that arcs through the dry needles.

Sight is a forgotten sense for them. In the absolute zero-value light, eyes are useless liabilities. Instead, they navigate through a complex, highly evolved form of bone conduction. Their entire exoskeleton acts as a microphone. They feel the world through the vibrations traveling up their legs from the root-floor. They can "hear" the friction of a vine tightening three miles away, or the stress fracture developing in a distant pillar. They map their world not in space, but in pitch and timbre.

The Tympanics are the inheritors of the primitive city. They do not live in the huts—those are dry, crumbling mausoleums—but rather *on* the infrastructure. They inhabit the tension lines. The vast, knotted rope bridges and the woven netting of the canopy are their home. They spend their lives traversing the void between the pillars, walking with a delicate, staggering precision on the swaying ropes. Their feet are split, multi-toed pincers that can grip a single fiber of sinew or the oily groove of a bark ridge with equal ease.

They are the musicians of the Hollow, though their music is a violent necessity. To communicate, they do not vocalize; they strike. They possess specialized limbs—club-like appendages made of dense, petrified wood—that they use to strike the ropes, the wooden pillars, or the hollow shells of the abandoned huts.

A conversation between two Tympanics is a terrifying percussion. One will strike a suspended bridge cable, sending a low, thrumming vibration through the web; the other will strike the trunk of a nearby tree, replying with a sharp, cracking note. They encode complex histories, warnings of structural stress, and mating calls in the rhythms. They "sing" to the trees, soothing the groaning vegetation with specific, harmonic frequencies to prevent the timber from snapping under its own internal pressure.

Their lifecycle is tied to the Great Cycles. When the temperature shifts and the forest expands, creating the thunderous booms, The Tympanics enter a state of ecstatic frenzy. They climb to the highest points of the canopy, anchoring themselves to the longest, tautest ropes. There, they vibrate in sympathy with the screaming forest, their internal amber oil superheating. This is their reproduction: a release of spores that are not powdery, but crystalline, fired into the dark air by the shaking of their bodies. These spores drift through the acoustic currents, finding purchase in the cracks of the bark or the crevices of the stone ruins, where they will begin the long, dry process of calcifying into a new generation.

To a human observer, The Tympanics would be invisible, a mere trick of the acoustics. You would not see them; you would only feel them. You would notice that the chaotic rattling of the abandoned city has a pattern to it—a Morse code of tapping on stone and wood. You would feel the subtle, extra vibration traveling through the rope bridges as they scuttle overhead, their dry legs skittering like the dragging of dried bones across a slate floor. They are the city's new heartbeat, a dry, rhythmic pulse keeping time in the dark, singing back to the forest that tries to crush them.
