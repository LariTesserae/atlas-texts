To live in the Under-Greenhouse is to be a stranger to bone and blood, to the quick dart of an eye or the beat of a heart. Here, in the suffocating wet twilight where the city has been digested, the inhabitants do not run or climb; they adhere, they creep, and they endure. They are the architects of the slow siege, a civilization of cellulose and chlorophyll that has claimed the rusted skeleton of the old world.

The dominant citizens of this sunken cathedral are the **Stranglers**, immense lianas that behave less like plants and more like thinking serpents of steel-hard wood. They are the ones who "hold" the room. Their girth is often thicker than a man’s torso, their bark scarred and textured like iron plating. They do not grow randomly; they grow with purpose, coiling around the rusted vertical supports of the pit, binding the failing metal together with a tensile strength that the original engineers could only have dreamed of. They are the silent masons of this place, and they are endlessly patient. You can see them tightening their grip in slow-motion imperceptible to the human eye, a constriction that will take a century to crush a beam, but which is as inevitable as the rising water.

Living in the shadow of the Stranglers are the ** Curtain-Ferns**. These are not mere undergrowth; they are a collective entity, a vast, interconnected colony that acts as the space's skin. Every surface—the catwalks, the handrails, the dials of the forgotten control panels—is plastered with a thick, felt-like pelt of their rhizomes. They are the filter-feeders of the atmosphere, drinking the heavy humidity and exhaling a thick, visible mist of spores. They react to the vibration of the floor. When the auxiliary pumps whine and clack, the millions of tiny fronds tremble in unison, creating a shivering green wave that ripples through the gloom. They are sensitive to a degree that borders on a nervous system; to touch them is to trigger a flinch that travels through the entire room, a botanical whisper passed along the fungal network.

Beneath the grates, in the black water that churns with the nutrient-rich slush, live the **Sludge-Eaters**. These are massive, amorphous mats of algae and bacterial colonies, floating like lush, islands of slime. They are the stomach of the Under-Greenhouse, breaking down the oils, the rust, and the decayed matter of the urban carcass. They glow with a faint, sickly luminescence—a phosphorescent reaction to the heat of the pipes. They are mobile in their own way; as the water level rises and falls with the pumps, the mats drift, oozing over the submerged grates, dissolving the metal and reclaiming the carbon. They are the clean-up crew, silently devouring the history of the place molecule by molecule.

And then there is the **amber silence**, a metaphorical inhabitant: the **Symbiotic Rust**. It is not exactly plant, nor is it strictly metal, but a hybrid corrosion that the ecosystem has cultivated. The vines exude acids that eat at the iron just enough to create a pitted, porous surface for their roots to grip deeper; the rust, in turn, holds the water the vines need. It is a living geology, a crust that protects and consumes simultaneously.

But the true ruler of this place is the atmosphere itself—the **Spore-Cloud**. The air is so thick with reproductive dust that it is almost a solid presence. It is a hive mind of potential life, hovering in the humid space between the wet leaves and the hot lights. Every breath taken in this place (were there lungs to take it) would be filled with the genetic memory of the Greenhouse. This cloud is the "bird" in the cage, the swarm that moves lazily through the catwalks, settling on the wet rails, germinating instantly where it lands.

There is no hunger for flesh here, only a hunger for structure. The inhabitants do not hunt; they replace. They do not sleep; they merely photosynthesize in the dim, moss-filtered gloom. They are the ghosts of industry, made of green and wet rust, holding the form of a human city while patiently erasing its function. They are alone, yet they are a crowd—a singular, sprawling, vegetable consciousness that has occupied the machine and learned to run it.
