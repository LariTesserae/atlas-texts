They are a people carved from the same grey mist that choked the valley, lean and corded like the roots they walked upon. To call them "inhabitants" implies a separation that does not exist; they are not living in the forest so much as they are a specialized tissue within it. They are the Interstitial Ones, the graft-folk, the architects of the quiet spaces within the roar.

Physically, they are shaped by the squeeze. Generations of navigating the vertebrae of the hollow have molded them into beings of liquid grace. Their limbs are elongated, giving them a simian reach that allows them to swing from the slick bridges of woven root without touching the dangerous, unstable floor. Their skin is pallid, possessing the texture of a mushroom cap—cool, smooth, and slightly damp to the touch, an adaptation that allows them to sense the subtle shifts in humidity and air pressure that presage a heavy deluge or a hunting predator.

They are perpetually shrouded in layers of oiled leather, cured from the hides of the great, slothing creatures that roam the upper plateau. The leather is dark, stained with the tannins of the bark and the grease of the native nuts, rendering them water-resistant and silhouette-less against the wet black stone. When they move, they rustle like dry parchment, a sound deliberately tuned to blend with the constant *plink-plink-plink* of the weeping cliffs.

Their faces are masks of intense observation. Eyes are large and adapted to the dim, gilded light of the canopy, possessing a reflective sheen that catches the glow of the fat lamps. They communicate less with sound—which is swallowed by the damp air and the roar of the biological marketplace—and more with a complex, tactile sign language performed through the brushing of sleeves and the tapping of fingers on the wet wood of the railings. When they must speak, their voices are low-pitched, resonant hums designed to travel through the wood of the walkways rather than the open air, vibrating warnings of encroaching swarms or falling branches to their neighbors.

Life in the High Hollow is a exercise in hydraulic management and botanical violence. They are master hydraulic engineers, possessing an intimate understanding of water’s behavior as a scale-3 element. Their days are spent tending to the "living joinery." They do not build; they guide. Using stone wedges and bone chisels, they split the branches of the great cedars and banyans, inserting grafts of flexible sapwood to create arches and doorframes that heal and scar into permanent structures. They prune the aggressive undergrowth with surgical precision, not to clear it, but to direct it into weaves that serve as containment walls for the terraced platforms where they grow their own fungal crops and medicinal mosses.

Their relationship with the fauna is a constant, low-grade war of containment. The rodents and arboreal predators see the settlement not as a fortress, but as a larder. The people live with a weapon in hand at all times—fire-hardened spears tipped with the jagged teeth of the local serpents. They hunt the swarming insects, smoking them out of the hollow boles with damp fires to harvest the protein-rich paste the insects produce, a staple of their diet. Yet, they also revere the cycle. When a great tree falls, opening a wound in the canopy that lets in too much harsh light, they mourn it as a fallen ancestor, for the tree was their roof, their wall, and their foundation.

Socially, they are defined by proximity. With an urban density of 1.8 packed into a primitive, organic frame, privacy is unknown. Families dwell in the "bubbles"—hollowed-out spaces within the great trees where the wood has been cured by smoke. The walls are thin; a cough in one chamber is heard in three others. This has bred a culture of extreme social cohesion and non-judgmental tolerance. To lose one's temper here is dangerous; a fight could rupture a water-gutter or compromise a load-bearing vine. Consequently, their society is matriarchal and counsel-based, governed by the "Keepers of the Flow," elders who dictate the distribution of water and the pruning schedules.

They are a people who pay their rent in patience. They sleep in hammocks suspended over the churning darkness, rocked by the wind, dreaming of the day the water finally wins and washes the hollow away. Until then, they are the calm eye in a hurricane of wet, green chaos, binding the sweating world together with twine and bone.
