They are a people carved from the same grey stone as the mesas, moving with a deliberation that suggests they understand the weight of the aeons as intimately as the mountains do. They are not tall; the altitude has compressed them over generations into dense, barrel-chested frames with powerful, sinewy limbs designed for climbing rather than running. Their skin is pale, possessing the texture and sheen of damp parchment, stretched tight over prominent cheekbones and knuckles that are perpetually swollen from the cold.

You would hardly know they were there at first, their silhouettes dissolving into the smog and the industrial brick. They dress in layered, heavy fabrics—waxed canvas and felted wool dyed the colors of rust and shadow—that hang off their frames like the draping of a cloud. Every inch of them is insulated against the "weeping" air, wrapped in scarves that are pulled up to cover their noses and mouths, not just for warmth, but to filter the thin, metallic air. Only their eyes are exposed, and these are large, dark, and reflective, adapted to the perpetual dim (1.3) of the twilight noon. They possess a tapetum lucidum that catches the faint bioluminescence of the mosses and the blue snaps of arcing electricity, giving their gazes a predatory, ghostly shine when they look up from the fog.

Their faces, when rarely glimpsed, are maps of the geography itself. They are smooth, worn down by the dampness, lacking the sharp angles of lowlanders. There is a softness to their features, a "final form" physiognomy that mirrors the erosion of the peaks. They do not smile often—the effort required seems too great for the thin oxygen—but their expressions are not hostile; they are merely static, like the stone they inhabit. The older among them begin to resemble the architecture, their skin taking on the grained texture of the ironworks, their joints stiffening into the angles of pistons and pipes.

They are, in essence, biological components of the machine. Because the density (2.4) of their world forces them to live vertically, their physicality is adapted to the void. They have an uncanny sense of balance, able to walk a girder slick with condensation as casually as a flat sidewalk. The "inches" that separate a bedroom window from a thousand-foot drop mean nothing to them; they are spider-like in their comfort with height. You will see them hanging from the rusted webbing of pipes, suspended over the abyss, adjusting a valve or scraping lichen from a flange with a calm that borders on the meditative. They do not fear the fall; they respect the gravity.

Their society is one of maintenance and consumption, defined by the active equilibrium (2.5) of their home. They are omnivores, but their diet is tied to the strange loop of the city. They cultivate the mosses that carpet the rooftops, harvesting the bioluminescent greenery and processing it into a nutrient-dense, gelatinous paste that provides the sugars needed to survive the cold. They eat the ferns that grow in the warm vents, and they hunt the sleek, oversized rats that scuttle through the undergrowth, treating them not as pests but as a vital protein source. When they eat, there is always a taste of metal in their mouths, a tang of iron that they claim is necessary for the blood, a literal internalization of their environment.

The technology is not something they use; it is something they inhabit. The grinding of the flywheels is not just noise to them—it is a heartbeat they feel in their teeth. Many of the inhabitants display signs of this connection: vibrational hums in their chests, calluses formed from turning heavy iron wheels, ears attuned to the specific pitch of a pipe under pressure. They are the "calcified" population, having merged with the industrial sprawl (1.6) to the point where the human and the mechanical are indistinguishable.

In the rare moments of stillness, when the wind sweeps a pocket of fog through the alleyways and the town holds its breath, the people stop too. They stand motionless on the gangways, heads bowed, listening to the distant clank of the press stamping out metal. They are waiting, patient and worn, content to be part of the mechanism turning slowly in the cool, damp dark. They are the keepers of the equilibrium, the ones who ensure the rust feeds the moss, and the moss insulates the steam, living out their lives in the lung of the world, grey and enduring and quiet.
