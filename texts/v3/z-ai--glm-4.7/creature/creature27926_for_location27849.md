They are not merely inhabitants; they are the friction of the place given form. If the noise is the blood and the pipes are the bones, then these creatures are the white blood cells, the scavengers, the microscopic ecosystem of a leviathan that breathes in centuries.

**The Architects of Silence**
The most numerous are the ones you feel before you see. They are the "Ceramic Weavers," small, crab-like entities that exist in the thousands. They are the color of the walls—a matte, slate-grey that seems to absorb the dim, sickly light. Their carapaces are not grown, but seem baked; they possess a smooth, terracotta-like texture that is perfectly aerodynamic, allowing them to slip through the dense, wet air with minimal resistance.

They are blind, or at least, their eyes are vestigial. They navigate the claustrophobic dark through the soles of their feet. Specialized sensory filaments on their underbelly read the vibrations of the tether: the groaning of the alloy, the thermal expansion of the conduits, and the footfalls of their kin. They do not hear the deafening roar; they read it like a map. To them, the screaming high-pitched whistle of escaping steam is a beacon, a dinner bell ringing with the clarity of a cathedral chime.

Their pincers are delicate, needle-sharp tools designed for a single purpose: shaving the mineral deposits that precipitate out of the super-saturated air. They scrape the walls with a rhythmic, ticking motion, a million tiny chisels contributing to the polishing of the cave. They eat the slag, the oxidized metal, and the rare earth minerals, processing the waste of the megastructure into new layers of their own shells. When a cluster of them moves over a pipe, it looks like the surface of the stone itself is liquefying and flowing, a grey tide rolling over the obstacles.

**The Wicks**
Clinging to the upper sections of the cavity, where the heat is most intense and the gravity weakest, live the "Wicks." They are less armored than the Weavers, resembling soft, pale anemones tethered to the rivets and joins of the ceiling. Their bodies are long, tapering tubes of translucent gelatin, shuddering constantly in the updraft.

They exploit the zero-gravity anomalies of the condensation. As water weeps from the ceiling, it momentarily hangs in spheres before falling. The Wicks unfurl feathery, fractal tendrils that catch these hovering droplets, absorbing the moisture instantly. Their bodies glow with a faint, bioluminescent pulse—cyan and magenta—which throbs in time with their feeding.

They are the stationary bait of the ecosystem. They do not move; they simply endure the heat and filter the air. In the gloom, they look like strange, underwater coral, pulsing with a heartbeat that is not their own, but the rhythm of the machinery cooling down.

**The Resonance Hunters**
Lurking in the darker recesses, wrapped around the thicker, insulated trunks of the main conduits, are the predators. They are sleeker, larger, and possessed of a terrifying intelligence. They look like enormous, flattened earwigs, perhaps two feet in length, with bodies that interlock perfectly with the grooved tubing of the walls.

They are ambush hunters, and their primary weapon is the very noise that drowns out all else. The Hunters have evolved a "sonic cloaking"—their shells are shaped to dampen their own vibrations, allowing them to move through the roaring chamber without creating a ripple in the sonic spectrum. A Hunter can crawl within inches of a Weaver without the victim ever sensing its presence, masked perfectly by the background thrum of the tether.

When they strike, they do so with a speed that blurs the eye, seizing a Weaver and cracking its ceramic shell with powerful, hydraulic mandibles. But the most horrific aspect is their feeding method: they press their bodies against the vibrating pipes to draw energy. They are parasites of the motion, feeding on the kinetic energy of the machine’s thrum, supplementing their diet of scavengers with the raw power of the orbital elevator’s stress.

**The Hive Mind**
There is no individuality here. The thousands of creatures operate as a singular, distributed organism. When a pipe bursts, spraying superheated steam, the Weavers do not scatter in panic. They move in unison, a flowing wave that seals the breach with their own bodies, using the heat to cure their secretions into a temporary patch. They die in the process, baking into the wall, becoming part of the structure.

This is the tragedy and the beauty of the place. The fauna is constantly repairing the cage that holds them. They crawl over the scaffolding, shearing, eating, breeding, and dying, their life cycles measured in the hum of the engine. They are the maintenance crew of a dead civilization, or perhaps the stewards of a living one, unaware that they are tiny specks crawling in the gut of a god, singing their songs into the endless, roaring dark.
