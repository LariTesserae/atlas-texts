They are known, if only to themselves, as the Hushed. They are not so much inhabitants of the Roof as they are its parasites, or perhaps its antibodies—a fragile, transient infestation crawling across the immense, sleeping back of the world.

Physically, they are sculpted by the humidity and the roar. They are tall and spindle-shanked, evolved to wade through the deep, yielding spongy-mats of the fungal floor without sinking too deep. Their skin is the color of the bruised-purple epidermis that surrounds them, a mottled camouflage that shifts slightly in hue depending on the heat, wet and slick to the touch. They have almost no body hair; hair traps moisture and encourages the growth of the smaller, parasitic mosses that seek to colonize anything that sits still for too long. To be still here is to be overgrown.

Their faces are the maps of their survival. The nose is small and sealed with a membranous flap, a filter to keep out the thick clouds of pollen and spore-dust kicked up by the grazers, but the mouth is wide and thin-lipped, designed for gasping the thin, wet air. But it is the ears that define them. The Hushed have no external ears. The sides of their heads are smooth, interrupted only by a series of concentric, bony ridges that can be voluntarily flexed shut or opened by minute muscle movements. In the "closed" position, the world is a muffled, tactile vibration felt in the bones of the skull. In the "open" position, the deafening biological static of the Roof floods in—a risky necessity, for to hear is to know where the predators are, but it is also to court madness.

They travel in bands of no more than a dozen, a number dictated by the "carrying capacity" of silence. Any larger group and the subtle visual cues they rely upon for communication would become lost in the visual noise of the swarming insect life. They do not speak. The vocal cords of the Hushed have atrophied over generations; to shout is to die, as sound travels differently here, bouncing off the dense plant-domes and attracting the wrong kind of attention.

Instead, they converse with their hands. Their sign language is not a simple code of nouns and verbs, but a fluid, three-dimensional grammar of frantic beauty. It involves the intricate fluttering of fingers, the angle of the palm, the tension in the wrist, and the posture of the entire body. They can tell a complex story about the direction of a wind current or the location of a fresh condensation pool without making a sound. When they must communicate over distance—say, from one fungal dome to another—they use semaphore flags made from stretched, cured sections of the purple moss-membrane, flashing them in patterns of staccato sunlight that are invisible to the blind, churning clouds of insect grazers.

Their technology is a testament to their transience. They carry nothing that cannot be replaced in an hour. The bone hooks mentioned in the lore are not merely tools; they are extensions of their arms. Carved from the hollow, lightweight bones of the floating predators, these hooks are used to grip the slick, wet flesh of the ground when climbing the steep slopes of the domes. They wear harnesses woven from living vines that still retain a tenuous grasp on life, constantly repaired and regrown as they travel. These vines act as safety lines, anchored into the soft ground with toggles made of carved stone.

When they need shelter from the blinding white light or the occasional acid-squalls, they do not build on the surface; they dig. Using sharp stone knives, they slice back the tough, rubbery outer layer of the moss-carpet to create small, hollowed-out burrows within the insulation layer of the dome. These burrows, lined with soft, dry fungal spores, are warm and humid incubators. They sleep in these wounds in the skin of the world, healing the cut with a paste of chewed roots when they leave, so as not to anger the host.

The most terrifying aspect of their lives is the "Scream." This is when the insect swarms—those shimmering, pollen-drifts that eat the algae—rise up in a frenzy. The noise level spikes from a roar to a physical blow, a wall of sound that can rupture blood vessels. When the Scream comes, the Hushed drop instantly. They flatten themselves against the ground, pressing their ears into the spongy mat to use the earth itself as a sound dampener. They interlock fingers and grip the root-hairs, anchoring themselves against the wind shear generated by a billion wings. It is a communal act of submission. They wait, eyes squeezed shut, trusting that the swarm will pass like a storm, stripping the algae clean but leaving the larger creatures alone.

Their diet is as strange as their home. They do not hunt the fast-moving predators, nor do they harvest the swarms. Instead, they are "tappers." They locate the vascular bundles deep within the root-mat valleys—the "veins" of the super-organism. Using delicate bone drills, they pierce these veins and collect the sugary, nutrient-dense sap that oozes out. It is a sticky, amber fluid that provides all the hydration and calories they need. They are careful to take only a little, sealing the wound with a clotting agent made from crushed moss. They view themselves not as masters of the Roof, but as its phlebotomists—necessary, perhaps, but insignificant.

In the rare moments of calm, when the wind dies and the insect swarms settle to feed, the Hushed perform the only rituals that distinguish them from beasts. They arrange the standing stones. These are not quarried rocks, but hard, calcified nodules found deep within the older domes. They drag them for miles to set them in circles on the flat, wind-swept tablelands. No one knows why they do it. Perhaps the stones create a resonance that cancels out the noise, creating a moment of true silence for their meditations. Perhaps they are markers for wandering tribes, cairns of memory in a landscape that erodes itself constantly.

Whatever the purpose, the sight of them—small, purple figures moving in slow motion against a backdrop of blinding green fuzz, arranging perfect geometry on the back of a sleeping giant—is the ultimate expression of their existence. They are the quiet in the loud. They are the stillness in the motion. They are the brief, waking dream of a world that wants only to sleep and hum.
