They are a people preserved, as much as the stone beneath their feet and the ancient bark surrounding them. Call them the Cliff-Bound, or perhaps simply the Dried—names they would hardly recognize, for they do not name themselves as a collective. They are the People of the Wax, and to look at them is to look at a biology that has conceded to the oven of the High Dry.

They are not tall; the architecture of the forest forbids it. Generations of ducking beneath low-hanging, water-heavy paddles and pressing through narrow gaps between fleshy trunks have compressed them. They are broad-shouldered and dense, with a low center of gravity suited to the swaying rope bridges and the precarious perches atop the giant succulents. Their skin is the color of the mesa dust—tawny, cracked, and tough. It feels less like flesh and more like cured leather, an evolutionary barrier against the desiccating wind that seeks to steal the water from their blood.

Their faces are dominated by eyes adapted to the permanent twilight of the under-canopy. Large, dark, and reflective, they absorb the meager shafts of golden light that pierce the gloom. To see them blink is a slow, deliberate motion, like the shutter of a camera aperture. They possess no excess body fat; their frames are ropy with muscle, stripped of all softness, leaving only the utilitarian mechanics of survival.

Clothing is minimal, born of the heat trapped within the forest and the scarcity of materials. They wear skins, but not furs—the heat allows for no insulation. Instead, they wear the cured hides of the giant beetles, chitinous plates strapped together with sinew, acting as armor against the thorns and the abrasive rock. Around their necks and waists, they wear elaborate scarves and sashes woven from the dried fibers of the succulents, dyed with the crushed bodies of the bright lizards. These are not merely decorative; in the dry air, sweat is a currency to be hoarded. The cloth is dampened with sponge-moss and worn close to the pulse points, a micro-climate of vapor against the throat.

They live vertically. Their homes are not built on the ground, which is the domain of the scuttling nightmares, but are anchored like barnacles to the sides of the stone-trees. Using a primitive, gritty mortar made from crushed granite and sap, they secure mud-brick huts and wooden platforms to the ribbed bark of the giants. It is a claustrophobic existence. A "front yard" might be a three-foot-wide plank suspended sixty feet above the roots, overlooking a neighbor’s kitchen just an arm’s reach away. The smell of their homes is distinct: the sweet cloying scent of roasted succulent pulp mixed with the acrid, greasy smoke of burning oil lamps.

And they are loud.

The silence of the mesa ends where the forest begins. The People of the Wax understand that in this compressed darkness, silence is the herald of death. Therefore, they sing, they clack, they chant. As they move along the swaying bridges, they strike wooden clappers hung at intervals, a rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* that echoes through the gloom. Children are taught never to move quietly; to sneak is to invite the unseen thing that drops from the canopy. Their voices are raspy and deep, evolved to cut through the drone of insects, projecting a constant, rolling stream of sound—the "The Wall of Noise," a communal audio-shield that keeps the predators at bay.

They share this vertical city with a teeming, hostile ecology.

The most constant neighbors are the beetles, the "Living Tanks." These are armored, many-legged horrors the size of hounds, with mandibles capable of shearing through the tough succulent flesh—and human bone. They scuttle along the larger branches and the floor, ignored as long as they keep their distance. The people do not hunt them; they are too hard to kill, and their shells make poor food. Instead, a truce exists: the beetles are the scavengers, cleaning the detritus of the settlement, crunching on the fallen leaves and the occasional unfortunate body that drops from the bridges.

Above, the canopy is ruled by the "Silencers"—leathery, gliding creatures that possess the wing membranes of bats but the bodies of pumas. They do not screech. They hang in the shadows of the highest paddles, waiting for a lapse in the rhythmic clacking. If a branch breaks and the noise stops for even a heartbeat, a shadow detaches from the ceiling. The fear of the Silencers is why the settlement never sleeps; there is always a shift of drummers and singers keeping the rhythm alive through the long, hot night.

Then there are the lizards, the jeweled alarm system. Bright orange and electric blue, they skitter over the walls of the mud-brick houses, shrieking insults at the inhabitants. The people tolerate them because the lizards are sensitive to vibration. If a Silencer drops nearby, the lizards explode into a frenzy of screams, adding their voices to the human choir.

Life here is glacial. A man born in a shack anchored to a high bough will likely die in that same shack. The concept of "progress" is alien; the only change is the slow, inexorable thickening of the tree trunks, which gradually widens the cracks in the architecture. Marriage alliances are based on the stability of one’s platform and the reliability of one’s water-cisterns. They are a people of extreme tradition, not out of piety, but out of caution. To do things differently is to risk the unknown, and the unknown is usually thirsty, sharp, and waiting in the dark.

They are a population suspended—between the abyss of the cliffs and the blinding sky, between the desiccation of the air and the water in their blood. They cling to their dying rock, shouting into the gloom, a dense, vibrant stain of life stubbornly refusing to fade away.
