They are the **Listeners**, though they do not listen with ears alone.

You see them first as shadows detaching themselves from the grey basalt of the towers. They are tall, elongated figures, their physiology stretched by the generations they have spent reaching upward, bridging gaps that widen with every passing hour. They are bipedal, but their posture is permanently canted forward, a hunch born from the relentless wind and the need to keep their center of gravity low on the heaving earth.

Their skin is the color of wet slate—thick, hairless, and possessed of a waxy sheen that repels the constant condensation. It looks less like flesh and more like the casing of a deep-sea mollusk, cool to the touch and armored against the sharp, glass-like air. Where the skin folds at the elbows and knees, it is pale and tender, revealing the warm, red vitality beneath the shell.

They are wrapped in heavy, primitive pelts—not fur, but woven mats of the neon-green moss and cured fronds of the ferns, bound with cords made from the sinew of long-extinct mountain beasts. This clothing serves a dual purpose: it provides insulation against the biting cold, and it muffles the sound of their movement, allowing them to hear the stone speak.

The face of a Listener is a haunting study in sensory reallocation. Their eyes are small, protected by heavy ridges of bone and nictitating membranes that seal against the mist. They do not look up at the sky; there is nothing to see but the featureless grey. Instead, their vision is short-range, tuned to the texture of the rock immediately in front of them, searching for hairline fractures and stress points.

Their true sensory organs, however, are their hands and feet. The soles of their feet are broad, flat, and ribbed with callous, allowing them to grip the slick, wet obsidian without slipping. But more than grip, the feet are receptors. They are hyper-sensitive to vibration. A Listener can feel the sub-auditory groan of the tectonic plate miles below, sensing the "heartbeat" of the land as it prepares to shudder and lift.

Their hands are their instruments of salvation. They are oversized, the fingers long and doubled-jointed, ending in nails that are thick and chisel-like. These hands are constantly in motion. When they are not walking, they are working. They carry with them the tools of their existence: massive levers made of iron-wood, shims of bone, and heavy stone mallets.

They live alone, each inhabitant assigned to a specific monolith or a stretch of the paved avenue. The population density is agonizingly low; a Listener might go weeks without seeing another of their kind, though they are acutely aware of each other’s presence through the conductive stone. When they do meet, there is no greeting. The wind is too loud for speech, and their culture has no use for pleasantries. They simply nod once—a sharp, jerky motion—and continue their work.

Their work is the definition of Sisyphus, stripped of myth and grounded in geology.

As the plateau rises, the massive dry-stone towers shift. A gap of two inches can open in a wall between heartbeats. A Listener will spot this, rushing forward to jam a wooden shim into the breach, pounding it in with rhythmic, practiced blows that syncopate with the wind’s drone. They are constantly reinforcing, constantly bracing. They reinforce the timber beams that act as the skeletons of the roofless towers; they tighten the leather straps that bind the stone blocks together; they clear the weeping moss from the load-bearing joints to prevent rot from weakening the structure before the stone is even set.

They do not repair things because they are broken; they repair things because they are moving.

Diet is a grim, quiet affair. There are no fires here—the wind would snatch the flames, and the wood is too precious for burning. The Listeners eat what they can find in the damp shadows of their towers. They harvest the pale, sightless crustaceans that scuttle in the moss, cracking their shells with their teeth and swallowing the soft, mineral-rich meat. They drink the water that runs down the stone, cupping their hands to catch the drips, tasting the ozone and the raw earth.

They sleep in the hollows of the towers, curled into tight balls on platforms of woven fern suspended high above the floor. This is to avoid the damp, but also to stay as close to the center of the structure’s sway as possible. Even in sleep, their hands twitch, reaching out to touch the nearest wall, ensuring the room hasn't shifted while they dreamed.

There is a profound sadness to them, a wet, patient dread that seeps from their pores. They are the custodians of a city that is fleeing the planet. They know that eventually, the ridge will rise so high that the atmosphere will vanish, or the water will freeze, or the stone will finally shear and tumble into the abyss. But they do not flee. They cannot. Their biology is now tied to the vibration of the ridge. To leave the stone would be to go deaf, to lose the sense that governs their existence.

They are the midwives of a geological event, standing in the mist, shivering in the shadow of their own fragile monuments, waiting for the earth to decide if it is done rising. When the ground groans, they do not cower in fear. They lift their heavy chisels, find the crack, and begin to hammer.
