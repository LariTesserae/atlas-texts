I should like to say I arrived as a traveler, and for a long time I believed that was the honest answer. A traveler is what I was the first time — straw-colored plain under my feet for a day's walk, the small rounded mountain ahead of me holding its wreath of mist like a woman holding her breath before speaking. I crossed the wet line without ceremony, because there is no ceremony to cross it with. My lips went dry-to-wet in a single step. My hair went limp. I stood in the first grove with four trees in it and listened to the pipes singing their uneven flute-notes and thought, the way everyone thinks: *I am only passing through. You can leave in an afternoon. Everyone does.*

And I did leave. But on the way out, at the wet line, I let my clothes drink first. I did not know why. I stood there in the warm thick air and bent down and let the hem of my coat take on water it did not need, from a pool that had no inlet and no outlet, and then I walked out into the dry country and the dampness stayed with me a day longer than cloth should hold it, and I could not say why, and I have stopped trying to say why, because the not-saying is part of what I am now.

Here is the thing I did not know on that first visit, and could not have known, and only learned years later when an old woman in a lowland kitchen took my face in her floury hands and turned my head gently to one side, the way you turn a lamp:

When the great western pipe fills over its slow hours and sounds — that one deep round note rolling out over the plain — some people hear it arrive. And some people turn their heads a half-second *before* it arrives.

I was the child who turned early.

I did not know that's what I was doing. You never do. It feels like nothing — a small uneasiness, an itch behind the ear, and then the note comes and the itch is satisfied and you go on stirring the pot or feeding the hens, and everyone in the kitchen says the Grandmother cleared her throat, and you say it too, because you heard it, didn't you, same as them. Except you didn't, quite. You heard it before it was a sound at all. You heard it as intention. The mountain deciding, the slow water climbing the flue, the note already in the stone the way a word is in a mouth before the mouth opens.

They spoke of me gently. I found that out later too — that there had been a season, when I was eight or nine, when the older ones watched me at the note-hour and said nothing and then said my name to each other in the lowered voice they kept for such things. Nobody told me. That is the courtesy of it. A child is not told they have been chosen, because being told would make it a job, and it is not a job. A grandmother has grandchildren; the visits are family calls. You cannot apply for a family.

So I grew up listening. Forty years of it, if you want the arithmetic, and I will give it to you because the mountain has cured me of hurrying past numbers. Forty years of hearing the note come and go, some years right and some years — I could tell even as a boy — a little flat, a little clogged, the sound of a throat that needed clearing properly. And in one of those years nobody came. I did not know then that nobody had come. I only knew that for a season the note was wrong, and then for a while there was no note at all, and the silence in the lowlands was the kind of silence that has a body lying in it. I grieved without knowing what I was grieving for. That is what the wet line does to some of us: it marks us at a distance, across all that straw-colored ground, and we carry a dampness we cannot account for, and one day we understand that we have been in correspondence the whole time.

The year the tuners came back and worked nine days without speaking, and the mountain cleared its throat, and the bird answered — I stood in my dooryard and listened to both of those things arrive in their proper order, note first and answer a fifth above, and I wept into the dishwater, and no one in my house said anything about it out loud for a month, and I understood then that the courtesy runs both ways.

The next spring, I walked up carrying a reed knife, a coil of twine, a lump of wax, a pouch of pebbles. Almost nothing. You learn that *almost nothing* is a full kit.

I want to be honest about the first visit, because the descriptions of this work always make it sound serene and mine was not, entirely. My hands shook. I knelt at a silted channel and put my fingers into it — you do not use tools for the clearing, you use your hands, and I understood within a minute why: the stone is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and the silt is soft as flour, and there is no way to do this work hurriedly, and tools are hurrying made solid. So I cleared with my fingers, slowly, and the water resumed its thread, and somewhere behind me the mountain *noticed*, the way a sleeper notices a hand on their forehead without waking, and my shaking stopped.

The wax work took me longer to learn, and here is where I confess the thing I was most afraid to confess: I had come thinking I would teach the pipes, and instead the pipes taught me. I wedged a pebble into a flat pipe's mouth and it went sharp, and I took it out and tried a smaller one, and it went flat, and I sat back on my heels in the wet and laughed until I had to wipe my eyes, because I had spent forty years listening to this mountain from below and I could not tune one pipe from beside it. The bird, somewhere in the grove, made a note that I can only describe as patient. I got it on the third day. You will want to know what *got it* means and I can only say: the pipe and I agreed. The wax went on. The note came out round. Neither of us had won anything.

I have made the visits now for — I will not say how many years; the count is not mine to keep, and anyway on this mountain counting is done differently. I know the warm hollows where the butterflies roost by the dozen and I have never once put my hand into one, though I have wanted to, the way you want to touch a sleeping child's hair. I know which seeps closed low this year and I have marked in my mind — nowhere else, nowhere that keeps — where the new one will open, higher up, and I am nearly always right, and I do not know how, and I have stopped asking. I know the grey bird. We do not duet. I am not an instrument; I am hands. But some mornings at the inhaling hour, when the mist draws down the flues and the stars show through and the stone shines wet under ordinary moonlight, I stand on the ring path — the path I have no business walking, the path worn to the exact height of somebody's shoulder — and I breathe when the mountain breathes, and that is our whole conversation, and it is enough, and it is more than enough.

I keep nothing. You know the law by now; everyone who reads this far knows the law. No shelter stands. No wall, no post, no cairn older than a season. My wedges will work loose, my wax will wash, the reed screens I weave at the springs are meant to rot and do, and every year I leave and the mountain folds my visit into its long slow morning and is entirely unbothered by having been helped. This took me years to accept and one afternoon to love. Hands may work here; hands may not keep. When I finally understood that the rule was not a deprivation but a kindness — that the mountain keeps me by *not* keeping my things, the way you keep a friend by not keeping receipts — I stopped grieving the impermanence and started practicing it. I am better at it now. I practice every visit. I am, you might say, tuning myself.

So: where would you find yourself in this world? On the dry side of the wet line, in the years of the listening, or on the wet side in the years of the hands. Both names are true, and I have been both.

And I will tell you what I believe comes next, though I say it the way the lowlanders say the browser rumor — not pressing the point. Some old tuners make a last visit carrying nothing at all. I am nearly ready for that visit. I have begun, in a small way, already: I come now with the wax and leave the twine home; I come with the knife and leave the wax; last spring I carried the pebbles in one pocket and brought back the empty pocket. One day, not a far one, I will walk up through the held breath with nothing in my hands at all, and I will clear a channel or I will not, and I will sit in a grove of four trees until the inhaling hour, and then —

Well. Certain springs have been known to open, higher up, in the years that follow. Blind, and unbothered, and climbing slowly toward a light they will reach in a hundred years. If you come to the Wet Crown in some season after me and find a new seep a terrace above the old gardens, weeping in a thin sheet too slow to fall, brimming and passing its overflow to the next small pool —

Drink from it. Let your clothes drink too. You will not be able to say why.

You will not need to.
