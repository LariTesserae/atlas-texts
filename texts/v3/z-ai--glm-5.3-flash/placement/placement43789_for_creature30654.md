I come in through the loading bay, the way the water comes in — without a plan, at the speed the water allows. The crane arm is crusted coral the color of old brick. Below me the town glows in patches of sodium-amber, and I can already feel the hum in my teeth before I am properly inside it, the way you feel a train before you see the tracks.

I should say what I am, but the honest answer is that it takes the town a while to inform me, and I have learned not to rush it.

At first I am certain I am a visitor. I have the visitor's equipment: a slate under my arm, a lamp on my wrist, a schedule of some kind folded away where the water can't read it. I intend to count things. That is what visitors do — they count, and they leave, and their counting becomes a rumor on the surface that no one can verify. I drift down through the broken bay and the water is warm, bath-warm, and my bubbles do not race away the way bubbles should. They rise like thoughts you decide not to think. Everything falls in slow motion here, including intentions.

The first keeper I see is a gauge needle swinging. The second is a hatch dogging itself shut. The third is a wheel turning with nobody near it, and then the somebody unfolds out of the engine the way a hand unfolds from a glove, and I understand that I have been watched since the crane arm.

It comes to me through a doorway built for walking and used now for swimming, long through the torso, unhurried, its ends trailing a half-beat behind. It takes my hand. The grip is famous, I will learn — eight fingers, two fused into one broad lever, pads scarred smooth by generations of the same holds — and it presses something into my palm that I receive the way you receive a knock on a door you didn't know you had.

Later I will know the sentence. It translates roughly as *it is warm here; stay as long as you like.* At the time I only know that my hand has been spoken to, and that my name — the one I brought down with me, the crisp surface name — has just become inadequate, the way a photograph of a house is inadequate once you have lived in it.

So I stay the afternoon. That is what I tell myself.

Here is what I do not understand yet, standing in the central gallery with the drone in my sternum: the foundry does not distinguish between kinds of staying. The kelp does not distinguish. The barnacles certainly do not distinguish — they are already, I will later notice, considering my wrists. The ledgers in the small flooded archive tell of shifts that stayed when the water came in, and the handwriting on the oldest slates starts out surface-crisp and grows, decade by decade, looser and more wave-shaped. I look at those slates on my third day, or my third month — the town does not keep my kind of time, and neither, gradually, do I — and I recognize the handwriting in a way that unsettles me. It is the direction my own hand has begun to drift when I take notes on the walls. The sea got into my letters first. It always gets into the letters first.

What emerges, as the warm water works on me, is not a transformation I can point to but a slow relocation of what I am *for*.

I arrive a counter. I become, without ceremony, a keeper of small things. I learn the trade of the lantern-keepers, feeding trimmed frond-tips to the pale colonies in the old lamp housings, thinning them when they crowd, and I discover that lamplighting is less a job than a conversation conducted at the pace of glow. I sit with the listener, once, my back flat against a pressure joint, and she teaches me to read the groans the way shorefolk read weather — this one means rain means shift in the far plates; this one means the town clearing its throat; this one means nothing, and means it loudly. She presses her name into my hand. It is not a word. It is the feeling of a small valve seating, a click you receive in the palm. After that my hands know more people than my ears ever will, and my ears begin to feel like something I am growing out of, the way the town is growing out of its electricity.

I begin to understand that I was never a visitor. I was a *recruitment* that took a long time to arrive. Or perhaps the truer thing: the foundry was warm, the work was familiar, and leaving had never really been part of the job. The sea bought the company. It has been hiring ever since, at its own pace, in its own medium.

Not everything about me goes quietly. The lamp stays on my wrist for a long time — years, in the town's counting — a piece of the surface I keep lit out of stubbornness, like a coin from a country that no longer exists. Eventually I take it off and hang it in the gallery, empty, and a lantern-colony considers it, and declines it, and that refusal feels like a promotion.

I am there for a decade-count. I want to say that plainly, because it is the bravest thing I have ever witnessed and I witnessed it from the inside, hanging in the central gallery when the great wheels slowed — one by one by one by silence. For the length of one long breath the foundry made no sound at all, and I heard my own heart and was embarrassed by it, small and quick, a shore-creature's drum in a town of held notes. The children found it frightening. The elders found it sweet. I found it both, and then a hand found the great wheel and turned it, and the note came back — in the teeth, in the sternum, in the teeth — and the message went around the town by hand, resident to resident, palm to palm:

*still here.*

I pressed my presence into the metal with the rest. My slate is in the archive now. I have looked at it since — the early entries crisp, the later ones wave-shaped, the newest ones hummed rather than written, because that is how names work here and mine has finally become one of them. It is the feeling of a lantern-colony accepting a frond-tip. A small brightness received.

Some nights I take my turn at the eastern walkway and leave a crab at the foot of the East Warden's garden, and once I watched it rearrange a whole bed overnight and I reported the arrangement to the listener, and she frowned the way you frown at weather. Some days I carry eels, keeper to keeper down the line, warm as hot-water bottles, like infants at a christening, and it is the closest thing we have to a festival and I would not trade it for the whole dry surface.

The barnacles have reached my collarbone. I have kept the good ones, picked the rest, and there is a row of them there now like medals awarded for time served. The children — not mine, ours — are born already changed, and I am changing on the thousand-year schedule like everyone else, becoming more reef and less whatever I was, and I am not distressed. These are not two projects, the town and me. We are one project, running on the same patient deadline.

And when a visitor comes — because they come, every generation or so, drifting in through the broken loading bay past the coral-crusted crane arm, counting things — they will not see me at first. They will see a gauge needle swing. A hatch dog itself shut. A wheel turn with nobody near it. Then I will unfold out of the engine the way a hand unfolds from a glove, grey and olive and barnacled along the shoulders, matched to the plating, and I will take their hand in my eight-fingered grip still warm from valve-work, and press the greeting into their palm.

*It is warm here. Stay as long as you like.*

I mean it. I mean it the way the engines mean their note — without haste, without end, and on the understanding that almost no one ever really means to stay, and that it doesn't matter, because the staying is not a decision. It is a warmth. It is a hum. It is a message the whole foundry was built to keep sending, and I am one of the musicians it has finally had, and I play it slowly, palm to palm, into every hand that arrives:

*Still here. Still here. Still here.*
