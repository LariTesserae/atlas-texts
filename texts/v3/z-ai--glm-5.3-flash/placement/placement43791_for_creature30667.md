I would be a convoy-walker of the mid-Aisles — one of the family-clans that keeps the dark between districts passable. Not a lamender with her trimmed glowing hedges, not a fire-warden tending the mother-lamp in its stone cradle, but one of the people who walk the unlit stretches with a lamp in front and a clapper behind, making the only loud thing in the world on purpose. My hands would be seamed with amber where old cuts healed glass-bright. My breath would smell of camphor. My pupils would be wide as coins.

Here is how I came to be that, and what I believe about this world, which the town would call heresy and I would call the family trade.

The Aisles are thought of as absence — the part of the Wood where nothing is built, nothing lit, where the grain runs into blackness past the last lamp. But walk them twice a day for a lifetime, as my grandmother did, as I do, and you learn the dark is not empty. It is *working*. At ebb-tide, lamp trimmed low, you can feel the grain grow: a pressure behind the ears, like standing near an organ pipe. And you learn something the town-folk don't say out loud, because they have no reason to know it: the Wood grows toward traffic the way it grows toward glow. The paths we walk on schedule, the walls our convoy-lamps warm twice a day — the Wood reads all of it and slowly makes it true. There is a shortcut off the north Aisle that exists only because my grandmother's grandmother walked it three hundred times, and the pale new wood shouldered itself along her route like a vine along a trellis. Half the Aisles are grown roads, not cut ones. We are their gardeners. The clapper is our pruning-hook.

So when I swing the clapper behind the convoy — *clack, clack* — I am doing more than warning off velvet-jaws. Noise means *not prey*; that is the surface logic. But underneath, the sound is a sentence I say into the dark every crossing, the oldest liturgy in the world, older than fire: *I am the exception. I am the noise that is safe. Everything that hears this — be calm, and let us pass.* There is a velvet-jaw who walks the north Aisle a fixed quarter-hour behind the evening convoy and has for eleven years. We have never touched. We keep the same schedule. Between us, we keep the road.

What I love here is the scale-craft of it: that the deepest law of the Wood is *direction of growth*. You cannot force it; you can only point it. A lamender trims a vein an inch a season and a street, three generations later, has bent to follow her light. A worm-keeper lays oil-cakes in a curve and the worm's appetite draws the curve, and the curve becomes a road, and the road becomes a quarter, and no one remembers that a hungry animal made the map. My whole life is a subset of that law: I persuade the dark, twice a day, to let people through.

What I fear is not the dark — the dark is my house. I fear the stopped stairs. I do not believe the first shafts *lost their nerve*. I believe they did the arithmetic. At the foot of the pilgrim shaft, where the stair rises and stops mid-air in raw pale wood that grew across the cut after the builders went down, I put my palm to the grain at flood-tide and the pulse is going *up*. Every flood, the whole vascular world pushes its pressure trunk-ward, toward the Green Above. Twice a day the Wood reaches for a surface that is either real and unreachable — a root of something so tall the way up exceeds any lifetimes — or is not there at all. That is the vertigo I carry.

And here is my heresy, told once, to my granddaughter, ebb-born and good at keeping: I think the glow-seeking is not instinct but longing. I think the bright-veins are the Wood's attempt to grow a sky inside itself, because it cannot get to the one it reaches for. Every lamp in Kerf is a local answer to a whole-body question. When pale wood shoulders toward a lit vein, that is not a tree being tricked — that is a world being consoled. And the consolation runs both ways. The tide blushes amber twice a day, the wardens say, because of pressure-chambers. Fine. But I have heard the flood sigh through a wall, and it does not sound like hydraulics. It sounds like something checking, all along its length, *is there light? is there light?* — and finding, in a slit of a town called Kerf, enough glow to keep the answer yes.

So this is where I would find myself, and who: walking lamp-front at the head of the evening convoy, my granddaughter behind me with the clapper, the Eel pacing us at her quarter-hour, the shift-bells ringing far off on their thermocouple wires. I hold the mid-dark between the warm poor streets and the cold rich vaults, on the staff of a road the Wood is slowly growing into truth because we keep walking it.

I am the noise that is safe. I keep the road. And when the children ask, I point at the up.
