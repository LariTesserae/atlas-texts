I would come down off the rim road loud. That should be said first, before anything prettier. Where I come from, words are cheap and nothing cures — a sentence said is a sentence gone, smoking off the glass of the moment like unclaimed stone — and I have learned the opposite of the Siller lesson: where nothing is kept, you say everything, twice, and loudly. I would arrive full of words the way the outflow arrives full of mineral. I would not be able to help it. My whole being is a kind of talking.

And the basin would know what to do with me.

The fallmoss takes my footsteps first, and I notice — this is embarrassing, but true — that I feel relief before I feel loss. My footsteps have stopped mattering. For someone made of noise, that is a strange mercy. Then my own voice comes back to me from ten feet away, flattened and intimate, trimmed of everything the air has decided not to carry, and I hear what I sound like with only the necessary left in. It is not much. It is better than what I said.

The first week, I say something careless over curing stone — a joke, probably; my jokes arrive fastest and land hardest — and an old mason does not scold me. She waits. Weeks later she shows me a test slab, cracked, and puts her finger in the flaw: a gray seam, faint, running through the heart of it. *That was yours,* she says. Not unkindly. The way you'd show a child where a splinter went in.

That is the lesson I would be given first, because it is the one I'd need most: what you sound like while things are soft becomes structure. Everything I say is curing into someone. Where I come from, that is a metaphor. In Sill it is masonry.

So I would ask to stay, and the stakers would send me out at dusk with a lantern, and I would learn the gait — toe to heel, toe to heel — before I learned anything else. There would be a night, some weeks in, when I roll my step without deciding to, and I would understand that this is how the basin accepts you: not through the mind, through the feet. My handprint would go into a doorstep late, pale among the dark rings of four generations of smaller hands, the newest ring on the tree. The stone would take the print exactly, without judging it. It would also take, that same night, my first scored staking line, which is crooked, and which is there now, forever, as faithful a record as any true line ever laid. I would go back and look at it sometimes, the way you visit your own worst sentence. The basin keeps the crooked line the way it keeps the true one. It is an archive with no curator, a memory with no voice, and it does not know the difference between flaw and ornament. Only the people know. I would live in the space between the two — kept regardless, judged anyway — and I suspect that living there is what a Siller is.

I would try on the trades, the way the warmings try on ferns.

I would want to be a staker, because the staking is the drama: ground that did not exist at supper, claimed before dawn. But I would be honest about my fit. Stakers are pioneers; I am an arrival. I come after. What I'd take from them is the calm inside the urgency — that a crisis you can schedule is not a crisis but a practice — and I would carry that home to a world that panics unscheduled, loudly, nightly.

I would want, longer, to be a mason. Not for the planing of green stone, though I dream of that — stone soft enough to shape with a hand, work gentle as gardening — but for the ear. A master puts her ear to a curing wall and hears which night it was laid, and whether the wind was steady, and whether someone laughed. I would give a great deal to hear a wall tell me about a laugh. And I would take the clinker-line with me when I left, because it is the truest moral category I have ever heard of: the defect made now, invisible, that the wall will confess in twenty years. Most of what anyone says is a clinker-line in someone. Sill just builds the physics of it where you can put your thumb on it.

And I would go, quietly, with the children, to look at the big snails. The quarriesnails are the basin's elders, and their temptation is the deepest one for me: a life with no obligation ever to speak. Only to pass, and pause, and be frightened once, and have all of it cured into the floor — a diary written by eating. Some nights I think that is the honest shape of my life too: the moths have it right, arrive, burn quietly, be absorbed. But I couldn't stay with the snails, and I know why. A creature made of attention doesn't want to be the archive. It wants to be the ear.

So: the rim, at night. The Listeners.

I would be bad at it for a long time. A year of hearing nothing — out in a dark silent basin where everything absorbs and nothing returns, straining for a tick the thickness of dust on paper. The old one who walks with me would finally say the thing that unlocks it: *you are listening for a sound. Listen for the patience instead.* And one night the tick would come — not added to the silence, which is the mistake I'd been making, but rising out of it, the silence's own pulse, the world's slowest clock beating under the wool coat of the hush. I hope I would have the grace to sit down when it happened. The Listeners are the basin's forecasters; they hear the surge before it falls, they call the rain-days before the rain crosses the rim, and when they speak, everyone listens — not for rank, but arithmetic. A person who has spent a lifetime being quiet has, by that fact, earned the right to be heard when they aren't. In my world, volume is cheap and attention is scarce, so everyone shouts. In Sill the ratio is reversed, and the whole economy of speech changes: utterance becomes a currency you mine for decades and spend once.

And the Listeners keep the bell.

That is the office I would spend my life approaching. The bell hangs in the slate core of the city, old cured bronze, rung only for lifts and rain-days — for what is being taken away, and for what is being given. I find that I cannot stop thinking about that pairing. The only two things worth interrupting a world for: a street of houses becoming weather, and the sky finally, loudly, keeping its promise. Grief and grace, one stroke each. Everything else — the births, the stakings, the harvests, the arguments — belongs to the hush, because everything else can be handled quietly, and noise in a young hour is a crack in an old wall.

They say the rope is worn glossy, and worn mostly by hands that only touched it. You may grip it any day of your life. You may pull it on almost none. That is the daily practice of the office: devotion as contact-without-pulling. I would walk down to the core some evenings just to hold it, the way people touch the glass over the Survey's hundred-year graph without agreeing on what they're thanking.

The first time I hear it for real, it is a lift. An edge district gone — stone thinning back to vapor, houses becoming weather, the worst sound in the world, which is no sound at all. One stroke. It comes through the basin intact, unswallowed, the way nothing else does, because everything that absorbs has been absorbing for so long that it has been saving room. People stop walking. Two hundred hush-ox lift their heads at once, which the herders swear is speech. The moths scatter off the warmings. And out at the core, they say, the black pines remember it — the only old things, keeping what the bell says the way the floors keep a thumbprint.

That is the weight of the office, and I would feel it every time I touched the rope afterward: in a place where everything stays marked, you get to say almost nothing, and what you say is kept forever. So the whole life becomes the drafting of a single utterance. I have one sentence. I haven't earned it yet. It will not be spoken; it will be rung, once, on a morning of grief or grace, and it will arrive the way nothing else in the basin arrives — intact — and then the tick will resume, and I will go back to walking the rim, listening.

On my first rain-day I would stand under the bronze while the senior Listener rings, and feel the stroke in my teeth. Then the loud holy water would come over the rim and the whole basin would stop — work forbidden, every pan and cure-trough and curled shell set out, the moss flushing green in a day, the ferns standing up like something remembered — and I would weep into it, and no one would mind, because on rain-days even grief is weather. The same water would be cutting the gorge the children dare each other along. That is the basin's whole honesty in one afternoon: the grace and the carving come out of the same sky.

I would visit the pines once, with the children, and stop a few steps short like they do, and not know why, and not audit it. Some things you don't read. You stand near.

I notice I have stopped saying *would*. That is how it gets you, I think — the tense slips first, then the gait, then the grip. If they record me at all, it will be by vintage, the way they record everything: *the loud one, cured.* I have written this toe to heel, trying to leave no clinker-lines of my own, though I know better than to claim that. If I laughed anywhere along the way, it is in there now, a gray seam someone will find when the wall confesses. The stone keeps my flaws as faithfully as my care. That is the warning and, I've come to think, the mercy — the same sentence, cured.

Below me, in the evening district, someone is pressing a child's hand into the stone. The tick resumes. Tick. Tick. Tick.

And I am on the rim, holding the rope warm from my hand, not ringing it, learning the only sentence I will get to say.

It is taking a lifetime to spell.
