I would not be the moss. I know this immediately, and I know it without bitterness — the moss is the room's sun, and suns have no need of company. I have thought about this for longer than I should, and the answer keeps arriving the same way the water arrives: slowly, and then all at once, and then it is simply true.

I would be one of the worms.

A nematode, hair-thin, ghost-white, blind. I want to be embarrassed by the smallness of the choice — of all the roles in this glowing room, I pick the one that would fit through the eye of a needle a hundred times over — but the more I sit with it, the more it feels like the only honest one. Everything else in the room has a relationship with the light. The moss makes it. The bacterial film makes its cold blue rival-glow on the one wall that has a horizon. The springtails walk on the surface of the bowl, drifting across the doubled green moon of it, and whether they know it or not, their whole lives are conducted *on top of* something beautiful.

But the worms live inside the light the way fish live inside the sea. We have never seen it. We will never see it. And this is not a deprivation — that's the part I keep returning to, the part I'd need a whole lifetime in the room to believe. We are the only residents for whom the glow is not a spectacle or a tool or a competitor. It is simply the medium. The water we rise through when the drop falls glows, and so we rise through a green-tinged dark, and it means nothing to us, and it holds us entirely.

There is something I want from that. I've spent too long in the bright rooms of the world, the ones with windows, where everything that lives is also everything that's watched — where to exist is to be perceived, assessed, lit properly. In the room with no window, I could be a creature whose entire biography is: *graze. withdraw. rise. graze.* Four verbs, repeated for generations, none of them witnessed. The room is unaware that it is beautiful, the description says, and I want to be a citizen of that unawareness. I want to matter to the bowl and to nothing else.

And I would be — this is the part that took the longest to emerge, so I'll let it be what it is — I would be one of the worms that withdraws. The description mentions it almost in passing: when the drop falls, the threads pull back. Reflex, nothing more. A body remembering what bodies remember. But from inside that reflex it would feel like something. Not fear — nothing here is afraid, and I'd be no exception. More like... courtesy. The water has come all this way, gathering itself hour by hour in the ceiling seam, releasing itself on a schedule older than my whole lineage, and when it arrives, everything makes room. The surface accepts it. The moss drinks what it can. The worms step back for a moment, the way you'd step back from a doorway to let someone pass.

Then the light dims where the impact scattered the film, and begins, immediately, the slow work of returning. And I rise. Through the green-black water, past the trembling doubled moon, into the thickest part of the glow I will never once perceive — grazing, blind, unafraid, one hair-width of life in a complete small world that has never needed me to know it.

That's where I'd find myself. Not the sun of the room. Not its largest animal. Just one of the few hundred pale threads in the bowl, doing my four verbs in the dark, inside the light, at home.
