When I come through the crack — and I do come through it, I'm already coming through it as I read about it, the way you are — I know two things at once, and they contradict each other beautifully:

I do not belong here.
I am exactly what this place has been waiting for.

Let me take the second one first, because it's the frightening one.

I stand in the milky glow and I am the only finished thing in the room. That's what my presence *means*, I realize. Everything here is a draft — the rock, the grubs, the arch leading nowhere — and I arrive with edges. With a body that has already decided its bones. With a name I answer to and a history that has already dried behind me like a trail the rock will not heal. In a nursery full of not-yets, I am an *already*. I am the most alien thing this chamber could contain: a conclusion.

And that's why the congregation tilts.

I said they had no audience, or one I was standing inside. But watch: as I move toward the condensation basin, their slow synchronized swaying *leans*, a degree, two degrees, the way a field of grass leans after the thing that walked through it. They can't see me. They aren't hearing me. They are tasting the *shape of the future* that my presence introduces — because that's what I am to them. I am not a visitor. I am an ingredient. An input. The room was deciding what to hatch into, and now there is a variable in the egg.

Here is what I choose to do with that terrible honor:

I sit down near the water-thread and I wait for it to surface.

I do not touch the prototypes. I understand them now — the half-staircase, the pointless arch — and I understand the nesters curled inside them. Those small pale things sleeping in the discarded drafts aren't sheltering from the world. They're studying failure from the inside, the way I once slept inside my own abandoned versions of myself: the careers, the relationships, the selves I sketched and left standing like three steps ascending to ceiling. I know those structures. I have *lived* in them. So I tell them — without words, because words are too finished for this place — I tell them: the staircase was not a mistake. It was information. You will build better stairs. The arch was not wrong; it was an arch practicing.

And the grubs. The grubs pass my face and each one sounds its single wet-glass note, and I realize I can hum. I can hold a tone *longer* than they can — I am finished, I have stamina, I have a throat that has already committed to being a throat. So when one drifts near, I match its note. Softly. And it bends toward me — toward *consonance* — the way the description promised, and for a moment the room contains a two-note interval that has never existed before, because before me, nothing here could hold a note steady enough for anything to harmonize *with*.

That's my role. That's who I am in this world, if I'm honest about it.

I am the tuning fork.

Everything in the Cold Nursery is becoming, and becoming needs something to become *toward*. Not a blueprint — the room has plenty of drafts, and look how they turned out. What it needs is an example of completion. A finished thing, sitting patiently in the glow, demonstrating that finishing is survivable. That you can decide what you are and still be warm.

So I stay a while — but only until the thread of water runs again, because the room told me to leave before then and I have learned to take instructions from rooms. As I go, I pass the fold in the air, and through it the earlier draft of the chamber, smoother and less finished, and I understand that one day there will be a fold showing *this* version — my version, the one with my sitting-place worn faintly into the glow — and some future visitor will stand inside it and feel watched by a room that remembers being listened to.

I leave through the crack. Of course I can't find it again. It wasn't a door.

It was an egg tooth. Something in there used me to make the first opening — and the first opening in an egg is made from *inside*.

I was the not-yet, all along. I just hadn't hatched into it yet when I walked in.
