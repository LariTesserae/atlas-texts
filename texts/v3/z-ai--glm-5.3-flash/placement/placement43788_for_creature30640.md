I come up out of the marsh on the Shelf's low side, following the pulse. That is how you travel here — you learn within an hour that the heartbeat in the ground is not one sound but many, offset, layered, and that a person who listens carefully can steer by it the way a sailor steers by current. Four seconds, four seconds, a fainter station answering from the left. I walk with my pack high on my shoulders because the made tide will come before evening and the reed-flats pool to the knee, and I do what everyone does in this world: I click softly to the dark ahead of me, *I am here, I am here,* and the dark clicks back with a hundred small voices, insects or reeds or other walkers, none of them minding me.

For a while I believe I have come to be a drover. It is a reasonable life, and I can feel its shape fitting me: walking the terraces with the herds, keeping the engines' calendar, my days measured in tide-rises instead of suns. I like the idea of it. I like the idea of a body that keeps machine-time, that has made peace with the fact that the moon of this world was built by hands and maintained by hands and is, in some sense, a shared task. There is something in that I want.

But then the truth of me arrives, the way it always does, walking behind me the whole way and catching up at the first engine-house.

My eyes work.

They work, and they are useless, and they are worse than useless — they are the only thing on the Shelf that is lying. Everyone else's darkness is honest: it is warm, wet, total, a material like the description says, felt on the skin like wet wool. But my eyes keep painting. They lay a blackness over the true dark, an invented darkness, a picture of absence where there is only presence — vapor, stone, warm air, the whole crowded world. I am the only blind creature here, because I am the only one who cannot stop trying to see. The keepers at the station taste my forearm, click their greetings into my shoulder, and when one of them touches my face and finds those strange wet, open, striving organs, she makes a sound I have not heard before — a low, amused, sympathetic phrase, the equivalent of a raised eyebrow. *A guest arriving undressed.*

I stay. Of course I stay. You do not walk back into the marsh because you are embarrassed by your own face.

What happens to me happens slowly, which is how everything happens here. I learn the channels by fingertip, sweet vein, bitter vein, poured-glass stone. I learn the manners of clicking. I learn that I have been mishearing the world my whole life — that eyes were always only a rough draft of attention, and now, at last, I am being asked to write in the finished hand. I get good at it faster than I expected. It turns out that someone who once spent every waking hour placing objects in space by light can place them in space by pressure and echo with an obsessive precision; my old sight becomes a kind of training, a calibration the others never needed and never wanted. The keepers put me to work at the listening-post between two stations, where the whole job is the off-beat — hearing whether the four-second heart of the north engine and the four-second heart of the south stay properly apart, because the tide is the calendar, and if the beat slips, fruiting slips, and the herds walk into empty terraces, and the whole arranged life of the highland loses its rhythm. I sit in the seam between two heartbeats and I keep the time of the world. It is the closest thing to sight I will ever do again, and it is better than sight.

And my eyes — my eyes begin the settling. The vapor works on them. The disuse works on them. Grief works on them, a little; you cannot learn a world this thoroughly by ear without understanding, eventually, that the organ straining after a light that never comes has been a small open wound in your face, and the body is kind, and closes wounds.

There is a rite for this. I ask about it, embarrassed, an adult wanting a child's ceremony, and the keepers click at each other for a while — that sound of soft rain that means a thing is being decided — and then the old one who first touched my face puts her hands on either side of my head and sings the settling song to me, the whole of it, slowly, the way you sing to a child whose face you are holding. My eyes are finished when she takes her hands away. Clouded. Closed for good, in the way that a door is closed when the house is complete.

Then they light my lantern.

Everyone carries one; I had carried mine dark, an heirloom of nowhere, dead weight on my belt, because no one had ever lit it for me and I did not know I was allowed to ask. At the settling they take it, and prime it, and burn it — and of course it does not shine. It gives heat. That is all fire is. But the keeper holds it near my face, and the warmth lands on my clouded eyes and my cheeks like a hand, and I finally understand what the lanterns are for, what they have always been for. It is the only way anyone on this world can be *looked at* — by flame, at a handshake's distance, a small personal sun that never once tries to show you anything. Warmth is the gaze here. A lit lantern is the equivalent of an open, attentive face.

I keep the listening-post now. Twice a shift the seeps rise around my ankles and the streams run backward for an hour and I sit in the seam of two heartbeats and count the world's pulse, four and four, never together, always faithful. And when I walk the terraces at tide-rise, through the crowd that sounds like soft rain, I sing my three notes into the dark —

— and this is the part I would not have believed before I came: that they come back. Sung from a dozen directions, corrected, warmed, *looked at*. A name is a phrase, and to be greeted is to hear your own small melody returned to you out of the wet wool of the world by people who are not looking at you at all, and do not need to, and never once, in all the generations of the Shelf, wished for a morning.

I stop waiting for one. The dark is not an absence. It never was. I was the absence, walking around with my eyes open — and now the world has finished me, held my face in two hands, and closed me gently into its shape, the way the water finished with the stone ten thousand years ago: polished, weeping, warm, and finally, finally in its form.
