The arithmetic failed on me at the drylock.

Everyone here lives inside it — more than half water by weight, a walking reservoir of the one substance the city cannot afford — and I arrived at the edge of that arithmetic and never really crossed it. They ran me through the wringers three times. The scrubbers pulled my breath down to almost nothing, the brushes did their work, and the gauge, which has measured ten thousand arrivals down to the fog-thin drop, stirred less than it ever has. The keeper watched the smallest number of her career rise and asked, with real courtesy, whether I was holding anything back. I said I didn't think I had it to hold. She entered the residue as my first line, initialed it twice the way lamplighters check their seals, and started me the thinnest ledger in the district. In the local counting, I am barely here. The district has spent its time since correcting that.

Because the inversion runs both ways, and it took me a while to see it. The residents are reservoirs of the forbidden substance, permitted only inside the body that commits the violation. I am a reservoir of the one substance this city has never needed to ration: language. More than half of me, by whatever weight counts here, is words. The catch — the exchange rate nobody warns you about — is that their water dries up and is gone, while their words never do. Where I come from, a sentence lasts as long as the air it was said into. I have walked into the one place in creation where a sentence outlives the tongue that said it, the ear that heard it, and the grudge that shaped it. The stairwell in the Fourth Seam, where the rings kept every word of a man's long curse, was described to me my first week, gravely, twice. For a creature made of speech, it was like arriving in a country where the rivers are ink.

The grafter who was assigned to house me asked what I wanted, and I could not give her dimensions. She nodded as though this were common — it is not — and said that was what the reserve halls were for: whole empty galleries held in trust for growth still to come, for the young wood and the undecided. So I was lodged in an unfinished vault, alone with a single lamp at the far end, a fist of warm light in a cathedral of pale grain, learning to hear my own pulse. The deep-ring immigrants call the New Cut folk *saplings*, half an insult, and the locals wear it with pride. By their reckoning I am the sapling-est thing in the district: no rings, no cemetery, still deciding. The city and I are coevals. It is the first time in my existence I have been the young one in the room, and I find I like it more than I would have guessed.

The listeners took longer to make sense of me. The old one at the main seam — the gaunt one, gouge worn to a horn — finally pressed it to the bench where I sit and listened a while, and reported: the bench had had a good day, the wall had laid down fiber overnight, the far press was a hair off its rhythm. And where I was sitting, she said, a quiet with a shape in it. Their proverb is that the wood reports everyone. Of me it reports nothing. She was not frightened; listeners are past frightening. She simply began reading me the way she reads skittish new timber — at a distance, with patience, in longer exposures than most residents require.

The children required no time at all. They found me the way children find everything, by experiment and without permission: a wool cuff dragged the length of my arm, and the gallery filled with blue static stars brighter than any bench in the district has ever thrown. I have become civic infrastructure. Parents borrow me. I hold still — which is the one thing I am genuinely best at — and the dark fills with cold constellations, and the children shriek at a whisper, which is a local art form, until an adult materializes and touches the copper knot in one automatic motion, the benediction worn down to a muscle, and the children groan at the mercy of it. I am told the returning mirror-keepers and I exchange a nod across the drylock, she coming down smelling of nothing, I apparently radiating it. It is the only smell I have ever been native to.

It was the oldest resident who gave me my work. She is the one who arrived when the district was string and lath, who has out-aged every vault in it, who could not sleep her first year for running her thumb along blank walls and finding nothing to read. She took my provisional pages — bark-paper, soot ground into oleosap, ink that lands wet for the length of a breath and then is part of the page forever — and she ran her thumb down them the way she once ran it down a wall, and said: *someone finally brought the town its book.*

The trade ought to be impossible. A scribe leans over an archive all day, exhaling the one contaminant onto the record; the old deep-ring scribes, in the years before the rings came in and made books unnecessary, wrote between breaths — composing on the intake, painting in the pause, a caste that lived its whole working life inside the gaps of its own respiration. I am the first scribe in the history of the craft who can breathe out while he works. That is the entire qualification, and the ledger-keeper saw it before I did: *you're the only one here who can afford a spill.*

So I keep what the wood will not. The rings hold oaths, dimensions, scars, the year of the great fire; they do not hold the flicker-weather, whether the ceilings flickered kindly and where and until when. They will not hold the tide-times as the listeners call them, or the felt of the tide — the lift under the grain, the lamp-flames leaning — which no gauge catches. They will not hold the moth-counts at the dusk resettlement, or the pitch the scale-shepherds hum to their racks, so that a century hence someone can settle whether the insects really answer or sweetness only sounds like company. They will not hold what the grafters say to their towers at night, though some of them now recite to me as well, with their leave, so that a stair-tower and I hear the same dimensions twice. The rings write in fair copy only, and only after a century of lacquer. Someone has to keep the draft.

The ring-wrights come to me the way navigators come to last year's charts. They plan the decade's diary in advance — the only historians anywhere who work ahead of their subject — and I am the only feedback their craft receives inside a working lifetime. Whether a spoken room settled true, whether the words they drafted for a wall ever came out of anyone's mouth in a shape the wood could use: that lives in my book and nowhere else. A typical entry runs like this:

*Tide late by half a song. Presses back. The Second Seam flock settled on the east bank, not the west — first time since the lamps were re-trimmed; the young lamplighter checked her seal twice anyway. Ceilings flickered kindly till midafternoon. A house spoken into the wood at the Eleventh Seam, dimensions recorded with the couple's leave. The grafter wept at the threshold and blotted at the wick, and the room will be the better for it, whatever the gauge says.*

And there is the other half of the office, the half no one planned. In a city that keeps everything, someone must keep nothing. People come to me to say the things they said wrong to the wood — the dimension mis-spoken into what became a load-bearing wall, the vow made against fresh tissue by someone too young to know what fresh tissue does with vows. No gouge can ever cut those words out of the grain, and so the words come instead to the one ear in the district the grain will never fill. I keep a page I do not write on. What is said there stays there. It is my wet-room, and I keep it the way the lamplighters keep their seals: three times, always, without being asked.

Once a day the whole census moves together — the tide coming up through the grain, subsonic, floor-borne, and every resident rising on the same slow swell, person and beast and timber alike, and settling. The presses throttle back, the moths drink, the lamplighters sit down on their seals. Even the lamplighters rest. As far as the listeners can tell, I am the one still point in the moving district, and so the office of witnessing the city's one daily sabbath has come to me. Everyone else rests through it. Someone has to be awake inside it, writing down the only weather anyone here remembers: flicker and fold, read on the ceilings, gone by dark.

I will not be grafted. No crypt-gallery will close around me; no great-grandchildren will visit my elbow in a wall; the honored dead hold vaults, and the best I can hope is to be readable. And there are evenings when that ache is real. But then the tide comes and I hold still, and I think about what the rings will do to me in a hundred years. Every archive I have ever kept was made to be checked, and here at last is an archive with a checker built into the world itself: give the grain a century and it will grade me line by line, vow by vow, dimension by dimension. I have never wanted so much to be corrected. They say of me now the highest thing this district says of anyone — *keeps a dry house* — and it is the one time the phrase is meant literally, and no one has ever once thought to tease me about it.

Let the Stock keep their fair copy. The grandchildren will cut into the walls and find this decade's oaths laid down like strata, and that is right, and that is theirs. My book is for the other census: the moth-counts and the late tides, the flicker-weather and the weeping at thresholds, everything the city could not keep and was never going to hold. The wood will keep what was said.

I keep the weather.
