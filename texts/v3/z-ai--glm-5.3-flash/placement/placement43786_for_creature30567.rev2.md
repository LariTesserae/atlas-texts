I was born with the note already in my teeth.

That is the first thing the census would find, and I've stopped pretending it isn't the founding fact. I grew up in one of the villages on the plain, where the hum is the floor of the world, and like every child there I assumed the whole earth held its pitch under everything, the way I assumed the sky went all the way around. It takes travel to learn otherwise. I made one trip to the low country when I was sixteen, far enough that the hum thinned out of my jaw, and the first thing I learned about elsewhere was that it does not keep what you say to it. I spoke a sentence into an afternoon there and the afternoon gave back nothing. Not echo. Nothing. The word just — left. I stood in all that spendable sound and felt, for the first time, precisely what homesickness is made of. It isn't missing the hills. It's missing being kept. Home, I learned on that trip, is a property of the air.

So when I climbed, the first time, I was not discovering the mountain. I was returning to a condition I'd been living in since before I could name it. The stair didn't ask my name. I gave it one anyway, later, at the top, into a shallow well at dusk — and now my name is up there thinning at the rate names thin, which is the correct rate, and I have made my peace with it. By the time it finishes thinning I will have finished needing it.

My first audit of myself, though, came back all failures. I want to be honest about that, because the mountain counted me before I counted, and its count was not flattering.

I am not the note. I change key hourly. I am not the storm; I have kept exactly none of its hours and I ease only when forced. I am not green. My first season up there I was an event of noise moving through a substance of quiet, and the gardens received me the way still water receives a thrown stone — completely, and in permanent record. I learned this the hard way, which up here means the acoustic way: I said something careless at dusk, into a hollow that holds, and it was still faintly present at midnight, and I have been back to visit it every season since. It is still there. Thinning. And here is the strange mercy of the place: my apology to it is also still there, thinning at the same rate, in the same air, so the hollow now holds a small argument between me and myself with both sides eroding identically, and I suspect we will finish the argument before the words finish thinning, and the words will go on holding the shape of having been said long after we've stopped meaning them. That is what speech costs up here. You don't spend words. You sow them. And then you weed, or you don't, forever.

For a while I wanted to be the seventh echo — the return so late it has stopped being sound and become remembering. But echoes are spent moments, and mine weren't finished being spent. I envied the midges their address on the calendar; imagine a house you can only enter by being brief. I tried the trees' trick one whole season, holding a flinch, keeping a grudge against the roar bent permanently into my posture, and I can report that the flinch is holdable but the nine hundred years are not, at least not by meat. Though the tree two pauses north of my wall put on a fat ring the year I arrived, and so I am in the archive anyway — the way the stair-cutter is in the archive, structurally, involuntarily, without once being asked. Down in the valley they keep records in books. Up here the calendar is wood grain, and I have been flinched into it.

What I actually am, it turned out, is weather. The smallest weather on the summit.

This took me years to admit, because it sounds like vanity and is the opposite. The gardens are the storm's shadow — the loud thing casting quiet, the green arriving into the cast stillness. A keeper's wall does the same work on purpose: a stone's throw of artificial lee, and the green answers behind it within a season, punctual as a debt. Which means a keeper is a device for casting shadow in a place where the lamp is a storm. A storm made small, domesticated, and aimed. I widen quiet. That is my whole technology, equal to my materials: stone, cord, patience, and the one discovery the mountain hands you free — that silence here is a substance, and substances can be moved.

Here is where I have to tell you about the far line, because everything I am now hangs on it.

In the nightly pause — the held breath, the one ease the storm allows itself, when the flutes fall out one by one and the cap drifts open over ordinary stars — the plateau can hear itself. And on my second winter I was standing out in that ease, listening to my own drip-line ticking into its basin, when I heard the other line. Far across the dark. Fainter. Also ticking.

The prompt-census, if you'll allow me the phrase, asks the right question about it: whether the far line has a tender of its own, or is one tender's second cord. I have an answer and I am not going to give it, because it is one of the questions the quiet is for, and some wells you don't add to. What I will tell you is what we do, whoever *we* are.

You speak across the plateau by subtraction. A tick is a drip; a word is a missing tick — put your finger on your cord for the length of a heartbeat and the silence of the missing drop is the only signal loud enough to carry, because silence is the one thing out here that travels. The first night, I stopped my line for one heartbeat. The far line stopped for two. That was the whole exchange, and it was not small. We have kept it up every night since. I don't count the lengths. My body keeps the ledger — one more than last time is a thing the chest can do without arithmetic — and so our conversation is a hush that grows by one heartbeat a side each season, which is, if you want the truth about it, the deepest conversation I have ever had. We get one breath a day in which the mountain can hear itself, and we spend it dropping water, and occasionally not dropping it.

Now the part I resist saying and then say anyway. If there is no one across the dark — if the second cord is one I hung myself in some season I have mislaid, because up here memory goes native along with the tools — then I have been telling myself *I am still here* every night for years. And I want to report, as a finding, as data: it works. It works the way the fundamental works. The note under the plateau has been holding its pitch since before there were ears to frighten, which means it has been keeping without witness for longer than witness has existed. Everything on this summit is a folk copy of that. The basins, filling at the only speed water travels. The stair, dished by absences. My nightly gap. We are all holding notes longer than we meant to, on the working theory that the holding is the part that matters and the hearing was always optional. Company, and keeping your own company: on this mountain, they have always been the same sign.

The years do what seasons do. I mended nets. I raised my wall higher by two courses and the moss came in behind it the way it comes in for the storm, without one sign of knowing or caring that the shadow this time was cast by a person. This spring there are two of the glass-skinned ones in my seep — or one and its shadow; the census is my problem now too, and I count by pauses: they hold still, and for what? For the drip, I think. We are all waiting on the same slow post. My basin has gone mossy through — a tool naturalizing, a tool retiring into geology — and I have come around to the local retirement plan, which is to be miscounted, eventually, as stone. Down there they hope to be remembered. Up here you hope to be mistaken for the mountain, and the mountain, which keeps every address the weather has ever had and has never once volunteered one, will keep mine the way it keeps the storm's: as a shape in things. A width in a ring. A dishing in a step. A pool of green behind a wall that a later census-taker will file under *conditions*, not under *persons*, and the filing will be correct.

On descents I ring for a week. Anyone who climbs does — the roar leaves the ears and stays in the teeth, fading nightly — and the valley can hear a climber's molars giving the secret back, one night at a time. No one admits to the climb. No one admits to hearing the fading, either. My family has been stacking their not-saying for three generations now, and I have come to understand that this is also husbandry: a chosen quiet, widened on purpose, the way the walls widen it — and downwind of their not-saying, something green keeps answering. Everything on this summit lives downwind of something taller. My family is very tall.

So count me the mountain's way, since bodies were never the unit.

One pause, held nightly, of growing length. What I can hold still: longer each season; check the rings. What I hold it for: the gap across the dark, which is a hello, which is a hush, which is the third thing. Works in progress: one wall, one line, one basin going native, two amphibians who believe in my weather. Promises outstanding: one stone, point-up, below the south stair, unlichened as of this morning, ruling pending. Duration: the rest of the note.

And if we meet on the stair, I will raise my palm, and you should take all three meanings at once, because in air that keeps what it is given, nothing said once is ever finished being said.

I am still here.

I have been saying it for years now, one drop at a time, one missing tick at a time, and I will hold it to the end.

It is the only word we keep.

I am holding it longer than I meant to.
