There is no stone here. That takes a day of walking to accept.

The plateau — call it that, though it is less a place that was raised than a plant that grew — is green all the way down. Not grass upon rock: the rock is gone, or was never there, or was eaten and replaced long ago. What you stand on is a mantle of living tissue, many paces thick in the old places, felted and layered like skin over muscle, and beneath that, root-stone: a lace of mineral the roots themselves secreted, so that even the geology here is plant-built. Cut into a terrace edge and you find strata like the rings of an enormous stump — pale bands, dark bands, the deepest black as peat and older than anyone's counting.

Underfoot it gives. Not squelching — the surface is dense, springy, nearly dry by midday — but yielding the way a drumhead yields. It is warm. At this height the sun comes down thin and white and straight, and the mantle is dark and drinks it, and by evening the ground is warmer than the air, exhaling its stored heat long into the dark. That breath is the engine of everything else here.

Stand on any swell and the horizon is clean and far, curving faintly at the edges of your vision, because the plateau is the top step of the world and enormous past the reach of walking. What you see is mostly nothing happening: a continuous low turf, shorn-velvet dark, rolling in long settled swells, terraced in places into shallow green steps, seamed with dry veins — ancient channels where the mantle once pumped water and now merely stands, healed over, like scars that became features. Nothing is being carved here anymore. The landforms are grown and worn and in balance, the way a tendon is in balance. The palette is two colors: the green of the ground, the white-blue burn of the sky, and between them, everywhere, the haze-pillars.

Because the plateau breathes. On a hot midmorning you can watch its exhalations stand up off the swells — columns of haze, wide as hills, rising with terrible slowness and leaning in the wind. They are its weather, made of itself. The moisture of the whole country rides in that breath: it climbs all day, drifts out toward the margins, and comes back at night as fog, rolling up the swells in slow grey sheets. Dawn is the dripping hour. Every stem beards with droplets; the whole continent glitters and hisses quietly as it sheds; the shallow basins — the sweats, the herders call them — brim an inch higher, warm and mineral-green, crusted at their edges with salt laid down over centuries. By midmorning the heat pulls it all back up, and the turf tightens — you can feel it contract underfoot on the hottest days, a slow clench, the sweats shrinking to their cracks — and by afternoon the land is dry-eyed again. Rain falls here rarely, and never gently: a storm is a grey wall you watch approach for half a day across the open swells, the herds turning to face it long before it lands. When it hits, the mantle drinks it in minutes. Puddles last an hour. And the water the plateau exhales rises, cools, and falls again at its own edges weeks later — the plateau drinks its own rain, twice removed. It is damp the way an animal is damp: by economy, by breath, by skin.

It is also, always, loud.

The turf is made of millions of hollow stems, each one an open tube, and when the wind moves across the continent it plays them — a drone, a true organ-note, vast and layered, changing key with the wind's direction, dropping a full tone as the air cools at dusk. Under the drone is the rumble: the mantle is riddled with borers and swimmers, animal tissue threaded through the plant tissue, and the ground hums with their passage, a held bass note you feel through your feet before you hear it at all. Over both is everything with a throat. At dawn, when the fog burns off, the dew-walkers sing — pale, tall, stilt-legged thousands strung along the fog-line with their fringed mouths opened into the moving grey, drinking the condensation out of the air — and their chorus is a ringing, a washing, a sound like the sky full of struck metal. Human voices do not carry against any of this. The herders speak in whistles and hand-shapes, and listen by pressing a staff or an ear to the ground, through which they can hear a herd a day away, a swimmer under the next swell, tomorrow's wind arriving in the far turf.

And there are herds. The cattle — call them that; no one here has a better word — are hills with legs. Smooth-sided, green-dark, photosynthetic along their flanks, they stand in the emptiness drinking light and mowing the turf with wide under-slung mouths, and a herd in the distance reads as a dark coastline moving: ten thousand bodies along a swell, their dust visible from a day's walk off, their calls too low to hear and impossible to miss — a pressure in the sternum, a door closing in your chest. Behind them they lay down new ground: their dung is packed turf-seed, and it sprouts, and the road of a herd becomes in ten years a ridge of richer, deeper green. The animals are not just on this world. They are one of its ways of making itself.

At the sweats the emptiness ends. The margins of any pool are crowded to a degree the open plain never shows — wallowers shoulder to shoulder, mud-skitters in films across the warm shallows, insects in columns, small frantic life stacked to the waterline — and then thirty paces out it is emptiness again, and you remember that density here comes only in pockets, that the continent is mostly space with events in it.

Some of the events move under you. A swell will travel through the turf like wind through a wheatfield, except against the wind, and everyone standing on it stands still until it passes. Once in a while one surfaces — a blunt pale head the size of a cart, no eyes worth mentioning — looks at nothing, sinks, and the ground closes over it as if it had never been. And the boundary between plant and animal is soft here in a way that takes getting used to. A stalk turns out to have a neck. A boulder turns out to be sleeping. A grazing shape with the exact rhythm of a beast turns out to be tissue, just tissue, growing in the form of an animal because forms get borrowed here. You learn to wait a full minute before trusting any silhouette.

Every few years, somewhere, the mantle flushes — a fruiting, hundreds of leagues across, all at once, sweet for weeks — and the flush travels as a slow wave that takes years to circuit the plateau. Everything follows it or waits for it. Herds converge on the sweetened ground from every direction; the dew-walkers shift their routes; the swimmers rise more often where the ground is fruiting; and the herders' whole year is spent reading the flush's approach — in the color of the haze-pillars, in the taste of the dawn fog, in how the cattle stand. The whole continent is one long pursuit of a green light that goes around and around and never arrives anywhere for good.

The people are few, and their marks are light. You will walk for days and see none of them, and then find a camp pitched on a swell like a group of beached boats: sewn membranes of shed hide stretched on frames of rib and mouth-plate, set on woven pallets, because a camp left in one place too long begins to root — the pallets sprout, the guy-lines grow bark, the camp becomes a garden and then merely ground — so they move before the ground accepts them. They dig small pits for the black nodules the mantle secretes in its root-stone and cold-hammer them into blades and awls; they work shed plates the way other peoples work ivory; they spin cordage; there is no forge and no wall, and nothing here that a wall would hold. Boundaries are not fenced but walked: a route crossed often enough sinks and ridges in the turf until the mantle itself remembers it, standing as a low green wall that outlasts the people who made it. The oldest roads predate every band now walking them. Nobody knows who paced them into being. Cut the turf and it scars for years in a wrong-colored tissue, so no one cuts it; at the sweats there are little woven hoods over favored seeps, left open for whoever comes, and cairns of nodules on the sight-lines, stacked and never taken.

One more thing is known, and said quietly: deep in the middle distances there is a country where the drone stops. The ground there is not dead — grass grows, herds cross it — but it holds its breath. Travelers describe the silence as a weight on the ears, louder, somehow, than all the sound around it. The herders route around. Nothing about the place is dangerous, exactly. It is only that on a plateau that breathes, a held breath is the most alarming thing the world can do.

At night the heat comes up out of the ground in visible sheets, the fog builds on every swell, the drips begin, the organ drops a key, and somewhere out in the dark a herd lies along a ridge like a black coast with its own buried thunder. The road you are on rises gently over the next swell because, a thousand years ago, someone wanted it to. And far off toward the rim, in the fourth year of the cycle, a band of the mantle has begun to sweeten, and every large animal on the plateau has already, imperceptibly, begun to lean that way — the ground, the weather, the beasts, and the breath of the whole thing being, here, one process wearing several names.
