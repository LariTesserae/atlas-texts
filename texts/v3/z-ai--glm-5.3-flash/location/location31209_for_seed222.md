## The Weeping Shelf

The plateau is dry by every measure except the morning.

You reach it by a day of climbing — the last hour nothing but grade, breath coming short in the thin bright air, ears popping twice — and at the rim you look out over a country of pale stone and rounded tors that runs farther than the eye cares to follow, ringed by peaks higher still. It looks like a desert. By noon it behaves like one. But it is a desert that wakes wet.

The mechanism is simple to state and strange to live inside. The ground here is warm — warm from below, in a way that has nothing to do with the sun. Sit on stone at midnight and it is like sitting against a sleeping animal. All day the rock breathes heat into air that the thin, clean altitude keeps cold; the sky above the rim is ruthless and clear; and every night, warm ground under cold sky pulls water out of nothing. Dew forms as if poured. By the hour before sunrise the whole Shelf is dark with moisture, every stone sweating, the wind fog-fenced and beaded, and when the sun finally clears the eastern ranges the plateau steams — a low gold mist rising off a country that will be bone-dry by mid-afternoon. Nine dry hours, fifteen damp ones. The rain, when it comes at all, is an event you watch happening to someone else: in the late afternoons, cloud towers build over the far rim, and sometimes a gray curtain detaches and crosses a single valley while the rest of the Shelf stands in full glare. Distant thunder arrives like furniture being moved in another room. The animals read the curtains the way other places read weather. Rain on the north valleys — the flocks will turn by morning.

The stone itself is old and finished with itself. That is the first thing a geologist would notice and the last thing anyone here thinks about: the Shelf is not eroding anymore, it is *polishing*. The tors have been rounded like loaves. Talus slopes settled centuries ago into angles no pebble disputes. The wind no longer carves; it sands, flutes the exposed rocks into long smooth grains, wears every sharp edge down to the same patient tone. Nothing here is fresh. Nothing here is falling apart, either. The land reached its own shape a very long time ago and has been keeping it since, the way an old man keeps his posture.

Out of this polish, life rises in thin, deliberate strokes. Lichen covers whole fields like dropped maps — gray-green continents with black coastlines. Cushion plants hunker in the lee of stones, dense as fists, each one a fortress of a few dozen leaves. Moss exists for one hour a day: brown and dead-looking at noon, green and gleaming at dawn, dormant again by the time you've knelt to look. Grass grows in rings around the water sources and nowhere else. And beside each water source stands a tree — one species only, deep-rooted, small-leaved, waxy, casting a hard little shadow, feeding on the seep. One tree per well. One well per valley. The landscape is so sparse that each presence reads as a sentence in an otherwise blank page, and the blankness is not absence; it is *attended* blankness, emptiness with a held breath in it.

The breath is held because of the listener.

There is a predator on the Shelf that hunts by ear alone. Its eyes have gone small and its face has gone to membrane; it never vocalizes; its feet make no more sound than falling dust. Everything else on the plateau has tuned itself down to survive it. The flocks are silent — not quiet, *silent* — signaling in flashes of white underwing, in formation, in the angle of a head. The insects stridulate below the threshold of hearing. The wind never rises to a whistle here; it hisses and stops. When the listener kills, the only sound is the sound the prey was about to make. So the silence of the Shelf is not an absence of noise. It is a posture every living thing is holding, all day, every day — the loudest thing anyone says.

And yet the fauna is rich. That is the paradox that takes a visitor two days to understand: the animals are not here despite the dryness; they are here *because of* it, gathered around a resource that arrives on a schedule.

The wells.

The tenders call them weeps. There are perhaps a dozen on the Shelf, one to a valley, and each is a low structure of stone that isn't quite any local stone — grain too fine, warm to the touch, veined with lines that conduct something the tenders only call *the draw*. Before a weep opens, you feel it in your ears and sternum, a pressure like weather arriving underground. The veins darken, flushing. Then condensation blooms on every surface within ten paces — your tent fly, your hands, the flanks of waiting animals — and then, with a hiss below a whisper, water seeps from the crown of the structure and runs down into dishes of stone, where it stays cold all day. Cold water, on a warm-stone plateau: it feels like a trick, or a gift. The tenders do not distinguish.

Nobody built the weeps — or rather, whoever built them did not build them the way anything else on the Shelf has been built. The ruins are human. The weeps are something else. In dry years a sink has opened near one of them and revealed a descending shaft lined in the same fine-grained material, thousands of hair-thin channels running down into a dark where something moves that is not water. The tenders once lowered a lamp on a line. The flame bent toward the wall and went out. The line came up dry but warm, smelling of hot metal and, inexplicably, of rain. They did not lower a second lamp. The rule on the Shelf is old and simple: you do not dig, and you do not ask what the weeps are. The water comes up. That has been enough for long enough.

But the evidence of asking is everywhere, because people have lived here a long time and kept trying. Old sluice channels are cut into ordinary stone, aimed at where a weep used to seep; the seep now exits a hand's width to the left. Each generation someone re-cuts them, an inch behind a machine that drifts like a sleeper turning. A crack in one wellhead's vein sealed itself over the course of a season with a dark sap that hardened into more of the same impossible stone — the tenders watched it happen and said only, *it scars*. In a cliff above the third valley there is a sheet of not-glass that holds a picture of the Shelf as seen from directly overhead, always current, always exact, and the tenders use it without ceremony to find lost animals, the way you'd use any map. They are not troubled by what things *mean*. They are troubled by what things *want*. So far the weeps have only ever wanted to give.

The people themselves are few and scattered — the built layer of the Shelf is nearly invisible from any distance, because the plateau weathers its own history into anonymity. Dew-fences: long combs of stone set on edge, angled into the night wind, dripping by dawn. Low-walled shelters the same color as the hills. Cairns that might be markers or might be graves — the dead are buried above ground, stacked dry, and the wind polishes the cairns into tors within a lifetime. On one shelter wall there are eleven hundred tally-strokes recording well openings, grouped in knots, going back further than any living memory. The oldest tenders can read a little of it. What they can say for certain is that the pattern *returns* — a long cycle of some hundreds of days that matches no season and no calendar — and that when it returns, a light has come over the north rim at night: a slow lamp, brighter than any star, taking the whole night to cross the sky, soundless, on its round.

No one has decided what that means either. The wells keep their clock; the light keeps its; the two agree; and the agreement is simply noted on the wall in small dry strokes, generation after generation.
