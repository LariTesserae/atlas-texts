The valley went under in one long storm some years ago, and it has agreed to stay that way. The rain never stopped so much as decided to keep going; the river rose past its banks, then past its name, then past its reasons, until the hayfields were a shallow lake the color of old bottle glass. The fence posts still stand. They soften a little more each year. From the rim, on a still day, you can look down through that cool standing water and see the whole drowned valley lying on its back, patient, going green.

In the low corner of the valley floor, where the land dips toward the old channel, a slab of the hillside broke loose during that same storm and came to rest like a dropped lid. The flood's single act of violence, saved for the corner. Beneath the slab is a pocket of dark, and the dark is a room.

Someone kept a spring there once, before the water came. A low stone house half-set into the hillside, roofed by that same slab, always cool, always dim, with a small permanent pool inside. The flood did not enter it. The flood rose around it. From the room's point of view, nothing happened except that the sky was moved a long way up and replaced with stone.

You reach it now through a crack at the slab's upstream edge, a gap the width of a hand, and at the gap you meet the line. The water outside is young: it moves, it tastes of rain and field, it carries grit and pollen and the busy cold of the shallows. The water inside does not move, does not mix, has not mixed since before the valley drowned. There is a boundary between them, standing upright in the dark like a sheet of difference, and your hand knows it before your eyes ever could. Inside that line the water goes cool and thick and still, and everything — light, sound, time — is permitted to cross, but slowly. The boundary passes one drop per season and almost nothing else. It is the door. It behaves like one.

The room itself is three paces by four, tall enough to kneel in and nearly to stand. The dark in it is total and not empty; it has a nap to it, like felt, and you learn the room by hand. The coolness never varies. Nothing here has ever been warm — not the water, not the stone, not the growth — and whatever warmth a visitor brings is taken politely at the threshold and given to the roots.

The floor is a weave. Rhizomes knitted so long and so closely that they've become a mat with the spring of a small boat's deck, and it remembers things. The water in this room is the same water that pooled here when the door was a door; it never consented to leave, and it holds every pressure it has ever received. So under your palm the past pays out, slowed to room-time: a heel-fall that takes a full minute to land, then another; the ghost weight of a shelf in one corner, where the stems grow in a neat rectangular skirt; a latch's click arriving some winters apart as a single tick of compression, deep in the weave. The past here does not fade. It waits, and is occasionally spent. Of warmth, of light, of company, those footsteps are the nearest thing the room has kept.

The walls are where the lushness lives. Every surface is grown — layered, fat, velvety, a garden pressed on itself until the garden became a body. The leaves have given up being leaves; they're all vein, no blade, shapes made for touch instead of for sun. A hand can travel the walls for an hour and find only growth, then more growth, and then — rarely — the room's bare young scars: the glassy fracture faces where the slab broke, still sharp-arrised, still glittering faintly with the crystal pockets the break exposed. The roots decline those faces. The room keeps its newest edges naked. Its stone is young — the fracture pieces would still nearly fit back together, like a dropped plate — and the water has barely begun its centuries of rounding. The first mineral haze lies on the stone like breath on glass, no more.

Nothing rots here. Dead growth is grown into; fallen leaves are incorporated; a stem that dies becomes root becomes floor. The room loses nothing. It only becomes more single. And it was already crowded with elders when the flood came: the plants were old, fist-sized, patient things back when this was merely a spring-house pool, and the flood's seal — one wet season that never ended — was all the invitation they needed. By the roots' own count the room is only a few hundred drops old. Its garden is older than the grass the fence posts used to hold.

The marks inside are the quiet puzzle. Over the doorway-shape stands an arch of ribbed root and pebble, corbels or callus, impossible to say. A shelf runs along one wall, half stone ledge, half grown lip, the two indistinguishable at the seam. A groove in the stone could be a tool-mark or a root-mark; a stack of shells has fused into something like a small chandelier, and it rings — barely, once per lighting — a note more felt than heard. Around the drip point lies a circle of smooth pebbles, laid by current, by animal, or by devotion. Here the verb *to make* and the verb *to grow* have stopped being different verbs. It isn't that you cannot tell which is which. It's that the question itself has begun to dissolve.

The drip is the clock. In the roof's stone there is a hairline crack, and once a season the outside water presses its face to the crack and works a single drop through — the room's whole commerce with the world, one drop at a time. It falls the length of your forearm past a stalactite the water has spent its whole residence growing: a whisker of stone, knuckle-long, the longest thing the room has made. And the sound. The old water carries sound without echo; nothing bounces here; a noise enters and travels and finishes. The drop is heard once, but for a long time — a wave of hearing crossing the room at the speed of attention, root to root to root. The plants taste the news in it. Pollen, ash, leaf-juice, the season's whole biography in one cool bead. A polleny drop, months later, opens a single flower somewhere in the dark, and the flower smells of hay and warm stone and a dry world that is now on the far side of a ceiling. If the room has a name, this is it: one syllable per season. The silence afterward is the rest of the word.

There is one animal. In all the drowned valley it may be the only animal that lives below the waterline and stays. It is pale, blind, hand-length, and it keeps the center of the room clear by browsing the newest growth there — the room's editor, its slow pruner. Without it the garden would have knotted shut already. The clearing is its wage; the plants lean toward it all year and are not permitted to arrive. It makes one lap per drop, and when it moves, the whole room knows at once, pressure passed along the weave like a word down a long table. It does not hurry. Nothing here has ever needed to.

The light comes rarely, and old. In the cold months, when the outer water is clearest and the sun stands at its lowest slant, a beam finds the crack and enters — and inside, light goes slow. The crossing takes most of a day. The ceiling brightens from black to graphite and the crystal pockets appear one by one: the room's constellations, which it has always had, and was only waiting for a slower kind of day to show. Then the walls, in every gray-green the garden owns. The membrane at the doorway glows like the seam of a pearl. The shell-stack rings its one faint note. The animal rises and hangs in the middle of the room, pale as the light — the room's moon, if rooms keep moons. Nothing in it is impossible. Everything is simply permitted to take its time. The tail of the wave passes on through the walls and is kept, spent over years, handed root to root like a meal down a very long table. By the roots' count, the room has been lit eleven times. It remembers all of them.

And the room is filling in. Each year the weave adds a leaf's width; each year the animal's lap shortens by a stroke. Someday the center will close, and the drop, arriving, will find no water to fall into — it will hang at the crack, and the two waters will finally touch with nothing between them, and the room will be done. What is done, here, is called a seed. Under the valley floor there are other slabs, other pockets, other rooms growing themselves shut in the dark at a leaf's width per year. The flood was not an ending. It was a planting. And once a season, somewhere down in the cool, a drop falls, and is heard for a long time.
