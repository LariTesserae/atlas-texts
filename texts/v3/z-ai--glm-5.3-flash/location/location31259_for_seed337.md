The old land above the rim is finished. It has been finished for a long time — rounded hills, drifted soil, the patient gray-green of a world that has already decided what it is. You can walk it for days and nothing surprises you. Then the ground simply stops.

The pit is called Newground, and it is the newest thing in the world.

It is a bowl the size of a small country's worth of valley: a day's walk to circle the rim, two miles or so across the floor, a few hundred feet down. Not a cave, whatever it feels like from above — the sky is right there, open and ordinary, and at noon the whole basin fills with honest daylight. But you are below the surface of the earth, unmistakably. The horizon is gone. The sky has become a ceiling with edges, a rounded window of blue, and everything that happens here happens inside it.

What strikes you first, if you have come from above, is the rawness. Nothing here is rounded. Every edge is crisp as broken crockery. The rock — pale, fine-grained, cool to the palm, smelling faintly of minerals and cold — still carries the marks of its own making: long polish where one mass slipped against another, gouges, fresh scarps with their bedding showing like the rings of a felled tree. The talus slopes are unsorted, big blocks and small chips jumbled together, because weather has not yet learned to sort them. There is no soil. Not poor soil, not thin soil — none, except in the places where something living is manufacturing it by hand, slowly, the way you might make a fire.

The water here does not flow. It moistens.

The basin runs on condensation the way other places run on rivers. Moist air rolls over the rim from the plateau, meets the pit's cold stone, and the stone weeps — all night, quietly, every ledge and face of it, the walls raining inward from nowhere. Toward dawn the cold air pooling on the floor condenses its own lake: a flat white fog, knee-deep over the lowest ground, so still that a voice arriving through it comes from no direction at all. Midmorning burns it off. Some afternoons a cloud catches on the rim like a sleeve on a nail and gives the basin one brief, businesslike shower — the only real rain it gets. The water gathers in basins worn into the rock, in cisterns, in the joints of terraces, and stands there, cool and impatient, until it finds the Throat.

The Throat is the lowest point: a crack in the young floor, no wider than a fallen man, where all the standing water eventually goes. It takes the pools with a slow, unhurried gulp, like a sleeper turning over, and says nothing about where they end up. It is the only place in the basin where the world is practicing erosion, and it is very, very early at it.

On the south and east walls, where the sun's short day lingers, the terraces rise in steps — partly the ground's own fault-benches, partly improved — and on those steps sits the town. Newground is built the way the ground itself is made: by hand, in stone, without mortar. Dry-fitted walls, slabs for roofs, stairs everywhere — stairs between roofs, stairs through walls, stairs that turn out to be someone's kitchen because down here the difference between street and house is a matter of opinion. Many of the rooms are not built at all but dug, carved back into the soft young rock with antler picks, because fresh stone that has never weathered splits clean and cuts like dense bread. The whole town is therefore half excavation, half construction, a lacework of chamber and terrace and roof, and there is no room in it that is not used for something. Every ledge carries a drying rack or a stack of dressed stone. Every seep has its cistern, covered with a slab, with a lid-stone, with a smaller stone on the lid to hold it against the wind. Even the air is full: on still evenings the smoke of a thousand hearths cannot clear the rim, and it flattens out at one height into a visible lid, a gray ceiling above which the upper walls stand in clear cold air and below which the town lives under its own breath.
