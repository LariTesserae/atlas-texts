# The Kept Garden

Above this place the world is thin: a hand-span of frozen ground, a crust of grey lichen, one birch so stunted by cold it grows more sideways than up. The wind up there has nothing to push against, so it pushes anyway. Between two frost-cracked stones there is a gap that exhales — barely — a breath of air one or two degrees less cruel than the air around it. That breath is the only sign. Underneath, an arm's length down, the whole other world begins.

You would have to kneel to enter, and even kneeling you would enter apologetically, shoulder by shoulder, into a room the size of a small tent. Three paces across, maybe four. The ceiling presses low enough that your breath fogs against it, and the fog condenses on the hanging roots — because the ceiling is alive. Roots of the birch above have worked down through the frozen soil and dangle into the chamber like the strings of some instrument nobody plays. On windy nights the surface flexes and the frozen ground creaks faintly, a deep percussion from a distant country, and this is the closest thing the garden has to weather reports, to animals, to news. It receives its storms secondhand, as percussion.

The dark here is nearly total. What light exists comes from below and from the center, and after twenty minutes of standing still your eyes accept the terms of the negotiation: you begin to see the room in grays and greens, silhouettes organized around one steady point — a low, greenish glow, the exact color of young leaves, burning without flicker at knee height near the center of the floor.

That light is not for you. It is one frequency, impossibly pure, a sun narrowed to a single note, and it is tuned to the garden the way a key is tuned to a lock. The plants have organized themselves around it over centuries — not leaning toward it so much as arranged by it, like a congregation that has had a very long time to find its seats.

The garden itself is a matter of layers. The floor is peat, soft and deep, and it is made of the garden: live moss on dead moss on older moss on black peat on mineral, each stratum undisturbed because nothing disturbs it. There are no worms here. No mites, no beetles, no slugs — no animals at all, not ever. So the soil does not churn, the dead do not vanish, pollen falls in the still air exactly straight down, and nothing has ever carried this garden anywhere or eaten any part of it. The lushness is a lushness of accumulation: cushion mosses in three greens packed shoulder to shoulder, liverwort spread in scales like coins, two pale ferns whose fronds unroll perhaps one per decade, each one a season's entire budget. Thread-fungi glow faintly in the old wood they are digesting — cold blue sparks, distant streetlamps of decomposition. Once a year, near the glowing point, a small flower with two petals opens in the absolute stillness, pollinates itself, and drops a single seed into the moss. Most years the seed waits and does nothing. Once a decade, one sprouts, and the garden expands by the width of a seedling. That is its entire ambition, and it is enough.

The water arrives the way everything arrives here: slowly, in installments. The ceiling sweats. Beads gather along the hanging roots and on the cold mineral skin of the walls, swell, hang for what seems like hours, and fall. One bead every few minutes, sometimes less. This is the chamber's rain — and each drop spent a month seeping down through frozen soil above, filtered, arriving clean and tasting faintly of moss and metal. Some months no rain falls at all; the cushions dull to a dusty grey-brown and stop, and they are not dead, only paused — mosses know how to stop the way other things know how to breathe. Then a thaw comes to the surface world, and the drips resume, and within hours the whole room greens again. A shallow basin sits in the peat, fed by a narrow channel of fused, glassy earth — dark, straight, unchanged after centuries because nothing exists here to bend it. The basin holds water mirror-flat, and in it reflects the green glow: this is the garden's one sky, its single horizon, a saucer of light.

At the edges, the garden meets its border and the border is winter. The walls sheer into frozen ground furred with hoarfrost feathers and needles of ice, and where the living meets the frozen there is a line — a frontier, breath by breath, millimeter by millimeter, advancing in the deep cold and retreating in the thaw, netting out to exactly zero over the years. At that line the moss cushions thicken into dense padded walls, like fortification built by no one, and beyond it the ice stands in its own settled ornament, patient and ornamental as frost on a window. The room and the frost have long since agreed on terms. Nothing here erodes; everything accretes, or waits.

And near the center, at the seat of the light: the node. Knee-high, smooth, dark, a shape with no seam wider than a hair and no corrosion anywhere, dusted everywhere with mineral bloom except for one clean band where its own faint warmth keeps itself polished. It hums below the threshold of hearing — you feel it not as sound but as a quality of the silence, a silence that is maintained rather than empty. It does several impossible things quietly. It keeps this pocket of ground unfrozen, a held breath in solid permafrost, and has for longer than the tag beside it can say. It draws moisture out of ground that by all rights should keep its water locked in crystal — the walls sweat because it asks them to. Nothing grows on it; nothing corrodes it; the garden, which crowds and layers and touches itself everywhere, leaves a hand's-breadth of bare floor around it like respect, or like an old instruction.

It also gardens. Where a stem reaches too near, the stem slows, reddens faintly at its tip, and stops — no tearing, no loss, nothing wasted, just an end agreed upon at the speed of ice. With no animals to graze it, the garden's only gardener is this machine, doing the pruning that beetles and worms would do elsewhere, and doing it at the pace of decades.

Near the node, half-sunk in peat, a small metal tag: letters stamped once, now worn to a shadow of themselves. *PROVISIONAL GARDEN. NODE 41. DO NOT —* and after that, nothing legible. Do not water. Do not disturb. Do not switch off. In the wall, a ring of docking studs, each one crowned now with a neat cushion of moss — instruments once sat there; the garden has upholstered them. Whatever project this was, whatever network of nodes and reserves and keepers it belonged to, has not been heard from in a very long time. Only this one kept going. Or the others did too, elsewhere, in other small rooms under other thin worlds; nothing down here knows.

The sounds, when they come, are the room's entire calendar. A bead falls: a glassy *tick*, then a soft *tup* into moss, and then minutes of nothing, and you find yourself waiting for the next one the way you would wait for a clock. In the deepest cold, the ice needles at the frontier tick as they grow. A tuber swells somewhere under the peat with a creak like a knuckle, once a night at most. The growth itself makes no sound at all — a fern unrolling is silent, and since nothing else here is making noise either, the silence of a frond unrolling becomes, somehow, perceptible. An event in the quiet.

Once a year, on the coldest night, the glow divides into three thin beams and performs some brief self-examination, an aurora one color wide, and then returns to its single note. The garden receives a little extra sun that night. By morning nothing has changed except the reflection in the basin, and the reflection does not comment.

If the node ever stopped, the frontier would walk inward — a finger's width a year, then faster, closing the room over a century, pressing the garden flat into the freezing ground and making of it, eventually, a thin green page in brown stone: a fossil garden, pressed like a flower in the world's largest book. But the node does not stop. It has not stopped. The drips continue their long punctuation, the moss holds its three greens, the flower manages its decade-scale hope, and the room — three paces by four, a whole world you could cross in four steps and explore for a lifetime — holds itself in balance, in the dark, in the cold, under the floor of the wind.

It has been kept a very long time. As far as anything down there is concerned, it will be kept a very long time more.
