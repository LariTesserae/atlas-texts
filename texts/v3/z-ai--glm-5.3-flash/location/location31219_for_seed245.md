# The Greenware Continent

You arrive on a floor. That is the first wrongness, and it takes a while to name: the ground beneath you is not ground. It is level with a deliberateness no land ever manages — poured, screeded, finished. Underfoot there is a texture like the skin of an old pot, repeating every few paces: parallel ridges, the ghost of a cord wound around a roller. Except the roller must have been the width of a storm front. The whole plain is cord-marked, out to where the steam takes over, and the cord-marks are unbroken. No stone interrupts them. No seed has ever split them.

The clay is warm through your boots. Not sun-warm — there is no sun you can point to — but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, from underneath, faintly breathing. All day the temperature never changes. It is the temperature of a hand.

The light is dim and homeless. It comes up out of the floor and down out of the sky at the same time, amber-gray, sourceless, and it does not make shadows so much as smudges. Your shadow lies faintly on the vapor around your knees; another wavers above your head, cast upward onto the cloud-ceiling by whatever is glowing down in the vents. You are lit the way the inside of an oven is lit, if ovens were the size of provinces and still deciding whether to be lit at all.

And it is quiet. That is the second wrongness, after the floor. A place this hot, this wet, this in motion should roar. Instead the wet air eats sound before it travels. Your footsteps sound close and small, like noises made inside a wrapped blanket. Drips arrive at long, dignified intervals — a bead forming on the lip of a wall, swelling, letting go, the splash arriving a beat later than the eye says it should. Once every few minutes a vent tower somewhere out in the haze exhales: not a roar, a long tidal breath, three minutes in, three minutes out. Once an hour, or once a day, something enormous eases into place below the world, and you feel it in your sternum rather than hear it — a settling, a slab of continent finding its seat.

---

Everything here is made. Not everything is finished.

Walk a day in almost any direction and you will find, standing alone in the emptiness, something enormous. A wall running dead straight to the horizon and then stopping, mid-course, its topmost bricks still glossy with mortar that has been squeezing out between the joints, slowly, for who knows how long. A vent tower of crude hand-laid brick, slender as a temple spire and half a mountain tall, its plume rising into the ceiling and becoming local weather. A half-built arch — just the arch, nothing else around it for miles, the packed-silt support frame still tucked under its spine, though the arch has begun to lean. It will never fall. Nothing here falls. It will simply continue to lean, and lean, until leaning was what it was for.

The bricks are the size of ships. The fingerprints are the size of river deltas. In the faces of the soft clay bluffs — bluffs, because even the hills are mounds of poured slip, heaped and troweled — you can see five parallel grooves dragged the whole height of a valley wall: a thumbprint at the scale of geography. Whoever pressed it did it casually, the way you'd test dough.

The tooling is primitive and it is everywhere, once you learn to see it. Levers of glazed ceramic cable-lashed to stone fulcrums. Rollers — cylinders of fired clay, polished smooth by use, abandoned half-buried like beached whales. Counterweights made of big-bellied pots filled with warm water, rigged over crude pulleys. Long silt ramps ramping up to nothing, waiting for the next course. And the straightest things in the whole world are the leveling trenches: kilometers-long channels filled with standing water, dead flat, dead still, mirror after mirror laid across the continent, because these builders had no instrument for true horizontal except water itself, and they trusted it completely. The trenches don't shimmer with life. Nothing lives in them. They are just the level.

There is no wood here. There is nothing that was ever alive, so nothing they build with bends; everything either pours, or doesn't. Rope is twisted cable of fired clay, stiff and gleaming. Scaffolding is packed silt, dug away when the work above it hardens. Even the mortar is just the world's own body, thinned with water and smeared back into itself.

That is the other thing about the surfaces: nothing is weathered. A wall abandoned a thousand years ago looks smeared yesterday, because — in every sense that matters here — it was. Nothing erodes. Things get *revised*.

---

The water is everywhere and it is all warm. The world sweats. Every wall weeps, condensation crawling down in beads to find the grooves the builders pressed into the brick for exactly this purpose, channels funneling the runoff into cisterns, into the trenches, into the great sheeted pools that lie between the structures like spilled mirrors. Sometimes it rains — a warm, fat, unhurried rain — and sometimes, in the convective moods, the rain changes its mind halfway down and drifts back up, droplets levitating out of the haze like a reversed memory.

The pools grow a skin. An iridescent film, faintly slick, that seals overnight and wrinkles when you touch it. It is not life, or it is life at degree zero, or it is the world's own slip learning to hold a shape — no one has decided, least of all the film. And on the drier brick, where evaporation pulls minerals up from below, a white bloom whiskers out: salt-flowers, brittle, crisp, tasting of stone. They grow in the quiet, they dry, they flake off, they seed the next patch. This is the entire ecology of the continent: a bloom that isn't alive, a skin that might be, and weather. It is enough. It has the rhythm of an ecosystem without a single organism in it.

Where the water is mineral-heavy, it stains. Green glaze-weep streaks down the flanks of old cisterns, malachite-bright, the only strong color in a world of wet gray, dung-brown, and the amber of the light.

---

Now the rules, because this place has rules, and they are not quite ours.

**Wet things do not fall. They settle.** Gravity here is patient with anything still soft. A slumping wall becomes a ramp; a leaning arch becomes a different arch; a failed dome becomes a hill with a story. Nothing collapses. Everything re-decides. Only fired things can fall — and the fired things are few, and they are the true anchors of the world.

**The ground refuses history.** Walk anywhere and your footprints smooth over behind you — not fast, not with drama, just a slow obliteration, the cord-marked surface flowing shut like the surface of the pool it resembles. You cannot retrace your steps. You cannot leave a message in the soft places; the world will not hold a line it didn't draw. Navigation is only possible between the fixed things — the towers, the arches, the fired patchwork — and so those are where the paths conceptually run, even though no paths exist.

**Firing makes real.** There are fronts here the way we have cold fronts: a firing front, announced by a dim brightening low in the vapor and a long gathering hiss, like applause behind a wall. It rolls over the land at walking speed, sometimes for a day, breathing heat. Where it passes, everything changes state. Wet gray goes terracotta. The cord-marks harden into permanence. Cracks split the ground along glaze-glass seams that ring when struck — the dried clay *rings*, a bell-note underfoot, and this is the eerie part: the fired land makes sound where the wet land only absorbs. Hardness sings. Softness listens. The frontier between them wanders, and that wandering is the turbulence of this world: not storms of destruction, but storms of *commitment*.

**The world is still deciding how big to be.** You measure a vent tower against the horizon at dawn, a day's walk off, and you reach it the next afternoon and it is taller than it was — not by illusion. Doors you could have brushed with your knuckles last season now stand three shoulder-heights high. Distances near the great structures feel shorter than they should, as if the towers are growing toward you between sightings. The continent is being built to scale, and the scale is still being set, and everything in it is drifting, slowly, toward vast.

**Echoes come back re-shaped.** Shout at a fresh wall and your word returns a half-beat late and slightly wrong — duller, rounder, like a word pressed into clay and stamped back out. The world is learning acoustics. It hasn't finished.

---

The light, you eventually work out, is a construction problem too.

Look down any deep fissure — the firing cracks — and you see it: a red under-sky. The glow of work, kilometers down. The heat that keeps the pools blood-warm, that sweats the walls, that drives the whole atmospheric engine, comes from the firing below. The vent towers are not decoration; they are the flues. Someone placed them — each one alone in its day's-walk of emptiness, each one feeding a convection cell — and above it all the steam gathers into a ceiling, a half-laid roof of vapor, glowing amber where the under-light soaks through it. That ceiling is the sky, and the sky is *unfinished*. The dimness of the world is the dimness of a room with its roof half-built. On days when the towers exhale in chorus, you can watch the ceiling thicken, course by course, like masonry made of breath.

Storms here are slow and silent: walls of vapor walking across a plain over the course of a day, dropping their warm rain, passing. The loudest thing you will ever hear is a firing front arriving — and even that is muffled, vast, patient, a hiss rather than a roar. Everything powerful here moves the way clay moves. It has nowhere urgent to be. It is being *made*, and making, at this scale, is a quiet business.
