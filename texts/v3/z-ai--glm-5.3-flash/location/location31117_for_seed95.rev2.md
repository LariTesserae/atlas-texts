The mountains here are finished.

That is the first thing to understand, and the plateau says it in every direction you look. What was once a range — peaks, cirques, snow-cut ravines, rivers shouting in every fold — has worn itself down to a single wide floor of stone, pink and gray, gently swelled, running to the horizon with almost no interruption. The erosion didn't stall; it completed. The rock has said everything it had to say. What remains are the last resistant inches of ten thousand summits, planed down to a nearly level table, and here and there on the horizon the hips and shoulders of the old range still poke through: whale-backed domes, the only vertical things left, standing like retired monuments to arguments nobody remembers.

You learn the altitude only at the edges. Out on the plain the world is just ground and sky, and the flatness is so complete that height becomes an abstraction — until you walk to the rim, where the table stops without warning, and you look down long dry ramps through a mile of warming air. At dawn a sea of fog fills the basins below, and the plain floats above it like a lid on the world. Then the fog climbs the rim — slowly, deliberately, the way water would if it were patient — and spends the morning being harvested.

Because the water here comes down rather than up. The air is warm at this height; this is a mild, thick-aired world that holds its heat the way the stone does, and the nights never truly chill. So the moisture arrives as warm dew, condensing out of the dark sky onto every surface, and as dawn fog, which the plateau combs with great efficiency. The stone is freckled with basins — thousands of them, worn by uncountable years of standing water: potholes the size of a hat, potholes the size of a bath. Each holds a world. An eyelash of a shrimp spiraling in a cup of water; a felt of green algae; a frog the size of a thumbnail that lives its whole life in one bowl and has never seen the plain that holds ten thousand like it. Some basins are linked in chains, and when one fills it spills to the next. Many of the spillways are natural. Some are not — more on those shortly.

The vegetation is a matter of patience rather than coverage. Lichen maps the bare rock in rings of black, gold, and gray-green that can be read like tree rings; some of the circles are older than the idea of a forest. Ferns the height of a finger live in the joints of the stone. Tall rosettes of spined plants — fog-combs — stand in loose colonies, their needles wringing moisture from the moving air; all morning they drip, and at their feet the potholes brim. And once every view or so, a tree: grotesquely ancient, a species that grows perhaps a millimeter a year, its canopy spread like an open hand, its bark polished to the grain by ten thousand years of leaning backs. You do not see young trees. You see the idea of a tree, arrived at its final size sometime before your civilization's word for tree.

And then there is the fauna, which is the plain's great secret and its great abundance. Nothing about this spare, high, finished-looking place prepares you for how crowded it is.

The large animals are grazers, tortoise-like, in numbers that defeat counting — herds so extensive that from a rim you can watch a single herd occupy a horizon. Their shells have been polished by dust and rain into smooth domes, and each dome holds a copy of the sky; when thousands of them lie still among the boulders at midday, the plain looks paved, a road that breathes. They graze the lichen mats and stand under the fog-combs at dawn, drinking drips off spines. They move perhaps a stone's throw a day. A herd's full circuit of the plateau can take eleven years.

Above them, the sky is gridded. Broad-winged gliders ride the thermals peeling off the warm rock, and they never flap — there is no need — so the air is full of wings and utterly without wingbeats. In the soil pockets between boulders, burrow-towns tunnel; at dawn the low air fills with drifting silk lines and seed-fluff; the potholes seethe.

And yet the plain is nearly silent. This is not emptiness; it is policy. On a floor this flat, a cry travels until it meets nothing. Everything that was ever loud here has been found out, over and over, across ten million years, until what remains is what learned quiet — or learned to speak where sound cannot go. The herds converse in knocks and stamps, down through the bedrock, and if you stand among them you feel it before you understand it: a sentence beginning in the soles of your feet, and a mile away, a change of posture traveling through four thousand animals like iron filings turning under a magnet. No visible signal. The decision arrives through the ground. When a herd turns, it turns together, and it is the closest thing this place has to a voice.

The scattered structures belong to the stackers — flightless, crow-sized birds that work the fog-combs and the basins. Their whole technology is stacking, and they are masters of it. Across the plain, in loose fields, stand their cairns: knee-high mostly, some waist-high, built one stone at a time by one beak at a time, maintained by lineages rather than individuals. Low walls run from beneath the comb-plants to the shared basins, guiding dew-drip in thin bright threads; the oldest walls are blackened with the same thousand-year lichen as the boulders, so that architecture and geology wear the same alphabet and can only be told apart by the seams — the way each stone sits fitted and chosen, carried, placed, kept. No one living remembers why a given wall bends where it bends. The bend is kept anyway.

And there is this, which the plain does not explain: some walls end at potholes that have been dry for three hundred years. The stackers built toward them anyway, generation after generation, and in the high country — this is recent, as the plateau measures things — water has begun returning to exactly those basins. No one says the stackers know anything. But the plain keeps its appointments.

So does the year. Once a year, a rain: a warm drizzle that comes in low for a day and a night, and everything on the plateau drinks with its back. The pothole chains fill and link, basin to basin to basin, and for six days the top of the world has a river — a slow, warm, braided ribbon running from one rim to the other, and the herds converge on it with their unhurried enormity, and the gliders come down in their thousands, and the whole dense layered life of the place — ground, joints, bowls, air — is briefly in contact with itself. Then the rain ends, the chains close, and the plain goes back to its longer sentences.

If you lie on the stone at dusk you will feel the day's heat leaving it by inches, hear the tick of cooling rock, the small metal ring of drip, the friction of shell against shell where the herd beds down around you like a cobbled beach. The stone stays kind all night; you sleep warm at an altitude that should punish you, and wake to find that every surface has wept, that the comb-plants are already working, that the fog has made its climb.

Nothing here is ending. That is the final strangeness, and it takes a while to accept. Everything in the visitor's experience trains them to read high, barren, old places as losses — as what remains after something. But the plain is not a remainder. It is a completion. It ended its becoming a vast afternoon before any counting, and what lives afterward lives here: in overwhelming numbers, in extraordinary quiet, in the warm, at the pace of stone. The light at noon is ordinary and honest and shows you exactly what is there, which is the plain's whole trick. Come at any hour and you will find it awake and crowded and calm.

It simply isn't in a hurry, and it has finished explaining itself.
