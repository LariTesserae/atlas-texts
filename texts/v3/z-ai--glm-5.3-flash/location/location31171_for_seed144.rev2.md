The first thing to understand is that weather happens below.

The range stands higher than weather. Rain, when it happens, happens down there, on the gray-green low countries you can barely imagine from up here. What this mountain gets instead is the sun, the stone, and twice a day, the sea. The cloud floor stretches from ridge to horizon in every direction — a slow white ocean with the afternoon light moving on it — and the peaks stand up out of it like dark islands, dozens of them, worn low and round-shouldered. This is an old range. Its arguments with frost and water ended ages ago; the summits became what they were going to be and have held the shape since. Windward faces are fluted and honeycombed where fog has gnawed patiently for millennia. Leeward faces are black with varnish, a patina of dust and manganese laid down grain by grain. Nothing here is eroding, exactly. The stone is in balance — losing a grain, growing a skin, keeping its ledger.

The air is thin, and the sky has architecture: clean ordinary blue at the horizon deepening to something near violet at the zenith, as though noon were quietly turning toward evening without asking anyone. The light itself is normal — clear days, honest nights, nothing strange about it. It is the altitude that makes light a currency. In the dry hours the sun is a weight you can feel through a hat. Bare stone will take the skin off a careless hand; the steel handrails on the harvest frames are wrapped in rag at every grip, because metal up here remembers the sun for hours after dark. And the nights are cold — a hard, squeaking cold, frost on the mesh, breath cracking in the throat. The mountain swings forty degrees between the tides and nobody remarks on it. There are no mild hours. There is the drink, and there is the bake.

The tide.

Twice a day — and there are almanacs for it, real tables, corrected by moon — the cloud sea swells. Not a storm, not a wind: a tide. Fog climbs the slopes the way seawater climbs sand, in long slow tongues, wrapping the shoulders, flooding the saddles, lapping over the summits until the archipelago shrinks to its tallest islands. On still nights a third tide comes, and no one has fully explained it. The moon pulls water; water is water, whether it lies in an ocean or hangs in a fog; the almanac notes the anomaly in fine print and the keepers have stopped arguing with the tables. The fog comes up over old wave-cut ledges — ledges the geologists say were cut by a cloud sea that stood even higher, once — and the water never falls here. It rises. Rain arrives from below like an answer to a question the stone has been asking all morning. It never strikes anything. It comes as a gray hush smelling of iron and wet dust, and every surface built to catch it begins to catch it.

The catch is the industry, and the industry is the lace.

On every broad ridge and bench stand the frames: harps of cable and mesh, some taller than trees, ranged ridgeline after ridgeline until counting them stops meaning anything. The fields are not dense the way a forest is dense. They are dense the way fabric is dense — thread by thread across miles, a lace laid over the whole island chain. In clear air you see only frames and a shimmer. When the tide comes, each field goes gray, then dark, then beaded: droplets swarming every filament, swelling, merging, running down, gathering in the gutters in ropes of water. The sound of a field drinking is not a roar. It is a crowd of very small sounds — tick, tick, trickle, a hiss like sand poured on sand — thousands of them from every thread at once, and it is the loudest thing on the mountain, and it would not wake a sleeping child.

Strangers always ask if the droplets are placed by hand. They are not. But they hang in strings so evenly spaced along each filament that on certain mornings the whole field looks written — line after line of identical syllables, a script in water, legible from nowhere, addressed to no one.

The mountain lives by the same clock as the machinery. Everything here either drinks or waits, and nearly everything does both. When the tide's edge comes hissing up a slope, the slope detonates — quietly; everything here detonates quietly — into life. Dew-beetles pour from the stone by the hundreds, matte-black and ridge-backed, tilting into headstands so fog beads on their shells and runs to their mouths. Lace-spiders, who were harvesting the sky long before anyone strung a frame, unpin their communal silk sheets and let them billow; the silk drinks like the mesh does, and the spiders drink from the silk. Hour-frogs unstop the seams they have waxed themselves into and sing — a chorus like pebbles conversing, small and urgent, over in an hour. In the overflow basins below the gutters, eggs that have waited through dozens of bakes hatch on contact, and fairy shrimp thrash in water eight millimeters deep, living their entire lives against evaporation. Midges lift off the moss in columns. Tide-swifts come riding the fog-front like gulls along a surf-line, working the edge where lifted insects strand by the thousand, and the falcons come behind the swifts and strike in perfect silence, because up here sound does not carry — you can watch a kill at two hundred paces and hear nothing at all. And once in a while, on the widest benches, something tawny and low moves between the frames at the edge of vision, and every moss-picker on the slope stops and faces the same direction for a while.

Where the overflow spills, the ground packs tight. Every crack the tide touches becomes a garden with no room left in it — moss over moss, fernlets the size of fingernails, snails no bigger than lentils grazing the film, lichen maps stitching the wet ribbon together from saddle to saddle. Tall gray grazers built something like a camel, the color of old rope, follow the ribbons up, licking green off the mesh with tongues like rasps, tolerated below the boundary cairns and pelted with gravel above them. The keepers' arithmetic is simple: a moss-picker eats a certain amount of garden and pays it back in paths — the trails they beat into the stone are the only easy walking across the benchlands. But that is the whole garden, and it is none of it wider than a stride. The rest of the mountain is stone. From the air, in the bake, the range looks almost dead. It is not dead. It is waiting with its mouth closed.

The recede is as punctual as the drink. The fog drains downslope like a tide over sand, and the closing happens in strict order, each species on its own station of the same alarm: beetles first, grubbing into cool talus; then the swifts lifting away; the grazers drifting to the boundary stones; the silk pinned shut; the midges gone; the frogs last, sealing their seams with wax and one final pebble-note. The dew-pools shrink from the rim inward, leaving rings of dried eggs and rings of salt — each pool ending as the ghost of a tide. Stall by stall, the market shuts. Within an hour of withdrawal the benches are bare again, and the sun takes the till.

The bake is the long part of the day, and the bake is when you finally hear the mountain, because the mountain is not silent — it only seemed so next to the drinking. Stone ticks as it expands, little pistol-shots down the ridgelines. The shimmer has a hiss if you insist on hearing one. The machinery hums in a register you feel in your molars — the cistern-chains glugging as their levels drop, the penstocks where harvested water falls through the notches to turbines below, the rectifier sheds idling. A pump somewhere runs on the very hydrogen it helps to make, ticking like a slow clock. And over all of it, so constant the residents no longer hear it, the power lines down the mountain carry morning's work toward the lowlands in a whisper.

What the water is for is fire. The world below is dry and getting drier — that is some other landscape's complaint — and the Shore sells it light. Gravity carries the harvest down; the fall turns the turbines; the current splits more water in the sheds; the pale hydrogen runs on in long catenary pipes, draped peak to peak like laundry lines. Nothing up here is spent. Water is only moved. It sits in cisterns large enough to have weather inside them, behind lids with numbered seals, and the understanding beneath the whole economy is that the sea will keep coming up the stone, twice, punctually, and that everyone's account will balance.

Except the sea is rising. Half a stride a year, the surveyors say, though they argue about why — and the industry moves upslope behind it like a village following a shoreline. Above the live fields stand the derelicts: older frames, mesh gone slack and gray, gutters bright with rust where the fog no longer reaches, tally-marks still scratched inside the frame legs in a dead contractor's shorthand. The keepers do not tear them down. In this light, at this height, they read less as ruins than as a tideline — an old mark left by an old sea, waiting to be touched again.

By night the cold comes down hard and the cloud floor goes dark, moonlit or black depending on the moon's cooperation, and the archipelago stands in it like the masts of a fleet at anchor. Frost silvers the mesh between tides. One lamp burns in the waystation window — the only lamp — and a line of laundry frozen stiff on the wire. The pipes tick as they cool. Down the mountain, at the edge of hearing, a pump keeps its slow time. The stars here are not decorated; they are simply, enormously, present.

And then, in the dark, before anything else, a sound: a hiss far below, growing, and the first gutters beginning to tick, ridge by ridge, top to bottom, as the sea climbs the old ledges again and the beetles unstop their holes. The frogs answer from their seams. The fog rises through the lace, and every thread begins, very softly, to count.

Here, rain rises. The quiet is not emptiness; it is storage. Everything the mountain will spend tomorrow is held in the cloud floor, in the waxed seams, in the rings of waiting eggs — and twice a day, at its pleasure, the sea climbs the stone and pays it all out at once. Briefly, densely, gently. Then it takes the silence back as change.
