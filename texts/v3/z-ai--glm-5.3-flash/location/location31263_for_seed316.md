The ground weeps.

Not as metaphor. Stand anywhere here — pick a direction, walk a day, stand — and the water stands with you, a hand's width down, pressing up through the paste with the patience of something holding all the time that is left. Dig with your fingers and you reach it before your elbows. Keep digging and the hole fills as fast as you empty it, and somewhere in that futility is the first lesson of the place: you have not dug a hole, you have opened a window, and the window looks into the sea, and the sea looks back as rain.

Because the plain has no rivers. This takes the longest to accept. Somewhere in its deep history the land lost its last slope — water moves for a reason, and downhill is the reason, and the plain has retired every reason. What remains is a surface so old, so endlessly argued over by rain and time, that nothing on it can fall farther than an inch. So the water stopped traveling and started *turning*. It rises out of the paste as mist, climbs into the low ceiling of cloud, condenses, and comes back down — blood-warm, fine as breath, nearly constant — on ground that was already wet. The whole hydrology of a world runs vertically here, at every point at once. There is no coast, either; there is instead a rumor of a coast, hundreds of miles wide, where fresh water thickens by degrees to brackish to salt with no line anywhere, until the tide gives up pushing against a land that barely rises and dissolves into the air to fall again somewhere inland. The sea and the land merged into a single condition. You could call the whole place a drowned continent, except that nothing drowned. Everything *settled*.

The rock itself has become a paste — gray rock-flour ground finer than sand, wetter than clay, blood-warm from the old heat still leaking out of the deep. It takes your footprint cleanly and then weeps water into the print until the print blurs and closes, in minutes. The plain erases you as you go. Nothing on the surface is older than a season except the surface itself, and the surface, by every measure that matters, is older than anything: this is what the end of erosion looks like. A world after its argument. Every shape that could be sharp has been negotiated smooth. The few hard remnants — low whale-backs of denser stone, a hand's height or a shoulder's, worn to the sheen of old bone — are the last words of mountains, and even they weep, beaded all over with condensation, cooler than the saturated air, sweating out the sky they are still negotiating with.

There is no horizon in the usual sense. The cloud ceiling sits low and permanent, lighter in one quadrant — that is the only sun this world admits, a brightening, not a disc, dim enough to stare at noon. Beyond a mile everything softens into the same wet gray. And because the flat offers no feature, no landmark, no gradient, distance itself stops working: a knoll an hour's walk away is indistinguishable from a knoll ten days' walk away, and neither resembles anything but the other. Direction dies of uselessness. Walk toward the bright quadrant for forty days and you will pass nothing that could prove you moved.

So you navigate by ear. You have no choice; the plain is deafening.

The roar is not one sound. It has a floor — the vast hiss of a world seeping, water forcing itself through a million pores per square mile, sizzling faintly where it meets the deep's residual warmth — and on that floor stands the rain, drumming on paste, on pools, on your shoulders, a percussion with no downbeat and no end. Beneath both runs the sub-bass: the groan of the plate itself, settling into its final flatness a hair at a time, felt in the sternum, in the molars, in the fluid of the eyes. The roar has weather. It swells and ebbs in slow tides; it fronts; it squalls. People who cross the plain — the few who still do — read those swells the way sailors once read ocean swell, steering by a change in the noise, holding a course by the rhythm of a chaos. You stop trying to speak on the second day. Speech is a luxury of still air, and no air is still here. You learn hands. You learn to hold a whole conversation in gestures while the world shouts over you, and you learn that inside this much sound there is a strange total solitude: the roar fills every volume of air completely. Your own skull is the last quiet room for miles.
