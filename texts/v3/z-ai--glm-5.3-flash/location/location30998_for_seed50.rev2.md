# The Cradle Sea

There is no dirt on this mountain. That is the first thing you understand, somewhere in the third day of climbing, when you notice you have left no trail. The rock has never been broken down into soil. It has barely finished being rock. Every edge of the summit is as crisp as the day the mountain closed, and your own footprints heal behind you on the pale stone — the mountain seals its skin the way water closes behind a swimmer. Nothing here has been worn. Nothing here has had time.

The cloud countries pass below you. The air is thin and cool and so empty of dust that distances lie; you can see the curvature of the world's rim without anyone to certify it. And at the top, set into the summit like a coin dropped into wax, there is a hollow of blue.

From the shoulder above, the sea is a coin. Forty paces across. You could throw a stone over it — people have; the sea kept the stone. Walk the last stretch and the scale inverts in your body before your eyes admit it: a smell of salt and iodine and cold kelp-adjacent brine, the smell of an ocean coast, rising from water you could spit across. The sea the way a kitten is a tiger — completely, ahead of the evidence.

The hollow was not carved. Look at the walls: no scour, no wave-cut notch, no talus. The fracture surfaces are fresh, conchoidal, gleaming like the inside of a broken flint. The hollow is a casting void — a space the mountain left in itself, the way a mold leaves its shape in metal. The sea did not make this room. The sea was poured into it, or perhaps secreted into it, and has been growing toward its walls ever since.

At the lip, the water ends in a curve. A horizon, at three paces. Your eyes insist it is a thousand miles wide; your legs know it is forty. Both facts are true at once, and the seam between them is where the strangeness of this place lives.

The water itself is a blue with no name yet — even the colors here are provisional. It is so clear it is a kind of deafness of sight: you can see too far, the way you can hear too much. The cold is the cold of water that has never been anywhere, never run, never warmed, never sat in anything. Lick your fingers after wading and it is brine. An hour later it is snowmelt, almost sweet. The sea has not decided what it will be made of. Its chemistry is still arriving.

And the waves happen with no wind. Small swells in rows, a chop with no origin, and once — a single swell the height of your chest that crosses the whole pool, one wave rehearsing, and breaks against the rim in a hiss that is over before you flinch.

Underneath everything: the pulse. Every nine seconds, a boom from far below the floor of the pool. You feel it in your sternum first, then your teeth, then your ears get what's left. The pilgrims do not count days here. They count pulses. A journey is *about twenty thousand pulses*. Time does not flow from the sun on this summit; it beats up from below. And on certain days — days of growth — the water jumps a finger's width with each pulse and does not fall back, and the notch-staff by the wall gets recut early.

At the northern rim is the throat: the birth-channel, a spiral throat of stone where the water swallows down and coughs up in a roar that out-thunders the sky. The throat exhales a spray-cloud that circles the hollow like private weather. Twice a day the sun crosses it and the channel wears its rainbow — the only fixed ornament the place permits itself. Where the stone is growing it is warm. Lay your palm on the newest rock, the only warm thing at this altitude, a sleeping animal's flank. The pilgrims warm their hands this way.

To go under is to enter the loudness properly. Dive from the eastern shelf, on the braided hair-rope, and the light comes down in clean columns — no dust, no drifting fiber, no plankton; a cathedral with nothing in it. No fish. No weed. Not one bubble holding itself together. The water is not full of emptiness. It is built of it. And the pulse arrives not as sound but as event: a pressure that closes around you gently, nine seconds apart, your vision jittering with each beat, the whole world snapping once like a flag taking wind. The rope runs out long before the bottom. Below the sun-columns the blue stops being a color and becomes a distance. Divers who went once describe — afterward, in sign — a moment when something moved past the edge of sight, a shape not yet committed to being any particular animal. It did not come back. In fairness, it never entirely arrived either.

Between pulses, sometimes, the surface forgets to move and goes to glass. In those seconds it does not reflect the sky. It shows coasts. A headland of white stone. An archipelago like a cast of teeth. A delta branching like a lung. Green shores with forests that do not exist yet, reflected in water that does not exist yet either. The sea is imagining what it will carve, the way children rehearse being grown. Certain dreams repeat — a bay, a particular cliff like a molar — and repetition, the pilgrims say, is intention. Then the pulse returns and slaps the dream away, and the surface is a courtyard of cold water again.

Life here is a handful of pioneers at the edge of their nerve. In the spray-ring a felt of black moss, drinking the throat's cough. Thread-weed in one crevice. On the southern shelf, the world's first lichen — a pale ring a hand-span wide that, the grandmothers say, took a lifetime to grow a finger's width. The green is slightly wrong: an apprentice green. Three white birds ride the throat's updraft and never land — there is no ground that stays, nothing that has arrived to eat. At dawn a goat-gray animal climbs the bare flank to lick minerals at the rim, drinks nothing, leaves, and the new stone seals its prints behind it.

No one lives here. It is too loud to live beside. But people come, four days from the green countries below, carrying everything: stakes of wood sharpened with river-stones that were also carried up — wood is so rare on this summit that a stake is worth a knife. A ring of stake-tips around the throat. Cairns stacked flat-stone-on-flat-stone in the still seconds between pulses; some whole, some half-absorbed, the mountain having taken the offering and kept the shape of it under new mineral. A pecked granite basin under the spray-line, never allowed to empty, its contents drunk by the sick in shells. And the rim rock is full of ghost hands — palms pressed to the stone by pilgrims dead and gone, not washed away but *buried*, clouding slowly inward like faces under ice. People walk the wall searching for their grandmothers' hands. Some find them. A hand-height inside the mountain by now.

Nobody speaks at the sea. Not from reverence — from futility. A shout here is a mouthful of nothing. The pilgrims' sign-language borrows the sea's own verbs: *swallow, cough, pulse, dream*. A conversation is two people gesturing storm-grammar quietly at each other. Leave in the afternoon and by dusk the silence of the lower slopes feels like injury; you keep touching your ears to check the world hasn't gone wrong; for a day you don't speak, not from ritual, but because your voice is searching for a loudness to push against and finding none.

Understand the rules and the place stops being strange and becomes only young. Nothing here erodes. Things grow, or arrive, or are kept. The water keeps the time; the mountain grows on the beat; the stone heals every wound, including its own. The sea is before its own shore, and dreams its shore nightly, because carving is the only way it will ever get out.

It will get out. The notch-staff says centuries — each generation, the notch climbs a hand's width. One day the rim's low southern lip will be breached and the sea will go down this bare newborn mountain as the first river, and everything it touches will be the first of its kind to be touched by water. The sea is already doing all of this, in advance, at a horizon three paces wide.

Stand at the lip at noon. The pulse comes up through your boots. The rainbow holds in the throat. Down there the blue is looking back — not at you. Through you. At everything it is going to be.
