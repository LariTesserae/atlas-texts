## First, the Ceiling

Before there are walls, there is light — and in this place the light has a direction no cave should allow. It comes from above, through the rock itself.

The roof of the Mew is fifty feet of translucent gypsum in most places, thinning to six in others, and daylight passes through it the way it passes through paper held against a window: diluted but true. The whole cavern country is lit this way — softly, evenly, bright as an overland afternoon with the sun behind cloud. You could read by it. Nothing down here has evolved to be blind. Everything down here has evolved to be *pale*.

The ceiling is a working sky. Clouds cross the surface plain overhead and their shadows slide across the Whites like slow hands smoothing linen. In the thinnest places, recognizable shadows fall — a hawk, far above, crosses the sun and a gray cross glides along the stone floor of the Mew, soft-edged, several feet off from where a shadow ought to be. In the thick places, the sun registers only as a dimming, a breath held in the light. The herders read the ceiling the way shepherds read the sky: brown-gold for morning, white-hot under the open wells at noon, rose and then slate as the day ends. At night the Whites go a deep ash-gray, and if the moon is up they brighten faintly, moonlight by way of stone. Stars never reach. Rain does reach — you learn of surface storms when the ceiling darkens unevenly, blotched, like breath on glass.

Except where the roof has fallen open. There are wells — collapse shafts where real sun falls straight through, hard beams, the only directional light in the Mew, and under them the pools steam and the stone bleaches bone-white. Everything is brightest and most brutal in those rooms.

## A Quarry Yesterday

Nothing here is old. That is the second thing to understand, and it takes longer to believe.

The erosion is embryonic — the Mew is not being worn away so much as *being decided*. The water table found this soft white rock recently, geologically moments ago, and is still choosing its routes. The galleries are raw: edges unhandled, corners saw-cut, surfaces dusted in gypsum flour so fine that every footprint — herder, beast, grub, beetle — prints crisply and stays printed, because nothing has arrived yet to smooth it. A crawlway opened last month reads as fresh as a quarry face. Collapse blocks lie where they fell, their bedding still visible, their corners unworn, so that the rubble fields look like rooms that were dropped rather than ruins that were abandoned.

You can see tomorrow being drafted on the walls. Darker seams in the stone, damp lines, faint traces where dissolution has begun its penciling — a gallery that does not exist yet, bleeding through. The water is patient. The water is still finding its sentence.

And the Mew answers this newness with newness. Roofs shed. Somewhere, every day, a slab lets go, and a boom rolls through the country and arrives everywhere at once, doubled and delayed by unseen galleries, and for days afterward the dust hangs in the lightwells like fog with an orange light on it. Passages open overnight. The herders' maps are not maps; they are diaries.

## The Breath

The water does not live here so much as *visit* — but it visits on a schedule, and the schedule runs everything.

Deep in the low country there is a siphon, a flooded throat of passages that connects the Mew to a warmer and deeper water. On a rhythm the herders count in strokes on stone, the Throat exhales: a gulp you feel in your teeth, a great withdrawal, and the low galleries drain over minutes, leaving mud, stranded pools, glittering wet everything.
