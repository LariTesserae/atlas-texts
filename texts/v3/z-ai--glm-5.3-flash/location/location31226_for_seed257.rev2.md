Start with the mountain, because everything else sits on top of it.

It stands on a plain of grey silt that goes flat in every direction farther than any eye could confirm, and its roots are lost in a dark that is not night — night ends — but simply depth, depth all the way down, depth as far down as water has ever bothered to be. The mountain climbs out of that. Nothing grows on it. Nothing has ever grown on it. Its flanks are bare basalt wearing a skin of settled dust, gullied and smoothed by currents so slow they count as geology rather than weather, and if you swim up along it — and something, sometime, will — you will notice that the cold gets neither better nor worse as you rise. It is one long even cold, patient as the plain below. The mountain's height is not a view of anything. There is no shore, no surface light, no air in any direction. Water is the sky here, and it has been the sky for longer than there has been anything to notice.

Then, near the summit, the cold stops.

It stops the way a thought stops — one stroke you are in the ancient chill, and the next your skin reports that the water has gone soft, warm as a lagoon in low summer, warm as something alive. The boundary is not a flicker. It is a wall you feel with your whole body, steady and unwavering, and it has been steady for ages; the animals on the far side of it know its shape the way you know the outlines of your own rooms. For many days' swim around this mountain, in every direction, this is the only warmth in the world. Everything that lives near here lives because of what happens on the summit.

What happens on the summit is a small thing. That is the first surprise: after the enormous empty rise of the mountain, the warm precinct is tiny. You could circle the whole of it in a hundred strokes, could hold it in a glance if there were light enough to glance with. A dozen rooms at most, packed tight, walls a hand's thickness, corbelled beehive chambers stacked and interleaved like cells — and every bit of it made. Not grown, not eroded into being, not found. Laid. Fitted stone on fitted stone, dry-stacked, the courses pecked into shape and ground together until they sit like knuckles. The terrace is paved. The rim is walled. Even the springs are built: each warm mouth rises through a collar of fitted blocks, and the water comes up inside stonework, through channels cut on purpose, through flues that carry its warmth into the rooms the way a stove carries heat into a house.

Someone stacked all this by hand, without metal — there is not one cut on this mountain that metal ever made; the marks are hammer-dress and lever-scar and patient grinding — and then went away. The treads of their stairs are worn into shallow smiles. In one deep room, low on a wall, there is a print of a hand pressed in ochre, the red gone a quiet brown in the dark: a hand, or something with a hand-shaped front, resting long enough to leave itself behind. Whether the builders raised this lodge on a mountain in the open air, before some drowning, or whether they were already creatures of the water and built it the way we build — the stone has finished telling. It is old past the reach of its own inscriptions. That is the second surprise: how worn and how whole it is at once. Every edge outside the sheltered rooms has been rounded to the softness of a river pebble. Lintels have sagged a finger's width and been caught there by cement. The warm water is rich in dissolved stone, and over uncountable time it has deposited its mineral inside every joint and flue, banded in fine layers like the rings of a tree — a ledger with a century to a line — until the whole lodge fused into a single soft-edged mass, a geode of rooms, hollowed and healed and finished. It has settled into its final form. Very little about it will ever change again except the parts the water still moves through.

The warmth has never heard about the drowning. That is the third thing, and the one that reorganizes everything else. The springs came up under this terrace before the builders found them, and they kept coming up after whatever happened to the builders happened, and they are coming up now, at their own pace, unimpressed. The hearth stayed lit under the sea. And because a warm room is rare beyond imagining down here, life did what life does: it crowded in.

There are no plants. Say it plainly, because the eye keeps looking for them: nothing here has ever leafed, or flowered, or put down a root. No green anywhere, not even a memory of green. The food of this world begins at the stone itself — a felt of microbes sheathing the warm walls, feeding not on light but on the chemistry the water carries, invisible until you look too closely, at which point it is everywhere. On this felt the whole economy turns. Flat coin-limpets mow it in slow overlapping arcs, and by day — by whatever counts as day, which is nothing — they stack themselves in the cooler corners in tight tiers, shell over shell over shell, nearly touching, patient as roof tiles. Pale frilled anemones line the flue mouths, hung open in the warm outflow like laundry in a current, closing by fractions when the flow stumbles. Above them, in the warm ceiling layer of every chamber, hangs a smoke of translucent shrimp-things so dense the water looks curdled, and when the smoke flicks all at once it makes a sizzle too soft to be sure of. Eggs and milt and larvae thicken the water between the bodies until the space itself feels crowded, until it is hard to say where the population ends and the medium begins.

And there are keepers. An octopoid lineage, soft-armed, themselves oddly handlike, lives in the flue rooms and does a strange, sensible thing: it cleans. It rasps the mineral crust from the channels, cracks the thick scale loose, carries the rubble out in its arms and dumps it on the apron. It is not remembering the builders; it is only that a clogged flue makes a cold room, and a cold room makes dead eggs, and so the ones who tend the channels leave more descendants than the ones who don't. But the effect is the same as memory. The builders' plan still works. Water still flows where they decided water should flow, because life has been paid, generation after generation, to keep the decision open. The intention outlived the intenders. It is the strangest thing in this place and it is entirely ordinary once you sit with it: architecture as inheritance, a drawing on stone that no one drew anymore but everyone maintained.

Death here is also construction. The warm water is saturated with stone, so whatever dies inside the precinct slowly turns mineral, and the mineral cements, and the cement joins the walls. Thresholds in the lower rooms hold shapes that might be ribs. The lodge grows by receiving its dead. Everything here ends up as wall.

Light is scarce enough to be nearly a rumor. The sun is a story told by deep-water animals that migrated down from somewhere brighter generations ago; no glow of it survives to this height. What light there is comes in three small kinds. There is the microbial rim: at the freshest seeps, the bacterial felt carries a faint blue-green luminosity, a cold spiritual glow that is nothing like leaf-light, a haze along the stone like breath on glass. There is the stirred light: the water itself carries luminous motes, and any motion blooms a slow map of glow through the dark — put a hand to a wall and the wall appears around your fingers, whole courses of masonry sketched in fading blue, and then the dark closes back over the drawing. The animals read the lodge this way, by bumping and blundering and reading the bloom. Most of their eyes are small. Many are gone. A few hang in the dark turned toward the seep-rims like faces toward a hearth. And there is the third kind: a pale lure on a long folded thing that hangs motionless above one flue mouth for months at a time, spending light the way the rest of the precinct spends warmth — sparingly, deliberately, once.

The sound of the place is its most radical feature, because there is almost none. Not silence the way air knows silence — water carries even a whisper across distances that would shame any air — but a silence of sources. Nothing bothers to make noise here. What there is, is small and slow: the tick of a wall warming by a fraction, one tick, then a long nothing; the papery rasp of a limpet's radula, a page turning in another room; the soft tap-tap of a keeper's arms rearranging a stone it has moved a thousand times before. Once in a very long while the whole mound emits a single low groan — a flue resealing, a band of mineral settling — a sound like a door closing in a world that no longer has doors. The warm column rising from the lodge's chimney makes no sound at all. It climbs through the cold dark above the summit, lazy and barely moving, a long standing breath of heat that reaches higher than the mountain does — the true elevation of this place is not the terrace but the plume, a ghost of the builders' fire climbing through the black toward a surface none of it will ever reach.

Time here runs on two clocks at once, and neither is hurried. The shrimp-smoke lives and dies in weeks, whole dynasties flaring and gone like sparks off a log; but the shape of its swarm follows the heat exactly, thickening over the warm rooms, thinning over the cool ones, a shadow cast by temperature. The rooms themselves cycle over centuries: a vent slowly silts, a chamber cools over a decade, its tenants move out room by room with no urgency at all — this is called nothing, there is no one here to call it anything, but it has the feel of a migration, a slow parade along the warmth-gradient to the next open hearth. A new crack opens under a wall; a new room warms over another decade; the parade turns and files back. Territory here is measured in degrees, and status is proximity to the springs, and every animal in the precinct knows the whole map of it through its skin. And under all of it, slower than any of it, the ledger thickens: a new band of stone, a hair deep, laid down along the flues each century, the lodge keeping its own quiet count of its own old age.

One room does not share in any of this. It is sealed — a door that was fitted and never reopened, its joints long since welded shut by mineral — and the water inside it has stood untouched since the fitting. Nothing lives there. It is the only water in the warm precinct that nothing stirs and nothing eats and nothing breeds in, a held breath in the middle of all that abundance. Stir it if you ever get in, and the bloom of light will rise and fade and nothing at all will answer it.

That is the place. A small warm room-cluster, hand-built by someone gone, on the summit of a drowned mountain at the bottom of a lightless ocean, fused by its own slow water into a single soft stone, holding the only heat for days in every direction, dense to its walls with life that feeds on the stone's chemistry and keeps the builders' channels open without knowing why. It is very old. It is, by the standard of eroding worlds, essentially finished — it has worn down to what it is, and what it is holds. The cold presses politely against it from every side, and has pressed for longer than there have been names for cold. And up through the dark from its chimney rises the thin standing column of its warmth, taller than the mountain, felt by pressure-sense from far away — so that anything out in that enormous cold that swims toward heat is, without knowing it, swimming toward the oldest intention still at work anywhere in this water: warmth, gathered by hands, kept by patience, still being kept.
