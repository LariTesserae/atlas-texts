You hear the place before you see it. The trail climbs out of the valley dark and into the cloud, and somewhere above you a ticking begins — a thousand drips, each finding its pool at a different interval, so the air never goes silent and never finds a rhythm either. Under the ticking, a hiss of water on leaves. Under the hiss, wings.

Then the fog opens like a curtain drawn by someone with somewhere to be, and there it is: a bench on the mountain's shoulder, nearly flat, green from lip to lip, no wider than a long walk and bounded like a held breath. Above it the mountain continues in cold scree and dwarf trees and then bare rock, going up and up into weather. Below it, the world simply stops. The trail runs off the shelf's outer edge into permanent cloud, and the cloud goes down farther than anyone here can say, because nobody here has gone down to check. You could shout across this place. You could walk it end to end in half an hour if you didn't stop. You will stop.

The water is not a feature of the landscape; it is the medium the landscape is suspended in. The rock itself sweats. Along a seam on the north face a row of springs weeps out of the stone in a line, moss hanging from each one like a wet beard, and from those springs the whole shelf has been plumbed by hand: stone-lined rills green to the waterline, split-bamboo flumes running on trestle legs, hollow-log chutes, woven fiber screens strung along the rim to comb water out of the passing fog — the screens drip in steady rows into troughs that feed the cisterns, which are hollowed and lined and older than any family now living. Under the canopy it rains even in sunshine, a soft leaf-rain falling from drip-tips long after the sky has stopped. And every afternoon the valley breathes its cloud up the slope, and the shelf wrings it out. Rain arrives like an appointment. The people set theirafternoons by it. Their own name for the place is also their verb for what rain does continuously; the nearest a trader's tongue comes is *to drip, without end*.

There is no bare ground. That takes a while to understand. What looks like soil is root-mat, thick and springy, a finger of it compressing like bread — the whole shelf is knit together underneath, and everything growing shares the same floor. The hedges are walls. The walls are hedges, planted generations ago as fences and thickened since into trunks, and houses were started inside them: a frame of saplings set in a ring, trained, grafted, allowed to fuse, then hollowed by hand into rooms. Nothing here was built. Things were *started*, and tended, and used while they finished. Doors are woven mats that reed themselves shut in the wind. Ladders are vines with rungs grafted in, and they are stronger than they look because the vine has had years to argue with the rungs. Above the rooftops, rope-and-plank lanes run through the crowns of the founding trees — the upper town, where children live half their childhood and the whole settlement can be crossed without touching the ground. It is a cloud forest that decided to be a city.

A few hundred people. Houses near-touching, layered by generations, your grandfather's fence now the load-bearing wall of your roof, and the roof of the next house rooted into yours. You give your address by its grafts: *the third fork past the kiln spine, the one with the red-flowered elbow*. The market sits in a natural hollow where the fog pools longest, and the trading is loud and constant — fruit, tubers, fish from the terrace ponds, eggs, honey from stingless bees, cordage, dried moss for packing, dyes, feathers, songs.
