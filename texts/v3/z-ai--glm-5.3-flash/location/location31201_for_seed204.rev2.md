To find Weirhold you must already know where it is. That is the first rule of the place, and the town does nothing to break it. High on the mountain's north shoulder, above where the last stunted grass fails and below where the summit ice begins, the rock is creased by a seam — a slot a few meters wide in places, four hundred paces long, thirty deep at its mouth. From the valley below, from the passes, from the air, it reads as nothing: a shadow line, a fold. The whole country of Weirhold hides inside it.

And the whole country is wet.

It rains inside the mountain; the sky is merely a spectator. The fault that lifted this shoulder did so recently — within the stone's own living memory — and a young mountain has not yet settled its plumbing. Groundwater sits perched in the fresh rock like water in a raised sponge, and the seam's south face is where it escapes. The wall weeps. Not in one place but in hundreds: beads swelling at crystal edges, threads that wander down the rock and braid, and at the seam's center, where the largest fracture vents, a single silver sheet — the mother weir — falling the height of a man and gathering in a channel the width of a hand. The water leaves the rock at a degree above freezing, mineral-bright, faintly sweet with dissolved new stone, and spends the rest of its short life trying to become ice. The town exists to catch it in between.

The rock itself is startling to anyone used to old mountains. There is no crust on it, no lichen, no softening. Fracture faces are conchoidal and glassy, edges unrounded, corners sharp enough to draw blood from a careless glove. Water stains make the only color: pale iron rust bleeding orange down gray stone, white mineral bloom where evaporation wins. Nothing here has been weathered. The landscape is weeks old in spirit, and still moving — the fault creeps a few millimeters a year, enough that the town's builders leave gaps in their walls and let the mountain close them at its own pace.

The town is strung along the wet face like beads on a wire. Terraces pin themselves to the rock in shallow shelves — a dwelling, a cold-store, a valve room, forty paces of bare stone, another terrace. The gaps are deliberate; the founders' one aesthetic was spaciousness, and it holds. Plazas here are outcrops left raw. The widest street is a rope's shadow. Most of the settlement's mass is behind the face: galleries cut into the seam wall, shallow halls and lamp-lit chambers stacked three generations deep, older bolt-sockets hand-cut, then cast rings, then the current anchors — self-tightening collars that hum faintly once a night as they take up the creep. A town built in layers against a wall that refuses to be still.

No fire is permitted anywhere in Weirhold. This is not piety but engineering: warmth is the only thing the seam cannot spare. All the town's energy is harvested from the difference between water and ice. The frostworks — low, quiet buildings terraced into the mother weir's channel — drink the freeze. Every night the temperature drops and the water wants to expand by a tenth of its volume; the frostworks let it expand against pistons, against pressure cells, against itself, and that slow deliberate pushing runs the town. Ice is their iron, their coin, and their calendar. Surplus cold is banked in the deep gallery, packed as graded blocks, and the town's wealth is read in winters stored. There are no engines, no smoke, no heat anywhere except three vent-halls, warmed by solid-state movers that pump thermal energy through their ceramic walls without any moving part — heat made to walk the wrong direction, an engineering the founders understood perfectly and explained to no one.

The light is thin and hard, the way it is at altitude, with the zenith gone a darker blue than the horizon. The seam lives mostly on reflected light. For most of the day the sun only touches the rim, and the slot glows with bounce — gray stone turning pearl, wet faces lighting up like solder. At true noon a coin of direct sun reaches the bottom for a few minutes and everyone in the galleries knows it without being told; the shadow line moving up the weeping wall is the town's real clock. An hour after dawn comes the second dawn: on the rim above, fields of mirror-dust — flakes scattered like snow, each no bigger than a moth's wing, each tilting on its own to follow the sun — catch the light and throw it down the slot in a warm amber wash. The seam's mornings happen twice.

The edge of the wall, where the seam opens to the sky, wears hoarfrost feathers every morning — crystals grown in still air, ferns of white on every projection — and part of the early work is brushing rime off the mirror-dust with long-handled swabs, standing on the very lip of the country, clouds pooled in the valley far below like a second, softer rock.

Life here is thin as the air. Nothing green has arrived; the greenest thing in Weirhold is a copper roof gone pale. What lives, lives as film. Black yeast-skins coat the damp shaded rock in velvet patches; rust-orange bacterial films graze the fresh iron the young stone keeps offering — this is the secret of the local ecology, that a newly uplifted mountain is a meal that has not yet been eaten. In the weir channels graze the seepworms: pale, finger-length, eyeless, ancient in the manner of things that never needed eyes. They are the town's only husbandry; a child can gather a tablespoon of them from the outflow screens, and a tablespoon is a feast, fried on a stone warmed over a heat-mover's exhaust.

In the warm afternoons, the vent-halls breathe out, and midges hatch from the standing moisture in the outfall channels — small white drifts of them, hanging in the amber of second dawn like slow snow falling upward. And some middays — not every day, some seasons never — a whitekite comes. A pale falcon that has learned the seam's schedule from somewhere on the summit ice, it drops into the slot, rides the thermal that rises off the warm outfalls, takes midges from the air in long stoops that end an arm's length from a frozen watcher, and is gone over the rim. Its single descending cry is the loudest biological sound in the country. Children keep counts. Some years the count is zero.

The quiet is structural. Stand still in the slot and you can hear a valve open across the seam — a knock like a knuckle cracking. The ambient sound is the drip-count: the wall speaking in a slow rainy language, though it has not rained here in the memory of anyone alive. The frostworks hum like a held breath. At night the ice talks — ticks, pings, long groaning syllables as curtains of ice that grew overnight on the weeping face shift and shed. Once or twice a winter a curtain calves whole, and the calving bell rings, one struck note rolling down four hundred paces of stone, and everyone steps back from the face until the ringing dies. That gunshot crack is the loudest sound the country ever makes.

Everything is governed. The day by valves and shadow. The year by harvest discipline: you may take only what the wall gives today, and the ledger is kept on the Ledger Wall, a smooth gallery face threaded by the town's single continuous nerve — a filament thinner than an eyelash, kilometers of it, grown through the rock in the founding decade by a machine they still maintain but no longer fully understand. The filament reports strain, temperature, water, creep, everywhere at once, and each morning its messages condense on the Ledger Wall as frost-script: fine white glyphs that subliminate away by afternoon, so the record exists only as long as it is true. The town reads it at dawn, before second dawn gilds it, before it fades.

Nobody here thinks beyond the rim. Maps in Weirhold end at the lip of the slot; the valley with its weather and its nations is a rumor below the cloud deck. The whole country is four hundred paces long, cold, young, wet, and mostly empty, and every soul in it can name the drips that matter. The mountain rose, the water found the light, and the town grew in the crack between them like a film of order — harvesting the difference between liquid and solid, one freeze at a time, while overhead the mirror-dust turns on ten million tiny hinges and pours a second morning down into the seam.
