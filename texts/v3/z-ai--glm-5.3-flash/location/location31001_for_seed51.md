# Tallymere

You come down to it by feel before sight. The ridges around the basin have been worn so long they are more suggestion than barrier — long gray shoulders of stone, softened into slopes that any flood could have smoothed, that any flood plainly has. The air changes on the way down: it thickens, cools by degrees you can count on your skin, and takes on a sound that arrives out of order — a drip here, a drip there, and underneath everything a single deep note, sustained, more felt in the sternum than heard. Then the trees open, and the fen is there, all at once, already surrounding you. It was surrounding you before you noticed.

The light is a problem the eye never quite solves. There is no sun, no bright patch, nothing to point at — only a vast diffuse glare, like standing inside a pearl, gray-green, shadowless. Distance doesn't darken; it pales. Two hundred paces out, the reedbeds fade into the haze not as silhouettes but as suggestions. The world reads at arm's length and dissolves past that, and you learn quickly to navigate by sound instead, because sound here is generous. The fen is loud. It drips with tempo.

---

**The water**

Water is everywhere and none of it is idle. The basin is a sump — the lowest ground for many kilometers — and the whole region drains into it, but not the way a region drains anywhere else. It arrives as seep. The rim cliffs weep: hundreds of spring-lines threading down the rock faces, each one polished into a groove by ages of its own passage, each one carrying a cold that doesn't match the weather. Put your hand under one and the water is cellar-cold, though the air is merely cool. The cold comes from below. Something under the fen runs chill the way the back of an old machine runs, and the whole basin sits on it like a hand on an icehouse wall.

The channels across the flats are what hold your attention longest. They are not straight, exactly — but they are not wild either. They braid and curve with a patience that feels *chosen*, worn glass-smooth, in balance the way a riverbed is only in balance when nothing has disturbed it for a very long time. Reeds grow along them in rows too even to be accident and too organic to be planting. When wind moves through, the reeds click against each other — small, dry, wooden clicks — and the clicking is not random. It comes in bursts. It keeps a rhythm. Stand still long enough and you realize the reed-clicks are keeping time with the hum.

The water itself is full of motes. Scoop a handful and at first it's just cold brown water; hold it up to the pearly light and you see the suspension — a faint grit of brightness, uncountable particles, drifting, then not drifting, then drifting *differently*, as if they'd noticed you noticing. In the fast stretches of channel, where the current quickens, the motes stir and give off a low pale glow, so that at the dim hours the whole hydrology of the fen draws itself in light — slow cold filaments of it, like lightning that has decided to take its time. Nothing built made this. No pipe, no weir, no post, no cut stone anywhere. And that becomes the strangest fact of the place, the one you keep returning to: the fen is *engineered*. It meters itself. The weeping rocks admit water in measured amounts; the outflow — a smooth-throated channel on the far side, called the Throat, where water visibly leaves and visibly goes nowhere, into mist — carries it away in equal measure. The level of the pools never rises. Never falls. The regulation has run, unattended, longer than the ridges have had their present shape. The machinery and the erosion shaped each other. What was built has become what is natural, and there is no longer any difference.

What it computes, no one is left to say. The motes hold memory — this is plain. The water remembers. Old rain is in there, and older things. When the mist settles close on windless evenings, it sometimes holds shapes: a corridor where no corridor is, a flight of steps in the air, dissolving when you approach. The fen is not haunted. It is *archiving*.

---

**The seams**

Every old landscape has its scars. Tallymere's scars are places where the rules have worn thin — and, like everything else here, they are not fresh wounds. They are weathered anomalies, edges rounded by centuries of their own existence, settled into the landscape's character the way boulders settle into a hillside.

There is a pool in the eastern flats, clear and shallow, that reflects a blue daytime sky. Above the pool it is the usual pearl-dim overcast; in the water, cheerful blue with a few white clouds, moving slowly. The water is not magic-looking. It just disagrees, placidly, with the sky.

Near the weeping cliffs, echoes misbehave. Clap your hands and the echo returns a half-breath before the clap finishes, as if the air there were impatient, or as if it had heard you coming. Once — only once — an echo came back that was not yours: your own voice, older, saying a thing you hadn't said yet.

And there are the Remembering Flats, where the mud keeps pressure. Footprints persist there — not drying, not filling, just staying — and so the flats carry the tracks of centuries, all at once, edges still crisp and wet: your boots alongside bare prints alongside three-toed prints alongside prints of something broad and shuffling that passed through when the alders were young. Walk across it and you join a conversation across ages. Your prints will outlast you. The mud will decide when they're done.

None of it is dangerous, precisely. It is simply *worn differently there*, like a stair dish by ten thousand feet. The anomalies drift, very slowly — a few meters a year — and leave thin lanes where the moss dies back and regrows, so their paths can be read across the flats like the moraines of glaciers.

---

**Life**

The green is riotous and cool-toned. Sphagnum in cushions deep as barrels, so saturated it hisses when compressed underfoot. Ferns black-green and heavy, every frond beaded, every bead falling on a beat. Alder scrub standing stilted on tangled roots over the quake flats, where the ground is a floating mat more than a floor and sways gently, disturbingly, when you breathe on it. Everything grows into everything — moss on stone on root on water, epiphyte on epiphyte, layers of green with no soil visible anywhere, life stacked near-touching in every available volume. The lushness is fed from below: the seep-water carries dissolved minerals that no ordinary drainage would carry. The fen exudes nutrient. It *irrigates* itself.

And yet almost nothing eats. The cold and the dim keep the metabolism of the place low; the niches are few, and each is held by something singular.

White thread-worms writhe in the moss like slow stitching — the base of everything. In the channels graze the pale eels, thick as an arm, so sluggish they seem poured rather than swimming, gorged on the mote-rich current; at night their bodies carry faint banded glows, the light they've eaten showing through the skin. And at the seams, at fixed intervals you could set a clock by, the wick-heron arrives: tall, gray, gaunt, standing in water that disagrees with its sky, drinking from the wrong side of the reflection. Its image in the pool lags it by half a breath. It takes the small eels where they come to graze the seams, and you find their ghost-pale skins drifted against the reeds, evidence of a predation so sparse and so punctual it feels more like ritual than hunger. The heron does not fear you. It simply registers you, the way the mud registers a boot.

But the great one is in the center. Under the Deep Eye — the heart-pool of the basin, black, still, cold past cold, so deep that weighted lines have never found bottom — something lives that is barely fauna at all. You never see it. You know it only by the hum: on rare days the deep note *bends*, swings a quarter-tone flat, and every pool across the whole basin rings at once like struck glass, and the mist lifts in a single breath, all together, and settles again. Something down there has turned over in its sleep. The eels go still for an hour. Then the note returns, and the fen resumes its tally.

---

**Rules, as the place keeps them**

The water level never changes. The heron keeps its schedule. Near seams, sound arrives before its cause; on the flats, pressure is remembered until the mud is done with it. Do not drink from the Deep Eye — not from danger, exactly, but because the water there is so dense with motes it is barely water anymore; it is more like swallowing a thought. And on the weeping cliff at the basin's western edge there is one wear-pattern shaped like a doorway, smooth and door-proportioned, worn by water alone over ages — the landscape's single almost-artifact, an absence in the shape of intent. Nothing opens it. It is only the shape of where something might once have meant to.

Twice a day the fen breathes: the mist rises off the pools in a slow tide, hangs, sinks. At the cold hour the hum drops a note. The channel-lines burn pale. And the whole basin — the weeping rim, the braided channels, the reed-rows clicking their endless rhythm, the motes sorting and resorting in every drop — goes on with its work, in balance, unowned, loud and dim, remembering everything it has ever touched. Standing in it, you understand that you are not a visitor to a place. You are an input. The water is already holding you.
