# The Pan

From the rim, you would call it empty, and be wrong by an entire world.

The Pan sits low — a shallow saucer of pale ground dropped inside old levees of heaped earth and poured concrete, a few hundred paces across and no more. It is hidden in the plainest sense: the surrounding flats are flat, the levee walls are high for their purpose, and from any distance the place reads as nothing, a blank in a blank. You have to come right to the lip before it exists. Then it opens all at once, a held breath of light.

The first thing to understand is that this ground is new. Weeks ago — a season at most — there was water here, standing water held by the works that built the levees and set the sheds, and then somewhere out in the flats a valve was closed, and over a slow number of days the Pan drank down and gave up its floor. What emerged has not had time to become land. It is still a surface: a skin of silt over mud, dried into plates the size of dinner plates, their edges curled and *sharp* — you could cut a palm on a crack here — with no rounding anywhere, no softened slope, no soil. No history. Between the plates run hairline seams, and the seams are the Pan's weather: dark at dawn, glistening, pale and chalky by noon, dark again with the night. Salt blooms in them overnight, whiskery white clusters crisp as frost, and by afternoon heat they slump and dissolve, and grow again in the dark. Set your ear to the floor after sundown and you can hear it — a faint, constant chinking, like light rain on a tent from a sky with no clouds. It is the crystals, growing.

The Pan is not wet. It is damp the way a cellar is damp, and its water is not a place but a *schedule*. The water table sits just below the floor, one bad idea deep, and the fresh ground has not sealed itself yet, so moisture moves through it on the day's terms: wicking up the seams at night as the air cools, beading on everything cold-blooded enough to drop below the dew point, then giving out again under a sun it cannot yet hide from. By afternoon the floor looks exhausted. By morning it is upholstered again in dew.

And here the first strangeness shows itself, because the dew does not fall evenly. It falls first and thickest on the metal.

---

The works left things behind, scattered the way tools are left when the leaving is hurried or simply unimagined: three sheds of galvanized steel going brown at the seams, a gauge post with one live dial, a line of stanchions that walks out of the Pan and up the rim and then stops, carrying a pipe that was removed before it was missed. A valve wheel lies half-buried near the Sweet Seep, turned green-white with salt, and the waders stand on it in the early light like officials on a dais. The main pipeline runs down the slope from the rim, crosses half the floor, and ends in the open — a clean cut, a mouth the diameter of a fist, standing a hand's width above a seep. Dew forms inside that mouth every night, beading down the bore, and at first light it drips. Each drop falls with a hollow note, the pipe playing one low tone over and over, slower and flatter as the sun takes the metal, until by mid-morning the flute goes silent and stays silent all day.

The greenest thing in the Pan grows under the drip line of the humming shed. That is worth saying plainly: of all the sparse vegetation here — the red-green fringe of succulents crowded into the wettest inch where rim meets floor, the dark algal mats furring the seep edges, the few salt-tolerant tufts — the single most thriving plant lives on water that falls out of a machine. The vegetation is sparse because water is sparse, and the water is sparse because the machines that still work are few. So the plants queue at the artifacts. The living world of the Pan organizes itself around its dead industry like moss around a ruin.

One shed still hums. Inside, a timer dial goes around and around; a centrifugal pump, left running when the works departed because no one was left to turn it off, cycles through the small hours, drawing from the largest seep and discharging — no one has found where. The water goes *down*. By the pump's midnight hour the seep has dropped an inch; by dawn it has refilled. The last act of the works was to forget this machine, and the machine has repaid the neglect by becoming the clock of everything alive.

---

Kneel down and the emptiness reverses. The floor of the Pan is the most crowded few hundred paces anywhere in the flats. The burrowers have seen to that: their flask-shaped burrows stipple every plate and every seam in spacing so even the field looks woven, near-hexagonal, a tilework of raised rims, each burrow capped at midday with a plug of compacted silt. By evening the whole floor wears its second skin of clay lids, thousands of them, and at night the lids lift a centimeter and the burrows breathe in the humid dark, banking the cool, closing at dawn with the day's ration caught inside. They are living condensers, and there is no bare ground between them — not a tile unclaimed, not a seam unoccupied. Late arrivals must settle for the cracks. The cracks are taken too.

The skaters graze the salt gardens. Small, dark, low things with damp-backed shells, they rasp the whisker-blooms down to nubs and move on, leaving grazing scars across the efflorescence so precise that the floor reads, by morning, like a ledger of who ate where. Their shells stay wet through the worst of the day — they carry the night's dew in the pores of their backs, condensation farmed on the body, drunk at leisure. Above the seeps hang the fly membranes: swarms so dense they are less a cloud than a *surface*, one you can dent with a hand. They part around your wrist and close behind it. When the membrane lands at dawn it does so all at once, and the seep margin goes black and glistening, wing to wing, no exposed water anywhere along the edge.

The waders know the schedule. They come over the rim in ones and twos at first light — long-legged, silent, their legs lacquered dark to the knee with dried mineral — and they do not hunt when they arrive. They wait. The hour they wait for is the pump's: when the timer clicks over and the hum deepens and the seep draws down, the fly larvae are stranded on the newly exposed margin, glistening and slow, and the birds come down the slope with the punctual deliberation of creatures who have set their whole calendar by a machine. It takes minutes. Then the waders drink at the Sweet Seep — only there; the brine pools are for the flies — climb to the valve wheel, open their wings to warm, and leave. The swarm rebuilds by next dawn. Nobody in the Pan has ever known any other clock. The timer is the only heirloom the works left that anyone still uses.

The rules, as far as anyone has written them down, are few, and all of them run on water. Standing water is territory. Condensation is income. The machine keeps the time. The rim is a border — the walls are steep for small bodies, and what falls in stays in, so the Pan fills the way a tide gauge fills, packing its residents edge to edge and making the newcomers take the seams. And nothing here is finished. The whole ecology is a first draft, days old: the predation improvised, the burrow-spacing converged on by accident, every relationship provisional, every niche still wet cement.

---

The sounds of the Pan would disappoint anyone expecting a crowd. For all its saturation it is a nearly quiet place. The pump's hum is felt in the teeth before it is heard, and only within arm's reach of the shed. The birds call at first light and then, apparently, consider the day's speaking done. What remains is small and constant: the tick of growing crystals; the drip-flute at the pipe's mouth; the dry *tok* of a lid settling; and once a day, brief as a page turning, the sound of the swarm lifting — the loudest event in the Pan, over in seconds, after which the quiet resumes like water closing over a stone.

Two mysteries are kept, quietly. Each dawn, the inside of the pipe's open mouth is polished clean, and there are slime trails leading in. Nothing has ever been seen leaving. And after warm nights, the floor shows a scatter of pried-off burrow lids and widened holes, work done by something with leverage and patience that no one has met. The burrowers rebuild. The Pan does not press these questions.

The machines, meanwhile, are being mineralized. Salt has grown inside the dead bulb of the shed lamp — a crystal sitting in the glass like a seed in a jar. The gauge glass fogs from within; the needle still climbs all night and slides down through noon, measuring the breath of a floor that is still settling, squeezing out its pore water, exhaling at night, inhaling heat at midday. The switch housings hold white drifts. The works built the Pan to evaporate things, and the Pan has kept the assignment while changing the subject: it is evaporating the works, one instrument at a time, converting machinery into geology while the last machine is still running.

Once in a while a rain comes — brief, warm, out of a season's allowance — and it is the only event the Pan cannot schedule. The fresh floor cannot drink; the water sheets across the plates, pools in the seams, finds every burrow rim doing exactly the work it was raised for. The skaters seal themselves into their shells. The waders stand on the rim and watch their hunting ground turn briefly into a lake an inch deep. By noon it is gone, absorbed or evaporated, leaving the cracks darker and one new rill begun on the rim slope — the first sentence of erosion, written on ground with no older words anywhere on it.

Night is when the Pan is most itself. The concrete holds the day and gives it back gently, so the dark hours are milder here than on the flats outside — a few degrees of held warmth, one more quiet reason everything crowds in. The lids lift. The seams darken. Dew assembles itself on the pipe, on the sheds, on the backs of ten thousand sleeping skaters, and the floor chinks faintly to itself as the salt grows. Somewhere under it all the pump turns over on its timer, patient, employed, forgotten, keeping a time that an entire world has learned to live by.

At dawn the drips start in the pipe, one hollow note at a falling pitch, and the waders take their places at the rim, and the swarm lifts with its page-turning sound, and the first draft of this small, packed, hidden world is quietly rewritten — fuller than it was, sharper-edged than it should be, and not yet dry.
