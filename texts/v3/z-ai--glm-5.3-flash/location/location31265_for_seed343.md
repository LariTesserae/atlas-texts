# The Mat

The ground is the first thing that argues with you. You step off the stone stair onto the plain and the plain gives — not the give of mud or snow, but a firm, dense springiness, like walking on stacked leather. The entire world up here is upholstered. To every horizon the land is covered in cushions: dome-shaped plants, knee-high, some the size of haybarns, green-grey and dense as felt, grown into one another until the seams have vanished and the whole surface has become a single continuous skin. From a distance it reads as geology — rounded green rock, softly weathered. Put a hand on it and it is warm, faintly, the way a sleeping animal is warm. Put an ear to it and you can hear it working: a tick and trickle of sap under pressure, and underneath that, everywhere, a dry rustling that is not the plant at all.

The people who come up here call the living surface the skin and the rock beneath it the bone, and the terms are exact. The bone is a plain of stone worn down past all argument — not mountains' bones, but the finished skeleton of mountains, ground level by the patience of water in ages before the water left. Grind your heel through the mat's edge where a herd has torn it and you find rottenstone, rock so old and leached you can push a thumb into it, gravel that has forgotten it was ever cliff. Erosion here has essentially concluded its business. Nothing is being worn down anymore; everything has already arrived. Only three whalebacks of harder stone still shrug above the surface — the ribs — bare, wind-scoured, warm in the sun, and it is on the ribs that everything social happens: the birds display there, the cairns stand there, the people camp there. The mat is the floor, the ribs are the furniture, the sky is the whole ceiling, and the ceiling is enormous.

Because that is the other thing: nothing here is tall. No tree has ever managed it; no structure, no tower, no living thing breaks the line of the cushions except the ribs and the birds standing on them. A walker can be seen from a day's walk away. The air is thin and holds almost nothing — a few raptors at an unbelievable height, circling; the odd cloud; otherwise it is empty in a way that feels less like openness than like vacancy. The place is nearly all floor and ceiling, with vast rooms of nothing between the furniture. Distances lie here; the thin air pulls far things closer, so you learn not to trust your eyes and to trust instead the hours it takes to arrive anywhere.

And yet the plain is deafening. This is the paradox the place is built on. From standing height it looks empty; kneel down, part the cushion's felted surface, and it is the opposite of empty. The mat is a tenement. Its interior is riddled with galleries like a sponge's — ants in nations, beetles by the fistful, caterpillars that prune the cushions with shearing jaws, scale-insects farmed in rows for their honeydew, springtails beyond any counting. A double handful of this plant holds more animals than a netful of seawater. The fauna doesn't stand on the plain; the fauna *is* much of the plain, swarming through the vegetable architecture the way blood moves through skin. The overwhelming majority of the life here has never once been visible from above.

The sound is them. All day and all night, but swelling at dusk into something enormous, the stridulation rises off the mat — not like crickets, though it is crickets, among ten thousand other instruments. It is a layered, sibilant roar, a sound with texture, like the plain is boiling gently, and because the air is so empty and carries sound so far it does not fade so much as continue. It reaches you from cushions an hour's walk away. By full dark it is loud enough that you stop hearing it as sound and start registering it as weather, as pressure, a thing your ribs receive. The harvesters don't shout over it; they whistle, long falling phrases, a whole language pitched to cut through the roar, and you can hear one whistler calling to another across distances where a person is a dot. When the roar stops, it stops everything. A raptor's shadow crossing the mat produces a moving hole of silence a hundred paces wide — the insects go still where the dark passes — so that on clear days you can watch silence travel across the plain like a cloud's shadow, ripples of hush converging on nothing. Local silence is an event with a shape and a cause. The locals read it the way sailors read water.

The water problem explains everything else. No river has run here in an age beyond reckoning; no stream, no spring worth the name. The plain sits above the weather — from the rim of it, on certain days, you can look *down* at clouds working the flanks of the world below, a slow grey sea against which this high stone country is an island. Water arrives from the sky, directly, by the driest possible means. At dawn the whole surface is slick with dew, and worse — better — the plain visibly *exhales*. For an hour after sunrise every cushion breathes out the moisture it hoarded overnight, and a fog shoulder-high covers tens of kilometers of land at once, smelling green and mineral, and you walk through it sightless, soaked, hearing the roar all around you with nothing to see, until the sun burns the breath off by mid-morning and the world snaps back to hard-edged clarity. In the afternoons, some days, the clouds climb high enough to *graze* the plain — drape over it for an hour, wet everything, and slide off. That is the irrigation. A true rain maybe a handful of days a year, and when it comes the mat drinks every drop in minutes; nothing runs off, which is why there are no channels, no gullies, no young erosion — the sponge takes it all. The only standing water lives in stone basins the old grinding left: tarns, shallow and tannin-dark and mirror-flat, dead still at dawn, ringing with dragonflies, the only places by day where the insects of the mat are absent and quiet.

The warmth surprises people. The air is thin and the nights bite, but the days are hot on the skin — the sun at this height is a physical weight — and the mat itself holds heat like a stove bank, so that sleeping directly on the skin of the world, as the harvesters do, is warmer than sleeping on stone. Frost is rare. It is this warmth, more than anything, that lets the vegetation be total.

The built marks are few and low. Cairn-lines on the ribs, marking paths that only matter when the dawn fog erases the world. Low harvest camps of dry stone, roofed with cut peat-cubes, occupied one season in three. Graves — cairns too, some already half-swallowed by the mat, grown over so gently that the dead here become slight green mounds, indistinguishable in a century from the ground that took them. And one wall: knee-high, jointed without mortar, running dead straight for two hundred paces across a rib and then stopping, its purpose gone, its stones furred with lichen older than any language spoken nearby. The harvesters cut peat-cubes from the mat's young margins for slow-burning fuel and medicine, stack them to dry on the ribs — fire will not live on the damp mat itself, only on bone — and hold to a single strict law: never cut an ancient cushion. The old ones are not plants to them but landforms, some older than their people's memory of arriving below. Felling one, they say, is felling a mountain, and it would not grow back within the lifetime of a nation. Their tools are stone scrapes still greasy with cushion-wax, cordage of plant fiber, and — rarely, hoarded — a single hammered-copper blade traded up from somewhere far beneath the cloud-sea.

The cycles run on several clocks at once. The day-clock: dew, breath-fog, hard clarity, cloud-graze, the dusk swell of the roar, cold clear night. The long clock: every few years comes the tide, when a locust-superorganism hatches across the margins and crosses the plain as a brown weather-front with a roar of its own, deeper and worse, stripping the young growth in a moving province, leaving the raptors gorged and the mat scarred green-raw — scars the cushions close over in the following years the way skin does. And the slowest clock lives under the old cushions themselves: peat, layered like pages, holding a thousand years of pollen and ash — thin dark bands where, long ago, something burned, or something was burned, and the mat grew over it and kept the record.

At night, lying on the skin, you feel the place for what it is: one continuous organism the size of a small country, breathing under you, warm, riddled with tenants, singing. The stars come down hard and close through the thin empty air. The roar runs all night without pausing for breath. Water never pools here; fire won't take; nothing rots quickly and nothing stands tall; everything is patient. The bone is finished. The skin goes on.
