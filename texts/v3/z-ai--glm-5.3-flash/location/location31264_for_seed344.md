# Knockhole

The plain gives nothing away. A day's walk from anywhere, it lies barely above the level of a distant salt lake, flat dark slabs of a young basalt field running to the horizon, stone so new that the elders of the far settlements still remembered it as fire. Nothing marks the place from outside — no crack in the ground you'd notice, no color, no steam staining the sky. You find it by ear. Long before you reach it there is a hiss, wide and steady, like the sea compressed into a single held note. And beneath the hiss, every twelve seconds, patient as a clock nobody wound, a knock.

Follow it to a stretch of slab that looks exactly like every other stretch of slab, and stand at its edge, and the ground simply stops being ground. The rim is flush with the plain — you could walk past within arm's reach and never know. Below is a hollow room of stone, a hundred and fifty paces across, sunk ten meters into the field. The way in is a slot on the east side where two slabs leaned apart in the collapse, with steps cut into the fresh rock by hands long gone and a line of white survey chips leading down. Three steps and the air changes: warmth, weight of moisture, the smell of hot stone and wet iron, like breathing the inside of a kettle.

The collapse is recent. That's the first thing the walls tell you. The basalt broke along clean conchoidal faces, and it hasn't yet begun to forget being broken — the curves still gleam, the edges still cut like knapped flint. No soil anywhere. The floor is a grit of glassy weathering, and beneath the grit, everywhere, is water. Press a palm to any patch of ground and it comes up damp. The whole hollow sits on a boiler: heat rising from young rock, an old water table lying close beneath the surface, the two of them married in the dark.

The hollow breathes. That is its central fact and its first strangeness. The vapor moves on a tide of about five and a half hours — at full breath the vents roar and a white cloud mounts the walls, cools, and condenses in beads; at slack the cloud thins, the pools drop a visible handspan, and the great hiss narrows until you can pick out single drips. Between breaths nothing rushes. The sky above is a plain bright square, ordinary daylight, honest and unmystical; for two hours around noon a shaft of sun drops in and makes the vapor luminous, and the rest of the day the light comes diffused down the walls, finding rust and white stone and the small vivid islands of life below.

The rock weeps. On the cooler upper faces, condensation beads form in ordered rows and run in rivulets, gathering into drip-channels that are themselves new — cut by years, not centuries, and still sharp-lipped. Where the hot air strikes the freshest fracture faces, the stone flakes and peels. Erosion here is young and hot and it does the opposite of what erosion usually does: it sharpens. The oldest weathering on the walls has produced edges, not smoothness, and the wettest faces shine black as obsidian. Touch nothing without looking.

The water lies scattered with a patience of spacing that feels deliberate. Eleven pools, no two close together — the emptiness between them is most of the map. The largest is ten paces across; the smallest would fit in two cupped hands. Each is rimmed in young terrace stone, white as unglazed porcelain, lips thin and scalloped, growing perhaps a fingernail's thickness in a human lifetime. And each obeys the same rule, which is the second strangeness: the surfaces are perfectly still. Glass-flat, mirror-still, while beneath them columns of heat rise through the clear margins like slow smoke. The water boils in secret. It convulses down deep and keeps its face composed, and you can stand at a rim and watch the heat ascend without a single ripple betraying it.

Where the pools overflow, life begins — the only life. Thin bacterial mats follow the outflow channels: rust-orange at the hottest lips, jade green in the middle runs, a charcoal skin at the coolest seep, where a single colony of pale reeds, knee-high and bowed, stands as the only true plant in the hollow. The mats are patchy, small islands of color with bare stone between them, and they are patient the way everything here is patient — advancing a finger-width a year, retreating in a bad season, building the terraces jointly with the mineral, stone and film growing each other. But nothing rides the mats. No insects. No birds come down to drink, and none ever have; the heat and the dissolved chemistry see to that. The food web of this place has no animals in it at all — just water, heat, and stone, eating each other slowly. The silence has no wings in it, and the loudness fills the absence instead.

The loudness has a maker, or had one. Pipes of iron rise out of three of the vent mouths, so thickly crusted with mineral that they look grown rather than made — pale, lumpy, like bones surfacing. They feed a condenser: a bank of copper coils on a rusted frame, sweating distilled water one drop at a time into a covered cistern. From the cistern runs the engine — a single-room boiler house of riveted iron, its roof long open to the sky, a pressure gauge on the wall whose needle never leaves the low quarter of its dial, because the fire that drives it is the ground itself. The piston — the Knocker, for the sound — lifts a mouthful of that sweet water every twelve seconds into a plugged pipe that exits through the west wall. Where it goes, whom it serves, no one on the plain can say. The builders are gone. Their works outlasted its purpose and keeps its schedule anyway, running on graphite and patience, expected to seize someday the way its sister engine already has — that one seized decades ago and is being slowly mineralized into a boulder. Elsewhere, at wide intervals, the scattered relics: a spare valve wheel resting on a slab like a giant's coin; a handcart reduced to a ribcage of rust; and on a lintel, an enamel sign — CONDENSERY No. 3 — which quietly implies a No. 1 and No. 2, hidden somewhere under this same featureless stone.

All of it sits inside the sound. The hiss is not one noise but the texture of the whole place, and it has grain: at the vents, a roar with grit in it; against the condenser coils, a silk hiss; along the weeping walls, the tick-and-run of a thousand drips; the Knocker's knock, wood-and-iron and muffled, felt in the soles of the feet as much as heard. Under everything runs the hollow's own bass note, a held hum, the room of stone resonating like a struck bottle that never stops ringing. And at one spot on the north wall there is the third strangeness: every drip from the far side arrives at your ear twice — the original, then its twin a half-second later, exact. The pocket repeats things once, as if learning them.

Nothing here hurries except steam's first instant, the flash of birth that immediately joins the slow cloud. The terraces grow in fingernail-thicknesses per lifetime. The collapse walls will take millennia to round, and the flaking that sharpens them now is measured in flakes, not tons. The breath-tide drifts by minutes across decades. Even the machinery ages slowly — the Knocker will knock for many more years, its interval lengthening by a fraction of a second at a time, and one day the interval will simply not end. And through all of it runs the water's law, which is the law of the place: nothing leaves. Everything that rises condenses and comes back down the weeping walls.
