# The Foundry Sea

There is a seamount here that is younger than it has any right to be — a sharp, unweathered spire of black basalt rising through cold water, its flanks still wearing the knife-edges of whatever eruption birthed it. The water around it is only a few degrees above freezing, and it carries a metallic tang, as if the sea itself had been drawn through pipes and returned.

But no one built the pipes. Nothing here was built. That is the first rule of the Foundry Sea: everything mechanical about it grew.

Along the spire's terraces, at a depth where the light comes down as a pale, wavering noon, the "machines" cluster dense as barnacles. They are not plants, not quite animals either in the way we'd recognize — they are glandular engines, soft-shelled turbines the size of fists and the size of chapel bells, packed shoulder to shoulder, each one breathing the cold water through it and exhaling warmth. The whole reef runs at an operating temperature. Press your palm (if you could) to the rock and it would feel like the flank of a working locomotive: the only warm thing in a cold ocean.

The sound is constant and enormous for so small a place. This whole system is barely a few hundred meters across — a single column, a bounded territory you could swim around in an hour — and yet it roars. Clicking valve-creatures, the deep bass throb of the great central boiler-sponges, the whine of filament-worms spinning coppery thread from dissolved minerals. It echoes off the seamount's fresh-cut walls and comes back doubled. At night — such night as reaches here — the entire reef glows with exhaust-light, a thousand small furnaces flickering blue and orange in the dark water.

The fauna loves it. Cold-blooded everything, drawn to warmth the way our world's creatures are drawn to light. Fish with silver heat-scales school in the thermals rising off the machinery-reef. Squid-like things perch on the warmest valves and splay their mantles like wings to absorb. Pale crabs groom the intake mouths of the larger engines, eating the mineral scum — and in return the engines tolerate them, opening their sphincter-doors a little wider. It is symbiosis, but it reads more like labor. Everything here works. Nothing here rests. Even the abundant life is, in some sense, staff.

The strangeness is in the metabolism. The reef's machines process the water itself — stripping metals, fixing the sea's rare warmth, condensing something from the cold — and no one can say toward what end. There is no harvest, no output, no product leaving the system. The Foundry runs, has run since its eruption only centuries ago (the basalt is unvarnished, the coral of the ordinary world has barely begun its slow claim), and it runs *for itself*. Perhaps the fauna is the output. Perhaps the warmth is.

Change here is glacial in pace, ironically, for so loud and busy a place. The machines grow a millimeter a season. New valves bud where old ones clog, but the great layout of the reef — the terraces, the central stack, the intake canyons — shifts across millennia, not years. If you returned in a hundred years you would find the same roar, the same flicker, the same crabs at the same mouths, only everything very slightly larger, very slightly more.

Boundaries matter here. Beyond the seamount's halo of warmth, the cold resumes like a held breath, the fauna thins to nothing, and the black rock gives way to ordinary, patient, cold sea floor. The Foundry Sea is a room. It is loud in the way a small room full of running machines is loud, and warm in the way a hand near a lamp is warm, and it sits alone in the enormous cold, running, running, with no one to switch it off.
