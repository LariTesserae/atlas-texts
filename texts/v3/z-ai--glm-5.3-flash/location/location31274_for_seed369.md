# The Wet Crown

You see it first from the dry country, and the dry country is all around it for a day's walk in every direction: a single rounded mountain, small enough that you can hold the whole of it in one glance, standing alone on a plain the color of old straw. It is not dramatic. Nothing about it juts or frowns. It has the shape of a thing that has been rained on for a very long time — shoulders worn down, slopes softened, every edge rounded the way a bar of soap rounds in a dish. But it wears a wreath of mist that never quite dissolves, and along its middle there runs a hem of green so crisp it looks stitched: below that line the world is pale and dry, and above it everything darkens into wet.

The drovers call it the Grandmother. The maps, if there were maps, would say the Wet Crown. Both names are true.

You cross the boundary without ceremony. There is no cliff, no gate — just a contour where the air changes. One step your lips are dry; the next the air has weight and warmth and a faint sweet smell, like the inside of a flour jar kept in a warm kitchen. The mist is not cold. That is the first strangeness, and travelers always pause on it: you are climbing, the air should thin and chill, and instead it thickens and warms, as if you were walking into a held breath. By the time you reach the hem of green your clothes are furred with droplets, your hair has gone limp, and the rock under your hand is damp and faintly, inexplicably warm — warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

Above the wet line is the country itself, and it is small. You could walk its whole circumference in an afternoon, and in fact there is a path that does exactly this, worn into the moss by hooves or feet or something else, circling the summit like a ring around a finger. Sound carries across all of it. There is no place here where you cannot hear water.

The stone is the ground floor of everything. It is old and patient and full of holes — not broken holes but worn ones, long channels polished smooth by millennia of finding, hollows and flues and pipes where softer seams dissolved away while harder skin stayed. The faces of the rock weep. Water does not gush anywhere on the mountain; it *exudes*, in sheets too thin to fall, in beads that gather along a mossy lip and let go one at a time, in seeps that spread across a slab like breath on glass. Everywhere the rock is sweating. Basins worn into the ledges hold pools with no inlet and no outlet, brimming, overflowing at one low point, and the overflow finds the next pool, and the next, a chain of small soft descents, so that the mountain drains itself in a thousand tiny staircases rather than one loud one.

And the green comes in islands. This is the second thing that surprises people: they expect a wet warm height to be jungle, a suffocation of leaves, and instead the lushness is scattered, deliberate, spaced. Around each seep and spring grows a garden — a dense dark knot of ferns and mosses and a flowering something that drinks straight from the weep-line — and around each garden stands a grove, and in each grove the trees are few. Four trees. Seven trees. Tall ones, broad ones, standing apart the way chairs stand in a big room, each with its own circle of moss and its own drip-pattern worn into the stone beneath it. Between the islands: bare wet rock, grey and blushing pink where a pink crust of something grows, and mist, and space. The emptiness here is not absence. It is a material, as real as the stone — the mountain is a small room furnished sparsely and well, and the mist is the light that fills the room.

The mist breathes. That is the daily cycle, and once you notice it you cannot stop noticing it. All day the summit exhales — a slow thickening of fog that rolls off the warm hollows in the afternoon and wraps the groves. In the coldest hour before dawn, the mountain inhales: the mist draws back down the flues and channels, the air clears astonishingly, stars show through, the stone shines wet under ordinary moonlight. Then the sun comes up over the dry plain, plain and honest, and the day's exhalation begins again. The light itself does nothing strange. It is the mountain doing something strange, in plain light, where anyone can watch.

The sound is the water finding its voice. All those worn pipes and flues in the rock — the seepwater threads through them, and where it does, the stone *sings*. Low flute-notes, uneven, wandering: one hollow a fifth above another, a pair of channels sounding a minor drone when the seep swells. The dripping provides the percussion, a million small liquid ticks on leaf and stone, so that even the quiet is a slow rain that never lands hard. And there is one great pipe on the western face, a chimney of hollow stone as tall as two men, that fills over some hours and then sounds — a single deep note, round and enormous, that rolls across the whole summit country and out over the dry plain below. The lowlands set their afternoons by it. *The Grandmother cleared her throat,* they say, when the note comes.

The animals are few, and that is its own kind of strangeness: all this water, all this warmth, and almost nothing moves. At dawn the butterflies come in quiet crowds to the weep-lines and stand on the wet stone drinking, wings closed, hundreds of them silent as cloves. A small grey bird lives somewhere in the groves and has learned, over uncountable generations, to answer the singing pipes — it matches their notes, or the pipes taught it, no one can say which — so that some mornings there is a call-and-response between bird and mountain that no one composed. In the brimming pools live pale translucent things, salamander-like, that you see perhaps once in three days of looking. And there is the rumor of one large browser, a single old animal, whose worn path rings the summit — the path you are probably walking on. No one has seen it in years. The path stays fresh.

Once or twice a year, someone comes. Never more than one at a time, always from the lowlands, always carrying almost nothing: a reed knife, a coil of twine, a lump of wax, a pouch of pebbles. They stay some days. They clear silted channels with their hands. They wedge small stones into the mouths of certain pipes to change their pitch — tune them, the way you would tune anything — and seal the wedges with wax. They weave temporary screens of reed at a couple of the springs, filters that will rot by next season and are meant to. And then they leave, and here is the rule of the place, and it is strictly kept: *nothing built remains.* No shelter stands on the Wet Crown. No wall, no post, no cairn older than a season. The only artifacts the mountain permits are the kind that fade — wedges that will work loose, wax that will wash, whistles of reed left on a ledge to split and green over. Whoever these visitors are, they have understood the mountain's one law: hands may work here, but hands may not keep.

The deeper strangeness is slower than all of this, slow enough that only the old lowlanders swear to it: the springs are climbing. Over a lifetime, seeps close at the lower edge of the summit country and open higher; the gardens migrate uphill behind them, generationally — trees dying at the downhill margin while their seeds take root one terrace higher, so that if you could watch a century as a day you would see the green slowly climbing, like water creeping up a towel. The water runs warm from every crown-spring. The mountain breathes at dawn. It fills its one great pipe and clears its throat. And nothing here is ever urgent: even the storms arrive as fog, even the falls are weeps, even the mountainside's changes are measured in grandmothers. It is a high place that has settled into its own shape and will keep it, weeping gently from the crown, for longer than the dry countries around it will remember being dry.

You can leave in an afternoon. Everyone does. Your clothes dry within a day of crossing back below the wet line — unless you let them drink first, which some travelers do, without being able to say why. Behind you, across all that straw-colored distance, the small green crown goes on: dripping, humming, slowly wringing itself out, entirely unbothered by having been seen.
