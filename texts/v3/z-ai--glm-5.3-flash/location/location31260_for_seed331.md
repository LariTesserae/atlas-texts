The land ends gently here.

The hills that ring the basin have spent themselves — whatever they were, they have been rained on past memory, and now they are simply the shapes rain makes when it is finally finished. They are whale-backed, green to the crown, and low; from the rim you can see the whole of the place, every tree, every pool, the far edge no more than a morning's walk away. The basin is a saucer of world, small enough to hold in one long look, and it sits so low that the water never truly leaves it. It only changes rooms.

Because the ground here is wet the way a sponge is wet. Dig anywhere — a hand's depth, two — and the hole fills before you finish. The rim-hills weep constantly: slow springs that never cut channels, just darken the stone and feed a constant drip down faces of moss. The water table breathes. Over hours the whole basin inhales and exhales, the sheet-water advancing and retreating across the flats by the length of a footprint, a tide with no sea behind it, patient as something on strike eleven of a twelve-hour clock.

The light is the surprise. You expect a wet place to be dim, but the sky here is a bright white-gold haze, sun diffused into glow, and the sheet-water throws it all back. At noon the flats turn to hammered tin. Shadows are pale, shallow suggestions; everything is lit from every direction at once, the way things are lit in the moment after lightning, except that the moment doesn't end. Warm air keeps condensing and lifting; distant trees swim slightly, as if the whole basin were behind glass that someone's breath keeps finding.

The green comes in individuals, not crowds.

Scattered across the flats stand perhaps forty-one great stilt-trees — someone has counted, and the count holds — each one rising on root-butts like the ribs of a drowned cathedral, holding a canopy of deep wet green high overhead. Each tree keeps its own moat of open water at its feet, its own cleared circle, its own congregation of animals. Between the trees: space. Wide, bright, deliberate space — floating lawns of rootfelt pale enough to walk on if you dared, trembling underfoot; reed islets with their own little weather of insects; long reaches of nothing but sky laid on mud. The emptiness is not absence. It is kept, the way a garden's lawn is kept. Every plant here is whole. No torn leaves, no blight, no broken branch — lushness without struggle, growth that has never once had a bad year.

And everywhere, silently, the animals.

This is the basin's true nature, and its one law. Nothing here has a voice it can afford. Over a depth of time the mind declines to measure, everything that lived loudly was found by the things that listen, and so the whole ecosystem converged on silence the way rivers converge on the sea. The herons — white as paper, wider than a person's arms — hunt by standing still for hours and give no cry even when they strike. The glide-flies sail in perfect straight lines on wings that cannot buzz. The grazer-herds, grey-green and lump-backed, big as carts, move through the shallows on soft cushion-feet and speak only by touch, trunk to flank, flank to trunk. Frogs display on the mud with throats inflated like lanterns, venting nothing. Every ear in the basin is enormous; every voice is small or gone. The water is threaded with still fish that hang like shadows and reveal themselves only in the act of taking.

So the overwhelming life of the place is a fullness without volume. Every pool holds someone. Every mudflat is dense with tracks, including tracks of things you never see cross. Motion flickers constantly at the edge of vision — a wing, a ripple, a long neck unfolding — and none of it makes a sound. Predation happens here every hour, quietly, the way it happens everywhere, except that here death also keeps the rule. A heron takes a fish in absolute silence. A still fish takes a bird the same way.

Once a day, something in the reeds remembers it had a throat.

The call is perfect — the sound of a fledgling fallen from a nest, or a fruit dropped into water — and it is the only voice the basin owns. Everything has learned it. Everything still flinches. The silence changes texture when it comes, the way a room changes when a stranger speaks, and a few of the always-curious always go, and the water closes, and the hush reseals. The caller is never seen. It is the basin's single loud lie, told sparingly, because even a lie works only if the truth of silence stays credible.

The built things are few and patient, and so old that age has nothing left to grip.

Nine stelae stand out in the shallows, dark and smooth and rounded at every edge, taller than a person, so old that the mud has climbed them to the knee. They are machines — you understand this the moment you touch one, because it is warm, blood-warm, and because it pulses, once a minute, faint as a heartbeat under a wrist. Scratches close on their surfaces like water closing over a thrown stone. Swallows — lintel-birds, the local kind — nest in the seams of their crowns, and the stelae permit it, or don't notice, which may be the same thing. One stela keeps a ledger: thin lines of pale light across its face, marks that shift rarely, a digit changing while you watch when a heron dies or a grazer is born somewhere out on the flats. The basin keeps its books. Nobody has read them.

A drowned causeway of flat stones links some of the stelae and leads nowhere — or leads underwater and keeps going, its last visible steps descending into green murk with the confidence of a path that knows where it's going. Half-sunk in the eastern flats lies an arc of some greater structure, curved like a rib off an impossible whale, its surface the same self-healing dark as the stelae. It serves now as a reef. Its whole length is tenanted — mussels with pearl-bright shells, nesting holes, the territory disputes of small silent crabs conducted in display and bluff. Once a day, or once a season, something the size of a leaf detaches from the arc and crosses the flats low and slow on no visible wings, pausing over wounded ground, and the ground is better afterward, and the animals ignore it completely, the way you ignore your own heartbeat.

The weather keeps cycles like everything else. Warm veils of rain walk across the basin — you can watch one coming, a grey curtain a half-mile off — and when it arrives it falls in fat slow drops that hiss on the sheet-water like breath, and the animals attend. They don't shelter. They stand in it and drink, heads tipped back, every species at once, a congregation at its brief loud church. Then the veil passes, the sun returns doubled, everything steams, and small rainbows stand in the mist between the trees like furniture no one remarks on.

The strangeness is in small deflections, never announced. A heron lifts off and its reflection follows a heartbeat late, as if the water is old and has its own tempo. Near noon, light arrives very slightly from where the sun is not. Distant things stand closer to you than their size allows. And the numbers hold: forty-one trees, nine stelae, the herons in their dozens, the grazers in their tens — populations that pulse and sway and always come home to the same figures, an equilibrium so deep it has stopped being ecology and become physics.

At dusk the gold goes long across the tin, the stelae warm your hands one last degree, the drip from leaf to leaf becomes the whole world's percussion, and somewhere the caller makes its one perfect sound and the basin flinches and forgives.

Then the hush reseals, the rain-warm air thickens toward another bright morning, and you understand what this place is. It is not a ruin and not a wilderness. It is a finished thing — rained on past memory, kept in its final form by servants too patient to notice, lived in by everything that ever learned to be quiet.

The basin keeps its numbers. The water keeps its sky. Nothing here is unfinished, and everything is alive.
