# The Scaffold Fields

There is no ground here in the old sense. You come up, and up — the ascent takes a full day through cloud-condensate — and at the top the world turns out to be **built**: a high plateau-structure of living wood and woven vascular tissue, miles across, raised on buttresses of green timber that dive down into mist. Nothing here rests on stone. Everything rests on everything else, grown into place.

The place is young. You can tell because the architecture is still sharp — edges, ridges, unweathered ridgelines of bark-glass and resin — and because it is still *being decided*. Whole districts of the scaffold heave and rearrange themselves between one hour and the next. A gallery that held a walkway at dawn is by noon a tangle of new shoots sealing itself over; elsewhere a wall splits open along a fresh seam and exhales warm, rain-smelling air. The turbulence is not disaster. It is the metabolism of a place that has not finished becoming itself.

Water is the master of the plateau. It weeps from every surface — the "weeping rocks" here are weeping *trees*, great hydraulic columns whose bark streams condensate day and night. Rain is nearly constant but soft, more a state of the air than an event: the atmosphere is so saturated that moisture beads out of it on any surface cooler than skin. Beneath the built levels, in the dark interstitial voids between buttresses, the water table sits just under the wooden floor, and you can hear it — a deep, tidal sloshing, as if the whole structure floats on its own reservoirs.

Vegetation is not a covering here; it is the substance. There is no soil, no mineral substrate exposed anywhere. The plants have made a world *of* themselves: bark for stone, woven roots for cable and beam, leaves in layered canopies where roofs would be. Pale fungal-leather membranes span the open spans, stretched between living masts, and they sweat in the heat. In the hottest hours the whole plateau steams faintly, a thousand small evaporations rising off leaf-roof and wet timber.

And yet — it is **sparse**. The structures are immense, but between them are vast open courts of nothing: empty terraces of bare polished wood, long plains of planking where nothing grows and nothing stands, gaps of air hundreds of feet wide between towers. The built-vegetable world is scattered islands in an ocean of platform and sky. You can walk for an hour across empty decking, hearing only water drip and the groan of distant growth, before reaching the next living district.

There are no animals. Not one. No birds on the masts, no insects in the flowers, no fish in the cisterns. The silence of the empty places is enormous — and then broken, because the place itself is *loud*. Not mechanically: biologically. The sound of growth here is audible — creaks, pops, the wet ripping of new tissue, long groaning chords as buttresses flex in the wind, the hiss of sap under pressure. Some of the great hollow columns act as resonators; wind through them produces tones that drift across the plateau, low flutelike notes that rise and fall with the weather. It is a landscape that talks to itself constantly.

The heat is tropical and constant, tempered only by the water — every surface is cool to the touch where condensate films it, and the shade under the leaf-roofs is dense and dripping. Light is ordinary daylight, grey-gold, diffused through the moist air; at midday the steam-haze turns the whole plateau into a bright, blurred room.

No tools exist here. Nothing is cut, fastened, or measured — the architecture is all growth, all graft, all self-assembly. Whoever or whatever built it, they built it the way a body builds a skeleton: from inside, by living. Whether the builders still exist is unclear. Some of the empty courts show signs of *intended* emptiness — smooth polished decking, exactly circular, kept clear by the plants themselves, like a room left ready for someone.

**The rules of the world, as far as they can be read:**

- Nothing is wasted, nothing dies visibly; dead tissue is immediately overgrown and absorbed into the structure.
- Change is constant and unbothered. No part of the plateau resists its own rearrangement.
- Water always finds the surface. Dig anywhere — if you dared — and you'd hit the reservoir within arm's reach.
- The place seems to *prefer* certain shapes: spirals, branching fans, hexagonal courts. Growth follows them like grammar.
- And it listens. Walk across an empty terrace and the decking hums faintly under your feet — not alarm, just notice. The scaffold knows where the weight is.

It is a strange, hot, roaring-green ruin-in-progress: a cathedral still being grown, empty of anyone, full of itself, floating on its own weather.
