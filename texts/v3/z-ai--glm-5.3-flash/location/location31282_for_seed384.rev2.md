You come into Slake by the flooded gallery, swimming a hundred yards of black tunnel with your lamp unlit, and the first thing the water tells you is that the place is small. That is the shock of it, after the tunnel: you expected an ocean, and instead you rise into a room. A room of water, walled in bedrock, floored in silt, ceiled in ice. You could throw a stone across the whole lake and touch the far shore, if stones were thrown here and if the sky — which is the lake's ceiling — did not stop them first. The basin is the lowest ground for forty leagues. Every drop of melt for forty leagues ends up here eventually, and the basin, being patient, keeps it all.

The ice overhead is smoked-glass dark, thick as a street is long. Frost has feathered its underside into white gardens — ferns, flowers, a whole winter planted upside down — and along the rock walls thin threads of meltwater find cracks and come down through the dark in slow, weightless lines. It rains here, in a manner of speaking. A rain that takes a minute to fall and lands without a sound.

The water is a fraction above freezing and it has body. It carries a perpetual slow snow — rust, silt, dust of old timber — that never quite settles, because the lake breathes and the breathing stirs it. You feel the cold as an event: it takes the warmth from your face through the suit, it makes the lamp's metal collar cling, it slows your thoughts down to something geological. Sound behaves strangely at this temperature. It arrives late and arrives small, like a memory of noise rather than noise itself. Mostly there is nothing. Mostly you hear your own blood, and the tick of your regulator, and — soon — something else. A count. You will learn the count.

Slake sits on the basin floor like a thing set down carefully. It is a real town — it has a square, lanes, numbered houses, a church tower with its bell still hanging in it, mute — and it is a small one, two hundred steps corner to corner, everything within reach of everything, built close the way people build when warmth must be shared. The houses stand shoulder to shoulder, chimneys near-crossing, pipes laced from wall to wall in black lacework, gangways strung between upper storeys like rigging. The water presses all of it gently and holds all of it up, and here is the strangest mercy of the place: nothing falls anymore. Rust finished decades ago — there is no oxygen left in this cold to feed it — and the wood is pickled hard as horn, and the silt lies in settled drifts like snow after a storm that ended a hundred years ago. Slake has eroded to its final shape. Whatever it is now, it will be that for a long time.

Look through the streets — water lanes, really — and you will see that some houses are lit. This is the town's whole light, because no sun has ever entered here: the Lamp House with one window glowing orange behind wet glass, the smithy where a banked fire breathes a faint red through its panes, a lit window on a house numbered nine. The lamp in Number Nine does not burn steady. It brightens and dims on a long, slow rhythm, four counts out, four counts in, like the breathing of someone asleep. Watch it long enough and you will find your own breathing adjusting to it. The water does not want you to forget that something here is alive, or was, or intends to be again.

The industry is not dead either — this is the town's engine, its heart, its reason. At the low end stands the pump house, stout as a keep, and the machines inside it run on a count you feel in your sternum before you hear it: a slow hydraulic pulse, patient as a pulse under a wrist. Seepwater gathers in the old head-frame's cistern, drops through the wheel — the Mill, the drowned hamlet's mill — the wheel turns the dynamo, the dynamo runs the pumps, the pumps push the water up the Throat and out through a standpipe that discharges in a steady black column down the far wall. It should have stopped. Every accounting says it should have stopped years ago; the loop loses more than it keeps, and yet it keeps. You can stand in the dark and count the possible powers — the trickle of warmth in the deep rock, the weight of the ice, something in the mine's cold ore — and the place will answer with none of them. The rails of the narrow-gauge line that runs from the square into the mine mouth are polished bright. Something still runs on them, between the fourth count and the fifth.

Life here is scarce and seems to know it. A felt of dark algae lies on every surface, thin as foxed paper — the lake's whole meadow. In the spill of the Lamp House window grow the only green things: ribbons of water-moss, a green so saturated it hurts against all that black. By the warm seam where the Throat discharges, sponges have grown up like organ pipes, pale and improbable. In the lamp beams the amphipods hang like thrown pepper, sculling backward, thousands of them in one cubic meter and nowhere else. A white fish — blind, or indifferent — hovers at Number Nine's window with its nose to the glass, keeping an appointment. On the floor of the drowned garden you can still read the rows: pickets, stalks gone to white skeletons, and by one door a willow, white as bone, still standing. Once, out on the Long Walk, you see a shape moving — a figure, you would say, carrying something, walking the gangway at the change of the count — and then the doorway of the smithy closes behind it, and you are left with the fact that seals do not live under glaciers and no one has come in or out by the gallery since the bell stopped.

The town keeps its schedules with no one, or with one. At each change of the count, one lit window goes dark and another, elsewhere, wakes — a shift change of lamps moving through the streets like a held lantern. The lake level rises a hand's width each day with the melt, and settles each night, the ice exhaling above you. Once a year the whole lake turns over like a sleeper rolling in bed, and the slow snow is redistributed, and the town wakes up wearing a slightly different coat. And once a year, for a single week, the ice overhead thins enough that a grey suggestion of noon comes down — a ghost of light, no stronger than lamplight — and everything in the basin turns toward it. The fish rise. The moss leans. The lamps are turned down, and here is the part you will carry out with you: someone turns them down. The town observes noon in the dark. It has never missed.

Understand what this place is, finally. It is not drowned. The water is not the disaster here; the water is the weather. Slake was built to hold its air the way a ship holds it — by stubbornness and good joinery — and the pumps were built to keep the galleries below dry, and the galleries are still dry, and the mine is still, in the strict sense, operating, for miners who are gone or who are the shape on the Long Walk; no one living remembers what the ore was cut for. Everything in this basin sinks and stays. Nothing finishes. Time here runs at the pace of timber, and the erosion has already happened, and what remains is the form the town will keep.

So you do the only right thing. You switch off your lamp. The town's own light is enough — four windows, one banked fire, a seam of blue-green cold-fire, the long breath of Number Nine. You hang in the water between the frozen sky and the patient streets, and you listen to almost nothing: a drip inside somebody's pipe, the count in your ribs, your blood. The almost-nothing is enough. It is exactly enough to measure the size of everything.
