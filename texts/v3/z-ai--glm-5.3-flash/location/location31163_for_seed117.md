# The Weep

You come to it across a country of bare wet stone — no turf, no rush, no moss, nothing green in living memory — where puddles sit in the hollows like hammered tin and never dry, and where the ground, if you press your hand into it, weeps upward around your fingers within a minute. The land slopes down by degrees, so gently you barely feel the descent, and then the rim is under your feet and the whole place is below you at once.

It is small. A strong arm could throw a stone across it, and someone has: near the center of the bowl the glass still carries a shallow scar where the stone landed, long ago, long enough that the edges have gone smooth. The sky above is a coin of milky cloud. Light falls into the bowl the way water finds a drain — from everywhere, in a dim gray evenness that makes noon and dusk nearly the same hour. Shadows are suggestions here. Color lives lower down, in small cold points.

The floor of the bowl is volcanic glass, and it has been glass for longer than anything here can remember. Erosion finished its work before the machines came, and has kept a gentle custody ever since. The glass is fluted into runnels and shallow channels, worn silky at every edge, dished where water has dripped from the same point for centuries. Nothing is new. Nothing is ruined, either. The place does not look abandoned so much as settled — a thing that arrived at its final shape and stayed.

Because everything weeps. That is the weather here: condensation. The air is so close to saturation that the walls sweat in visible beads, the stones blush dark with wet, and if you wipe your brow the damp returns before you've counted ten. Twice a day the mist tide comes — a slow inundation of fog that pours over the rim like something poured, fills the bowl to the depth of a person's chest, hangs there breathing, and drains away again into the stone. The water is never far. It is not even far from *up*. It stands in the runnels, it glosses the walls, it hangs in the air as a fine permanent drizzle that falls so slowly it seems to be considering it.

Nothing here has ever grown a leaf. What grows instead is film: a thin iridescent skin over the wet glass, part mineral deposit, part something alive — too organized to be chemistry, too unformed to be garden. After each mist tide the film blooms into faint patterns, branching lines that run uncannily straight for a finger's width before going organic again, and the grazers come for it before it fades.

The machines came at some point, long enough ago that they no longer read as intruders. Three condenser towers stand in the middle distance of the bowl, slender riveted iron skeletons, their fins bearded with beads of water. A pump house crouches beside them with a flywheel that turns once a minute, patiently, like a large animal chewing. Scattered around: a stair that climbs ten steps to nowhere, its treads dished by feet that stopped climbing long ago; a porcelain plaque with lettering that belongs to no alphabet anyone uses; a white-painted curb, the paint renewed by no one, still somehow white. The machinery is not dead. The whole system breathes. Every twenty minutes it fills, holds, and exhales — a long hydraulic sigh you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it — and the water moves through old pipes and copper veins, combing the mist out of the air and delivering it to the runnels, which carry it in slow spirals around the bowl and back.

What keeps the machinery alive is the strangest bargain in the place, and the fauna are party to it.

Because the fauna is abundant. There is no unclaimed surface anywhere. At every scale the bowl is occupied: film underfoot, and on the film the lickers — soft velvet things, a foot long, moving in unhurried queues along the runnels with mineral file-teeth, eating the skin of the world. On their leavings, pickers — small many-legged groomers — work the condenser fins, eating the scale that would otherwise choke the towers, so the machines stay clear and the pickers stay fed. At knee height drift the comb-jellies, living funnels that sift water directly from the air, tiny condensers of meat. At head height, the balloons: drifting iridescent sacs that rise with the mist tide and sink with it, a slow vertical traffic of bodies, twice a day, like weather made of animals. In the old tanks, clouds of larvae fruit and scatter. When you walk, you must negotiate, as one does entering a crowded hall — the basin rearranges itself around you, closes behind you, offended by nothing.

The color in this dimness is mostly signal. In the generator house, along the one warm seam in the floor's stone — the single faint breath of deeper heat anywhere — sleeps the sleeper: an eel-length of pale muscle coiled in the old housing, feeding on the warm seam's trickle, and discharging, idly, a thin current into the copper. Not much. Enough. Enough to keep the accumulators above their floor, to keep the flywheel's heart beating. The whole basin reads its pulse — a slow blue breathe, breathe, breathe — the way coastal towns read a lighthouse. And around that pulse, the little glimmerers have learned the machines' language: worms and finger-fish along every rail and pipe that blink in the exact cadence of the old indicator lamps, green-white, patient, until it becomes genuinely difficult to say where the machinery ends and the animals begin. Once, watching, you may see a real lamp flicker in answer to a glimmerer, a half-beat late, and you will decide not to have seen it.

The loud thing, when it moves, is a stilt. A heron-thing of glass-colored jointed limbs that stands among the runnels with the stillness of equipment, and is not equipment. It can wait through two mist tides without shifting its weight. Then the neck goes, faster than the eye agrees to, and one licker is gone from the queue, and the queue closes over the gap without ceremony and keeps filing. Nothing pauses. In a place this dense, grief would be a traffic problem.

The quiet, then, is not emptiness — it is the price of company. Loud things do not last here; every sound is spent carefully. So the bowl's soundscape is an inventory of small spends: the tick of condensation finding its appointed drips, the metal purr of the pump's exhale, the lickers' fingertip drumming on glass, the papery shifting of balloons, and under everything the sleeper's hum, which you feel in your teeth more than hear. The bowl returns every sound half a beat late, softened. Sometimes a drip comes back twice — the echo, and then a third drip that no stone accounts for. The animals do not react to the third drip. That is somehow the strangest part.

The basin has three laws, and everything in it keeps them. *Nothing dries* — no surface escapes the weeping, no puddle surrenders to the air, and the fog is the closest thing the sky ever offers to an opinion. *Nothing is loud* — the loud have been edited out over long ages, leaving a world that speaks in drips, pulses, and colored light. And *everything is tended* — the pickers tend the towers, the sleeper tends the current, the machinery tends the water, the water tends the film, the film feeds the queues, and the queues feed the stilt, who by some accounting none of them understand also tends the machines, because without it the lickers would multiply past the film's patience and everything would stumble. No one tends the tenders. It has worked so far. It has worked, by any measure you can bring to this little drowned bowl, a hundred thousand times.
