# Undernoon

## Descent

Descend any of the stair-shafts and you expect the dark. You get noon instead.

The Vault lies just under the world — a chamber-system miles across, its roof in places thin enough that you can feel the desert's heat-hunger pressing down through the stone. The desert above it is old beyond sense: a plain worn flat by wind that has been wind for longer than anything has been anything. And beneath that finished surface, impossibly, this: a cavern of white stone lit wall to wall like an overcast noon, bright enough to ache at, bright with no sun in it anywhere.

The light is alive. That is the second thing.

## The Sheen

It is not a plant. The people who live here are very clear about that, and they would know, because they farm it. It grows on stone the way frost grows on a window — a moisture-fed film, pale gold-white, that brightens where the stone is damp and dims where it is dry. They call it the sheen. It needs nothing but mineral and moisture, and the Vault supplies both, so the sheen's tenders do less farming than herding: they channel damp toward chosen walls and away from others, and the light flows where the water flows. Whole districts are lit by the mood of their own moisture. A hall that has been sealed will go dim and sleep; reopen the drip-lines and it warms back to brightness over weeks, like a face remembering how to smile.

The sheen gives light and no heat. Everything here is cellar-cool, evenly, endlessly — a coolness you stop noticing until you touch a wall and find it bead-slick and cold, sweating lightly, all the time.

## The Breath

Twice a cycle, the Vault breathes.

No one knows what moves it — some slow negotiation between the deep cool and the desert's thin pressures, a tide of air the size of a region, turning over. But on the exhale, the whole cavern sighs at once: a wind that is felt in the chest more than heard, and with it comes the damp. Every surface beads. Threads of water gather and run — down wall-channels cut so long ago they are worn to glass-smoothness, into drip-gutters, into the dew-ponds, shallow white basins that hold a hand-span of water and are guarded like temples.

There is no rain here. There is only this: a timed, continental condensation, the Vault exhaling moisture it has inhaled from somewhere no one has gone. The people keep their hours by it. *Third weep* means the north galleries are dripping. *After the exhale* is their morning. Children learn time the way surface children learn the sun: by which walls are wet.

And the stone remembers every spill. Water never quite dries here — it sinks into the pale rock and stays as a darkening. Old districts are maplike with stains: the ghost-shapes of ancient jug-slips, centuries of clumsy hands, a dark continent where a cistern failed eight hundred years ago. You can read the city's accidents in its floor.

## The Flocks

The moths own the air.

They are white, soft, silent, sized from hand-small to hawk-large, and they move in flocks that behave less like birds than like weather — slow fronts of pale wings drifting across the Vault from one glow-field to the next, grazing the sheen off the walls in long migrating arcs. Where a flock has passed, the stone is clean and dim; where they settle, brightness thickens. Their keepers — there are keepers — walk beneath the flocks with spreading nets and patient hand-signs, steering the weather of light from field to field the way surface folk move sheep between pastures.

They give three things, the flocks. They spread the sheen, carrying its dust to new walls. They shed, constantly — a fine white down that settles into the channels and grates, which the gatherers rake and press into a thick grey-white felt the whole city is lined with. And they are beautiful, though beauty is a word the locals would not think to use.

Because the felt is the reason for everything else. It is the silence.

## The City That Wears Stone

The city is called Undernoon, and it is enormous and mostly empty.

Its halls could hold ten times the people who live in them. Whole wings stand sealed and dim, kept dark on purpose, their drip-lines closed, their sheen asleep — an inheritance far larger than its heirs. The population is a thin skin on a vast body: a porter crossing the central plain with dew-jugs, alone; a felt-press works with two workers where forty could stand; long colonnades where the only motion is the light moving as a distant flock grazes past the mouth of the gallery. The stone was finished by hands no one remembers. The present people maintain it, re-face it, live in its margins — and extend it, at exactly one place, at the rim, by hand, slowly, forever.

The technology of Undernoon is bone, hide, fiber, sand, and a little soft copper. But every tool has been shaped across unmeasured generations toward a single quality: it must not ring. They do not strike stone here — they *wear* it. Bow-drills loaded with wet sand, rasps of tooth-row bone, copper scrapers, levers and sledges and ramps. A city carved entirely by abrasion and patience, because a struck chisel would echo, and there is nothing to absorb an echo in a place like this except what they make.

So the felt goes up wall by wall, and the hush is total. The Vault does not echo. A shout dies within a body's length, muffled to a puff. Visitors from the surface — the rare ones, the ones who come down the stair-shafts out of the ancient desert — tend to panic in it. The loudest sound in Undernoon, on most days, in most halls, is your own heart. The locals read silence as health, the way we read clean air. A hall that has acquired an echo is a hall whose felt has failed. Crews come running, silent, with pressed panels on their backs.

They speak to each other, past a certain depth, entirely in hands. It is not piety, exactly, and it is not law, exactly — it is craft so old it became instinct, the way a spine is instinct. Their hands talk constantly, fluently, all day: a whole city of conversation happening in silence, in the bright halls, between people carrying water under their arms with the carefulness of a sacrament.

## The Rim

And yet the place is not calm. Watch it for an hour and you understand that Undernoon is in a state of permanent, furious industry — a bazaar's worth of motion, an anthill's worth of energy, conducted entirely without sound.

At the rim of the Vault, the city is being born right now. A hundred crews work within sight of each other on the new galleries: grinding the living rock with sand and water, drilling with bone, hauling spoil in hide sledges, levering new blocks into walls whose faces will take years of rasping before they are smooth enough to take the sheen. Moth-flocks cross above them like slow thunderheads. Porters thread between the works. The dew-channels run and glint. Dust hangs in the light and turns. Everything moves. Nothing sounds. It is like watching a storm through glass, or a market held at the bottom of a lake.

The Vault itself, for its part, is finished. Its stone is worn past all argument — smoothed by time itself before anyone arrived, primordial, settled into its final form an age before the first stair-shaft was cut. The geology here ended long ago. All the turbulence left in the world has been inherited by the small things moving over it: the flocks, the crews, the water-threads, the Breath turning over twice a cycle like something asleep that still dreams of the sea.

## The Skywell

One opening remains, of all the shafts that once reached the surface. It is a round hole in a high gallery, and through it falls a single column of true daylight — hard, yellow, desert day — that lands on the white floor like a dropped coin.

Stand in it and look up. The sky through the well is glare and nothing else, but on windy days you can see the dust-devils cross the opening: spinning columns of the ancient plain, whirling past, one after another, in the silence that a mile of stone makes of a storm. The people gather there in ones and twos. It is the only place in Undernoon where a sound from above ever arrives — the faintest breath of that desert wind — and it is the only place where the city's own hush feels, briefly, like something chosen rather than absolute.

Below, the felt waits. The flocks graze. The walls weep on schedule, the stains on the floor keep their old maps, and a thousand hands converse without a whisper, building the city's newest inch in a place that has otherwise been finished since before quiet was invented.
