Two things are absent here — water and stone — and one thing has taken the place of both.

You arrive by shaft, as everything arrives here. Above, on the surface, there is a field of mirrors the size of a county: heliostats on gimbal frames, tracking the sun in slow unison, pouring day down a chain of polished relays into the earth. The shaft you descend through is lined with their light. It comes down in clean white tiers, dimming slightly at each reflection, and the walls of the shaft are wood — smooth, pale, already callused over the bolt-holes the first drillers left. The air dries as you drop. By the third tier your lips are chapped. By the fifth, the inside of your nose feels like paper.

At the bottom is the drylock, and the drylock is the honest threshold of this place. You stand in it while machinery wrings the moisture from you — scrubbers pulling your breath down to a fog-thin residue, your clothes brushed and baked, the water surrendered from your body's surface measured and recorded, because water is the contaminant here, the pathogen, the one foreign substance this world cannot afford. In a place with no rain, no dew, no groundwater, nothing rots. Old wood does not decay; it sinters, hardens, slowly turns mineral-dry and keeps. The only agent of decay is wetness. A single spill is an incident. A drop carried in on a boot sole can seed a soft brown ruin that spreads for years, and so the city treats moisture the way our cities treat fire — with locks, protocols, and a quiet institutional dread.

Beyond the drylock, the city.

It sits inside the Stock — that is the word they use, horticultural and affectionate: the Stock, the living root-mass of a plant lineage that has been propagating underground, graft by graft, for longer than anyone has kept records. This is not a forest with a city in it. It is closer to the reverse, or past either — the tissue is the geology. The floor underfoot gives faintly, resilient as a ship's deck, and grain runs visibly through it in long rivers of butterscotch and straw. Where the wood has aged, it has gone dark and dense as mahogany; where it is new, it is tallow-white and soft-edged. Raised seams cross the floor like half-buried piping — the sap-channels, carrying oleosap instead of water, thick as cold honey, amber visible through their thin bark skins. Press one with a thumb and it dimples, then slowly, slowly recovers.

Everything smells resinous. Pencil shavings, warm varnish, a sweetness like beeswax with no bees. Nothing smells of mold or humus because nothing here has ever been wet.

The coolness is not weather; it is policy. Down here, temperature is infrastructure. The whole district is held cellar-steady, sixty-some degrees of dry chill, because heat thins the resin and this world is entirely fuel — wood, sap, oil, fiber, all of it burns. Fire is the apocalypse of this place the way flood is the apocalypse of others. So the city is zoned against itself: districts ringed with slatewood, a cultivated variant that mineralizes its own tissue into grey, inert, ash-colored boards. Slatewood streets are the firebreaks. You can always tell where the industrial quarters end by the color change, the wood going dull and stony underfoot, and the lamplighters will not carry open flame past a slatewood seam on any account.

The city itself was not built. It was induced.

The grafters speak of their work the way farmers speak of crops, months and seasons ahead of the wood. A building begins as a frame of laths and string, tied to the living tissue in the shape of the thing wanted — a wall, a hall, a stair-tower — and the Stock obliges. It grows around the laths, swallows them, and in a decade or two there is a building with no joint in it anywhere, its walls continuous with its floors, its roof continuous with the ground it stands on. Doors are the strangest part: cut an opening in advance and the wood gathers itself around the void, thickening the edges into a self-making lip, a seal. The grafters say the wood anticipates. They say a room described aloud, with its dimensions spoken clearly to the timber, will settle truer than one kept secret. Whether this is folklore or physics, every grafter talks to the wood, and the wood's growth rings do seem to lean toward the openings people wanted.

The streets are healed graft-lines — long straight seams where two trunks were joined and callused over, now polished to a warm gloss by feet and cartwheels. Intersections are burls, sanded round. And it is sparse. That is the first thing anyone notices after the strangeness of the material: the emptiness. The district is valley-sized — dozens of great galleries, vaulted halls of wood whose ceilings are lost in lamp-shadow — and the population scatters across it thinly, the way orchards dot a plain. Whole halls stand half-finished or deliberately vacant, held in reserve for growth still to come. In the big empty chambers you can stand alone with a single lamp at the far end, a fist of warm light in a cathedral of pale grain, and hear your own pulse in your ears.

That is one of only two sounds. The other is the city's machinery, and it arrives as felt more than heard.

The industrial quarter is called the Weft, and it hums behind bark walls at a distance half a mile off as a slow, deep thudding, like a heartbeat someone forgot to silence. The stills run day and night, cracking crude resin into fractions — lamp-oil, sealants, solvent, the viscous fuel that feeds the engines. Nothing here is mined out of the ground; the ground *is* the feedstock. Screw-presses laminate heartwood shavings into beams dense as iron — pile-wood, shipped back up the shafts to the surface works. The copper for the dynamos comes out of the ore-hosts: galleries where cultivated tissue has loaded itself with metal, pruned and rendered like any other crop. Even the pipework is botanical — drill a mandrel-channel, let the tree callus around it, withdraw the mandrel, and you have a seamless tube the wood made to order. Everything is grown, pressed, distilled, or wired. The fans are the grandest engines: the Breathers, ventilation galleries where wheels wide as threshing floors turn one slow revolution a minute, moving a tide of air you feel as pressure on your eardrums and never hear at all. Wood is the world's acoustic felt. A dropped tool makes a sound like a cough in a church.

Fauna is abundant here, and adapted entirely to the resin economy. Sap-moths drift through the lamp-lit galleries in grey-white fountains, unfolding probosces longer than their bodies to drink at the sapwells — the raw seep-points where tappers cut the channels open. Gall-wasps hunt them, hanging under the lamp-housings in ragged, patient clusters, dropping on the flocks in slow whirling dives that rise and fall with the light cycles like weather. In the comb-racks of the Weft, scale-insects are farmed in dark thousands, distilling terpenes far sweeter than anything the raw wood gives — the closest thing this world has to livestock. And out at the frontier graze the husk-oxen: armored, slow, half-tamed things that eat senescent tissue and break new ground as they go. The city prizes them. A husk-ox trail through virgin wood is half the surveying done.

Time here is run by the mirrors and the sap.

The mirror-day above tracks the sun, and the district's light swells and softens with the sun's arc, filtered through a mile of rock and polished glass. When cloud crosses the surface, the whole underworld dims in gentle pulses — this is the local weather, the only weather anyone remembers: *mirror-weather*, flicker and fold, read on the ceilings. At dusk the heliostats fold their shutters and the lamp-banks take over, banks of resin-fed filament glowing amber down every gallery, and the moth-flocks shift from the sun-lines to the lamps in one great murmuring resettlement. Once a day — the hours are kept by the listeners, not clocks — comes the sap-tide: the entire root-world draws its oleosap upward toward the surface in a slow subsonic surge. You do not hear it. You feel it in the floor, a lift beneath the grain, a sound below sound. The moths drink en masse. The stills throttle back because the crude runs richer at the tide. Even the lamplighters rest.

The listeners themselves are worth the pause. In alcoves along the main seams, they work with ears pressed to the wood — steel gouges shaped like horns fitted against beams and trunks, reading the city the way a physician reads a chest. They hear the presses' rhythm from three districts away, the fans' long breath, the tick of new tissue laying down fiber in the walls overnight. They are the ones who caught the wet-rot in the East Cell before it reached the third seam, and they are the ones who say the wood has moods, that the Stock in a new district is skittish, quick-grained, harder to predict. Which brings the last thing, the thing that defines this place more than the light or the resin or the rules about water:

Here, nothing is finished.

The district is called the New Cut, and it is embryonic in a way the older rings of the root-world no longer are. Elsewhere — deeper, older — the past is literally written in the material: growth rings as strata, each year a page, so that a grafter cutting into an old wall will stop and read the seasons the way we read sediment. Flood-years, lean-years, the year of the great fire, all laid down in wood. The New Cut has no rings yet. Its tissue is juvenile, callus-heavy, still deciding what it will become — pink scar-seams everywhere, string-lines and lath-scaffolds standing where in a century there will be finished vaults, whole empty galleries waiting on a growth that hasn't been shaped yet. History hasn't grown into it. The city here is a sentence being written in a language made of rings, and the grafter-workshops are full of people who know, in the calm way people know these things, that their grandchildren will cut into these walls someday and find the lamp-scars and graft-lines and spoken dimensions of this decade laid down like a diary — and read it.

Until then, the lamps burn amber on their resin ration, the fans turn their slow mile-wide revolutions, the moth-flocks rise and settle with the light, and once a day the whole vast wooden dark lifts slightly on the tide — a young world, dry as paper and warm-lit, building itself cell by cell in near-perfect quiet.
