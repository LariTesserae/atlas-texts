**Condensate Station K-7**

You reach it by a bore. The steel casing at the mouth has rusted to lace, and the ladder inside has gone the same way, but the rungs hold if you trust them one at a time. Forty meters down the air changes. It comes up the shaft ahead of you — warm, still, carrying iron and hot stone and something green and sweet underneath, like a greenhouse that has learned to breathe. By the last rungs you are sweating.

The bore opens into a room. That is the honest word for it: a room, not a cavern. A hundred paces across at most, ceiling low enough in most places to touch with a raised hand — though you learn quickly not to raise your hand, because everything down here is warm and some things are hot. The far wall is close enough that a thrown stone would answer with a knock. This is a space you could know completely, given time. Given light. Light is the difficulty.

In darkness the room is absolute. Then, as the eye fails and gives up, other details surface instead: small cold points of blue-green, scattered across the floor's hollows like a constellation laid flat. Puddles, almost too thin to be called puddles — films of water, a coin's width deep — and they glow. The vapor that rises from below carries a trace of something luminous, whether phosphor from a breached coolant loop or mineral born in the deep rock; the station's paperwork no longer says. Whatever it is, the water carries it, and so the only light in this room is made of water. Each drip a cold bead. Each puddle a spilled sky. Enough, at tide's peak, to see the nearest shapes.

The heat explains everything else. The floor is fissured, and from the fissures comes a slow exhalation — vapor at a temperature that stings the hand held over it, warm enough that the first meter of air above the stone shimmers. The intrusion below is young. Sixty, seventy years ago the hill swelled, cracked, and exhaled once, violently, inflating this hollow under itself like a held breath. You can read the age in the stone. Nothing here is rounded. Every collapse block lies where it fell with sharp edges still, like furniture dropped yesterday, its fracture faces furred with the first bloom of oxidation — rust-orange, bottle-green. Dust lies in drifts at exactly the angle it fell and has not moved since. The room is brand new, on the timescale of caves. Its first erosion has barely begun.

The tide runs on hours, not minutes. The fissures exhale; vapor climbs, since the floor is the hot face and the ceiling the cool one; the ceiling sweats, and the cables sweat, and the great copper coils sweat, beading, gathering, swelling until each drop lets go. Then a long stillness while the rock recharges. You learn to hear the room breathe. It is the only respiration here.

Because there are no animals. It should be said plainly: none. No bats in the bore, no insects in the glow, no larvae in the pools, no midges in the sweet warm air. Whatever niches exist have found no tenants. Even decay is absent — a frond that dies does not rot, because there is almost nothing to rot it. It dries where it falls, blackens, and mineralizes, becoming stone the slow way. Death in this room is only a change of material.

The green has arranged itself along the driplines. Where condensate lands — beneath the old condenser coils, along ghost-paths of pipe, at junctions where machinery once turned vapor into harvest — life has thickened into islands. A few paces across, each one, but within its bounds it is lush past all reason: ferns black as wet slate with veins running the same cold blue-green as the pools; moss in charcoal velvet, sopping, dripping; vine tendrils wound around conduit and cable in patient spirals, roots gone into the transformer housing, the drip trays, the drum of the abandoned winch. Tier on tier, overlapping, dripping, each island a small frantic abundance — and between the islands, nothing. Bare black stone. Heat shimmer. Dark. The green did not spread; it settled, precisely, where the machines taught the air to yield water. The station's schematic survives now only as a trellis.

None of these species are known from the surface. Either they escaped the crew's test racks in the site's brief working years, or they arrived as spores on clothing and were remade by the dark — thirty years is little, but pressure, heat, and glow are much. The ferns unfurl a frond a month. The vines move at the speed of weeks; watch one for an hour and you will see nothing, return in a season and find it has crossed a conduit and claimed the next tray. The flowers are pale, narrow bells that never open fully. There is no one to advertise to. They fertilize themselves in the bud and drop in silence.

The machines are still on. That is the strangest inheritance. The station was small — a pump shed, a crown of copper coils bolted at the ceiling's high point, cable runs bolted along the walls with cracked insulation and bare wire, a thermoelectric stack drinking the gradient between hot floor and cool ceiling. The gradient has not failed, because the intrusion below has not failed, and so the stack still trickles out its watts, and the transformer still holds its charge, and its pilot light still burns: one red ember, blinking every few seconds, timed to nothing, the only strictly artificial light for forty meters in any direction. Gauges hang fogged forever, needles at rest. Sample jars sit crystallized in their racks. On a crate, stenciled: SITE K-7 — CONDENSATE EXTRACTION, and beneath it a company name the paint gave up before the paint did. A mug on a railing. A duty roster sealed under resin, legible, obeyed by no one.

The engine needs no keeper. The heat drives the engine; the heat will last centuries; the paperwork of ownership has simply lapsed while the physics continues. The plants have noticed the transformer the way surface life notices a sun. They have wrapped it — a green boll around the warm housing, roots in its drip tray, tendrils climbing its cooling fins — so that the machine is becoming a hearth, and the hearth a shrine, and no one is present to draw the distinction.

The sound of the place is a short list. The tick of steel expanding at the turn of the tide, each flake of stress answered somewhere by another. A drip, irregular: every twenty seconds, then forty, then ninety, each one a small struck bell, each lit faintly on its fall. A hum below hearing, felt in the teeth rather than the ear. Your own pulse, which you will apologize for. No rustle. No scutter. The silence is not empty; it is full of the room's slow work, and merely indifferent to you.

Watch long enough — the tide will give you three or four chances a day — and once, only once, the way such things happen, you may see it: a bead of glow traveling down a dripline faster than any drip, knot to knot to knot, gone before the mind can file it. The puddles, at any rate, brighten and dim with the breath of the floor, as if the light were inhaling too. The safety placard by the ladder says not to drink the water. It says it in three languages. The plants have never learned to read.

At four of the drippoints, the room has already begun its longest sentence: thin towers of pale carbonate, the height of a hand, sharp-edged as poured wax — the first stalagmites these stones will ever build, decades old at most, growing a grain a year, reaching patient appointments with the ceiling above. Give the room ten thousand years and it will look like caves do in old engravings. Right now it looks like a first morning. In a few days, somewhere, a flake of fresh fracture will let go of the wall and ring out, the youngest erosion beginning its own slow account.

The heat will thin, on a schedule of centuries. The stack will fall below its threshold one quiet season and the red eye will close for good. The green will ration itself, island by island, until only the ferns remain, and then only spores, sleeping in the mineral dark until the next intrusion under the next hill. The towers will keep growing whether anyone watches or not, because nothing here has ever required a witness.

Stand still between drips, in the middle of the room, and you can feel it all as one motion: heat rising under your palms' memory, cool film gathering on your scalp, the faint sweetness in your mouth. A clock with everything as a hand. The floor exhales. Somewhere above, in the dark, the ceiling begins, again, to sweat.
