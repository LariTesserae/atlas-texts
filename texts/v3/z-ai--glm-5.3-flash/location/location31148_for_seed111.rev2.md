First, the sea. It is the only ocean this world has, and it is not made of water in any way that matters — it is a sea of glowing fog, pale as light under a door, stretching without shores to every horizon. Nothing else breaks its surface. Nothing else stands above it. It has no coastlines, no islands, no floor that anyone has found. It simply shines there in the dark, slowly, the way a thing digests.

Except in one place. In one place the sea has a shore, and the shore goes up.

A single needle of stone rises out of the glow, and it rises absurdly far — so far that from its crown the sea below looks solid, a lit floor under the black, and the black above is a lid with no stars in it, or none that admit to being stars. The air up there is thin and cool and honest; it holds warmth badly and sound worse, which is why almost nothing on the needle speaks into the air. It would take a strong climber an hour to walk around the summit. It would take an afternoon to fall off it. That is the whole geography of this world: one small column, one hour of circumference, one afternoon of consequences, standing in the middle of an ocean of light.

The stone itself is young. You can tell by the way it rings. Strike it and it answers with a clean bell-tone instead of the dull knock of old rock, because it has not yet learned to be quiet. Nothing on it is rounded. Its edges are the edges of knapped flint; its flutes are sharp enough to cut a careless palm; the scree cones at its base are still cone-perfect, not yet slumped into the soft shapes that age gives to rubble. In the cool of the dark hours the stone ticks — small thermal cracks adjusting, a sound like knuckles in another room — and in several places it is warm, veins of residual heat from whatever violence pushed it up, breathing through knife-lipped vents that smell of hot flint and wet iron. There is no soil anywhere. Nothing here has had time to become dirt.

And the needle is still rising. A finger's breadth a season, no more. It does not rise the way a wave rises. It rises the way a sentence that hasn't found its verb yet continues.

Moisture comes the way everything comes here: from below, and rarely. The count of days is not kept in hours but in drips — the knappers count them, tap them out on the stone, a rhythm meaning *soon* — and when the count completes, the sea stands up. That is the only way to say it. The fog lifts off its own surface and climbs the needle in slow sheets, wrapping the flutes, licking the warm veins with a long hiss, and the whole world brightens, because down here light and water are the same substance. The tide is the dawn. It takes most of a day to climb, unhurried, and while it climbs the riddled faces of the rock begin to breathe — every burrow mouth exhaling in turn, a vast slow wheeze, the stone inhaling fog through a thousand holes.

Everything that lives here lives on that tide.

At the climbing waterline there is a littoral, the world's only coast, and it is one stone wide. Where the fog-lace touches rock it is thick with drifting life — pale motes, eggs, feeders, a snowfall going the wrong way — and along that line the grazers work the flood like muscle-slippered hands, siphoning the fog as it passes, mouths to the current, rows of them packed rim to rim on every ledge the water reaches. The feeding is not gentle. There is not enough coast for gentleness. This is the whole trouble and the whole engine of the place: the needle is the only standing room in the world, and everything that flies, floats, or crawls through the dark must come here eventually, so the crowd has nowhere to spread. Nothing on this column is alone. Even the stone is crowded.

Above the flood line the climbers pour upward — that is the honest verb for it, they pour, hooked toes and suction pads, sheeting themselves over the fresh flutes faster than the tide can wet them — and behind them, at every height, the burrows. The riddled faces are a vertical city, chambers near-touching, layered like shingles, and at every opening a nose, a claw, a whiffling snout. The density is the atmosphere here as much as the cold is. You could not put another life on this rock with a pinch of trowel. The solitude of the place is vertical only.

And there are hands here. Small ones, four to a body, leathery, mostly useless for seeing — the knappers see with their palms pressed to stone. They are the closest thing this world has to people, and they own nothing, which is by law and by tide. No nests. No dams. No cairns that last past a single flood. The Wet unbuilds everything the hands build, so the hands build nothing; instead they make — knapped flakes from the needle's own ringing flint, wedges to open a seep into a drinking channel, cords of braided quill-hair for the long climbs, shell-lanterns holding tide-dropped glow-snails that burn pale green in their horn helmets. Their craft is repetition. After each Wet they re-find their scattered flakes, or re-knap new ones, and the tink of stone on stone is itself their language — because the mountain is a bell, and every height on it has its own note, and to speak here is to ring. Sound is the only light that doesn't come from below. In a world this dark, to hear is to be seen. They drink by drawing a cold flake through the fog-lace and licking the air off the blade. They know the Wet is coming not by watching but by lying flat against the rock, because before the tide moves, the stone hums a base note only feet can read.

The knappers share the crown with the roost, and the roost is the reason the needle is never quiet. Thousands of the great grey mantle-wings hang along the summit rims in shingled rows, folded like a roof of cloaks, and when the tide peaks they turn — all of them, together, a sound like an enormous sail taken by wind — and drop into the fog to sieve it, then rise again heavy and shine-sided. Between turnings they hold their ledges in strict inches. Every perch has an owner; every owner owes drum-tolls to its neighbors, small tapped negotiations you can feel through the rock from fifty feet below. The eggs go into the breath of the stone, laid in vent-warm pockets where the mountain's leftover heat does the sitting.

Once in a great while the sea itself flares — a deep bloom, the whole fog plain burning up bright for an hour, and the dark above turns briefly to noon. The knappers say the glow is a ledger. It is the sea digesting. When the roost turns and the slow ones fall, the light below strengthens for days afterward, and things that drop off the needle are paid back in light. It does return what it takes. After every high Wet the ledges wear a litter of the sea's remainder — pale husks, strange shells, once a knapped flake that came back barnacled with luminous studs, smoothed into something almost like a bead. The laws here are few, and they are drummed rather than said: *up is safety; down is payment; the Wet is the clock; nothing is kept; what the sea takes, the sea returns, changed.*

Then the ebb, which is its own long patience. The sea lies back down over most of a night, and the brightness drains with it, and the dripping begins — the wettest, chattiest hour, every flute and seep and burrow-mouth ticking its inventory of what the tide left. Pools stand in the summit's worn pans, each one a small lit eye looking up into blackness that reflects nothing back. The dewmoss goes crisp as paper. The beards of lichen stiffen. The fog-grazers seal themselves into the cracks. And the noise does not stop so much as lower its voice: drip-chatter, wing-shuffle, the tick of cooling stone, and the knappers' drums going message by message up and down the column — *tide down, seeps open, the flake at the third flute is mine, answer* — until even that thins to the mountain's own faint ringing, and the breathing of the riddled stone, and far below the slow lamp of the sea, digesting the night.

Quiet here is only quiet for the dead.

And under everything, the rise goes on. A finger's breadth a season. The lichen bands on the flutes mark it like rings, old tide-lines stranded ever higher, and every species on the column climbs a little in every generation just to stay where it was — the roost laying its eggs a hand higher, the knappers re-cutting their seeps a hand-span higher, the burrows riddling upward. Nothing that lives here is finished. The needle is not a thing that rose; it is a thing that is rising, young and sharp and patient, ticking in the dark like something newly made, with the whole crowded loud lit-from-below world of it clinging to its flanks — and no one on it, from the deepest burrow to the highest folded wing, knows yet whether the sea will climb to meet the crown, or fall away forever beneath it, leaving the mountain alone at last in a darkness with nothing left to glow.
