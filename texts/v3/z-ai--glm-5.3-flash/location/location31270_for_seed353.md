There is no dawn here, so you stop waiting for one. The Shelf rises a mile above the Cold Field, and the Cold Field is a country of dust with nothing in it — no riverbeds, no salt pans where a sea once was, no memory of water at all, just a hard gray plain that wind has been smoothing since before anyone was around to be smoothed along with it. The sky above is a black lid with a few nail-holes of stars. And across the whole high plateau, rim to rim, lies a glow: dull red, dense, gridded, like a bed of coals the size of a county.

The people who might have named it called it the Work. No one calls it anything now, because there is no one. That is the first thing to understand about the place: nothing in it breathes.

You come up the old ramp at night, which is to say you come at any time. The rim district is dead — has been dead so long that death here has become a kind of geology. The trees that once lined the outer avenue drank minerals as they cooled, and are now brown stone with their rings intact; wind has rounded every edge, dished every stair. Ceramic bark-tiles lie fallen like roof slates. Here, and only here, your breath shows in front of your face. It is the only humidity you will encounter in the entire Work. All the water in this world is what you carry in. The rim sweats a turpentine-dew on cold iron — old exhaust condensing, amber beads on rust — and that oily slick is the closest thing to rain the plateau has ever seen. Nothing drinks it. Nothing drinks anything. The Work drinks heat.

Cross the rim and the warmth comes up through your boots before you notice it in the air. Then the smell arrives — resin, hot oil, smoke that has been sweetened — and then the first light, which is not sunlight and never was. Vein-glass pipes run along the ceiling of the entry gallery, glowing a dull orange where the sap runs hottest, spaced far apart, and between them is true dark. You learn to navigate by the glow-map: orange lines are the sap-mains, deep red seams mean you are near a Heart, a white flicker somewhere off in the black is a grinding wheel eating metal. The lamps of this place are its circulation. When the sap cools, the light goes out of it, which is why the light always stays.

Nothing here is wild, and nothing here is ruined. Those are the two facts that take the longest to sit together. Every column grew around an iron spine the builders left for it; every floor is root-laminate, polished by ten thousand years of traffic; every pipe is vein-wood carrying hot oil where water ought to go; every machine is grown into its frame. There is a lathe two galleries in whose bed is ironwood, and whose ways never wear out, because the tree that is the lathe feeds new grain into the cut surface as fast as the tool removes it. The factory heals. The soil, if you can call it that, is made ground — fired clay gravel laid down deliberately, the only substrate on the Shelf that was never alive. Not one plant on the plateau descended from a windblown seed. Every living thing here is inventory. Wilderness was abolished on the Shelf before it was ever invented anywhere else, and the Work has kept the discipline for an uncountable span.

The industry of the place is real industry — boilers, forges, looms, presses — just grown rather than assembled. The Hearts are the beginning of everything: deep shafts where gas drawn from the black wells under the plateau burns in a slow, enormous respiration you feel in your sternum before you hear it, twelve beats to the minute, like something enormous sleeping. Their exhaust runs through lung-galleries, vast pleated chambers of smoke-eating foliage that strip the carbon from it, and the air that comes out the far side is sweet and thick and entirely breathable — the Work breathes for you, though you are the only thing on the plateau with any use for breath. Above ground, the surface is plowed into the Vane-Fields: mile on mile of fin-ridges, heat-shedding furrows like plowed land, because heat is the harvest here and the field must not choke on its own crop. Heat is water in this ecology. It is drawn up from the Hearts, carried in sap, spent in muscle and lathe, and radiated away at the margins. Where the heat leaks — at the rim — the plant-machine dies. That is the whole geography of the place: a warm island the size of a small country, cooling outward into its own fossil.
