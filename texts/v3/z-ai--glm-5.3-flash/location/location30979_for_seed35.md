**The Chitin Works**

You descend into it through a maintenance shaft — that's the only way in, and the only way anyone has ever found — and the first thing that tells you this place is *not* like the surface is the sound. It arrives before the light does: a deep, total hum, like a city heard through a wall, like machinery the size of a thought.

The Works are a single chamber, roughly hamlet-sized, and the boundaries are absolute. Smooth mineral walls, pale as old bone, curving overhead — this is not a cave; it was *made*, or extruded, or grown, no one remembers which. The rock shows none of the usual teeth of erosion. Every edge is crisp, every shelf sharp-edged, as if the room was finished last week. In a sense it was. In every sense it is.

There is no water here. Not a drop, not a hint of damp. The chemistry of the Works runs on something else entirely — a slow, dry metabolism of mineral salts and exhaled gases, cycling through the room in invisible loops. Nothing evaporates; nothing condenses. The air is warm, body-warm, faintly sweet, like the inside of a closed instrument.

The vegetation — if that's the word — is sparse and strange: a few dozen stands of glassy, amber stalks growing straight out of the floor's mineral crust, each one identical to its neighbors, none of them taller than a person. They don't sway. There is no wind to sway them, and nothing here moves unless it has a reason.

Because everything else is fauna. The chamber is *crowded*. They hang from the ceiling in layered tiers, cluster along the walls, pack the shelves and ledges nearly touching: soft-bodied, blind, warm-colored things the size of fists and platters, breathing in synchrony. They do not flee you. They do not react to anything. They have reached their final forms and are simply, densely, *here* — a congregation of animals holding perfectly still, humming.

The hum is the industry. Embedded in the walls, half-sunk like fossils, are machines: pumps with no pipes, engines with no fuel, crankshafts turning at constant speed in empty bearings, all of it faintly lit by dim orange indicator-lamps that have burned at exactly the same brightness for as long as anyone has been here to watch. The machinery does work no one can name. It turns; the room breathes; the fauna hum along with it in harmonic lockstep.

Scattered among the machines and animals are small structures — a tin-roofed booth, a single stair to nowhere, a few wall-markings like tally strokes, a workbench with tools laid down mid-task and not one millimeter moved since. Artifacts of a handful of keepers, perhaps, who finished what they came to do. Or didn't need to do anything more.

Nothing here changes. The temperature never shifts, the lamps never flicker, the creatures are never born and never die, the machines never wear. The Works are a solved equation, an equilibrium made physical — dense, warm, loud, and perfectly, permanently still. You can walk through it. You can leave. But it will be exactly here, exactly thus, humming, whenever you come back.
