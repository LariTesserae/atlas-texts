# The Waking

Begin with the stone, because the stone is the news.

Every rock face in this place is younger than the oldest grandmother living under it. Sixty years — no more — since the hill's flank let go and the first of the shafts found air. Nothing here has been rained on. Nothing has been frost-cracked, wind-smoothed, or sun-bleached. The stone has never had a weather. So it still wears its birth colors: rust and ocher and blue-green mineral sheen, wet-looking fracture faces sharp as the day they split, edges so new that running a hand along them is a form of reading — you can feel where the rock made up its mind. The people call the untouched stone *blind*. When a wall is first opened to the light of the shafts, they say it *wakes*. And they say it without ceremony, the way farmers say a field is in. Here the whole landscape is in.

The cavern lies under the brow of a hill like a valley turned upside down and buried shallow — shallow enough that the diggers could ream out nine great shafts from floor to sky, square them with masonry, and keep them open. The daylight that comes down those shafts is real daylight, unhidden. At noon the main floor stands in stripes of full sun and deep cool shade, exactly as a shaded valley would, and the town tells its time by where the columns stand. Clouds cross the shaft-mouths overhead and their shadows sweep the terraces below. When it rains on the hill, you hear it as a far drumming on stone, and sometimes a stray thread of it slips down a shaft and evaporates before it lands.

That is the only rain this world has. The rest of its water arrives more quietly.

The raw stone sweats. Newly exposed rock holds the moisture of its own making, and cold surfaces under moving air weep a fine condensation that gathers in beads and runs. Every wall weeps somewhere. Over the granaries the diggers found a seam that sheds a hundred threads of drip in loose parallel, and they named it the Loom, and built their clay-sealed store rooms under it so the threads fall into catch-basins they can tend. At midday, when the sun heats the air at the shaft-mouths and pushes it down into the cool, the warm humid air meets cold stone and lets go — a brief, fine ceiling-drizzle in the brightest columns, ten minutes long, regular as a bell. People step into it with vessels. Children run through it shrieking, which is the loudest thing that happens here most days. After the drizzle, streamlets wake in the low places: one walks forty paces across the Long Floor, glittering, and then thinks better of it and sinks back into the rock.

Water pools too, on stone shelves in the side galleries — the Pockets, the town calls them — standing water cold as buried things, so clear it reads as empty air until you touch it. In the Pockets live small pale shrimp-things, eyeless, unbothered. They were here before the light was.

For vegetation, think first colonists, not forest. Moss films the drip-lines in green felt — and the moss is the eldest resident of the world below, older than any person's memory of the opening, which tells you how young everything is. Ferns crowd the base of the brightest shafts, pale and fragile, fed on caught water. Lichen spreads across the newest walls in slow rings like ink dropped on paper, and the diggers have learned to read those rings the way you'd read grain in wood — to tell an old working face from a fresh one. In the widest column of light, on a built terrace, there is a garden: beans on string, cress in stone troughs, watered bucket by bucket from the Loom's basins. It is a patch of maybe forty paces, and it is treated like treasure, because it is the only field in the world.

The town fills the cavern the way water fills a cup — completely, to every ledge and lip. This is the striking thing about it, more than the light or the newness: the saturation. Terraces of cut rooms step up both walls of the Long Floor. Stairs and ramps stitch every level. Rope ladders and windlasses hang in all nine shafts, hauling grain, timber, and stone up from below and down from above. Every niche has a use: a shelf of lamp-stones, a rack of drying rope, a granary mouth sealed with clay, a smoke-blackened hearth-alcove vented to the nearest column. Even the ceiling is occupied — hanging racks in the dim high places, and the mud nests of birds in the shaft mouths. There is no idle surface anywhere. There is no room between presences. The town and its works have saturated the space the way water saturated the stone.

The rooms are carved more than built, because here digging and building are the same act. The walls behind the town were, within living memory, solid rock; the town is made of the absence of rock, and the spoil of it went up the shafts to build the hill's new crown. Tools are antler picks and stone mauls, wooden drills, ropes of braided hide. The best technique is the damp itself: holes drilled in a line, wooden wedges driven dry, then wetted — and the wet wood swells through the stone and splits it overnight with a crack you can schedule around. The water that weeps from every wall is, among other things, the town's oldest tool.

Animals are few and clumped, like ore in veins. Life concentrates at the water and the light and avoids the dry galleries entirely. Midges breed above the Pockets. Swifts found the shafts within a few years of the opening — no one knows how — and now they own the columns, pouring down at dusk in screaming spirals to claw nests on the shaft walls. Mice came down with the grain and live in the granary walls, and a fissure no one has widened shelters a colony of bats that pours out at dusk and is gone. That is nearly the whole census. Between drips, nothing moves. A visitor walking the back galleries hears their own blood.

Sound is quiet, and quiet is a technology. The base note is the drip — the Loom's hundred threads, each with its own interval, so the granary has a sound like slow applause. Chisels carry astonishing distances in the cold air, and everyone knows the town by pitch: the high tap-tap of the Fern Galleries, the duller knock of the Dig at the far end. Once or twice a day the stone itself talks — a shelf somewhere lets go with a knuckle-crack, a hiss of falling grit — and the whole town pauses, mid-motion, and listens, and counts. Children are stationed at the shafts as listeners. It is considered an honorable post. Nothing has fallen in sixty years that hurt anyone, but the stone is young, and young stone is still deciding.

Because that is the truth at the bottom of this place: nothing here has finished happening. The Dig at the far end advances every season, opening new blind walls to wake in the columns' light. Behind it, the town itself is the only weather this stone will know for a thousand years — hands polish, soot dulls, moss takes, drip-carved channels deepen a finger-width per generation. The first dulling of color is visible already, a softening of those birth-bright faces, and the people mark it without sadness. Erosion, when it finally comes, will be the second thing ever to touch this place. The first was them.

Cool always, damp at the edges, loud with silence, packed full of itself and still under construction by two hands at once — the slow hand of settling rock and the quick hand of the town — the cavern and the settlement are growing into one another, and neither yet knows its final shape. Everything is first draft. Even the light, falling in its nine columns, seems less like illumination than like introduction.
