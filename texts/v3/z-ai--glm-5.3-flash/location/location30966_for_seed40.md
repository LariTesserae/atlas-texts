# The Stills

They call it that for three reasons, none of them official: it distills, it is quiet, and it barely moves.

Come to the parapet first, at the southern rim of the works, and look down before you look out. Below the last gallery there is nothing made — only the cloud-sea, a cold gray floor of vapor stretching to the edge of sight. The Stills do not rest on ground in any sense you would recognize. They were lifted, or poured, or grown upward from some lower country nobody remembers, and now they ride above the weather instead of inside it. The cloud deck is the region's only wilderness left, and even that is harnessed: it rises and falls on a tide slower than any moon, and once in a long while it swells up through the parapets and floods the lower terraces with mist for a season, then withdraws.

Above you, and above everything, is the other sky: the grid. Lamps hang from gantries so far up they read as constellations, banks and banks of them, and their combined output is what passes for day. The light here is strange in one specific way — it does not warm. It is a cold, high, industrial brightness, glare without heat, and every morning (when the grid comes up through its long cycle) the whole landscape flashes silver, because everything is beaded with dew.

That is the first thing to understand about the water here: the Stills are damp the way a cold bottle is damp. Moisture arrives as film, not flow. The air is dry; the surfaces are not. All night the metal sheds heat into the thin high air and all night the air gives up its water to the metal, and by "dawn" every railing, flue, cable-tray, and blade of sedge carries a skin of droplets that light up like seed pearls. There are gutters — thousands of kilometers of them, pitched in long shallow falls toward the Basins — and they tick, one drop at a time, in ranks. The tick is the closest thing the Stills have to birdsong. Once or twice a year a cooling line vents somewhere overhead and a thin, apologetic rain falls on a single district, a rain so fine it cannot be said to fall so much as to reconsider descending. The old-timer fauna know the sound of the vent coming — a hiss like a held breath — and they gather under the modeled awnings.

Rain, when it happens, accomplishes nothing. This is young country — startlingly young — and yet it will stay young for a length of time that makes a mockery of the word. The ground of the Stills was cast, not born: strata of pressed composite, laid down in pours, and its edges came out of the molds sharp. A raindrop arrives, considers the surface, and is defeated. There is almost no wind to speak of at this altitude, and what wind there is carries too little grit to matter. So the terraces keep their fresh-cut profiles, the drip-noses still hang from the gallery edges the way they did the day the forms were struck, and the whole region has the look of a quarry photographed the same afternoon it was opened. Somewhere far out in the works, geology is being maintained rather than suffered: you can find an edge half-worn and half-restored, old weather meeting new casting at a visible seam, the place's entire history written as a maintenance schedule.

And yet nothing hurries. That is the master rhythm of the Stills, and once you acclimate to it, everything else makes sense.

The industry here is real — enormous, hums below the floor of hearing, and utterly patient. In the Pressfields to the north, machines the size of ridgelines are making land. This is the product of the whole works: ground itself. Strata are pressed and cured and stacked; river-channels are extruded and bolted in, dry and gleaming, waiting decades for the condensate season to commission them; cliff-faces are installed like cladding. A piston the length of a train completes one stroke a year, and when it seats there is a sound — more a sensation — like a door closing in a neighboring valley. Cranes stand over their loads for seasons at a stretch with the patience of herons. On the slow conveyors, finished landscape moves toward installation at the pace of fingernail growth. The works run to a plan, presumably. No one has come to check on it in a very long while.

The silence is what undoes most visitors. An industrial country this size should roar, and the Stills instead sit in a padded, almost underwater hush. The great engines move too slowly and too massively to sing at any pitch an ear can own; their labor arrives as infrasound, a pressure you feel in the teeth and mistake for mood. The cast material itself is dense and dead acoustically — it eats sound — and the fauna contribute almost nothing to the day's audio budget. What you actually hear, on a given hour: the gutter-tick. The collapse of a dewdrop from a cable into a pool. The tick and groan of metal settling as the lamps cycle. And in certain places — the surveyors stake them, plain steel posts with no legend — the sound dies altogether. Step across the stake-line and the world mutes: your own footsteps vanish, a dropped tool lands in perfect silence, and you find yourself breathing carefully, as if noise were a debt being audited.

The vegetation is sparse and knows it. Green here is gossip, not forest. Moss lawns cover the shadesides of the great stacks where the condensate lingers longest — velvet-dark, oddly lush, the single richest habitat in the region. Sedge clusters crowd the gutters, drinking the drip. In the rust pockets, where some iron component has wept orange down a wall, a stringy vine roots directly in the stain and drinks nothing but dew and oxide, and its leaves come out the color of old blood. Everything that grows, grows on the machine's leavings — its damp, its warmth, its corrosion — and everything green orients toward the same thing: the thermal map.

Because that is the true map of the Stills. The air is cool everywhere — thin, high, jacket-weather cold, carrying a smell of ozone and wet iron and, near the fresh pours, hot chalk. Warmth exists only where the machines are: flue-mouths, exhaust galleries, the flanks of working pumps. The fauna have organized their entire civilization around these islands. The mossers — low, broad, capybara-patient grazers — work the moss lawns in herds and have worn actual roads into the terraces, smooth通勤 routes from heatsink to heatsink, each road polished by a thousand generations of hooves. The lickers hunt the condensate itself: small salamander-dark creatures that cling to cold pipe and groom the moisture off it with tongue-strokes like squeegees, working a gutter-line the way a fisherman works a stream, obeying the tide of the dew. In the flue-mouths nest the stack swifts, pale and moth-heavy, riding the warm updrafts all "day" and feeding on the airborne film that blooms in the lamp-heated columns. Above everything, riding the thermals off the hot machinery, the lamp-hawks hang motionless — bright-eyed, patient, cataloguing the movement below.

Predation here is a test of stillness, since everything is. There is a wading thing at the Basins, heron-shaped but wrong in the shoulder, that will stand over a condensate pool for a full day without shifting its weight, waiting for the pool's translucent larvae-strainers to rise. The pools themselves are a marvel: gray, still, mineral-slick, each one a pocket of standing water in an otherwise dry cathedral, and each with its own tiny closed world of filterers and grazers and one patient murderer.

For the fauna are the fastest things in the Stills, and this gives them a strange dignity. Against the machines' tempo, a mosser's lifetime is a flicker; from the works' point of view, the animals are weather. Their generations are the only clock in the region that a person could watch actually move.

The weirdness, when you find it, is found mostly at the margins — and you should understand that the margins are being added to. The Fresh Margins, out past the Pressfields, are the newest districts: land poured within living memory of no one, still uncured. Walk there and the ground is faintly warm and slightly yielding, like skin an hour after sunburn; your bootprint fills itself in over the course of days, matter not yet committed to being what it will be. The mossers will not cross it. Even the swifts route around it. The old fauna instinct treats soft ground the way animals elsewhere treat fire.

And in the cured districts, the seams show. Where two pours meet and their batches of reality don't quite agree, you get alleys where a single shadow falls in two directions, faintly, like a double exposure. On the Long Stair — eight hundred terraced steps between the Basins and the Gantry Forest — the count changes between ascents, and the distance to the top sometimes arrives before you do, the upper landing appearing briefly closer than its own geometry allows. The surveyors' stakes mark these spots too. Nothing enforces the stakes. The fauna simply honor them, the way animals elsewhere honor cliff edges.

When the Long Dim comes — the lamps easing down over weeks, glare settling to a merely daylight brightness, the whole sky exhaling — the Stills enter their most honest hour. The cloud-sea begins its slow rise. Dew blooms across ten thousand terraces at once, silver under the fading grid. The gutters tick in ranks. Somewhere out north, a press begins a stroke it will finish next season, and its sound is a vast held breath that no ear will hear and every tooth will feel. The animals queue at the condensate lines in the near-dark, orderly, damp, alive, each of them a small fast life riding a world that is building itself too slowly to ever be seen finishing.

Everything here is made. Nothing here is done.
