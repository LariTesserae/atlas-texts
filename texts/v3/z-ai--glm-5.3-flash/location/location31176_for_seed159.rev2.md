Underground, and no night anywhere.

That is the first thing to accept about this place. It lies beneath stone — a valley's worth of underworld, vaulted and pillared and connected by crawling-ways — and yet you could read a map in it. The ceilings of the great chambers carry a living overcast: films of luminous growth, so thick and continuous that from the floor they read as a sky, green-gold and cloud-mottled, shedding a light like late afternoon under heavy clouds. No point-source, no hard shadows. Brightness simply accumulates wherever life is busiest, so the map of the light is a map of the traffic — around a fresh rockfall the ceiling flares; where a herd has grazed the film down to nothing, the dark spreads like a scorch. Newcomers keep looking up for the sun that isn't there. There is no sun. There has never been a sun here.

The warmth comes up from below. The rock of the floor is body-warm — lean on it and you are leaning on a flank — and the air holds that heat the way a held breath does. Every surface is faintly beaded with condensation; sweat does nothing; the air smells of wet iron, warm yeast, and hot flint. In the low fields of pillars the humidity hangs visibly, a soft mist at chest height. In the Steamfields, where seep-water finds the young, still-cooling veins, the mist stands up in slow white columns.

Nothing here is old. That is the second thing to understand. Erosion has barely begun its handwriting: every edge is a break-edge, every corner unrounded, every face bearing the pale, raw look of stone split yesterday. No wind has ever blown here to sand anything smooth; the only weather is water and appetite. The country reads as embryonic — chambers whose shapes are still being decided, ceilings still dripping themselves into their final curves, a whole drainage system that exists mostly as rumors of seep.

The water is dampness more than water. Moisture lives inside the dryness rather than defeating it. Bleeds — seeps that start for no visible reason — run for a day along some fault-line and then quit. Pockets perch in broken ceilings and empty without warning, which every creature here has opinions about; the ceiling dew beads in perfect rows along certain mineral seams, and the ones who read such things read the rows like charts, foretelling which pocket goes next. A few pools persist in shallow basins, milky-warm, faintly steaming, each pitched differently when the drips strike them. Once or twice a season a hidden reservoir breaches and a flash stream runs for hours, recarving a floor, and then the seam reseals itself and the new streambed dries to a pale scar. Nothing is riverine. Nothing lasts.

Where plants would be, there is the velvet. No grass, no moss, no trees, nothing with a leaf — but nothing sterile either. Every surface carries a film, dark green shading to black, faintly luminous, part fungus and part bacterial mat, feeding on the minerals in the seep-water and on everything's leavings. It is the grass of this world. It grows back in days. Everything else is built on that fact.

And everything else is a great deal. The fauna is not a presence here; it is the condition of the place. You cannot look anywhere without something already looking, or chewing, or drinking. In layers: the air holds the motes — flyers from fist-sized to skull-sized, filter-feeders on spores, moving in flocks so dense they register as weather, flowing around the pillars like liquid, their combined wing-wash a constant rain-on-tent sound overhead. The floors belong to the querns: barrel-bodied grazers with grinding plates who scrape the velvet off fresh rock and, in the scraping, chew the country bigger. A herd sounds like distant millstones. Their trails are the beginnings of tunnels; a quern-road becomes a gallery within a season. Along the driplines the lippers lie flat and patient, taking whatever comes down to drink. In the cracks, an industry of mites recycles everything the light forgets.

And among all of it, the masons — long-limbed, leathery, moving in work parties of eight and ten, the only hands in the world. Their structures are scattered, small, and everywhere tied to a worksite rather than gathered into towns: a tamped-mud dam holding one of the warm pools; a causeway of flatstones laid across a mudflat; a little laced membrane strung on fungal bootlace-cord, an awning to shed a drip-line; a cache of tools under an overhang — stone wedges, straight levers, tamping rods, spongy fruiting-bodies used to carry water. Their technology is mud and wedge, soak and pack and dry; there is no fire here, no metal, and no need — warmth is ambient, fuel is nonexistent, and the work is stone against stone. Half the engineering is plumbing: plugging pockets, redirecting bleeds, borrowing an hour of stream. Every finished dam gets a single pebble set on its crest — surveyor's mark or gratitude, no one from outside has been able to say. Older works lie half-swallowed in velvet, with handholds worn glossy on certain ledges, so the place has a short but real history, written in abandoned repairs. And in one tool cache there is a bored stone bead, too regular, too smooth for the wedge-and-mud repertoire — heirloom, or evidence of someone else entirely. The masons do not discuss it.

It is loud. Not violently — busy-loud. The motes' wing-wash is a constant ceiling-rain; querns grind like far-off mills; every pool has its own drip-note; the masons knock on stone in a tapped code that carries a mile through the vaults, so their conversations arrive from several directions at once, intimate and distant simultaneously. Collapses come as soft thunder. The Steamfields hiss. And underneath everything runs a low resonance, the whole country humming like a struck bell too large to finish ringing.

The rule of the place is that nothing holds its shape long enough to become a landmark. A street is a route the masons cleared last week. A wall is a herd's next meal. And collapse is not disaster here — it is delivery. A fall opens fresh rock; fresh rock grows velvet; velvet draws the herds; the herds carve new chambers; the new chambers fill with new traffic, and the ceiling over them brightens. Appetite is the erosion engine. The fauna are the weather. By the time a map is drawn it is already a document of nostalgia — redrawn by the day, by the hour in the busy seasons.

The churn is not violent, though. Stand still long enough and the place rearranges around you: a stream finds a new floor, a herd rounds off a corner — the first rounding of an edge, erosion's very first syllable — a dam goes up with its pebble on top, a pocket overhead finally lets go and the work party is already there, wedges and sponges out, before the water finishes falling. This is a geology younger than the habits of its inhabitants. They build for a future that keeps relocating, so they build constantly and lightly, ready to abandon everything.

It is a world still wet from its own making — midwife and demolition crew, both, and no one here has yet had time to wonder which is which.
