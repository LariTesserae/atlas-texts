# The Sough

You find it by ear before you find it by eye. On a cold morning the headland itself breathes: a thread of vapor rises from a crack in the turf near the cliff's foot, thin as wire, and the ground under your palms carries a hum with a slow pulse in it — the sea, somewhere under the stone, keeping time that has nothing to do with the sky. Gulls will not sit on that stretch of grass. Whatever lies beneath it speaks too loudly for them.

The mouth is a low slot in a lip of smooth black limestone, barely a crawl's height, so worn at the shoulders — polished like a ship's rail, by centuries of elbows — that you understand before you enter how many bodies have come this way. You would never find it on your own. The coast here is a mile of similar stone, gray on gray, a raised storm beach of cobbles, an old ocean's bench. The sea cut this room when it stood higher in the world; since then the land has shrugged itself up a little, and the ocean now visits only as voice.

Inside, the space is small. Three paces of floor. Five steps of echo. A dome of roof low enough to touch without stretching, hung with a cold black film of water that never dries and never falls. The chamber is mostly air — cold, dark, salted air — and this is the first thing to understand about it: there is almost nothing here, and the almost-nothing is what makes everything else enormous. Stone reflects. Air carries. There is nothing soft to swallow a sound, except one corner of moss, of which more later.

The wedge of light from the mouth reaches in about a body's length and stops. Beyond it is darkness that your eyes never fully beat, even after an hour; you learn to stop trusting them and go over to your hands. The floor underfoot is dry stone furred almost invisibly with damp. The walls are an older ocean's work — scalloped, rounded, settled into a shape it has clearly held for a very long time and intends to keep. Nothing is actively wearing here. A flake of roof falls in a generation. The drip has drilled a pothole the size of an eggcup in the floor, and its rim is polished; a fingernail's depth in a century. Your visit is not an event in this room.

The sound arrives in a fixed order, and you learn its anatomy quickly because you have no choice.

First comes pressure: a push against the chest, ears popping, the thighs of air. Then the bass — not heard so much as arrived — a boom that comes up through the floor and the long bones and stands your teeth on edge. Then the vent: a hiss that climbs to a shriek as the chamber exhales through a needle-thin crack in the seaward wall, a sound like the whole room being wrung out, and it decays in a long tail of echoes, each one crossing the small darkness and coming back off the dome, so that a single boom takes eight, ten seconds to die. In the gap that follows, you can hear your own blood. Sometimes a moth crosses the darkness there, in the quiet between booms — whether it times itself to the gaps or is simply thrown by each one, you cannot tell.

Then, before the next breath, the drip.

Because here is the rule of the place, the one the people who cut the basin long ago evidently understood: there are two waters in this room, and they come from different worlds. The crack in the wall is the sea's. On big swells it spits — a brief salt spurt, a yard of spray that wets a fan of stone and smells of kelp and cold iron, and beneath it a scallop of rock holds a little brackish pool that drains over hours. Once, on the largest springs, the spurt has thrown a shore crab in. You meet it on the floor sometimes, walking with its sideways patience, and when it clicks its claw, the click is the only creature-sound in the room and it stops everything. But that water is the sea's, and you cannot drink it.

The sweet water comes from above. Rain falls on the moor behind the headland — rarely, thin, the whole country being dryish and cold — and sinks, and takes its time through a fathom of limestone, and arrives here, at a seam in the roof where a band of fossil shell has fractured, as drips.
