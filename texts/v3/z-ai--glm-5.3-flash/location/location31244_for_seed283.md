# The Sink

Forty miles southeast, past the terraces and the far tree-line, there is a Works — pumps, furnaces, flare stacks, a town of machinery — and you never see it. You hear it. In this country everything arrives as weather or as sound, and the Works is both: a low note under all other notes, felt in the chest at the still hours, with a slow beat in it like something enormous counting. On clear nights — they are rare — its flares stand on the horizon as a stain of orange on the underside of the dark. The moths fly toward it. All night, all across the basin, they go south in a silent river, thousands of wings, a whole order of small lives streaming toward the only false light in the world.

The basin itself is called the Sink, though by whom it is hard to say, since no one lives here. It is a saucer of country perhaps thirty miles across, the lowest ground in every direction, and it used to be a lake. The lake is the Works' doing. Once the pumps began, downstream, the water table dropped a body-length and kept its new discipline; over twenty years the lake stood down, slowly, without drama, the way a fever breaks. What remains is a plain of black loam that was mud until recently and remembers it.

Nothing built stands here, and nothing built has been tried. There is no jetty from the lake days, no fish-camp, no road, because the pipeline — the one piece of the Works that reaches into the basin — runs buried, and where a line is buried no road is needed. Above it the loam dries pale and the thorn scrub takes the place of reed, a lane of low growth running dead straight from horizon to horizon. The ground there is faintly warm at midnight, the only warm stone in a stoneless country, and snakes lie in the lane at night like dropped belts, collecting the heat of the world's only machine. It is the single straightness the Sink contains. Everything else here wanders.

Rain comes twice a year if the year is generous. It falls for an afternoon, stands in the low places, and is remembered for months afterward, because the tarns — shallow sheets of water, skin-warm, ringed with the densest growth in the basin — spend the whole interval shrinking. You can watch a season in a tarn's edge: the wet green ring, then the cracked black ring, then the pale dust ring, each one wider than the last. The fish grow easy as the water goes. The herons grow fat. It is a slow banquet, conducted without haste by everyone involved.

The real weather happens at leaf-height, all night, at the pace of condensation. The air holds its water the way the basin holds its air — the Sink takes in whatever drifts in and forgets to let it leave — and every surface runs a degree or two colder than the night, so the air pays all of them. Under the big figs on the old shoreline it rains. From around midnight to mid-morning, water falls steadily out of the canopy, drop by counted drop, the plain's own private rain, and by the gray hour the ground beneath is darker and the leaf-edges are beaded and steaming. The cracks in the loam breathe with the same rhythm: they exhale damp all night and gape by day. The Sink is lush on dew. Nothing here has ever needed a storm.

The figs deserve their own accounting. They stand along the old waterline in a broken gallery, enormous, buttressed, their roots gripping the bank where the water used to lean against the shore — and the shore itself is legible still, a gray ribbon of standing dead reed marking the old high-water, ten years high now, preserved exactly because the basin is too damp to let it burn and too dry to let it rot quickly.
