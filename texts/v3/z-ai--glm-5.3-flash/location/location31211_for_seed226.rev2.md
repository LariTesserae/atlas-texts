You come to it over the last shoulder of the massif, where the air goes thin and cold and your breath starts to travel with you, and then the ground simply stops and you are standing on the rim of the Foundling — a bowl of young rock perhaps half a mile across, high enough that on most mornings the cloud lies below you like a second floor. You could walk the rim in an hour. You could stand in one place and see nearly everything. And yet the place does not feel small; it feels *early*.

That is the first thing to understand about it: nothing here has ever been taken away.

There is no scree at the rim, no talus fan, no gully anywhere, no sediment, no strata. The rock has no history. Its faces are smooth in the way of cast metal or cooling wax — pale gray-green, unpatterned, ungrained, without a single lichen patch except one celebrated saffron colony the size of a shield on the north wall, which the people of the town below can name the arrival-date of. The geology is not old and weathered; it is new and *finishing*. Along certain seams in the bowl's floor the stone is still arriving — barely, patiently, a finger-width across a human lifetime — welling up with a skin like cooled tallow, faintly warm. Press your palm to the Nursery stone and it feels like a held hand; press it to any other rock in the bowl and it takes your warmth and gives back cold. The law of the town is simple and very old: nothing is built on the Nursery. The growing places are left as open courtyards, and every structure nearby stands off from them politely, with gaps in its stonework — growth joints — where the mountain's slow new inches can pass through the architecture without argument.

Because the mountain is young, the water has not yet learned its own paths.

That is the second thing. It rains often here, softly; more than rain, the air itself condenses. Cloud moves through the bowl at chest height on most days, and every rock face weeps — thousands of thin threads of seepage, a constant cold surf-hiss. Drops form at every lip and ledge and hang there for a long time, minutes, sometimes what feels like the length of a conversation, surface tension and cold conspiring to make the water reluctant. The whole amphitheater drips, and yet there is not one natural stream. No ravine has been cut. No channel exists that the water made for itself. The water leaves the bowl the way it can — invisibly, through the body of the rock — and the only visible waterways are the ones that have been *given* to it.

This is the town's work, and very nearly its whole identity. The town is called Sough, which in the old speech means both *murmur* and *drain*, and its people are waterwrights before anything else. With hammerstone and chisel and wooden sluice and clay and fiber — a handful of heirloom copper tools, treated like saints' bones — they cut channels in the young stone: gently graded, painstaking, a channel taking a season's patient work. They gather the springs into weirs and settling pools. They line cisterns with clay. They grade the channels so precisely that the water, they say, *learns* the way — and this is their quiet creed, that erosion is not destruction here but education. They are giving the water its habits. Somewhere in the town there is always someone who understands, though it is not much spoken of, that these cuts are a draft: that the widest channel, the First Cut, running from the great spring down through the terraces to the rim, is being drawn as the bed of a river that will be a gorge in ten thousand years, and a valley after that. The town is writing the mountain's future canyons in advance, one chisel-stroke per lifetime.

The town itself is a strange arithmetic. It has the plan of a city and the population of a chapel.

On the east terrace, stakes and hemp strings lie out across the moss in long confident lines: streets, laid out for a town of thousands, to be built at perhaps one building a year, and only where the stone beneath is judged finished. Here and there along those strings stands a single wall, or a full cistern brimming and waiting, its house unbuilt for another century. The emptiness is not neglect. It is an inheritance being saved. Two hundred people inhabit a bowl that was drafted for a city, and they move through it the way early movers move into a vast house — occupying a few rooms, keeping the rest swept, breathing in the space. The buildings that do exist are low and dry-stone, roofed with turf or with certain mosses deliberately seeded as shingles, doorways raised and lintel-grooved so the drip passes *past* the door in a neat curtain. Each house sits in its own green room, out of sight of its neighbors, connected by causeways and stairs and, always, by sound. Near the Nursery the houses stand on wooden rafts and are re-trued every spring with hand-wedges, the whole family turning wedges by ear and by spirit-level of water until the house sits true again — a town that tunes its own buildings the way other towns sharpen their knives.

One wall, on the low terrace, has a stone window: an aperture left open nine generations ago for a knuckle of rising rock that is now exactly level with the sill. Children are taken to see it. It is the town's clock.

The loudness of the place surprises everyone. Nothing moves fast, nothing crashes, and yet you cannot hear silence here. The bowl is a resonator of hard young stone, and everything in it rings. The weeping walls make a constant distant surf. The channels each hold a note — the waterwrights have learned, over generations, that narrowing a channel's lip raises its pitch, and so the town's channels have been *tuned*, unintentionally at first and then very deliberately, into a slow chord. Stand anywhere in Sough and you hear three or four sustained tones at once, plus the hiss, plus the ticking of the hanging drops, and the whole thing carries and returns off the smooth walls quick and bright. The earkeepers — a small devoted office, three people at any time — keep the chord to its traditional intervals, and they listen for more than music. When a new spring breaks somewhere deep, the chord shifts by a hair and they will find it. When stone grows somewhere under the floor, the bowl's deepest note deepens by a hair over decades, and they track it, and mark it, and say nothing. Because voices carry so far here, everyone speaks low and formally; the etiquette of the town is a quiet people inside a loud place. The only sounds in the bowl that come from neither water nor people are two notes of a gray bird that nests on the warm seams — the warmth hatching its eggs — and the earkeepers will not tune any channel near that nest. Even they do not know exactly why. It is simply agreed.

The garden takes care of the rest. With no soil to speak of — only a dark felt of moss living on its own dead, inches thick at best — the bowl is nevertheless lush past sense, because the moisture never stops and the moss has never been disturbed. Moss in deep shaggy mounds and pillows; ferns marching up every seep-line in ranks; liverworts like sheets of green leather; saxifrage working into the raw stone's hairline cracks, prying them open — the first erosion the rock has ever experienced, and done gently. A few trees: stunted willows and one birch, each called an anchor tree, each known by name, roots plugging some seep that would otherwise undercut a terrace, so that a tree is treated as a piece of civic infrastructure, and mourned when one fails. In the pools and films of water live the glasslings — pale, translucent, finger-length creatures that spend their whole lives inside the water films on the vertical rock, moving so slowly you must watch to be sure they move at all. They are the only animals besides the bird. Children are told not to breathe on them, because breath is warm and the world here is cold. There are no fish. The pools are clean, cold, and brand-new, and the people of Sough say, without sadness, that the water hasn't learned fish yet. They keep a Ledger of Arrivals on the meeting-hall wall: first moss, before memory; first fern; first lichen, four hundred years ago; first tree, ninety years; first bird; first glassling. Next expected, they have written, in a hand that expects to wait: fish.

Even the ground keeps a slow memory. Footprints in the moss-shag do not spring back — the pile stays combed. The town's paths are pale lines worn through the dark green where a thousand careful feet have gone the same way; and a single stray walk, one person cutting across a courtyard a hundred years ago, still shows as a faint bruise in the nap. The earkeepers can find the path where someone once carried a sick neighbor down to the warm seam, and it is still there, and they point it out to children without explaining who it was, because that, too, is part of the patience.

On the rare clear mornings the bowl is almost too bright to look at — every surface beaded, the walls strung with light, a spray-bow standing over the big spring until the sun climbs — and on pearl days the cloud sits inside the bowl and everything goes soft and near. But the character of the place does not change with the light. It is the same in any weather: a young world, wet and cold and loud, nearly empty because it has barely begun to fill, tended by a small people who have understood, earlier than anyone else ever has, that they arrived at a beginning — and who have chosen to match it. Down at the rim, decades of seepage are gathering under the rock toward some first overflow that has not happened yet. One day a thread of water will find the outer slope and begin the long work that will carve everything below. The earkeepers say they will hear its note before anyone sees it. By then the Nursery will have risen another handspan, the town will have laid perhaps one more wall on its drafted streets, and the first of this water will be on its way to a sea it has never reached — with the whole of Sough standing politely aside, giving it room, the way you do with anything newly born.
