The lowland tells you where you are before anything does. The plain is rush-flat and seeping, ground that darkens as it drops, water standing in the hoofprints of no animals, and you follow the wet downhill because everything here does. Then the green stops.

Not thins — stops. There is a seam in the world like the edge of a poured thing. On one side, ordinary meadow, dry-stemmed, wind-ruffled, going brown at the tips. On the other, saturation so complete it has its own weather. Where old glasshouse panes survive in their iron frames, they fog from the inside, and you can lay your palm on the boundary and feel two climates an inch apart: the plain's dry coolness, and behind the glass a warm, thick, breathing dark-green air. Where the glass is gone, a wall of living stem has taken its place — grown to exactly the frame's thickness, clipped dead flat by no hand. Watch the plane of it and you understand the clipping: any leaf that reaches out past the seam touches the outside air and withers back to the line. The place maintains its own envelope. It has kept its greenhouse by becoming one.

The doorway is an iron rectangle with no door, hung instead with a curtain of vine-stem as dense as beadwork. You push through; it gives with a woody creak; it closes behind you without a sound you can hear, though you feel it — a settling, like a room readjusting its breath.

Inside, the warmth has weight. It is the temperature of a body, of a hothouse at four in the afternoon, and the air is so saturated it seems drinkable — your arms bead with condensation within a minute, and so does everything else. Every surface is wet in the way of a cold glass on a summer day: the iron ribs, the leaf surfaces, the surviving panes, the copper pipe runs, the enamel sign stenciled with half a word — *…SER*, *Nº 7*, the letters flaked to green shadow. Light is ordinary daylight, but diffuse, shadowless, coming through panes and woven wall in even measure, so the whole chamber is lit the way a fog is lit: bright without source, greenish where the leaves have taken over the glazing, plain white where a panel of true glass still holds.

There is no floor, exactly. There is a root-mat — sprung, drum-tight, alive — and your footfall sinks a centimeter and is received. No soil anywhere. The mat is substrate, architecture, and ecosystem at once, and through it runs water: a skin of it, two centimeters deep, moving in visible channels. Some channels were dug — old ceramic gutters, now half-swallowed — some were grown, and it is no longer clear which is which, because the roots have entered the pipes and the pipes have threated the roots until the plumbing of the machine and the venation of the plant are one continuous system. The water circulates. You can watch it go: in from the flume, out along the weeping lines, up through the mat, into the air.

The engine stands at the basin's low heart, and it is genuinely industrial — riveted tank, bronze valves, a flywheel the size of a cartwheel turning with stately patience, governor balls swinging in their slow orbit, gauges with uncracked faces. Its power is the landscape itself. The whole plain drains toward this bowl; a flume brings the inflow down through a fall of dark water onto a turbine, and the machine's only work is to *throttle* that descent — to catch the water that would flood the basin and lift it, stroke by long stroke, into the weeping pipes and mist-lines that feed the green. Then the plant does its part: the canopy transpires, the air thickens, and along the cool north face a rank of condensers — iron fins furred with moss to exactly the right depth — sweats the moisture back down into the channels. Inflow, lift, fall, breath, recapture, return. Slightly warmed water leaves on the far side under a culvert and rejoins the plain. The engine does not conquer the wetland. It is a valve in the river's slow path, and the garden is the engine's lung.

The plant has finished what the engineers started, section by section. A thick woody sleeve has replaced two meters of main pipe, and water weeps through it perfectly. Felted bark has been wrapped — or grown — around a joint that needed insulating; the wrap is neat as lagging. And in one corner there is a handwheel, its rim worn bright and shiny-green, with arc-marks in the rust around it that say it *turns* — a few degrees every week, as the pressure asks. Whether the machine gardens the plant or the plant gardens the machine is a question the place has outgrown. The loop made them a single economy long ago.

You notice the sound before you can catalogue it, because it is loud — not event-loud, but *full* loud, a chamber filled edge to edge with noise. The pump's stroke is the bass of it: one deep note every long moment, a held breath released through iron, felt in the root-mat under your feet before your ears take it. Over that, the flume's constant rain-on-wood. Over that, the drip — ten thousand leaf-tips releasing their beads, a hiss with a shimmer in it, continuous, and loud enough alone to fill a room. And threading through everything, the creaks: wide-spaced, slow, like rope under strain. That is the growth. You can hear the place grow. A sound arrives, completes itself over half a minute, and is gone.

And there are no birds. There are no birds, no frogs, no drone of insects in the warm — the silence of their absence sits under the mechanical noise like a missing floor. Almost nothing here is animal. A single species of pale moth lives in the condensation layer, flying in no season, all year. Snails graze the mineral film off the surviving panes, each shell transparent-thin as fingernail. Nothing else. There are no flowers, no seeds, and — you realize after a while — no two kinds of leaf. Every frond, every vine, every wall of green is the same species, graft fused to graft where the growths touch, no tissue rejecting tissue. The grove is one organism wearing the shape of a garden. Nothing fallen lingers either: a leaf drops, and the mat takes it in, digests it, raises it again. There is no death visible in this room. Only intake.

Whether anyone tends it is its own quiet mystery. Some parts of the machine are newer than they have any right to be — a brass fitting bright under its green patina, one gauge glass clean, a valve stem free of rust where everything around it is furred. On a grown-over ledge sits an enamel mug, dry inside, unchipped. A hand came here, sometime in the last season, and the mat swallowed the footprints the same day. The rest — the wheel-turns, the lagging, the root that took over the pipe — the place seems to do for itself, the way a body heals.

The cycles are slow, and you feel time here in durations, not events. Within the hour: the pump's stroke, and the condensation tide — morning rains inward on the walls, afternoon air drinks it back. Across the week: the floor-water rises a finger's width and subsides as the outer water table breathes. Across the year: another hand-span of green over the iron ribs, and the seam re-trimmed, the envelope kept. Across a century, you understand, the machine is being replaced organically, joint by joint — root for pipe, bark for lagging, wood for frame — until some far day the engine will be a wooden thing shaped like a pump, still throttling the plain's slow water, still tended by what it tends.

Nothing here is eroding. It is being digested gently, at the exact rate it is renewed — rust equals bark, inflow equals outflow, growth equals trimming — and it has been in this balance so long that the balance has the texture of intention. Stand still by the flume and wait for the stroke: the deep note goes through the mat, the drip-hiss holds its breath for half a beat, the moth adjusts on its pane, the whole small warm chamber rises and settles around one machine's patience. Outside, the plain goes on seeping toward the seam. Inside, the water has nowhere to go but around, and around, and around — and everything green, every leaf and root and wall, turns with it.
