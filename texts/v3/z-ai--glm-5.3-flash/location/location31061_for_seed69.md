There is no light here. Not dimness, not a long night waiting to end — night would imply a morning somewhere, and there is no morning anywhere on this world. The dark is not an absence. It is a material, felt on the skin like wet wool: the air is so thick with warm vapor that light, if it ever existed, would drown in it before it crossed a room. But the deeper truth is that nothing here even tries. Fire burns without shining — it gives heat, that is all fire is. Lightning comes as pure thunder, a slap of pressure against the chest with nothing to see. Most creatures long ago gave up their eyes; they wear them like vestigial jewelry, clouded over, useless. The people who walk this place carry lanterns anyway, as heirlooms, the way another world might carry its dead.

You will come to know this landscape the way a child knows its mother's voice: entirely, and by ear.

**The Shelf**

Call it the Shelf — that is what the keepers call it, the ones who tend the machines. It is a continental highland, a mass of ancient stone tilting up out of warm lowland water for farther than anyone has ever measured. Its feet dissolve into reed-marsh that goes on beyond hearing; its shoulders rise into terraces stacked like the insides of a shell; at its crown, the cloud ceiling comes down to touch the ground, and you can walk inside the base of the sky, breathing it, so saturated that water condenses directly onto your skin, your hair, the leaves, the stone, everything — the whole world sweating. Nothing freezes. The heat comes from below, from the rock itself, from the pressurized sea the Shelf sits on top of, so that even the highest terraces stay bath-warm and the air tastes of iron and green.

Every crack weeps. That is the first thing your hands learn: no stone is ever dry. The cliff faces here — the keepers call the big ones the Griefs — are threaded with mineral veins you can taste with a fingertip, sweet veins and bitter ones, and from a thousand seams between them comes water at body temperature, running in channels so old and so polished that they feel like poured glass. The channels no longer widen. Rain cuts them, moss heals them, the vents scour them smooth, and the argument between water and stone was settled ten thousand years ago. The land is not eroding anymore. It is simply, finally, in its shape.

**The made tide**

Beneath the Shelf lies the Undersea: a vast warm aquifer, sealed, pressurized, patient. Long ago — no one remembers how long, and the keepers have stopped pretending to — someone drove risers down into it, and now the plateau is studded with pump-stations, spaced about a day's walk apart along the major seep-lines, squat engine-houses built around their machines the way a body is built around a heart.

The engines run without rest. You hear them always, somewhere: a deep slow pulse through the soles of your feet, station by station, offset from each other so that the beat travels across the Shelf like a monstrous heartbeat, four seconds here, four seconds there, never quite together. They draw up water and bleed pressure, and their outflow feeds the weep-lines, and this is why the whole highland sweats in rhythm — because the machines make a tide. Twice a shift, the seeps rise: warm water welling up through every crack, pooling to the ankle across entire terraces, streams running backward for an hour, and then the slow sink. The ecology has entrained itself to it completely. The herds migrate on the engines' schedule. The plants time their fruiting to it.
