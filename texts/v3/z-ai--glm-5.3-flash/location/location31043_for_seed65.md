# The Shelf

There is no weather here, and there has never been weather here. That is the first thing to understand, and it explains most of the rest.

The Shelf sits so high that the atmosphere has given up on it — not abandoned, exactly, but settled into a truce. The air is thin and cool and utterly still except for the Sound, which is not wind. The Sound comes up from below, from the deep vertical country under the Shelf's edge, and it never modulates, never gusts, never dies. It is a vast, even roar, like a river the size of a sea falling forever into a hole the size of a sky. The people who live here — and something lives here — do not hear it anymore. It is the silence, and other sounds are the noises.

The terrain itself is old in a way that feels less like age than like completion. Erosion finished its work uncounted millennia ago and then simply stopped, as if the land reached a shape it could not improve upon. Great pavements of pale stone stretch for miles,grooved and fluted in long parallel runs, softened at every edge, everything rounded into the forms that water and frost had agreed upon and then let be. No scree. No fresh collapse. No talus slopes waiting to fall. The mountains here do not erode; they *are*. It gives the whole region an eerie polished quality, like driftwood, like the bones of something that finished dying a very long time ago and is now simply arranged.

Water exists, but grudgingly and in secret. Every few days — though "days" is a word the sky here wouldn't recognize; the light simply is, a flat brilliant white with no sun anyone can find, shadowless noon forever — a moisture condenses out of the thin air in slow beads on the cold stone. It gathers in the flutings, runs downhill in threads, and pools in the deeper grooves where the rock has worn itself into shallow basins. These pools are the whole economy of the Shelf. Everything arranges itself around them.

The vegetation is a rumor more than a presence. Crust lichens in gray-green maps, thin as breath on glass. Tufts of something like moss huddled in the lee of stones. In the wetter basins, a fringe of tiny rush-like stems, two fingers tall, that have never once been seen to grow and never once been found dead. They are simply, always, at their present size. Growth here doesn't seem to be a process so much as a condition.

The fauna is the surprise. Abundant — startlingly so, given everything else. The creatures of the Shelf are small and dense-furred or dense-feathered, cool-blooded in spirit if not in fact, and they are *loud*. Pika-things that whistle in sequences too regular to be calls and too varied to be signals. Flocks of something between a finch and a moth that sing in unison, hundreds strong, a single voice distributed across a hundred bodies. Marmotine animals that sun themselves on the warm south faces of the boulders — warm being relative; the boulders hold the light's flat heat well — and that have stopped fearing anything, because nothing here has ever died of anything except old age, and old age is visible from a distance.

They are not tame. They are simply not afraid, because in a world with no weather, no night, no predation anyone has witnessed, and no change, fear never got a foothold. The whole ecosystem is a frozen truce: the pools give, the lichens give, the grazers graze exactly what regrows, and nothing oscillates. Population curves here are not curves. They are lines.

And the people — because there are a few, because tech 0.8 means someone knaps stone and scrapes hide — live small and quiet along the pool-lines. They are not primitive in demeanor, only in means: there is no ore at this altitude, no fuel to smelt with, and no urgency to invent. They build nothing. Not tents, not cairns. They wear stitched skins and sleep in natural hollows that their grandparents' grandparents also slept in, and they have perhaps forty words and use six of them. Why elaborate? Nothing changes. Their tools are handed down until the handle is more repair than original, and this is not poverty; it is continuity, the material version of the landscape itself.

The one thing they do, the one act that might be called culture, is listen to the edge. Twice in what they call a turning — their only unit of time, and even it is not a duration but a repetition — they walk to the Shelf's rim and stand in the roar of the falling-air country below and listen to the Sound as if hearing it for the first time. It is the only gradient in their world, the only place where anything is more or less than it was. They stand there in the bright shadowless light, small figures on a polished stone shelf above an endless roar, and then they walk back, and nothing has changed, and that is the point.

The place holds its breath the way a held chord holds — not silence, not motion, but pure sustained tone. You could visit. You would leave. The Shelf would not notice, and would be exactly as it is.
