# The Ten-Day Sea

You go under at the reed line, where the last stems still break the surface, and the first thing you learn is that this is not the water you were promised by stories. No dark. No green murk. Noon comes straight down through it, and the drowned summit lies below you in full light, pale stone and standing grass, so clear that for a moment your eyes refuse it — a meadow that has learned to breathe sideways, a field with the sky let into it.

Above, once you tip your head back, the whole dry world has been compressed into a single trembling disc: the sun, the clouds, the far ridgelines, all bent and gathered into one round window overhead. Beyond its rim, the surface turns to shaking silver and then to mirror. This is the first rule of the place: the dry world is visible only through a porthole, and everything outside the porthole is a locked door.

The dome itself is small. That surprises people who expect seas to be large. It is the top of a fell — a rolling plateau three hundred paces across at most, the shallowest point of a flood that came up the valleys in the night and kept going. You could cross the entire bright country of it in a hundred unhurried strokes. From the reeds to the rim is forty. The rest, below the rim and all around, is water with almost nothing in it: light, and a slow snowfall of silt, and distance.

That snowfall is worth watching. The flood arrived thick with ground-up mountain, and it is settling now, grain by grain, a pale rime accumulating on every stone and blade like frost that falls upward slowly. The water is clearing itself, sorting itself out, and it will be days yet before it finishes. Nothing here has had time. The lichen on the summit rocks is still alive and gray-green, gripping. The edges of everything are still sharp — wall-crests you could cut a hand on, a fence rail whose split wood still shows pale heartwood. No water has leaned on this landscape long enough to round it. Ten days ago this was a mountain. The seafloor it is becoming does not yet exist as a fact; it exists as an event, and you are present for the first line of it.

The meadow is dying politely. The grasses are still green, still rooted, standing upright and combing back and forth in the surge like the fur of some enormous patient animal. Near the rim the sedge has done something tender and strange: it stretched. The water came slowly enough, at the end, that the plants lengthened, chasing the surface — reeds a full arm taller than their neighbors, thin and desperate, the meadow's one attempt to keep its head above its own sea. The reed tips still break out into the air. Grasshoppers climb them and sing from the air side. Underneath, a pike hangs in the drowned grass, motionless for a quarter hour at a stretch, then a single long flex of its body carries it the width of a field. Trout that used to own a tarn no bigger than a barn now circle the summit cairn with the proprietary calm of barons.

The built things sit among all this like sentences left unfinished. A drystone fold, circular, its wall a hand below the surface; inside it, stray wool drifts like pollen and never quite settles. A fence runs downslope, then bends over the rim of the pocket and keeps going — posts descending into the murk at a steady interval, and you follow it out of curiosity until the light quits, and the fence does not. The fold's one iron hinge has bloomed orange. Everything here was made by hand, and it shows: turf roof, stacked stone, a peg where an iron fitting would have been cheaper.

On the high point, beside the cairn, stands a little bell-cote. The flood reaches the bell. Every surge rocks it, and it tolls — low, warped, the note dragged slow and strange by the water, arriving in your ribs before it reaches your ears. In this world you do not hear with your ears alone. Water conducts sound through the chest, the jaw, the teeth; the bell is felt, and then, secondarily, heard.

And heard is not the word for what this place does to you. It is deafening in a way no open-air landscape manages, because there is no quiet version of it anywhere, and no direction to the sound either. The roar of the flood arrives from everywhere at once. Rain on the surface far above is a steady frying hiss. The sun-warmed stones tick and ping as they heat and the cold weight beneath them stays cold — the mountain cooling and heating like an engine, cracking its knuckles. Somewhere far below, on the drowned flanks, a boulder lets go and its landing comes up through the water as a soft enormous *thoom* you feel in your back teeth.

The loudest thing is at the col. There the inflow still pours over the old mountain pass, and because the pass is now under water, the waterfall pours into water — a churning white column that falls without falling, more pressure than image, and at its base a roar like a held note. The flood is cutting its first canyon there. You can see the rock-flour rising from that wound like smoke from a chimney, a pale plume in the murk. This is the new sea's first act of erosion, happening on day ten, and its sound is the loudest single thing in the world: you approach the col and the noise stops being sound and becomes a wall, a pressure you lean against and then stop leaning.

The hut is mid-slope, turf-roofed, door shut by someone's hand. Inside it, the flood did something merciful: the interior held its air, and the hut is now a diving bell. Water stands knee-deep within, rising a finger's width a day as air seeps out through the turf. A crook leans in the corner. A bowl on its shelf. The trapped bubble smells of peat smoke and lanolin, the last dry room of the old world, and when you put your face into it, the roar outside goes on but smaller, bone-carried, and the doorway is a window of trembling light. There is no one to explain the arithmetic of it, but the water does the counting for you: the bubble is shrinking. When it fails, the hut becomes only walls.

The sky-window above gives you the rest of the world in glimpses. A cloud crosses and the caustic net of light on the stones tightens and relaxes. Once, a rowboat — from below it is a dark keel drawn across the sun, oars as paired flickers.
