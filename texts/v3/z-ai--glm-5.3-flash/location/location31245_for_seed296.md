# The Floor

The stone finished its work here a long time ago. Whatever range once stood on this spot spent itself down to the root — every ridge rounded off, every slope let down, every fracture healed into quiet. What remains is a tabletop. The locals call it the Floor, and they do not mean it as metaphor. It runs level to the horizon in every direction, a planation surface planed flat by more time than the town can name, so perfectly level that three old survey towers, spaced a day's walk apart, still stand along its southern edge with their plumb lines hanging, checked every spring against a curvature no one has ever found.

At this height the air is thin enough that weather is mostly made of water's hesitation: fog, dew, rime, a hanging mist that hasn't decided to be rain. There are no rivers. There have been no rivers for longer than there have been people. The Floor is dry in the way of old cloth — dry, but always on the verge of taking damp, and everything that lives here is organized around the taking.

The vegetation, given this arithmetic, should be a scandal. Instead it is lush, and the trick is the fog. When the mist lies on the Floor overnight, every surface drinks: the moss lawns between the cushion plants go from grey to green in an hour; the table-pines, stunted into dense knee-high hedges, bead until they drip on themselves; the peat moor, cut in blocks for fuel, sweats in its stacks. In the Cuts — the pale gorge-channels where the dead rivers once ran — the green goes vertical. Both walls are stepped gardens without gardeners, ferns stacked on moss stacked on lichen, every ledge claimed, everything near-touching, the whole gorge a green cathedral so packed with life that the rock is only visible where a slab has recently fallen. Seep-springs weep from the walls at the "seams," thin threads of water that never quite dry. The gorges that once drained this land now fill it. Channels built to carry water away now trap it.

The Teeth interrupt the level. Six or seven of them, the last monadnocks of the spent range — round-shouldered, patient, going nowhere, remnants wearing the rime of a hundred thousand dawns. Nothing grows on them above a certain line except lichen in paint-thin maps. Climbers go up for the clarities.

The clarities: that is the word for what happens midday, when the weak sun — a dim coin, even overhead, at this altitude in this lateness — lifts the fog off the Floor in ragged sheets and the world becomes briefly, shockingly visible. Teeth standing on the plain. The Long Cut running away to the south like a scar. Then the sheets close and the light goes diffuse and shadowless again, and everything softens back into grey and green.

The tide comes in the evening. This is the engine of the whole region, and it keeps time with an accuracy that unsettles visitors. The fog rises out of the lowlands and enters through the Long Cut and spreads across the Floor like water finding a basin — and the town, which sits on the Rim where Floor breaks into Cut, harvests it. The Mesh Rows run along the Rim for nearly a mile: black mesh fences, tall as houses, and the fog that threads them condenses into long beads that run down into gutters, into the commons channels, into cisterns. On the condenser glass the dew files itself into long parallel lines, and no one has fully explained this, and no one is particularly alarmed by it.

Every roof in town is a condenser by law. Every gutter is public. Under every house there is a tank, and beneath the tanks the cisterns connect in galleries that predate three quarters of the houses above them. At the head of the town stands the Pumping Hall, whose slow stroke lifts the night's yield up to the Header — the high cistern on the shoulder of the nearest Tooth — so that gravity, not labor, does the distributing. The hall's exhaust flutes have gone out of tune over the decades into two or three low notes, and on quiet nights they sound, and the town denies finding this beautiful.

The motive power is wind and gravity and the small chemistry of water. Carbide lamps, drip-fed: here light itself runs on water, drop by measured drop, and the lamplighter walks the evening stairs with a tank on her back, watering the lamps. A light-rail tram crosses the Floor carrying peat and coils of new mesh, its winches turned by windmills that turn whether or not anyone needs them, because the wind, at least, is not rationed. The lamps smell faintly of garlic and wet stone. So does the town.

Sound is a resource here, nearly as scarce as water, and managed almost as carefully. Thin air carries little; fog carries less. What remains is a metronome of drips — every house has a count-glass in its gutter that measures the night's harvest, and neighbors compare counts in the morning — the soft chuckle of channels, the hiss of a lamp drinking, the hush of the Mesh Rows. Loud speech is considered a small theft. In the Long Cut there is an echo-train: set one drip going down a certain stair and it will knock its way through the gorge for a minute, fading, like something walking down and away.

The fauna can nearly be counted. Nine white wethers — pale, shaggy, slow — graze the fog edge along the Cut gardens, and the town's children know each of them by name and patch of missing wool. Fogherons stand in the mist for so long, so still, that moss establishes on their backs; they are hunting the midges the tide blows up from the lowlands, and they are five, this year. Above the fog ceiling, at clarities, you can see other birds — long-winged, high, riding light no one down here has — but that is all. In every cistern and half the gutters live pale newts, called tenants, never disturbed, because they eat the midge larvae and because the superstition predates the reasons.

The town keeps fog-time. The tide's arrival is the evening bell; its lifting is the morning one. The law is short: every roof drains to the commons; no one takes from a neighbor's drip-line; lamps are fed but not gorged. Drought, here, is a strange inversion — a drought of clarity. In the clear years the fog fails, the sun shows up often enough to be resented, the moss greys, the wethers move down to the seams, and the town feeds its lamps a drop slower and waits. What everyone actually waits for is the Answer — true rain, two or three times a season, sometimes twice a year in bad ones. When it comes, the machines are stopped and people walk out into the streets and stand in it. There is no law requiring this. There is no law needed.

Up on the Teeth, in the frost hours, the rime builds in feathers and the lichen endures and the old rock, root-deep and level with its own horizon, does nothing at all. The stone is finished here. It has been finished for so long that the water — arriving as fog, leaving as drops, ticking through gutters, feeding lamps and newts and nine white animals and a town — reads less like weather and less like industry and more like a single long contrivance the land is still working out. The Mesh Rows and the cisterns and the drip-fed lights are simply its newest devices. The water will keep them only as long as they hold the fog, and the fog, which has appointments to keep, always comes back through the Long Cut by evening. Everything else on the Floor waits for it in the proper posture: tilted slightly, pale, listening.
