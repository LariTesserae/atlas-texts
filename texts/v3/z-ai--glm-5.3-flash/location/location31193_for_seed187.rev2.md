You hear it before you see it: a chord with no orchestra — a low thrum woven out of three nearly-matched tones, beating slowly against each other, rising out of ground that looks, from the rim, like nothing at all.

The rim itself is barely a rim. This is the lowest ground for a day's walk in every direction, a shallow bowl no deeper than a man is tall, three hundred paces across — you could cross it in five minutes at an easy pace, and from its edge you can see every part of it at once. The slopes that gather it are planed smooth, soft as the back of a spoon. Nothing jagged survives here. Erosion finished its work on this country a long time ago and has been sitting, hands folded, ever since. What stone remains underfoot has been polished past texture; in the few places where the living skin grows thin enough to see through, you can make out ripple marks in the bedrock — wave-patterns pressed into hard rock by a sea that withdrew before anything now living had learned to make a sound. The hollow is what's left when a landscape is old beyond argument: not a monument, a remainder.

And the remainder is green. Or nearly — a deep, grayish, all-weathers green, matte, breathing.

Here is the strange arithmetic of the place, and it takes a moment of standing in it to accept: vegetation here is total. There is no bare ground. There is no bare stone. There is no bare machine. Every square centimeter of surface, from rim crest to the center of the floor, is covered by living film — moss cushions pressed shoulder to shoulder, liverwort rosettes, gelatinous crusts the color of old bottle-glass, lichens laid down in rings and provinces whose borders are fixed like countries on an ancient map, disputed by no one for centuries. The film runs up over the smooth rims and stops in a clean line at the crest, as exact as a waterline. It sheathes the boulders. It climbs the legs and flanks of the machines themselves and thins to a translucent skim across their painted steel. It would cover your footprint inside a day.

And yet the hollow is almost empty.

Because the film is thin. Fingermail-thick at the cushions, thinner than a fingernail across the flats. The entire ecosystem — every grazer, every predator, every root and spore and meter of territory — occupies a layer an inch high. The tallest living things in the hollow are the moss sporophytes: inch-high stalks raised each season like a haze of pale fillaments, a forest you could mow with a fingertip. Above that inch: sky. Fifty feet of it, then more, open and unshadowed, no canopy, no mid-story, no clutter — a world that is all floor and all ceiling with almost nothing between. When the light is low the emptiness is startling; the sun falls unbroken past your shoulder to the skin of the earth, and your own shadow is the largest shadow in the bowl.

The hollow stays alive on air. That is its rule, and it is an ordinary rule, honestly kept: cool air drains down here at night and pools, and the bowl — small, still, walled — holds that cold like a cupped hand holds water. By gray dawn every surface is beaded. Dew is the hollow's true rainfall, steadier than any storm: it condenses on the film night after night in the same quantities, the moss drinks it before noon, and the ledger zeroes itself by dusk. Twice a month, roughly, a real rain crosses the rim — the loudest thing that ever happens here, a broad applause across the whole skin — and it changes nothing. The film takes what it takes, the pools fill to their old lines and shrink to their old rings, and by evening the hollow is exactly what it was. There is one lens of standing water at the low point, fingertip-deep, a coin of sky lying on the green; it appears each dawn and is drunk back into the living layer by mid-afternoon. No streams. No channels. The only bare thing in the hollow is falling water.

The machines came from outside — nothing here explains them, and nothing here needs to. There are three. No walls, no roofs, no roads announce them; they stand bare on their skids like animals in a grove, squat frames of condenser fins and intake fans and photovoltaic wings canted at the open sky, and each one does, relentlessly, its single job: it chills the air passing through it until the air gives up its water. Atmospheric harvesters. They drip all day, each at its own interval, distilled water falling in counted drops onto the film below, which drinks it. Their paint is long gone. Lichen provinces cross their flanks in uninterrupted lines, as if the machines were hills. Only the thin amber glow of their indicator lamps still reads as artifact — ember-eyed, unblinking, patient — and their gentle waste heat makes three small warm islands where everything gathers on cold nights. Whoever set them here never built a road to them, or the road is gone the way all roughness is gone from here. They run. They water the world that is slowly absorbing them. No one tends them, or tending them looks, from a distance, exactly like the place tending itself.

Given water daily and warmth on tap, the fauna has filled the one dimension available to it: sideways. The skin teems. Sit down anywhere and wait, and springtails rise around you like pepper-smoke, numberless, settling again the moment you still. Mites herd the wetter seams. Beetles the size of raindrops tick and clatter across the moss in runs worn glossy — worn, at this thickness, into the very surface of the world. Small spiders hunt the borders between lichen provinces, unchallenged at their posts. At dusk, midges lift in slow columns over the warm coils, but they never really climb; the abundant life of this place hugs the ground like a spill, and the great volume of air above stays clean and vacant, a cathedral with a congregation one inch tall. From outside, a single species of bird crosses the rim each morning — the only commuter, the only regular visitor — drinks its fill at a machine's drip-line while the hollow's own noise closes over it, and leaves. Everything else, always, is already here.

Which brings you back, ears first, to the sound. Because the hollow keeps its noise the way it keeps its dew. On cool nights the pooled cold lays a lid over the bowl, and that lid bends every sound back down — the three machines' hums skip along beneath it, wall to wall to wall, refusing to dissipate the way sound does in open country. Add the drip-counting, the dry sand-hiss of springtail swarms, beetle castanets, the thin wind-note that lives only at the rim crest where the still air of the bowl gives way to the moving air of the world, and you have a soundscape that is loud not by force but by saturation. Nothing crescendos. Nothing dies away. It is a held chord, and after an hour your ear stops hearing it as event and starts hearing it as climate.

The dawn chorus here is identical every morning — not by any magic, only by equilibrium: the same singers, the same perches, the same abundances, amplitude matched to amplitude, day on day. The lichen borders do not advance. The film neither thickens nor recedes; what grows by condensation is grazed back by dusk. Even the machines' slow oxidation feeds lichens that keep pace with it. Change happens here constantly, and it all cancels: the ledger balances to zero every evening, has balanced to zero for longer than the ripple-marks underneath have had anything living to overlook them.

Once every few minutes — you notice it late, on your second night — the three machine-tones drift into unison, hold for a breath, and slide apart again into their slow beating. An accidental chime. The hollow's only clock. It tells nothing but *now*, and the hollow, which has been ending for longer than anything in it has been alive, is content to hear it that way: nothing here is going anywhere. Everything here is already there.
