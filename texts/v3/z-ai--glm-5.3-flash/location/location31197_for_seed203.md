# The Undergrown

There is no sky here, and yet there is light. That is the first thing to understand about the Undergrown — the vast cavern-world that lies beneath the shelf of the old country, and which, in the four hundred years since the Falling opened it, has quietly been winning.

## The Light

No one agrees on the light's source. It enters through the million fissures, sinkholes, and collapsed chimneys that pierce the crust overhead — shafts that funnel daylight down for what the surveyors claim are eleven kilometers in the deepest places. The light arrives changed: white as frost, cold-edged, arriving in columns so wide that weather happens inside them. It pools on the floors of the deep vaults and does not seem to diminish, as if the rock itself has learned to give it back. Where there is no shaft, there is lumenmoss — vegetation so total in its occupation of this world that even the light is grown. Walls of it, ceilings of it, whole hallways of glow two hundred meters thick and unbroken for a hundred kilometers in any direction. You do not walk through the Undergrown's dark places; you walk through its dim ones, and the difference is a matter of shade, not absence.

## The Green

And the vegetation — the vegetation is the world. This is not a forest with a floor. There is no floor. There is only plant, down to the stone, and the stone itself is less substrate than guest. Root-mat dozens of meters thick forms the actual terrain: swollen, braided, breathing. The great trunks of the pillar-willows rise from it like architecture — yes, like columns and vaults and buttresses, because that is what they are. The willows grew to hold the ceiling up. They grew to span distances no mason would attempt. Where the cavern roof gapes open into some void too tall to see the end of, the willows arch across it in woven roots, and you cross on them, and you do not look down, or you do, and you see that the crossing below you is also willow, and below that, willow again, green going down and down like the rings of some vast breathing onion.

The cold keeps everything slow. Frost silvers the upper leaves near the light-shafts; the deeper vaults hold their breath in a perpetual late autumn. Nothing rots quickly here. A fallen trunk is still a trunk — now a bridge, now a wall, now a long green hill — a century after it fell. Death is a building material in the Undergrown, not an ending.

## The Water

Water is scarce and precious and arrives on the world's own schedule. There is no rain as such. Instead, once or twice a day, a given region *exhales*: the root-mat shudders, the moss-light flickers, and a fine cold condensation-fog beads out of every surface at once, running in thousands of thread-thin rivulets toward the gutters the plants have grown for exactly this purpose. The locals — the few who live here — call these moments the *drinks*. Everything stops for a drink. Beetles, birds, people, the moss itself. You learn to carry a folded leaf-cup. Missing the drink means a dry, itching night.

In the deepest vaults there are true pools — black, glass-flat, ringed in white frost-flowers — where the water has gathered over centuries and the willows will not approach. Nobody knows why they won't. Nobody swims.

## The Turbulence

For all its cold patience, this is not a still world. It is young — the Falling that opened it is recent, geologically speaking, and the Undergrown is still *settling*, still deciding what it is. Vaults collapse and are regrown within a season. New shafts crack open and pour daylight into places that have never had it, and in those places the vegetation goes briefly, gloriously insane — a riot of vertical growth, tendrils climbing tendrils, a green fountain racing toward the light, so fast you can sit and watch it happen. The surveyors' maps are obsolete before the ink dries. The plants redraw the world weekly.

Sometimes the whole root-mat moves — a slow, deep swell passing beneath your feet like the flank of a sleeping animal, a kilometer-long wave of growth rolling toward some need you'll never know. The quiet that follows each swell is enormous. Sound here is a budget, spent carefully: dripping, creaking wood under strain, the papery rustle of a million leaves adjusting to a change in light no human ear detected. Once an hour, somewhere far off, a trunk splits with a sound like a muffled cannon, and the echo takes a long time dying.

## The People and Their Marks

Human presence is thin, scattered, and humble. There are no cities. There are *stations* — single structures, each one grown rather than built in the truest sense: a willow trained over forty years into a sheltering spiral, a room with a chimney of living wood and a door that is a grafted joint. Tools are bone, hardwood, sharpened stone; iron is rare and treasured and mostly inherited. The station-keepers are gardeners, really, and their work is negotiation — pruning here, redirecting there, asking the green, in the slow language of cuts and grafts and compost, to leave a floor, hold a roof, spare a well.

Every few days a traveler passes through: a sap-tapper, a seed-runner carrying cuttings between stations, an occasional surveyor with a brass instrument and a haunted look. They trade in what the Undergrown offers — lumenmoss pressed into candles, frost-berries, willow-sinew rope — and they leave marks: a name carved into a training-post, a handprint in dried sap on a lintel. That is the extent of the built world. Small warm rooms in an ocean of green, each one a candle of intent in something that was never made for people and has, so far, tolerated them.

## The Fauna

Animals are rare enough that each one is an event. Pale moths the size of hands, drifting up the light-shafts in spirals. A species of flightless, moss-colored grazer — the *tumber* — whose low hooting is the closest thing this world has to birdsong. In the deepest vaults, sometimes, something large moves in the periphery of the glow and does not come closer, and the keepers have an agreement with it, unspoken and ancient-feeling, though the Undergrown is far too young for anything to actually be ancient here. Everything is new. Everything is improvising. The moths and the tumbers and the thing in the deep are all making it up as they go, same as the willows, same as the keepers, same as the light.

## Standing There

You stand on root. Above you, a shaft of cold white light descends through kilometers of green dark, wide as a cathedral, and inside the shaft there is weather — a slow snowfall of pollen and condensation, glittering. The mat breathes under you. Somewhere a trunk groans, and the sound travels away through the green like a rumor. Everything is quiet, and everything is busy, and the world is growing so fast, in the dark, in the cold, that if you stood still long enough — the keepers swear this is true, and they have no reason to lie — you could feel it.
