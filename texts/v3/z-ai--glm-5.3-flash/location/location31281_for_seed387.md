The sound arrives first. Down here it always does, because sound in water is not a visitor but a substance — it has weight, it has weather, it fills the hollow of your skull the way the water fills the hollow of the rock. You will hear the city for ten minutes before you see any of it: a roar with a floor to it, a bass note that never quite resolves, and woven through it a clangor of rhythm — pistons, chains, the ticking of something metal being turned — as if a factory were running inside a cathedral organ inside a storm.

The way in is called the Throat, though the keepers don't call it that where you can hear them; they call it nothing, because to speak of it you would have to stop, and you cannot stop there. The Throat is a gallery of rock below the floor of the ocean, and the ocean leans into it twice a day like a lung. The current takes you. It is warm — that is the first surprise, the warmth of it, bath-warm and mineral-thick, water that has passed through the hot bones of the earth on its way to you — and it is loud, and it is going where you are going, and there is no arguing with it, only steering.

Then the hollow opens.

It is not large. That is the second surprise, after the roar and the warmth: a whole sea, a whole city, a whole sky of black water, held in a cavity that a good walker could cross end to end in ten minutes. The mine-shaftmen who opened this place, generations before anyone now living, broke into a void beneath the seabed no bigger than a village square squared and stacked — an amphitheater of stone — and when the sea found it, in whatever year the sea found it, whether it broke in or was let in (the keepers tell it both ways and will not commit), the void became this: a pocket of drowned world with an entire town folded inside it, visible all at once, rim to rim, like a thing built to be looked at. Every lamp in it is a dim sodium-orange point. You can count them. There are forty-one.

Everything between the lamps is water. That is what the emptiness here means — not absence but substitution. The streets are canals. The squares are cisterns. The market, such as it was, is an open volume of shimmering current with the stumps of stalls still bolted to the floor, and the space where the crowds were is now the clearest thing in the city, limpid, warm, moving in slow vortices, almost nothing in it per cubic meter except pressure and noise and light falling off toward the walls. A city built for six hundred holds, at last count, twelve.

The architecture has given up on straight lines. That took centuries, and the centuries were thorough. Nothing square survives down here; every edge has been rounded, every corner kneaded, the whole settlement polished like river stones or sea glass. Archways have eroded into spillway curves. Building fronts have gone from facades to the smooth leeward flanks of reef boulders. Bronze has aged to the color of wet charcoal, with green weeping out of the seams. The stair treads are dished into shallow cups — worn by feet or by current, and after this long, no one can say, because both have used them equally. This is the paradox the place runs on: it is violently, continuously changing, and it has not changed in a hundred years. The turbulence is the steady state. The storm is in balance.

Twice a day the tide comes down the Throat and breathes through the city, and the boulevards become rivers with standing waves that hold their shape like sculpture. The flood tide turns the great shafts — vents that rise toward the seabed overhead, capped now except one — into organ pipes, and the whole hollow becomes an instrument played by the moon, a note rising through the water that you feel in your molars, in the flat of your sternum, in the water your body is mostly made of. During hurricanes on the surface far above, the pressure pulses come down the uncapped shaft like piston blows, and every window in the city flexes. The keepers can tell you the weather topside by the ache in their hands.

And threaded through all of it, the machines.

The Pump Hall sits at the city's center, and it is the reason anyone is still here at all. A rank of engines — thermals, the keepers call them — drink warm water from the vents beneath the floor and cold water piped up from a deeper gallery, and turn the difference between them into motion. Flywheels the height of two men spin in their housings, throwing moving shadows against the stone. Connecting rods walk in their long slow stride. A water-driven lathe still turns in the adjoining foundry, cutting parts for engines that cut parts for the lathe: an industry whose entire output is its own continuation, plus three rooms of air.

The three rooms are the truce. A tower, a hall, one low house with a garden wall around a courtyard that has held nothing but darkness for a century. The pumps keep them dry. Not mostly dry — dry, the way the inside of a bell is dry. The water takes everything else; the pumps hold exactly these; and neither side has yielded an inch in living memory, so the war has become a treaty, and the treaty has become a fact of geology. Inside the dry rooms the silence is so total that visitors flinch. The keepers, who are born down here more often than not, sleep badly anywhere else. They say the quiet screams.

Light is rationed like everything else. The lamps are sodium-orange and few, and their glow dies within a few meters, which suits everyone, because the routes are marked instead with paint made from scraped bacterial mats — pale, faintly phosphorescent smears along the walls at hand height, refreshed by someone, always, and always slightly luminous, so that a keeper moving down a dark gallery looks like a constellation being slowly assembled. And through the lamplight, everywhere, drifts the snow: white motes fine as flour, bacterial, blooming off the warm vents, feeding on the minerals, settling on every leeward surface in a felt so thin it looks like frost on bronze. Nothing here that could rot has not rotted. What remains is only what the water cannot digest: stone, bronze, glass, and the snow, which the water carries and cannot kill.

The fauna keep to the margins the way the lamps do. Pale shrimp gather at light and scatter like sparks when your lamp swings. Ghost-white tubes stand at the vent mouths, soft as candles, withdrawn into themselves when the current rises. Once a year — the keepers mark it, though it does not keep a schedule — a blind eel as long as a rowboat comes up the deep gallery and cruises the Roof, the high ring of rock where you can see the whole city at once, and whoever is abroad douses their lamps and holds still in the warm water and lets it pass, feeling the push of it through their body like a slow door opening. The humans are the rarest fauna of all. You will see a moving lamp along a gallery; you will hear, passed hand to hand along a rail, a single knuckle-rap of greeting traveling faster than any swimmer; you may spend a season in the city and meet two of the twelve. Enough to grease what squeals — a squeal means metal is being eaten — and to ring the bell.

The bell is bronze, in a small campanile that the current has eroded into the shape of a flame, and it is rung once at every turn of the tide, by hand, because the keepers say a bell should be rung by something that owes it something. The rest of the time the city speaks for itself. The acoustics here are strange in a mild, patient way: a wrench dropped three streets over arrives at your ear as if set down beside you, whispered through the stone. And sometimes, just at slack water, in the gap between the roar leaving and the roar returning, the residual sound in the cave — the echo of the echo — organizes, for a few seconds, into something with a shape. The keepers do not call it music. They say the water is practicing, and let it go at that. Down here that is not a mystical claim. Everything is practicing. The stone is still learning the shape the current wants, and the current is still learning the shape of the stone, and after all these centuries they have gotten close enough that the fit is almost quiet.

Almost. The flood comes back down the Throat as you are leaving — it always comes back — and the note rises in the organ shafts, and the engines take up their stroke, and the standing waves stand up in the boulevards, and the forty-one lamps burn orange and steady in the brown-black water while the whole pocket-world, small enough to hold in one beam, fills itself with its own voice from bottom to top. Nothing here has ended. It has only agreed, at last, to the shape of the noise it makes — and the noise, being deafening, being warm, being continuous, sounds very much like a city that intends to keep its three dry rooms, and its twelve souls, and its bell, for a very long time.
