# Dewhold

You come down to it by the Low Stair, forty steps cut through rock that still remembers being cut — the borer's spiral is crisp on the walls, unsoftened by any stain, the stone wearing only the first thin bloom of age, like the skin on a plum. The Ash Door above is iron and mostly kept shut. Below it, the air changes before the light does. It thickens. It warms. Within twenty steps your skin begins to bead, and by the last step you understand that you have walked out of weather and into breath.

The chamber is small. That is the first surprise. Every rumor makes it a cavern, and it is not: it is a single pocket in the earth, forty paces across, terraced up its walls in four galleries, floored in warm stone, vaulted overhead in cool. You could cross the whole town in five minutes. Dew Street — the main causeway, lashed timber and copper, running the chamber's long axis — takes four of those minutes to walk end to end, and in those four minutes you pass two districts, a registry office, a distillery, a seed vault, and a gutter that has been the subject of a civic dispute for eleven years. The town is the size of a chapel and behaves like a city. Everything here is close enough to touch, and everything is touched.

The second surprise is the light. It is dim, but it is alive. The vaulted rock above is seeded with lampmoss, cultivated in constellations, and it gives off a cold green glow just strong enough to read a ledger by, if you are patient. Which everything here is. At the work-stations, amber wick-lamps burn in glass hoods, small warm suns for the hands only. Otherwise the chamber keeps its soft submarine light, green and dim and everywhere slightly different, so that the whole town has the feeling of standing at the bottom of an aquarium.

Water is the industry, the clock, the god, and the weather, and it arrives by no road at all. It comes out of the air.

The heat is local and comes from below — young fissures in the floor, still widening at their own patient pace, exhaling a slow constant warmth that rises through the chamber and loads itself with what moisture the gardens and the gutters give back. Twice a day the Warm breathes harder. This is the Breath, the town's tide: for hours the hot fog climbs, thickening, clinging, until the whole chamber hangs at the edge of rain. Then the air passes — slowly, slowly, drawn by the great fans — up through curtain after curtain of condenser fins, cool metal fronds bred from some old fern and now generations past resemblance, metal-sapped, frigid to the touch. The fog sheds itself on those fronds point by point, and the whole ceiling of the town becomes a beaded string, and the harvest hour begins: a rain that never lands, ten thousand threads of drip feeding the gutter-lines that lace overhead like a canopy of plumbing. The gutters chuckle. The sump-basins brim. Then, for a few hours, the air goes almost dry — a small untrue desert hour, dust visibly adrift, the only dryness in town — until the Warm breathes again.

The clock that measures all this is itself a drip: a fine pipe over the registry, feeding a marked flask, the hours counted in drams. The town has no bells. It has a polyrhythm of water instead, irregular and never silent and never loud, the sound of slow rain indoors, forever.

The machinery is the third surprise, and the strangest. Dewhold does not build its machines. It raises them.

The valve-pods are grown — woody pods trained over decades to open at pressure, clicking softly as they vent. The pipes are glassroot, coaxed along wire armatures until they fuse clear. The great condenser fronds are tended like orchards, pruned, grafted, mulched with salt-scrapings from the sumps. Even the pumps are animals of a kind: slow lumbering things of lignified fiber and brass, warm to lean against, and twice a year the keepers walk the big pumps back from the heat — because the pumps creep, a handspan a season, always toward the Warm, the way a beast drifts toward a falling feeder — herding them back to their berths with pats and low humming. Machine-husbandry, the registry calls it. Every pump in town is sung to. Not for sentiment: steady low vibration keeps the valve-pods limber, and a pump raised without song sticks by its third year. The lullabies are agronomy. Apprentices learn them before they learn to solder.

At the chamber's heart stands Grandmother — the one true engine, raised, not grown: a heat-differential machine, a flywheel of laminated horn and copper taller than a man, driven by nothing but the difference between the hot floor and the cool vault. It is an engine of pure gradient, doing slow arithmetic between temperatures. One revolution every forty heartbeats. You do not hear it so much as feel it, in the teeth, in the floor, a hum beneath the drip like a note held under water. Its axle turns the great lazy fans in the vent shafts, turning with the unconcern of weather. Everything else in town is quieter than Grandmother, and Grandmother is very quiet.

The gardens drink first. That is law, old law, and no one argues it, because the gardens feed the machines and the machines feed the gardens, and the people are what's left over — which the people find a comfortable arrangement. And the gardens are lush past all reason for a place with no river and no rain: every terrace wall is planted, every ledge, every pipe-run wickable is vine-wrapped, wicking stray drip back to root. Mushrooms farm themselves in the old pipe-banks. Fern-fronds crowd the walkways until a person passes through green. Nowhere in the chamber — nowhere — is there a bare hand-span of surface. The density is total: plants, pipes, gutters, machinery, dwelling-niches, all interleaved, all touching, a town grown to the exact volume of its cave. It takes ten minutes to walk the top gallery and you will still miss your turning, because the turn is a root-stair disguised as a hedge.

The fauna is sparse and the keepers count it like heirlooms. The spring census records forty-one tenders; one hundred and six dew-snails, small and clear as beads, migrating up the condenser fins at a pace you can out-walk while standing still; three fin-frogs who live on Grandmother's warm axle housing and are named; gutter-moths, uncountable, which emerge only at harvest hour to sip from the drip-threads, their wings the loudest animal sound in town. Deeper creatures keep to the sumps — pale shrimp, eyeless, faintly glowing, retreating into gravel when the basins drop. The tenders themselves are quiet in the way of people who live inside an ear. Sudden noise spoils the Settle — a dropped pan can clot a whole harvest into gruel — so the town keeps a hushed register, and the children are taught tellsign, a grammar of fingers, before they are taught speech. Conversations happen at a whisper's distance
