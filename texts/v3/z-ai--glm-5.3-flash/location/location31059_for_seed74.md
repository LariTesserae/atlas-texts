You drop into the Weft the way you'd drop into a cellar. There is a rim — low, mossed-over, barely worth calling a ridge — and you walk down its short slope and the air changes against your face: cooler, thicker, holding its moisture. Mist pools here because this is the lowest ground for miles, a shallow dish the river left behind when it cut itself a deeper bed elsewhere and stopped bothering to visit. The maps give the basin a name because names make things findable, but the Weft is small — a half hour's walk across at an unhurried pace — and bounded, and old, and it has the evenness of a place that has been in balance longer than anyone keeping records has been alive. Nothing here is dramatic. Nothing has been eroded into spectacle. It has simply settled, the way a stone settles in a streambed, and stayed.

The ground itself is the first fact and the governing one: it is alive, all of it. Not covered in plant — *made* of it. Moss turf a forearm deep, springy underfoot like a mattress that has been growing for centuries, stitched through with the brown threads of old generations of itself. At the margins the turf thins to lichen crusts, grey and brittle, and at the rim it gives out entirely onto bare mineral ground, which is how you know the Weft ends and the ordinary world resumes. Paths here are not scars but agreements: where people walk, they sweep the turf back and keep a thread of pale mineral bare, and the turf — polite, patient — holds itself off the path's edge and waits. A path on the Weft is a treaty. Let it lapse for ten years and the ground closes it like a wound.

Water is present everywhere and nowhere. No streams, no springs worth the name, no rain you can rely on. Instead the Weft drinks the sky. Every clear cold night the whole basin sweats: dew beads on every leaf-edge, every grown rafter, every wire and rail, and toward dawn the air above the turf turns faintly silver with it. At dawn the ground exhales — a low mist lifting off the plain in one soft motion, the night's harvest given back to the light — and drip-lines form under every eave and gutter, ticking, counting down the morning. There are dew ponds, shallow clay pans that fill overnight and stand glittering by ten o'clock. There is the seep at the north rim, a wet gleam no wider than a hand, which trickles into the town cistern and is rationed by custom older than the cistern. And there is the Sough: the dead channel, a long smooth curve in the ground with banks worn soft as driftwood, dry forty-eight weeks of the year. Some years the river remembers. Water comes down the old bed — for two days, or six weeks, nobody can say in advance — and when it does, the town puts its flat barges on the water and ships a year's pressed peat downstream to wherever the river goes. Nobody here has followed it. The barges come back empty, which is answer enough.

The town is called Wringsend, and it sits in the west of the basin with room to spare. It is small — a few hundred souls — but it is a *town*, not a hamlet, in its manner: bells, shifts, rights, a ledger, a works foreman, a lamplighter for the eleven electric lamps. Its buildings are grown, not built, and layered the way a tree is layered.
