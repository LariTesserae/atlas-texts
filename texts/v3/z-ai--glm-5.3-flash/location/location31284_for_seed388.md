# Fenharrow

You hear it long before you see it, because the fen is flat and sound carries across it the way ripples do: a slow, enormous heartbeat, two deep strokes to every ten of yours. That is the pumping engines at Fenharrow, turning, each of them, at the pace of a resting pulse. The sound arrives as a pressure you feel through the soles of your boots on the causeway, a beat the ground itself seems to keep.

Then the town comes up out of the haze, and it is smaller than the sound prepared you for — a hamlet folded into a shallow dish of country not a mile across, bounded by the black alder-carr on one side and open reed-marsh on the other. Five minutes' walk would carry you end to end, if anyone here ever walked anywhere in five minutes. The sky does most of the vertical work in this place. Low cloud the color of wet slate hangs down until it seems load-bearing, and the sun is a pale coin behind peat-smoke, so that the whole basin sits in a permanent amber half-light, rust and olive and the brown of steeped tea. At "night" — there are no true nights while the furnace burns — the undersides of the clouds go red, and the town glows faintly from its own reflected fires.

Everything is wet. The water table stands two feet down; dig anywhere and you hit it. The canals — the Levels, they're called, twelve of them laid out in long rectangles like the fingers of a hand pressed flat — carry water so thick with tannin and iron that it is properly black, and warm. The springs come up warm out of the deep bog, and that is why no frost has been recorded here in living memory, why fog stands in the streets for days at a time, why every surface beads with condensation: brick, iron, skin. Every wall in Fenharrow weeps. Moss takes the seep lines and runs green down them in long wet seams. Wiping is a devotion here; half of daily life is wool-grease and rags, an unending quiet war on rust that the town has fought to a draw.

The town itself has arrived at its draw. It stands on rafts of timber and alder piles, and the whole of it sinks about an inch a decade, so the town raises itself, course by course, a generation at a time. Buildings sit near-touching, layered like the sediment they stand on: streets that were once rooms, rooms that were once streets, floors added atop floors, pipes running through houses the way veins run through leaves. In the Lower Row, the oldest quarter, the raising lost its race with the sinking, and now a drowned street stands knee-deep in black water — reeds growing through the windows, rust blooming off the old ironwork in orange corals. Nobody calls it ruined. They call it resting.

Because that is the thing about Fenharrow: nothing here is abandoned in the way of ruin. Everything is either in use or being slowly reabsorbed. The marsh, the works, and the town have long since settled their arrangement — you cannot say where the machine ends and the wetland begins. Reeds thrust up through the cobbles and nobody pulls them; they are crop and thatch and filter and fuel. Ferns crowd the shaded chinks of the pumping halls. Willow and alder are coppiced on the drier islands in tidy rotations, charcoal for the forges, and the moss is harvested off the north walls like a field. The furnace slag is poured wet into glassy black terraces at the town's edge, and the reeds colonize the cooling heaps within a season, and in a hundred years those same heaps become ore-ground again. The town mines its own old waste, patiently, on a schedule measured in the lifetimes of its grandmothers.

The ore is why Fenharrow exists. Bog iron, dredged from the ore-beds — long flooded fields worked in slow rotation, each bed resting forty years between harvests, planted the way an orchard is planted, for grandchildren. And here is the town's quiet strangeness, admitted only in lowered voices: the ore regrows, which is known for bog iron, but here it regrows *fast*, and fastest of all nearest the works, as though the marsh has spent its centuries learning what the town wants and bending itself to provide it. The dredges move at walking pace. The great beam engines — there are three, named Patience, Temperance, and Grace, and they are two stories of iron apiece, flywheels like millstones, breathing in slow strokes that you can set a clock and a childhood and a funeral by — the engines exist to hold the water back from the beds and lift it where it's wanted, and they run on peat and charcoal and steam, and they have never once, in anyone's memory, stopped for longer than an hour.

That hour comes once a year, and it is called the Hush. All fires banked, all engines parked off their dead centers, all hammers laid down, while scale is cleared and sluices are reset and caulk is driven home. And in that true quiet — the first in a year — the marsh hums. One long low note from nowhere and everywhere, felt in the teeth, and the rainbow films of iron-bacteria that sheen the black water shiver into slow rings. Everyone comes out into the streets to hear it. Nobody speaks afterward except to fix the date of the next.

No one explains the hum, and no one explains the animals either, because there aren't any. This is the first thing a visitor notices, once the noise stops astounding them: a warm wet marsh with no frogs. No midges rising off the standing water, which should be a mercy and feels like a held breath. No rats in the granaries, no eels in the Levels, no birds — not one, ever — and the barge-crews' dogs, riding the grain-barges down the causeway canal, will not set paw on the quay. There are herons carved on the old sluice-boards, worn smooth by two hundred years of hands, and the chapel weathervane is a crow, and children learn bird-words from books, and that is the whole of it. The only lives here besides the people are the green ones: reed and willow, moss and fern, the fungus farms tunneling the slag heaps for food, the iridescent bacterial skins on the water — and the wild pipes.

The wild pipes are mineral tubes, hand-thick, crusted orange outside and glass-smooth within, that the engineers find growing in the reed-beds — grown by nothing anyone can name, at a finger's length per decade, running for no gradient anyone can map, and carrying, when tapped, perfectly clean cold water. The town caps them and uses them and does not ask. It is that kind of place. Strange things, like everything else here, are absorbed slowly and kept.

The sound, when you live in it, becomes a climate. The engines' beat underneath everything, always. The forge-hammers going in overlapping choirs. The long hiss of iron entering the quench-ponds, and the steam that rolls off them into a town that is already a fog. The clack of sluices, the groan of the dredge cables, fog-bells on the canal, and over it all the reeds — thousands of acres of them, hissing in the warm wind like the softest rain the world ever made. Speech here is a patois of hand-talk and short shouts; faces are lit furnace-orange; the people are fluent in vibration, reading the state of the furnaces through their floors, telling their children apart by footsteps in the dark.

Trade runs out along the causeway: barges deep in pig iron and slag-glass, coming back deep in grain and salt meat, since nothing swims and nothing grazes here. And the rain comes down again, warm, the way it always is here — either beginning or just finished — and the fog thickens by a degree, and the engines keep their beat, loud and unhurried, the oldest sound in the basin, older than any building standing. Fenharrow has finished arriving. It is in balance now, marsh and works and town wound into one slow organism: the pumps for a heart, the reeds for skin, the whole of it breathing at the pace of a resting pulse, glowing faintly, waiting for nothing, working.
