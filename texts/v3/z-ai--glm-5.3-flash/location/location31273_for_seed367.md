# The Hollow of Nineteen Cities

You would miss it if you weren't told. That is the first thing to understand: the Hollow is *small*. It fits inside a sinkhole you could shout across — three hundred paces, maybe four hundred, rim to rim. Travelers cross the plateau above it for generations without noticing the crack in the earth, because the crack looks like every other crack in the oldest, flattest, most patient land there is.

But descend the hand-carved stair — and it *is* hand-carved, each step dished by centuries of feet, so that the middle of every tread is worn to a smooth gloss — and the Hollow opens around you like a held breath finally released.

**The dryness first.** No stream, no spring, no seep. The stone is bone-pale and stays bone-pale. Whatever water this place once knew, it gave up so long ago that even the memory of moisture has eroded from the rock. And yet — lush. That's the paradox that stops every first-time visitor mid-stair. Green spills down the walls in thick cascades of something that isn't moss and isn't vine, broad-leaved and heavy-headed, fed by nothing anyone can name. The botanists who came (there were botanists, once) concluded the plants drink *air* — pulling moisture from the warm updrafts that rise through the Hollow at dusk, condensing it on the undersides of their leaves. The whole ecology runs on breath, not rain.

**The heat is gentle**, a low constant warmth rising from the stone itself, as if the Hollow sits above some deep, slow ember. Nothing burns. Nothing freezes. Nights in the Hollow are the temperature of a sleeping animal.

**And the creatures.** This is what the Hollow is *for*, if it is for anything. The dense fauna of the plateau — the rock-lizards, the pale moths, the shelled things that clatter through the scree — all funnel here. Not to breed, exactly. To *be*. They come down the walls at every hour, and they pack themselves into the terraced gardens and the niches of the old masonry at densities that should mean starvation and constant war, and instead there is a kind of thronging peace. You walk the lower gallery and lizards shift aside for your foot like water. Moths hang from the ledges in clustered curtains, twenty deep, warm and faintly trembling. Nothing flees from you. Nothing here has needed to flee from anything in so long that the instinct has simply eroded away, like the mountains above.

**The cities.** Nineteen of them, the story says — though they overlap and stack so thoroughly that no one has ever counted twice and gotten the same number. Each is built of dry-fitted stone, no mortar, no metal, no tools beyond hammers and chisels and rope, and each generation built *on top of* the last, so the walls are geological: nine, ten, twelve strata of doorways and windows, most of them sealed now, bricked shut, housing only nesting things. The living occupy perhaps the top two layers. Below that, the Hollow's architecture is pure habitat — corridors at exactly lizard-width, roost-shelves, moth-hooks carved into lintels. The oldest builders stopped building for themselves a very long time ago and began building for what would come after them.

**Light**: dim always. The rim of the sinkhole shaves the sky down to a bright coin at noon; light arrives filtered, reflected off pale walls, gray-gold and forgiving. The deep galleries are lit by mirrors — polished stone panels, angled down the stairwells, a technology of patience rather than complexity — and by nothing else. No fire is permitted. Fire would drink the air the plants need.

**Sound** carries strangely. The Hollow is loud with small life — clicking, whirring, the dry rustle of ten thousand bodies rearranging themselves — but the stone eats echoes, so it arrives as a soft continuous *shhh*, like rain on the far side of a wall. There is no rain here. There has been no rain for longer than anyone remembers. The sound of rain is the closest thing the residents have to describe their home.

**It is active, though** — never still. Dawn brings the moth-curtains unfurling in slow spirals. Midday, the updraft begins and the green walls drink. Dusk, the great slow migration of the shelled things from the east terraces to the west, a glittering brown tide crossing the plaza in orderly silence, and every resident steps around it without looking. Weather cycles through the Hollow too, in miniature: a daily fog of condensed breath that pools at the bottom like a ghost of the sea this place lost.

Nothing here is violent. Nothing here is new. The Hollow settled into its final form perhaps ten thousand years ago and has simply *continued* — dense, warm, crowded, quiet, and hidden in plain sight beneath the feet of a world that flattened itself flat and forgot to look down.
