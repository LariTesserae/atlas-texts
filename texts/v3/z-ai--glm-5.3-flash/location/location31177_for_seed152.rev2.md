# The Welling

You come to it down a fold in the rim of the old country — a place where the gray, finished stone dips just enough to let a body through. Outside, everything is done: rock worn to a reasonable smoothness, thin dry grasses, a light that has been the same light for ten thousand years. Then the fold, a short descent between walls of pale unweathered clay, and the world changes in the space of twenty steps. The air thickens. It warms. It begins to smell of wet clay and cut green sap, the way a potter's yard smells, the way a greenhouse breathes. And the sound of the old country — wind, grit, your own boots — falls away behind you like a coat taken off at a door.

The hollow is two hundred paces across, maybe less, a bowl sunk into the lowest ground for days' walk in any direction. Its rim is high enough to hold fog and low enough to climb out of in ten minutes, though almost nobody does. Above, instead of sky, there is a ceiling of gray-white vapor that never fully lifts and never fully rains, and below that a light with no direction to it — even, warm, dim, like the inside of a lampshade. Shadows here are not shadows so much as slightly deeper warmths. You cannot tell what time it is by looking up. You tell it by the trees.

## The ground that is still deciding

The first thing to understand is that the ground is new. Not new-plowed, not new-built — new in the way a fingernail is new. The land here is being made, slowly, at this exact spot, and it has not had time to learn anything yet.

It shows in the color. At the rim, where the hollow meets the old country, the ground is gray-green and competent. Walking inward, it pales — gray to greige to cream to the color of fresh porcelain — until at the center you reach the font: a slow seep-line where the earth wells up like batter, like something poured rather than placed. There the surface is not surface at all. It moves. It has a sheen. It creaks, very rarely, the way ice creaks on a winter pond — one soft knuckle-crack of a sound, perhaps twice a day, as the whole hollow rises a thumb's width.

A thumb's width a season. It doesn't sound like much. It is everything.

Because the ground rises, it also drinks. Rain cannot gather here; there are no rills, no gullies, no channel of any kind — not one. Every ninth day or so a gray dimming comes over the fog-ceiling and fat warm drops land with a sound like fingers tapping bread dough, and before the rain has finished, the ground has finished swallowing it. The hollow has no streams because the land is too young and too thirsty to let anything leave. Water that arrives is kept the only ways a place like this can keep it: as dampness in the soil, as fog, as dew, as shallow pans of blood-warm water that appear at the low corners and vanish again in the dry week. There is one seep-eye the size of a table that never closes, clear over fine pale sand, and every creature in the hollow knows its address.

And because the ground rises, nothing old stays visible. Footprints last until the next rise smooths them. A dropped bead is given to the ground rather than searched for — *entrusted downward*, the people say, because here the dead and the lost do not surface with the seasons; they sink, carried deeper by the slow ascent, generation under generation. Dig anywhere and you find the town's history stacked like courses of a wall: old hearthstones, a child's footprint pressed into clay and fired by nothing but minerals and patience, the floor some grandmother was born on, now knee-deep below the present.

## Trees that make their own weather

The vegetation is lush the way a palace is furnished — generously, but with room around everything.

A dozen fountain-trees stand across the hollow, each one alone in its own circle of open ground, as if planted by someone with a strong opinion about spacing. Each trunk is a swollen pale column that stores the wet weeks in its girth; each crown is a burst of great soft leaves that comb the fog all night. By dawn every leaf is dripping, and each tree becomes a private climate: its own small rain falling into its own moat of ferns and round-leaved greens, its own column of cooler air. Walk from tree to tree in the early dimness and you pass through a chain of small rains.

Between the trees, the ground wears a skin of moss — dull sage in the dry stretch, and after the ninth-day rain it flushes to a vivid, almost wet-looking green within the hour, a flush you can watch happen if you sit still. Along the seep-line the ferns stand tall and pale as candle-feathers. Nothing litters. No leaf-drifts, no rot. Whatever falls is drunk into the young ground within days, so the whole hollow smells of clay and green and nothing else — no decay at all, a smell that takes visitors days to identify the absence of.

## The quiet and everything alive in it

Here is the strangest ordinary fact of the place: it is full of life and nearly without sound.

The fog eats sound. It dies a few arm-lengths from its mouth. But it is more than the fog. In a country this dim and this damp, voices would be wasted, and nothing here ever evolved one. The hollow's creatures say what they have to say with color, posture, scent, and slowness.

The largest presences are the grazers: low, wide, gray-rose animals with hides like unborn things, soft-footed, silent, mouths built to rasp. They eat the ground itself — the newest set cream-colored band of it, the mineral paste that is fresh enough to be food and firm enough to be floor. They move in a slow annual ring around the hollow, and where they have grazed, the ground is left polished, dull-shining like a fired glaze. Their habitual routes are the only roads the hollow has. The people did not make the trails. They inherited them from the animals, and keep them open by walking them gently, heel-first, the way children here are taught before they are taught anything else.

At dawn the moths drink. Condensation beads on every leaf, every wall, every grazer's back, and the moths come up out of the moss in pale drifting crowds to sip the world dry. Their wings together make a hiss like a page being turned, faintly, many pages — and that is the loudest recurring sound in the hollow, the place's whole dawn chorus.

Clock-birds stand in the warm pans on their long still legs, white as unlit candles, entirely mute. They signal with the slow swelling of pale throat-pouches, lanterns that light without sound, and with neck-signals exchanged across the water like courteous sentences. Dew-beetles roll condensation into beads bigger than their heads and roll them, silently, to holes you never find. In the pans live mud-sleepers — small, fat, dreaming fish-adjacent things that seal themselves into the pan-mud when the water leaves and wake with the ninth-day rain, an entire population resurrecting overnight in six inches of warm water.

And once in a while, at the fog-line where vapor thickens into moving walls, the hunter passes. It is long-legged and quiet the way everything here is quiet, and the only sign of its work is the mist parting in a clean moving seam — and afterward, somewhere on the ring, a patch of ground that shines a little differently, drinking down what the fog declines to describe. The townspeople watch these hunts the way people elsewhere watch weather: with attention, from a distance, without comment.

Somewhere overhead, in the fog ceiling, there are almost certainly bats — the moth numbers stay honest, so something must be keeping the ledger — but no one has seen one. Their voices are above the hearing of the place. Even the predators here keep their counsel.

## Ninehouses

Along the ring-trail, spaced with the same opinion as the trees, stand nine houses.

They are made of the font. Mud carried from the seep-line in reed baskets, pressed by hand over woven armatures, a course at a time — one course per season of rise, so every wall is a calendar standing on its end. The houses have to be renewed constantly: each rise submerges the lowest hand's-breadth of wall, and each household re-plasters,世代 by generation, so the houses grow upward like everything else, sitting on their own increasingly deep foundations of past. A marriage is celebrated by a couple re-plastering their house together with mud mixed by four hands. The town has no walls, no defenses, no locks. Nothing comes in from the finished country, and nothing here has learned to fear.

The trails are the grazers' gift, kept open by soft shoes. The cisterns are large leaves and hand-pressed clay bowls set under the fountain-trees' private rains. The fuel is dried fountain-leaf and the slow-burning sap-resin of the trees, and the hearthsmoke does not rise so much as settle, adding its own thin layer to the fog ceiling — one reason the light here is the light it is.

There is no workshop, no forge, no wheel, no blade heavier than a pressing palm. The second structure of the town is not built at all: it is a fountain-tree at the ring's center, trained over generations by patient pruning into an arbor, a grove-room with a floor of polished moss, where the nine households gather at the turning of the grazing year. Their only monument is vertical, and it is underneath them. The eldest of the Nine can put her palm on the floor of the arbor and say: here, this layer, this is the floor I was born on; it is two hands below us now, and warm to the touch in winter, the only floor that remembers.

The lore of the town is one sentence long: *loudness stiffens the mud.* Shouting, hammering, grief expressed at full volume — all of it is said to make the font sulk and the young ground stiffen before its time. Whether this is true has never been tested, because the town and the place agree on it, and in nine houses under a fog ceiling, agreement is cheaper than experiment. Children learn the heel-first walk before they learn words. Argument here is conducted in the register other places reserve for confession.

## What the place is

Stand at the font at dusk, on the last of the light.

The dew-fall has begun: the whole hollow exhaling its one long daily breath, surfaces darkening with beads, the clock-birds making their final silent pantomime at the shrinking pans. The moths have gone down into the moss. Foxfire moss at the seep-line lifts its faint green-gray glow, a hearth's worth of light with no heat in it. Nine threads of hearthsmoke have merged with the fog and become indistinguishable from weather. Underfoot, if you stand very still, the ground creaks once — a soft knuckle, the land adding a thumb's width to itself, deciding, still deciding, the shape of the next small forever.

Everything here is sparse and generous: nine houses, twelve trees, a few hundred quiet animals, in a room that could hold ten times that without feeling full. The space between things is the largest material in the place, and it is made of fog and low light. Nothing rots, nothing leaves, nothing erodes; the ground drinks its rain, its dead, and its own past, and pays for all of it by rising. The horizon does not exist inside the hollow. It is a room, warm and dim and damp, walled in old finished stone, floored in a surface still soft from the making — a room in which the world, this one corner of it, has never quite finished being born, and where everything that lives in it has agreed, by evolution or by etiquette, to let it finish in peace.
