# The Fumarole Market

Beneath the plain — only forty meters down, barely a burrow, really — a city has grown the way mold grows: fast, dense, and in the dark. It is young. Nothing here is older than a generation, and the walls still remember being rock. Edges are raw where the excavators chewed; ceilings show fresh pick-scars and the pale dental marks of bore-bits. No drip has had time to carve a single channel. The stone is unfinished, like furniture before sanding.

The heat is the first fact. The rock itself is hot — near-boiling in the deep seams — and the city exists because of it. Water is precious and intermittent up on the surface, so the founders did the arithmetic: the earth below sweats. Every structure in the city is, at heart, a condensation apparatus. Copper fins run along the walls like gills. Huge slow fans — industrial, belt-driven, oil-black — drag the hot humid air through stone galleries cooled by seeping groundwater, and the moisture beads out of the air onto the fins, runs down channels in the floor, and collects in squat glazed cisterns that sit in every doorway like fat brown hens. Water is the currency here. You are paid in condensate.

The scale is almost domestic. No cavern could hold a cathedral; the galleries are room-sized, the tunnels single-file in places, and the city compensates with density — every cubic meter is claimed, layered, bracketed. Bunk-niches stacked five high. Pipes and pulley-ropes and pneumatic message tubes braided along the ceilings like vines. A family can reach out from its bunk and touch the neighbor family's laundry. It is intimate the way a hive is intimate.

It is not quiet, exactly — but it is quiet the way a beehive is quiet: a uniform thrum. The fan-halls tick and whir. Steam valves exhale at intervals on a city-wide schedule, so the whole place breathes together — a long hiss every eight minutes, felt in the floor. Voices stay low; the stone carries them too well.

Nothing larger than a mite lives here permanently — no rats, no insects, the heat and dryness between condensation cycles see to that — so the ecology is the human one, plus the slime-cultures. Biofilm farmers tend pale translucent mats on warm stone terraces, scraping a sulfur-tolerant growth that ferments into the city's bread and its beer. The mats glow faintly — this is the light. Dim, green-gold, sourced from the vats themselves, supplemented by a sparse grid of electric bulbs whose warm orange pools mark the drinking stations and the forge-hall.

Because there is fire down here — small, managed, fiercely regulated. Smiths work the local iron in hooded forges where the draft is engineered to perfection. The city exports condensate upward by bucket-chain and imports grain, cloth, lamp oil. It has no animals, so it has no beasts of burden; everything is moved on rails pushed by hand, by children, by the old. The rails shine.

Rules of the world, as the locals would tell them:

- You do not dam another district's seep. Water flows where it was dug to flow.
- You listen before you dig. The rock is young and grumbles.
- When the breathing-hiss comes, you stop moving. It is the closest thing they have to a bell, a clock, and a prayer.

The surface, when anyone goes up — and someone must, for grain and for sky — is a hard bright pan, cracked clay to the horizon, and the miners come up blinking like moles, and the wind up there is so dry it hurts, and they always, always go back down to where the world is warm and wet and sweating, and everything drips.
