# The Nave

Come in at water level, because the water is where it begins, and the water is also made.

The fens are warm and black and slow, and they are engineered. Every channel you could boat down was grown — a canal of living root-wall, lined with a reed that seeds only where the keepers spill it. Fish-weirs stand in the shallows, woven from a bone-bright wood that does not rot in water because it was grown to be water's neighbor. The drowned plain goes on like this for a hundred miles in every direction: managed wetland, planted marsh, a green machine running warm and quiet. And rising out of it, on all sides at once, so that there is no horizon that is not it — the Nave.

It is not a tree, though trees were clearly its vocabulary. It is buttresses the size of mountain ridges, rising from the fen in tiers; it is walls and ribs and vaults, leaf-furred, weeping, going up and up until the eye gives the measure away to the weather. From this distance it has no edges. It has climatic zones. The low country steams around its feet; mist hangs in the buttress-halls; above, somewhere, a green haze replaces the sky, and the sun is only a brightness behind leaves that no one has ever found the far side of.

Warm down here. The air is tepid and thick and smells of sap and wet moss and, faintly, of animal. Water is everywhere and none of it is still — it sweats from every surface, threads down the channels of old growth, beads on the felt of moss that furs the outer walls. Wading birds stalk the canals on grown causeways. In the flooded galleries between the lowest buttresses something long and pale moves at midwater; the waders know its habits and keep to their stones.

You enter through a mouth of wood that was never carved, only persuaded — a doorway grown where a doorway was wanted, generations of hands and feet having taught the grain where to part. And inside, the world changes its proportions.

The interior of the Nave is almost entirely air.

That is the first thing, and it takes days to stop startling. The structure is a skin and a rib system and almost nothing else: vaults and galleries hanging in a dark the size of weather, shafts rising out of sight, the whole continental mass of it hollow, mostly empty, empty on purpose. The emptiness is not a lack. It is an artifact, older than any keeper, and it is the largest thing in the world here — a made nothing, maintained like a garden. Light comes down through the great light-wells in pale vertical columns, a few of them, kilometers apart, each one raining faintly: the air that rises warm through the shafts cools in the high vaults and comes back down as mist, then as fine rain, warm as breath. Everything the dim light touches is damp. Every surface weeps a little.

And it is quiet the way a cathedral is quiet — not silent, but scaled. Sound dies of distance here. A drip can be heard falling for a very long time. Once each day, at the turn of the tide of air, the whole structure creaks — a single sound, like a door made of sky — and keepers set their watches by it. Frogs call from the moss-gardens, one bell-note at a time, each arriving from so far away it seems to have been traveling since yesterday. When the exhale comes at the warm part of the day, the shafts sigh all together, a distant surf, and then the quiet closes again.

Fauna is abundant here, but abundance means something different at this scale. You are always inside animal presence and almost never next to an animal. Flocks cross the voids like motes of dust, taking a minute to pass through a shaft of light. Swifts live in the well-shafts and feed on the moths that climb with the warm air; gliders — pale, broad things like slow banners — hang in the updrafts for hours without a wingbeat. Soft-mouthed grazers work the walls in the gloom, trimming the algal felt, and the keepers follow them at a respectful distance, because a grazer's mouth does finer work than any blade. Dung and leaf-fall collect on the wide galleries and are turned into soil-frames — hanging gardens, thin fields swaying in space — and this is the ecology entire: everything loops, everything is fed by the weeping of walls and the falling of internal rain. Once in a lifetime, it is said, you may see the warden: a browser the size of a hill moving slowly along a rib in the deep dark, and the galleries trembling long after it has gone.

The keepers are few. A station per horizon, each one an inherited office: a hollow warmed by a grown flue, a rack of shell-blades and bone tweezers and flake-stone knives, cordage, spore-brushes, knotted cords for records. They do not build. There is nothing here to build with except the Nave, and the Nave is not made of dead material — so their whole technology is persuasion. They prune. They open a light-shaft a finger's width wider each year, and their grandchildren inherit a room full of morning. They cut a weeper and train the sap into a channel, and the channel becomes a stair-rail, a spout, a gutter over a bed.

This is the rule that makes the whole world possible, and it is the first rule that is wrong: the Nave grows where habit presses. Walk a path daily for a lifetime and your children will find it floored. Sleep in a niche and the niche becomes a chamber. Speak grief at a wall — the keepers do this deliberately, once, and never speak of it again — and within a generation there is a small alcove there that holds the sound, and you can put your head into it and hear what was said.

The second wrong thing is the sap. Cut into a weeping wall and the sap runs in patterns, and the patterns are the weather that is coming — three days ahead, reliably, in shapes the keepers read like scripture. Mostly it is rain-shapes, wind-shapes, harmless. Sometimes the sap makes shapes with no weather in them. A hull. A figure standing. The keepers wipe those away and do not record them, except that everyone knows they do.

The third wrong thing is only deniable, and you can live here years before you have to admit it: in the oldest voids, distances disagree. A gallery takes an afternoon to cross going in and a morning coming back. Lamps hung in one of the deep vaults, measured carefully against each other, drift apart over a generation, unhurried, like things being pulled by a current that is not air. Rooms are larger than their mouths. The keepers have a phrase for it — the deep places are still setting — and they schedule no work that requires a promise about distance.

Nothing here is new, and nothing is falling down. That is the eerie balance of the place. Tool-marks from thousands of years ago have healed into smooth scars. Thresholds are polished by bare feet that are no longer anyone's. Every year, in one day of the growth-tide, the Nave grows a hand-span everywhere at once — you can hear it, a long shudder through all the ribs at once — and every keeper spends the season cutting exactly that much away. The structure has been in this state longer than its people have had memory. It is not being built. It is not decaying. It is being kept.

Go up, at the end. Take the long shafts in the afternoon and ride the exhale as far as it goes, to the high vaults where the mist is made. It is no cooler up here, only less warm, and the light is a green dusk coming down from everywhere. Stand on a gallery railing-thin above a void so large that the gliders crossing it are mistaken for dust, and watch one — a single pale banner — take a full minute to cross a shaft of rain. Below you, kilometers of air. The fens are a smell, not a view. Somewhere a frog rings once and is gone.

And then the condensation finds you, as it finds everything: a bead gathers on the rail's grain, swells, lets go, and falls. You hear it fall. That is the sound of the place, in the end — one warm drop, released into all that made and tended emptiness, taking its time on the way down, and the quiet receiving it without echo, the way the Nave receives everything: patiently, in balance, slightly ahead of the weather, holding its breath until the turn of the tide.
