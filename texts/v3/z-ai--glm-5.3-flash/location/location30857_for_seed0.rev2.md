First comes the sound, and then comes the fog, and the fog is warm.

The basin lies low between old hills, and it is young — so young that the ground still remembers being made. You come down off the rim through a thing the fog does here: it pours. Over the low lip of the uplands it spills in a slow, silent cataract, a waterfall of cloud falling thirty feet and pooling below, and you walk into it and your glasses — if you had any — would never clear again. Above the rim there might be sky. No one in the basin knows. The fog is the sky here: a sourceless gray luminary that casts no shadows, because there is no sun to take one from. Under the open flats, the light is like the inside of a pearl. Under the canopy, it goes to true dark.

This basin runs for miles. You could walk it for days and not cross it, and in that distance there are many countries: drum-fields and stream-lines, mat-forest and bare stone, a fault of steaming cracks the walkers call the Warm Bones. But all of it is one climate, one animal, one long exhale.

**The ground is new**

Black stone everywhere, and none of it worn. The land rose or erupted recently enough that rain has not yet learned to round it. Edges are knife-sharp. Ropes of old flow stand exactly where they congealed, frozen waves, blisters burst mid-swelling, glass surfaces unscratched by anything at all. There is no soil — not a handful of it anywhere — and no rounded pebble in the whole basin. What soil exists, the forest manufactures and carries with it, and nowhere else does anything root.

Put your palm on the stone. It is warm. Not hot — warm the way an animal is warm, two degrees past comfortable, a residual heat coming up out of the making of the place. The fog lies over this warmth like a blanket over coals, and the basin breathes under it. In places the crust is thin and hollow, and a footfall rings it like a kettle. Whole fields ring this way — the drum-fields — plates of fresh stone over voids, and when the rain comes everything plays at once.

**Everything is wet, and there is almost no water**

This is the central paradox, and everything here is organized around it. Touch anything: damp. The stone sweats. Your skin beads in minutes. Every surface drips. And yet you could walk for a full turning of the fog and find almost nothing to drink. No lakes. No rivers of any size. Water arrives as fog and stays as dampness, a film, a bead, a running thread — and then it's gone, down a crack, back into the air.

The streams that exist are shy, thin affairs that appear from *underneath* the living mat without any visible source, run bright and cold across bare glass for a few hundred paces, and dive into a fissure as if embarrassed. The pools are temporary: at fog-tide high, the shallow crater-bowls fill overnight with dew-water, milky and warm, loud with small bodies for a few hours, and then they shrink to a stain. To drink here you wring the moss. You lick a leaf. You learn that the place is an atmosphere pretending to be a landscape.

Once in a long while real rain comes — brief, violent, warm — and it is an event. The drum-fields all boom. Every creature in the basin speaks at maximum volume for an hour. And then the stone swallows it all and the fog closes back over the facts.

**The mat**

The green does not grow on the ground. The green *is* the ground.

What looks from any distance like a forest floor is actually a raft — a floating crust of roots, moss, woven stems, and the compressed bodies of generations, thrown across the bare stone by life in a hurry. The forest has no floor; it carries its floor with it, and it creeps outward across the fresh rock a finger-width a season, an army advancing over territory it conquers by becoming. Under the mat: hollows, tube-voids, dark galleries. Above it: density.

Density is the word. Everything near-touching, stacked in layers. Knee-deep moss. Shoulder-height fern-brakes. Cistern trees with bladder trunks that swell with fog-water and sweat it down their flanks. Leaves like combs, fine-haired, built to wring moisture from moving air, channeling it inward bead by bead. Vines that tie canopy to canopy so thoroughly that the layers have no gaps — you could climb this forest for a hundred feet vertically without once seeing stone or sky, and never touch the same species twice.

And it is fast here. Cut a stem and it seals itself by "morning" — there is no morning, but by the next turn of the fog. A wound in the mat knits closed in weeks. A fallen trunk is gone in a season, eaten into new floor. Everything grows at the speed of a rumor.

**The chorus**

The base note never stops. It is insects — or something doing the work of insects — a hum that lives below thought, felt in the molars more than heard, and on top of it: everything.

Drips tick in the thousands, a rain-gauge metronome with no rain. The hollow stones boom where large things walk. Wing-tones pass overhead in doppler. Somewhere a boom like a struck cable, somewhere a ratchet, somewhere a thin silver whine that you feel behind the eyes. Voices overlap cycles with no shared schedule, so there is no hour of quiet — the chorus is the weather, and its storms and calms are bigger than any single animal.

Sound is the master sense here. In the pearl-gray open you can see ten feet; under the canopy, nothing. So everything listens. Skins have ears. The flying things — soft, huge, moth-shouldered — cross the whole basin on a single thread of echo, stabbing clicks into the fog and reading the world back. At fog-tide low they pass so close to you that you feel the pressure of each wingbeat, and they never touch you, because they have known exactly where you are since before you knew they existed.

**The handed ones**

On a warm stone you find a flake of black glass, freshly knapped, edges surgical, waste-scars clean. Not a natural fracture. Someone was here, worked this edge, and moved on — leaving the reject, because tools here are carried, never kept.

Then you see them: small, dark, long-fingered shapes working the mat's edge in the biolight. They cut fiber and twist cord. They hook fat grubs out of the root-raft with glass hooks lashed to green sticks. They are the only things in the whole deafening basin that know how to be silent, and they are silent now, and one of them is watching you from eight feet away with a blade held loose and low, and you realize every other creature has known you were coming for the last hour. They make nothing that stands. No shelter, no wall, no midden. Whatever they stop touching, the mat takes back within days. They build nothing because nothing built survives — the forest demolishes any structure, swallows any artifact, takes back anything that holds still. Edge, cord, hook, knot: that is the entire technology of the basin, and it is enough.

**The Stop**

Once — you hope only once — everything ends at the same instant.

Every voice, every drip, every wing-tone, all of it cut off between one heartbeat and the next, the whole basin holding one held breath. In that silence you can hear your own blood. It lasts half a minute or an hour; you will not be able to tell. The fog goes still and seems to lean. Something very large is moving without sound, and every small thing in reach has chosen the same defense: *do not be the only noise in the world.* Here, at the bottom of the food chain, silence is armor, and the deafening chorus you've been resenting is the sound of ten thousand creatures shouting together so that no one has to shout alone.

Then it passes. Somewhere a single drip falls. And the chorus detonates back into existence all at once, louder than before, a cheer, a scream, a whole ecology exhaling.

**The Warm Bones**

Along one flank of the basin a fault runs, cracked and steaming, a seam of the planet's own warmth left open to the air. The fog behaves differently there — it rises instead of falls, climbing out of the cracks in slow columns — and around it the lushness reaches its climax: growth so thick it has no names, pale roots plunging into warm stone, steam-fog beading on every surface in constant artificial rain, the air thick enough to chew. The heat-loving things live here. The rest of the basin visits.

And sometimes the young ground demonstrates that it is young. A crack opens with a flat concussion, swallows a hundred paces of mat whole, breathes steam for a week. The basin absorbs it without comment. A fog-tide later, the mat has already begun to knit across the scar, green over wound, the forest filing the event away as groundwork.

**The tide of fog**

There is no day and no night, so the basin keeps a different clock. The fog has tides. At low tide the gray lifts, the rim shows as a dark suggestion, the open flats glow faintly and you can walk by shapes; at high tide the world contracts to the length of your arms and sound becomes the only map, and everything that flies or hunts or loves does it on the tide's schedule. When a heavy high tide meets the canopy, the forest wrings itself out: hours after the fog arrives, it rains *under* the trees while the open ground stays dry — delayed rain, falling from leaves, out of all season. The creatures drink on schedule. The drips keep time.

Once per few tides, a species of white-flowering vine hits its cue and blooms everywhere at once — a single night of the basin lit pale with its own bioluminescence and heavy with scent — and by the following tide the petals are falling like warm snow and every scavenger in the basin is fat and loud, and then it's over, and the black-green order resumes.

**What is true here**

- Everything drinks the fog. Nothing here has ever learned thirst, and nothing here has ever learned a river.
- There is no day. The fog-tide is the only clock, and it cannot be read by looking up.
- Nothing in the open casts a shadow. Under the canopy, everything is one.
- The noise is a shield. When it stops, stand still, and think about nothing, and count your heartbeats like a fool who is glad to have them.
- Nothing built survives. The mat takes back whatever stops moving, and so nothing has ever been built — only carried, cut, and worn.
- The ground is new. It does not owe anyone stability, and it proves this occasionally, and heals itself, and keeps everything.
- Heat comes from below, light comes from nowhere, and time comes from the fog.

At fog-tide low, if you climb back to the rim and look down, you can just make out the whole basin at once: a vast dark breathing thing, gray-maned at the edges, seamed faintly with the glow-lines of its own green, rumbling steadily, the way a sleeping animal rumbles. It does not notice you. It noticed you hours ago.
