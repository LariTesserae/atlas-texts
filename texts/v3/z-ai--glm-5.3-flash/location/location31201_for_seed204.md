# Cradle

The mountain does its best to keep the secret. From the windward shoulder — from ten paces away, even — it is nothing but a fresh dome of shattered grey rock, sharp-edged, wet, smoking faintly with its own cold. There is no visible road, no tower, no glint. Then you come over the lip of the summit and the ground simply opens beneath you, a bowl four hundred paces across with a town folded into it, streets and terraces and a flat grey lake, all of it hidden the way a thing is hidden inside a cupped hand.

The bowl is a settlement scar. Eleven years ago, when this land was hauled up through the Shelf Sea, the crest sagged where the first great machines pulsed too hard, and the ground settled into a pocket. The engineers did not level it. They built inside it, because the bowl breaks the wind, gathers the water, and holds a few degrees of warmth like a palm holds a coin. Everything else on this mountain is new and brutal; the bowl is the one place that behaves.

**The stone remembers being a sea floor.** Cut any foundation here and the spade goes through grey mudstone laid down in quiet water, through seams of crushed shell, through a band of almost-black clay that smells of iodine when it's wet — and everything here is wet. The whole mountain is a squeezed sponge. It came up carrying its water with it, trapped in its pores, and it has been wringing itself out ever since. The weeps are everywhere: along every bedding plane, under every overhang, at the base of every fresh scarp, water standing on the stone in beads and sheets, running in threads that find each other and become rills, that find each other and become streams. The streams are weeks old. Every watercourse on this land is a prototype, feeling its way downhill for the first time, and the engineers in the town keep maps of them the way other places keep parish registers, because the first river of a continent is being drafted right now, in pencil, a few hundred meters long.

The springs taste of salt. Faintly, less each year — you can drink the land's childhood out of a cup. White crusts of efflorescence bloom on the stones where the brine evaporates, and at night, frost flowers grow out of the same cracks, so the rock carries two kinds of blossom, salt and ice, and neither is alive.

That is the rule of this place, spoken plainly in the town's charter: *nothing green yet.* Not a ban out of cruelty but out of sequence. The land is too young; soil does not exist here yet; the project below the horizon has an order for these things. So life, such as it is, lives thin. Along every waterline there is a skin of the simplest living — black and rust-red mats of bacteria, felted films of fungus advancing a finger-width a season, staining the weep-channels like old blood or rust. There are pale worms no longer than an eyelash in the wet mud at the spring mouths.
