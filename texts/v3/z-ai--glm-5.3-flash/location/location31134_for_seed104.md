There is no wind here, and there has been none for so long that wind has become a rumor the water tells about its youth. No one has named this place in an age. Names dissolve here at roughly the rate of stone.

Understand first that it is all sea. Not a sea on a world — a sea *as* a world, or as much of one as anything has bothered to map. There is no shore because there is no land to be a shore of. There may be a floor. Nothing that has gone looking has come back with an opinion. What there is, is depth in both directions: below you, water thickening and warming and going dark for fathoms beyond fathoms; above you, water thinning by gradations too gentle to find a border in, until it is not sea anymore but sky — breathable, luminous, going on. The transition takes what you would call miles and the sea takes no notice of, and the horizon here is a thing that happens overhead. You live, when you live here, inside the bright undercurve of a world, and the light of the high thin layers arches above you like a ceiling of hammered amber far away.

So the high country of this place is water. The downs are currents and the roots of drifting lawns. Elevation is measured in nearness to the ceiling, and the ceiling glows.

The light is strange, and it takes a while to understand why: it comes from everywhere and nowhere, dim as tea, warm as an ember under ash. There is a sun of some kind — a pale coin that brightens the upper water on one long slow day that lasts most of a year — but the sun is not the main source. The main source is the sea itself. This water has been drinking daylight for so long that it gives a little of it back. Call it old light: a directionless glow soaked out of a billion absorbed afternoons, dimming almost never. Swim under the vault of a kelp forest, into what should be blackness, and it is not black, only *less* — the dark here is always just dimmer water, and night, when the sea has one, is the whole world banking down like coals and never quite going out.

The warmth comes from the other direction. Real oceans are cold below; this one is inverted. The deep breathes heat. Every so often a column of it rises — a slow geyser, a warm throat opening in the abyss and climbing for hours, wide as a city, lifting weed and grain and you if you are in the way, wobbling the old light like a struck bell of temperature. The sea is warmest far down where nothing can see, and coolest near its own sky, and life here arranges itself in the gradient between: the gardens go up where the light is, the warmth-loving things ride the breaths, and everything else haunts the middle depths, the great mild latitudes, where the water is blood-warm and heavy as a held hand.

Because it is heavy. That is the first thing the water teaches you. It is dense past the logic of water — saturated, layered, syruped with minerals and spore and dissolved light — and it does not particularly believe in falling. You cannot sink so much as *descend*, slowly, by agreement. Stop swimming and you stop. Hang there. The sea holds you the way it holds its light: without effort. Moving through it is like moving through weather made solid; you feel the strata, sheets of current sliding over sheets of current, and they do not mix. They have not mixed in a very long time.

Here is the sea's deepest strangeness, older than the light: its turbulence has died. Every ocean churns; this one finished churning ages beyond counting, wore its own chaos smooth the way a mountain range wears itself to downs. The waves at its ceiling are not waves anymore — they are swells the length of districts, low and glassy as the backs of sleeping animals, final form, the last flattening of a motion that began when the world was young and has eroded itself to this. Currents run in ribbons of laminar glass, sliding past each other without a burble. Swim hard and look back: your wake is already gone. The water closes behind you with the smoothness of long practice. It is expert at forgetting the small, and only the small.

The life, by contrast, is everywhere — just not the kind with eyes.

The gardens are continents. Drifting lawns of weed, meters thick and hundred-mile wide, mat together and tear apart on schedules of centuries, nations of sargasso with internal borders of current you could sail along for a lifetime. Out of them rise the kelp: columns, true cathedral pillars of it, dark green gone almost black, growing upward for a kilometer toward the dim ceiling, each frond feeling along the old light the way a face feels along a wall.
