# The Weeping Terraces of Ansil-Keth

The town clings to a shelf of stone so high the clouds don't pass over it — they pass *through* it. That is the first thing to understand: at this altitude, the sky and the water have come to some old agreement, and the air itself is saturated, silvered, bright. The light here doesn't fall so much as seep, radiating from every direction at once, bouncing off the wet black rock and the shallow standing water until shadows are only faint suggestions, gray-blue smudges under ledges. There is no night worth the name; there are only degrees of white.

Water is everywhere, and it moves. The shelf the town occupies is terraced — long steps of worn limestone descending in irregular levels, and from every crack, every seam, every joint in the rock, water weeps. Not waterfalls, nothing so dramatic: ribbons and beads and constant threads, the cliffs sweating from a hundred thousand small mouths. The rock is black with centuries of this, slick and glinting, furred in places with stone-moss the color of tarnished copper. Beneath the terraces, the water collects in pools and channels the townsfolk long ago learned to guide, splitting the flow with low stone weirs, hand-stacked, mortarless, patched and repatched across generations. The whole settlement is an argument between people and water that has settled into an uneasy, working truce — erosion here is not violence but patience; the place has found its shape the way a riverbed has, and everyone knows the truce could end any afternoon.

Because it does, sometimes. The town is small — you can cross it in twenty minutes if the bridges are calm — but it is *dense*, packed tight as a hive. Houses of stacked slate and sod rise in crooked tiers, their roofs seeded with the only tall vegetation for miles: stunted pale sedges and a kind of rush that grows in hanging baskets from every eave. The buildings lean on each other. Walkways of rope and split plank thread between them at three levels, sagging with damp, and every flat surface is occupied — drying racks hung with lake-moss, fish-skin, bundles of reed. In the gaps between structures, water flows openly through carved stone gutters the size of a forearm, and children (there are always children) crouch at these, netting the small pale things that ride the current.

The fauna. This is where the strangeness lives. The wet, the cold, the relentless light have conspired to breed *abundance* — not large creatures, but uncountable small ones, layered into every niche. White moths the size of fingernails cloud above the pools at all hours, so thick at dawn they look like breath. Water-spiders skate in multitudes, and the cliff-faces are pocked with the mud nests of a swifter that builds in colonies of thousands, each colony humming — though here is the second strange thing: the humming has no sound. Place your hand against the rock and you feel it, a vibration like a struck bell that never decays, but the air stays silent. The town is noisy and mute at once. Everything moves, everything works, everyone talks — and yet the sound lies flat, swallowed. Voices don't carry here. The atmosphere is too thick, too wet, and something in it *drinks* noise. To be heard across a courtyard you must come close, touch shoulders, speak near the ear. The result is a culture of whisperers, of people who stand very near each other in the bright cold, a whole town leaning in.

Life under the water is stranger still. The pools are clear as glass and full of pale, slow animals — translucent shrimp-things, blind eels with frilled gills, and in the deepest cisterns, the *lamps*: soft-bodied creatures that glow faintly from within, the only light in town that isn't borrowed from the sky. The townsfolk never eat the lamps. They keep them the way other cultures keep fire, moving them between cisterns on wooden paddles, breeding them in the dark, and when one dies they bury it with a kind of grim gratitude under the hearthstones.

The tool-work is simple: bone, horn, slate, a little cold-hammered copper traded up from lower lands, shaped by hand into hooks, gutters, needles, weir-pegs. But the craft is not simple — you cannot be simple, working against water this determined. Every joint is fitted with a patience that looks like devotion. Every house has a sump. Every family keeps a child assigned to *listening* — pressing an ear to the foundations, feeling for the vibrations, learning the rock's moods.

And the rock does have moods. Twice or thrice a season, without warning, the terraces shudder and a whole district's weeping reverses — the cliffs *drink*, gulping down their own springs for an hour, the gutters running dry, the pools dropping — before the water bursts back out in a single gargantuan exhale, sometimes drowning a bridge, always flooding a cellar. Nobody builds at the weep-line anymore. Nobody has for three hundred years, and the scar of the old drowned quarter is still there at the town's lowest step, black roof-ridges just breaking the surface of a pool, furred with copper moss, a warning shaped like architecture.

The people of Ansil-Keth are not unhappy. They are wet, and busy, and hushed, and bright — a hive of small weathered lives stacked on a stone that bleeds, above the clouds, below a sky that will not let the water go.
