**The Rack**

You would miss it if the mountain didn't want you to find it. It's inside one — that's the first thing. Not on the peak, not on the slope: *inside*, in a vertical shaft the width of a grain silo that drops through eight hundred meters of blue-gray granite near the summit of a range that shouldn't be climbable. The shaft is the world. Everything else is rock, cold rock, the kind of cold that lives in stone and never leaves even in summer, because up here there is no summer worth the name.

The rack is what the inhabitants call the structure that fills the shaft: an industrial term, and accurate. It is a lattice of iron and brass scaffolding bolted into the living rock on all sides, dense almost past sense — platforms, gantries, ladders, pipes, cable-runs, chain hoists, condenser coils — layer stacked on layer with barely an arm's width between them. There is no floor and no sky, only up and down, and up means a narrow shaft of gray light falling from a broken vent near the surface, and down means the far-off tick of machinery you never quite see.

It is dry here. Utterly, aggressively dry. No water has touched this place in anyone's memory, and the machinery has been engineered around that fact: everything runs on **hygro-cycle oil** rendered from the fat of the local fauna (more on them), and the air is so arid that a spilled drop of anything wet is an event, mopped with felt rags and mourned. The walls weep mineral dust instead of moisture — a fine glitter of feldspar that settles on every horizontal surface and has to be swept daily, one of the settlement's oldest and most beloved chores.

The builders are the *skitterlings* — and this is where the world bends a little. There are no plants, no birds, nothing green. But the fauna is thick beyond belief: the shaft hosts a complete, teeming ecosystem of chitinous industrialists, none larger than a fist. The skitterlings themselves — pale jointed things with socket-jaw mandibles and a genius for tension-load calculations — build and maintain the rack. Other species fill the niches: *borer moths* that tunnel heat-exchange galleries through the granite; the squat, gloved *vanner beetles* that haul loads along the cables in long squeaking caravans; translucent *wickworms* that glow faintly amber from their combustion organs and serve, strung in glass tubes along the gantries, as the settlement's only lighting. The dim light you see by is animal. It breathes. It flickers when the worms are startled.

No single skitterling has ever seen the whole of the rack. Each knows its platform, its bolt-pattern, its four neighbors. The city functions anyway, the way a termite mound functions — which raises the question the visiting naturalists always ask and never get answered: is this a city, or one organism with an iron skeleton? The skitterlings, asked directly, tap their mandibles in a way the translators render as *both, obviously*.

The sound is the surprise. Given the density of activity — thousands of bodies moving constantly, loads swinging, pumps chugging on their oil — the shaft should roar. Instead it is mostly quiet: the rock absorbs everything, and the dry air kills echo. What you hear is close-range only: the *tick-tick-tick* of mandibles, cable creak, the soft hiss of a borer moth at work inches from your ear, and beneath it all a deep, patient hum from the deep-engine somewhere below, the great reciprocating heart that drives the ventilation fans and keeps the lower galleries from going dead-still. Conversation between platforms happens by tap-code on the railings, a dry wooden rattling language that travels beautifully through the iron.

And it is turbulent — not with weather (there is no weather down here; the temperature holds at a cool twelve degrees, always, the granite's own climate) but with *throughput*. The rack is perpetually being rebuilt. Sections are demolished and re-erected on a weekly cycle; scaffolding migrates through the shaft like slow weather fronts; the whole structure is never once, in any hour, the same structure. Skitterlings are born, mature in nine days, work for three years, and die mid-task, their platforms reassigned before the body has cooled. There is a ledger for everything, kept in mandible-punched brass foil, and the ledger is always behind, and everyone accepts this.

Young, this place is — by its own account maybe sixty years old, a fresh wound in ancient rock, still sharp-edged, bolts still bright, nothing yet softened or settled. The mountain was here for forty million years. The rack intends to be here for forty more, and then forty million after that, one rebuild at a time, in the dark, in the dry, in the cold stone heart of the tallest thing on the horizon.
