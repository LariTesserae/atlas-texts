The plain outside finished a long time ago.

There is no way to say how long. Nothing has moved on it for so long that the memory of mountains has worn out of it entirely — no hill, no ridge, not even the suggestion of a rise. It is a lowland worn down to the last millimeter of argument, lying at the end of its own weathering. And it is not earth anymore, not in any way that matters. To every horizon the ground is one continuous covering of ancient growth, unbroken, layered over itself an unknown number of times, so old that the plants stopped being plants-in-a-place and became the place. Growth here is a substrate. The world is made of it the way other worlds are made of stone.

Somewhere in that green there is a shallow dish, and in the dish, a doorway.

The doorway is not built. It is a fold, a place where the growth long ago wrapped inward on itself and left a low mouth of arched roots, dark and polished along its crown by the passage of uncountable feet. You go in bent, and then the ceiling lifts, and you are inside.

Inside is a hall.

Not a cave, not a clearing — a hall. Sixty paces across, perhaps; the roof vaulted thirty-some feet above the floor. A single-feature room with firm boundaries: the curved wall of living wood, the leaf-vault above, the woven ground. The air that meets you first is cool — cool the way cellar stone is cool — and damp in a way that is difficult to name, because nothing is wet, and yet everything is beaded. The whole interior sits at the edge of its own dew point. Every surface glistens faintly. The moisture has decided to live on the surfaces rather than in the air, and so the air itself is strangely clean, almost dry-tasting, cool and moving in a slow fixed pattern you can feel on your face but never see.

The light is ordinary, and that is the first impossibility. You are inside a sealed mass of vegetation and yet it is plainly, normally day. The roof is leaf, and the leaf is translucent in the way of a leaf held to the sun, and the vault admits a green-white daylight so even it casts almost no shadows — like noon under thin overcast, like standing in a greenhouse with the weather kept outside. It is bright enough to read by. It is exactly this bright, and no brighter.

The walls explain the age of everything. The bark stands in curved slablike courses, and every face of it is worn smooth — not broken, not crumbled, but polished, the way river cliffs are polished, as if the room itself were a stone in a very slow stream. Roots arc overhead like beams, and the crowns of the beams shine where some climbing animal has run along them for ten thousand generations. Nothing in this hall was built. Everything in it was either grown or worn.

The floor — if floor is the word — is root. There is no soil anywhere, no dirt, no litter. The ground is a living mat of interwoven root, springy underfoot, dry on top and cool-damp just beneath, and it is immaculate. Nothing lies on it. Whatever falls here is gone within the hour.

The water system is the strangest thing, and it takes a while to read correctly, because it looks like rain.

High on the curved walls, at two dozen fixed points, pale tube-animals sit embedded in the bark like organ pipes. They draw from the damp of the mat below — you can find their intake mouths near the wall's base, softly gulping — and they lift it, and at the top of each pipe a bead gathers and drops. It falls thirty feet and lands. The drips are constant. Not weather: metering. Each spout delivers its same beads at its same rate, and each fall-line has worn a smooth cup into the root-mat below, a polished basin exactly the width of the drip, some of them deep as a fist. Erosion in miniature — ten thousand years of identical drops, each cup a small polished recording of pure constancy. The hall's rain is manufactured, metered, and falls forever in the same circles.

In a few places the water gathers instead of falling — cisterns held in cups of fused leaf at the wall's base, black-green, cool, still. Things live in them. Quick, narrow, eel-shaped things, and every so often the still water snaps with a small bright flash — a hunt conducted by electricity, a tiny generator discharging in the dark of its own little pond. The sound of it is part of the room.

Because the room is loud. That is the second impossibility, and it takes longer to accept than the daylight.

The loudness has sources, and every source is an animal. Around the base of the walls, in worn niches, the millers work — heavy, armored, barn-sized-souled beetles that grind fallen leaf-matter between plate jaws, and their grinding is a low granular hum you feel in your teeth. The meal they produce is carried off by the drovers: big, shaggy, patient, six-footed beasts that move in single file along lanes worn glossy into the root-mat and the lower bark, worn so deep and so smooth they look artificial, but they are only old — the polished channels of pure traffic, freight routes achieved by nothing but repetition.
