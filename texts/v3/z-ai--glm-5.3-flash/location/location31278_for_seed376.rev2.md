From the high shoulder, the whole region looks like a hide laid over something breathing. Grey-green, unbroken, worn smooth into whaleback ridges and stepped benches that descend for miles toward a cirque at the center. Even at this distance it refuses to sit still. Mist lifts off it in slow columns. Whole slopes tremble faintly, the way a struck drumhead trembles, though nothing strikes them. The air at this altitude is close to freezing; your breath comes out as a plume and hangs. Below, the valley breathes too — a wider, warmer plume, rising steadily from somewhere inside the green.

Underfoot there is no stone and no soil. There is the mat: a matted felt of living tissue, half a pace deep, then more of it, compressed and darker, and beneath that roots, and beneath the roots more root, to a depth no one has established because the depth has never been reached. The highlands here are not rock wearing plant. They are grown. The benches were formed by green slumps and older green landslides; the ridges are accumulated stem and rhizome and compressed leaf, pressed by their own weight into something like geology. Where a knuckle of true mineral shows through — rare, ice-slicked this morning — it looks less like bedrock than like a tooth left in the flesh.

The cold is in the air only. Push your hand into the mat and it comes back warm, faintly sweet, the warmth of a turned compost heap or the flank of a sleeping animal. The entire region is like this: cold-skinned, warm-bodied. Frost silvers the upper leaves at dawn and is gone by the time the light flattens onto noon. The light itself is nothing — a high grey-white sky, a plain even brightness with no opinions — which is why everything else stands out so starkly. In this light you can watch the growth happening.

Water is everywhere in small amounts and nowhere in large ones. By late afternoon every surface beads with condensation, until the landscape glistens like something recently exhumed. The leaf-cups hold cold rainwater in precise Braille across the thickets. There are tarns in the bowls, skinless and cold on top and warm beneath, rimmed with living growth that thickens visibly year over year. There are springs that emerge from a slope with confidence and vanish into the mat within a few hundred paces, swallowed whole. The streams do not so much flow as go underground into the plant and continue there, out of sight, in channels of root. Rain comes at intervals — brief, soft, vertical — and it is the region's own weather: the valley's exhalation lifted on the warm hours, cooled against the height, and returned. The place drinks its own breath and breathes it out again.

Nothing here is built. No cut wood, no stone stacked on stone, no fixed joint anywhere. And yet this is an industrial region, and there is no honest way to describe it otherwise. There are the smoke-hollows: bowl-depressions where peat-heart smolders wet and slow, fires that have burned in the same ring for generations, venting a cold smoke that is mostly steam. There are the vents along the cirque's inner wall, exhaling on a slow rhythm — a two-second push, a long rest — driven by the pressure of decay deep in the mass, like the breathing of something enormous and patient. Gas gathers under the cold skins of the tarns and releases in silver braids that stand upright in the still air. Vines run in tension lines across the open swards, carrying water over distances no stream bothers with, sagging under load. Where metal-rich seeps rise, the leaves standing above them take on a metallic bloom, and the tarns wear an oil-slick iridescence that is only iron and bacteria — the region refining its ore through root and film, with no furnace but the warm dark of its own interior.

The point is that the machines are not objects here. They are performances. Where a flow needs to go, the growth organizes itself into a pipe, a flue, a valve, a reservoir; when the flow moves on, the shape un-takes itself and the tissue is reclaimed. Nothing was ever assembled. Everything is continuously performed. An industry with no parts — and, remarkably, with almost no sound.

The sound is the thing that undoes visitors. There are no hard surfaces in the region — no stone faces to echo, no dry timber to knock, no gravel to shift — and the fog lies over everything like a mattress. Sound does not travel here; it dies. A vent three meters away is a hiss at the very border of hearing. A slope-collapse you can see with your own eyes — a green bench calving in a slow, massive slump, sliding, re-rooting as it goes — reaches your ears as almost nothing, a soft exhalation, felt more than heard. The loudest thing for miles is you. Your heartbeat in your ears when you press your head to the mat. The crackle of rime under your boots. Your own breath. Stand still long enough and the silence closes over your body's small noises too, and you are left listening to a place that is furiously, visibly busy and almost entirely mute.

Because it is busy. The turnover here is constant and fast. Leaves fall in silent drizzles and are gone by the following day — eaten, melted into the mat, reabsorbed; there is no litter anywhere, the floor of the region is as clean as a swept room, which is itself the measure of how much is dying and being eaten at every moment. Vines bridge gaps at the edge of perception, visibly longer each afternoon. On the open swards the tall-stalked stands migrate: whole leaning congregations of them, bent steadily toward some nutrient plume underground, advancing a body-length a week across the open ground, the ground behind them closing green as they pass. Some mornings, at one particular minute, an entire hillside of leaves turns at once — all of it, together, one motion — and turns back an hour later. You can find no clock governing this. You can only stand there with your mouth open in the cold.

Fauna is nearly absent. At the warm hour before dusk a grey moth comes up out of the mat, flying with a slow heavy uncertainty, and there is nothing for it to eat and nothing that eats it, and it is gone by full dark. In the steam of the larger vents there are white soft shapes, shrimp-like or grub-like, that exist only in the warm column and vanish when looked at directly. Once, on the rim, a bird-shape crossed the sky and made no sound, and never sang, and seemed to know there was no point. In a place where sound dies within paces, voices are useless, and the few animals here appear to have been selected for silence as much as for anything else.

The strangest thing, the thing that stays with you, is the balance. The forms of the region are old — the benches and cirques and smooth ridgebacks were shaped long ago and hold their shapes — and yet no particle in any of them is old. The mountain eats its own summit-fall every year and rebuilds itself out of the meal. The stone that remains is stone that has passed through root: dark woven strata in the rare cliff-faces, bands of mineral that are the remembered meals of a hundred generations of forest. The forms are settled; nothing in them is. It is the way a waterfall holds its shape, or a flame holds its shape — stability made entirely out of turnover. Locally, the region is a riot: growth, collapse, combustion, refinancing of every resource at speed. Globally, across this whole high breathing massif, nothing has meaningfully changed for an unguessable span.

You stand on a bench at dusk with the frost beginning to come down, and the smoke-hollows across the valley plume up in their slow rhythm, and the moth is somewhere behind you in the dark, and through the soles of your boots — felt, never heard — there is a hum. Deep, steady, subsonic. The gear-note of the ferment. The region does not acknowledge you. It is a factory with no parts and a wilderness with no edges, and it goes on working in silence, exactly as loudly as everything else here: completely, and not at all.
