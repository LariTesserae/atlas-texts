# The Weeping Stair

The mountain runs hot. That's the first thing, and it's backward. You climb for half a day through slopes that should be cooling toward snowline, and instead the air warms against your face degree by degree, until you're stripping layers at an altitude where your breath ought to be fogging. The smell changes with it — cold leaf-rot giving way to warm stone, wet green, and a thin thread of sulfur somewhere off to the left, struck-match faint, like a match lit and extinguished years ago in a room you can't find.

Then the trees open and you see what you've been climbing into. White ground. Terraces — not one basin but a stair of them, rimmed pool above rimmed pool, climbing in shelves toward a summit dome that's still veiled in its own steam. It isn't a single spring or a single falls. It's a country of steps. From here you can count a dozen vapor columns standing in the daylight between you and the far rim, and beyond them more, hazed to blue, miles off. From marsh-head to the last basin the stair rises two vertical kilometers, and every step of it is pale stone holding water.

The stone is warm underfoot — blood-warm, kettle-faint — and it weeps. That's the honest word for it. Every wall and face beads with seepage; water doesn't fall here so much as exude. Press a stick into any patch of ground that looks solid and warm water rises around it within seconds, as if the whole mountain were soaked to its crown. Along the margins the springwater spreads into marsh: reedbeds standing in shallows the color of weak milk, because the outflow carries so much dissolved mineral that where the current slows it cements its own pebbles into cobbles, makes its own concrete, lays its own road down the mountain.

Up close, the terraces show you their logic. Each pool is a vat: a flat mineral floor ringed by a rolled lip like a brace of pearls, spilling over one low point into the next. Around the water's edge the heat-loving mats draw contour maps of temperature — black at the scalding center, then rust, then orange, then a green fringe where the reeds take over. In the old channels the mineral crust has built up in ridges and tubes, scaled like the inside of a kettle no one has ever descaled. And here and there a cone vents clean steam, exhaling on a count you could time against your own pulse. Not a roar. A long sigh, three seconds, done.

That's when you understand what's odd about this place, and it isn't the heat. It's that nothing here was built, and yet everything here has a function. Deep below there is a boiler — rock heated from depth, water forced through it. The fracture systems are the plumbing, sealed and lined with mineral until they're pipes in fact. The geysers are valves holding pressure on schedule. The cones are stacks. The cooling work is done openly: every vapor plume drifts downwind until it hits cooler forest and condenses, and there — in a band you can see from above, a slope permanently beaded with warm drizzle — the mountain distills itself and rains itself back. Even the erosion is in the machine's favor. At the lower edge of the stair-lands, first-generation terraces stand as white cliffs, dissolving as fast as new stone precipitates at the spring-heads above. Stone is born at the top and carried down stair by stair, water to water, over ages. The whole landscape is a conveyor. It runs on depth. No one made it, and no artifact anywhere will tell you otherwise — but it would be hard, standing here, to say nothing here works.

The lushness follows from the machine. Heat from below, water at the surface, mineral food everywhere: the vegetation doesn't grow at this altitude so much as crowd. Moss grows in carpets thick enough to leave bootprints in. Ferns come up to your shoulder on the old terraces. On every weeping face hangs a garden — moss curtains, orchid-like epiphytes, small pale flowers fed by seepage alone — layered on layered until a cross-section of bank looks like upholstery. Trees grow straight out of the old spill-channels, roots gripping stone the way hands grip railings. And in the condensation band, where the mountain rains on itself, the forest is wetter than rainforest has any right to be: every leaf beaded, the air itself drinkable.

Life fills every layer of it. At dawn the mist over the warm shallows is thick with insects — in the lee of each steam column they drift upward in slow columns, like snow falling the wrong way — and where the insects are, the birds are: finch-flocks preening in the vapor, a wader with bare coral legs stalking the milk shallows, something with a heron's patience standing so long at a pool rim that moss begins the work of claiming it. Creatures here follow the warm edge like a tide. By day the heat spreads wide and the animals scatter into the steam-warmed woods; by night the cold falls from the sky (for the sky above the vapor is thin and quickly dark) and the animals contract toward the vents, a daily tide of bodies flowing in and out around the fixed heat.

The drinking hour is the only drama, and it keeps its own schedule. At last light the grazers come down out of the forest to the warm marsh margin — you'll hear them before you see them, the slap of broad feet in shallow milk-water — and something tawny keeps that appointment too, from downwind. The waders go quiet a full breath before anything happens, a silence that spreads outward like a dropped stone, and then it passes, one way or the other, and the marsh resumes its small noises: sip, drip, the rolling of a fish in a warm channel.

Because for all this life, the place is mostly quiet. The vapor eats sound. A hundred paces of mist will swallow a voice; the abundant fauna is an abundance you watch, not hear. What reaches you instead is the machinery at low volume — the hiss of steam so soft it reads as silence, the metronome drip of each terrace edge at its own rate, overlapping into a slow irregular clock. Under everything, and beneath hearing, there's a hum. You feel it in the sternum more than the ear, like standing near a substation. The only punctuation is the scheduled sigh of a distant vent, and once — mid-morning, from somewhere over the rise — a bell-clear bird note, twice, then nothing.

The light, meanwhile, is simply daylight, which is its own strangeness here: hot white-gold sun, ordinary and bright, diffused by vapor into a glare that overexposes everything until a mist bank passes and the colors snap sharp — green beyond green, white beyond white, a standing rainbow in the spray of a spill-lip that holds for an hour and leaves.

The days cycle like the rest of it. Dawn fills the basins with fog, each pool wearing its own lid of milk, and the sun lifts it in slow columns that lean downwind. By early afternoon real rainclouds have built over the high rim and walk down the stair, and when a storm passes hard enough, a channel reroutes — a lip gives, water finds a new low point, and a new terrace begins to grow while an old one, cut off, whitens and cedes itself to moss. You can read the whole history in cross-section wherever a fresh collapse shows the strata: banded stone, dark years and pale years, centuries laid down like the rings of a tree. Nothing is raw here. Every edge is worn smooth by overflow, every form has settled into its own long habit, and the region has held this shape — losing at the bottom what it gains at the top — for longer than the forests on it have stood.

Toward evening the steam turns gold, then gray, and the warm stone under your hands is still warm. The vents keep their counts. The drips keep their clocks. The stair builds itself downward grain by grain, unhurried, unaudited, a factory in full production with no workers, no walls, no made thing anywhere on it or in it — except, standing on a terrace rim with the mist closing over your shoulders, you.
