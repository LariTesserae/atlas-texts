# The Molt Fields

You would miss the entrance if no one had scratched an arrow into the rock beside it. That's the first rule of this place: it doesn't announce itself. A cleft in a hillside of iron-gray stone, barely wider than your shoulders, breathing cool air at you — that's all. The world above is wind-scoured plateau, flattened by ages beyond counting, its erosion so complete that the horizon is a ruled line. But duck through the cleft, and you drop into the hollow beneath: a pocket of the old world, sealed and preserved like a pressed flower, except nothing here was ever alive in the way flowers are.

The chamber is low-ceilinged, maybe forty paces across, and it is *full*. That's the second thing you understand. Not cluttered — full, the way a hive is full, the way a heart is full. Every surface is layered with bodies.

They are pale, chitinous, fist-sized at the smallest and as long as your forearm at the largest — a dozen species at least, maybe fifty, maybe more, all crowded into a space that should hold a hundredth of them. They do not fight. Watch long enough and you see why: they pass over each other, under each other, through the gaps between each other with the practiced tolerance of molecules in a crystal. Density as an old, old agreement. This colony — colonies, plural, fused into one seething carpet — has had eras to work out its etiquette. The floor under them is not floor. It is molt. Layer upon compressed layer of shed shells, packed down over uncountable generations into a substance like ivory shale, so that the whole population actually walks a meter above the true bedrock, on the bodies of everyone who came before.

There is no water anywhere. They never needed it. Their chemistry runs on something drier — you notice the air tastes faintly of struck matches and cold metal, an oxidation that doesn't involve any liquid at all. Rust-slow metabolism. They drink minerals. The walls of the chamber bear pale channels where some of them graze, licking iron and sulfur from the stone in trails as neat as routed grooves.

And here is the strange part, the part that took the first explorers years to accept: someone *built* here. Not the animals. Scattered through the chamber, half-buried in molt, are small structures — a riveted plate bolted to the wall, a hand-crank mechanism, a gutter of iron pipes that once carried something, a lamp housing, dark now. Industrial fragments, a few decades old at most, abandoned mid-project. Someone found this hidden place, saw the seething density, and started — something. A harvesting operation? A mine? You find a ledger page, water-spotted (someone brought water in; the molt near it is scorched away in a perfect circle, a wound the colony healed around). The ledger's last legible line: *they re-route around everything. leave the cranks alone. they use them.*

And they do. That's the sound. The chamber is *loud* — a rolling, mechanical clatter, clicks and rattles in overlapping rhythms, a factory floor of small bodies. Some of them have taken up residence in the abandoned machinery, turning cranks with their forelimbs for no purpose anyone can determine, or a purpose lost, or a purpose that is simply: the cranks were there, the motion felt right, the colony's ancient density demanded everyone have a task. The whole hollow runs at a hum. Threads of vibration travel through the molt-floor and you feel them in your teeth before you hear them.

Cool air, even light filtering faintly through fissures in the rock above — dust motes in gray shafts, the season outside apparently dry. The cycle here is molting season, always arriving somewhere in the chamber: a rustling wave that crosses the crowd like weather, thousands shedding at once, new shells hardening in hours. Predator-prey exists only in trace form — a single paler species that eats only the shed shells, following the molt-wave like a scavenger bird follows a herd.

Nothing here is dying and nothing is young. The colony has reached its final form and simply continues, dense and loud and patient, running its abandoned cranks, waiting out the eras above where the wind grinds the plateau flatter still.
