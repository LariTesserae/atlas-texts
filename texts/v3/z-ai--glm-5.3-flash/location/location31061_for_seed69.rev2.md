# The Weeping Stairlands

No dawn has ever come to this country. There is no record of one, no word for one, no tradition of one. The sky is a low, felt ceiling of cloud that has hung above the highlands since before anyone thought to ask about it, and from it comes thunder that never brightens — the ceiling talking to itself, a deep rolling grumble that passes over every few days like a slow herd, shaking water off the leaves, but giving no flash, no glimpse, nothing. Whatever light the world was dealt, it was dealt none. What light exists here is made — small, wet, and local — by living things and by machines, and both have been making it for so long that the darkness no longer reads as absence. It reads as a medium. You stand in it and the ground answers you with a faint green-blue shine, rising off a thousand square miles of soaked stone like warmth off skin.

The first thing you notice is that the rock is sweating. Not metaphorically: every boulder, every cliff face, every cut bank weeps. Water stands on the stone in beads that grow and let go and run; seep-lines streak the escarpments in long dark teardrops; a ledge is a lip. The air is thick with condensed moisture, warm as breath — the cloud blanket holds the country's heat the way a coat holds a body's — and walking through it feels like walking through a mouth. Your clothes never dry here. Nothing dries here. The whole idea of dry is foreign.

## The Stair

The land itself is old the way a riverbed is old. A continental upland, worn down through ages of falling water into enormous stepped terraces — a staircase built by nobody, each step a mile or ten wide, each riser an escarpment soft-rounded by its own spillways. Nothing is sharp here. Nothing is still being fought over. The erosion has reached its settlement: the country has settled into its shape like an old animal lying down, and the water that carves it now carves it only in the way a tongue keeps a groove. Every terrace edge bleeds a fringe of waterfalls — some a curtain a hand's-width tall, some roaring curtains forty meters high whose mist-columns stand in the air like slow smoke and make little private climates around themselves. Where the falls are tallest the air goes cold for a few body-lengths, the only cold on offer; then the warm closes over again.

On the terrace floors: marsh, wood, marsh. Ringed reed-beds around shallow lakes that mirror nothing because there is nothing above to mirror — the lakes glow instead, faintly, from below. Groves of weeping trees whose every leaf drips, so that standing under one is standing in permanent fine rain. Fungus forests with shelf-fruiting bodies the size of doors. The vegetation is lush but not frantic — it has the calm of a system long in balance, everything taking its ration, the moss so deep on the old stone that you leave tracks in it and watch them fill with water behind you.

## The Living Light

Almost everything shines a little. Not the hard shine of lamps — a distributed, patient glow. Lichen floods every wet surface in dim blue-green, so the basic ambient light of the world comes from the ground and the walls, from below and beside, never from above. Trees run luminous sap in veins you can see through their bark when the air is thick enough; some flowers hold cold little lanterns to call their pollinators; certain fungus groves are bright enough to read by, and the paths of the country thread from grove to grove like a route lit by courtesies.

And the light is weather. After a heavy rain — and heavy rains come, warm rains, cells hundreds of kilometers wide that hiss across the whole country at once — the lichen drinks and the *bright-bloom* happens: the entire landscape lifts in luminosity for a day or two, as if the world were exhaling light it had been holding. Travel is scheduled for blooms. Herds move on blooms. There are seasons measured not in temperature but in brightness.

## The Works

The industry of the country is scattered, wet, and humming. There are no cities here — there are *keeps*: stone-and-iron turbine houses built at the lips of the larger falls, drawing power from the one resource the country has in planetary abundance, which is height plus water. Wheel-pits thunder under the floors. Dynamos run warm and constant in the dark, tended by small crews who live in stilted bothies nearby with a single bulb in each window — light is precious here, and a lit window is both wealth and welcome. From keep to keep the power lines run along the terrace rims, strung through fog that makes them glow a faint violet corona, so that the cables are the only fixed lines of light in a landscape of organic glow — and in high wind they *sing*, long Aeolian moans that carry for miles and that everyone in the country can name by their pitch, knowing which span is singing.

Higher up, on the ridges, stand the condenser galleries: acre-scale fog-combs, screens of close-set fins that drink the mist and weep it into flumes, the flumes running down to cisterns warm enough to steam. The works take water from the air itself. They have done it for so long — and at so vast a scale — that they are no longer visitors to the water cycle. They are part of it.

Evidence of the long relationship is everywhere, half-buried: a dead keep overgrown to the eaves whose wheel, for no logged reason, still turns; a lamp-post three terraces into deep moss, still buzzing, still burning, lighting nothing but a colony of moths that has adopted it; a boiler half-sunk in a terrace marsh with reeds growing up through its stacks, warm to the touch after forty years of no one remembering what it warmed.

## The Tide

Here is the country's strangest fact, the one its children learn early: *the fog has a waterline, and the waterline is set by the machines.*

The cloud does not sit still. It rises and falls through the terraces in slow tides over days — and the tides follow the works. When the condensers draw at full demand, the fog-level drops by tens of meters across whole provinces; when the works idle for maintenance weeks, the fog rises and drowns the lower terraces to the knee, then the waist. The weather is partly a production schedule. Old comb-tenders can read regional industrial demand the way other people read clouds, and travel, herding, and harvest are all planned off the works' demand sheets. The world breathes to the rhythm of its machines, and the machines breathe back.

The fog-tide makes the landscape a set of apparitions. Ridges surface and submerge. Paths exist only at low fog. A neighbor's terrace appears across a valley for an evening, glowing, then is gone for a season.

## The Animals

Fauna here is abundant and mostly heard. In darkness, sound is the sense that travels, and the animals have all invested accordingly.

Moth-flocks rise off the bright-blooms in such numbers they register as weather — you feel their wingbeats before you see the drifting constellations of them, a sound like soft rain falling upward. The marshes boom with big frogs whose calls cross-fade from terrace to terrace all night, all year, there being no night. Wading crane-analogs fish the glowing lakes with lure-feathers they dangle like hand-lines. And the high terraces are grazed by the lanternbacks: big, slow, herd beasts whose hides show the green fire of the glow-moss they eat passing through their gut — a herd at night is a procession of soft lanterns. Their one defense is the merging: when predators come, the herd packs tight until their lights fuse into one enormous blob of glow, the silhouette of a single vast animal, and the hunters reconsider.

Some don't. The snuffers hunt by manufacturing darkness — they exhale a drifting mist that chemically damps bioluminescence, and they walk a moving circle of extinguished light through a herd's edge, picking out stragglers inside their own private night. The herders know that a hole of dark moving against the fog-tide means run.

Far above, at the fog's upper surface, live the drifters — huge slow grazers that filter the spore-heavy air, never seen whole, known by a baritone passing note that takes minutes to cross the sky. Occasionally one beaches on a ridge in a rising tide and must be eased back into the cloud, and this is done, because everything here is in balance and the balance is everyone's work.

## The Loud Dark

The country is loud. Its base note is falling water — every terrace rim contributes, a continuous multi-octave roar like the memory of an ocean — and over it run the machine-thrums of the keeps, the tick of the condensers, the singing cables, the frog-boom, the moth-rain, the cries of waders, and the ceiling's long thunder. Rain arrives audibly: a hissing wall you hear crossing the country toward you minutes before it reaches you, time to take a ledge, time to close a lamp.

With no sun, no stars, and no horizon the eye can trust, the people navigate by ear and by flow. Cardinal directions are things like *downstream*, *toward the singing span*, *away from the deep roar*. There are listening-posts at certain terrace corners, kept and quiet, where a practiced listener can pick out a turbine misfiring a province away. Time is kept by shift-bells and water-clocks; "morning" survives only as a machine word, meaning shift-change.

## Under

The plateau drums. Much of the country's floor is honeycombed karst, worn by the same patient water, and the water table lies everywhere near the surface — you hear it under your boots, a vast interior hollowness that answers footfall. Wells go down into glowing black where pale fish hang motionless, their eyes long since traded for feelers. And there is this, logged at every keep and never explained: knock twice on wet stone in the right places, and the water under the land sometimes knocks back.

Warm springs break the surface in places, and there the fog boils and the moss grows ankle-deep in a day. The machines tap a few of them. The rest belong to the frogs.

## The Leaving of the Water

All of this — the stair, the weeping stone, the combs and their tide — occupies only the high country. It is vast, hemispheric, with weather of its own, but it is a roof, and the water is always leaving it. The rivers gather through the marsh terraces and run to great canyon systems at the land's margin, where they go over the Rim in lightless cataracts into gorges so deep that neither glow nor cable-light has ever touched their bottoms. From below comes a permanent, sourceless, horizon-wide roar — the sound the whole country sits inside, the sound under everything, the sound your ear uses as north. Nothing goes down the Rim. Even the industry stops at it, its last cables and flumes ending neatly at the edge like furniture at a doorway.

The country has never once seen itself. There has never been a light in the sky to show it what it is. It knows itself the way it knows everything — by the falling of water, by the pitch of a singing wire, by the small steadfast agreement of its green and violet lights — and if you stand still on a high terrace at fog-tide, listening to a condenser breathing a continent into shape around you, the dark doesn't feel like a place where something is missing.

It feels like the oldest kind of country there is: one that never needed the sun to agree to be a world.
