They cross the Old Floor in the flat white light of midmorning, and for the first hour there is nothing to see. The basin's floor is ancient — gray clay gone to cracked pan, dry as old chalk except in the low seams where the night's condensate gathers in slivers of slick. It is cold enough that breath hangs in front of the face and stays there, and the sky above behaves like any sky: even, overcast, unremarkable. Whatever strangeness this place holds, it keeps none of it in the light.

The moss is the first sign. It comes up out of the pan in a green line, thin as a drawn stroke, then wide as a road — lawns of it, deep and damp, drinking nothing from the ground, which has none to give, but combing water out of the air. Beyond the moss the land begins to rise in long, shallow terraces, and beyond the terraces the town: a crescent eleven kilometers across, hugging the basin's curve, low-built, dense, steaming faintly along its far edge where the newest ground is still curing. Steam and fog hang over the whole crescent in a slow ceiling. The flocks are up there somewhere, thousands of birds, and you cannot hear them. You can hear almost nothing at all.

This is the Groundworks. It is a town whose industry is ground.

The rule the moss obeys, the whole town obeys. No river comes here. No river has ever come here — the basin is too young, or too old, for that; there is only the floor and the air. So the water arrives as weather in miniature: fog off the cold basin air, dew, rime, the sweat of every cold surface at dawn. The condenser farms sit in a belt along the crescent's outer skirt — fog-comb hedges planted in ranks, gutters cut to catch what the combs strip from the air, dew-ponds sealed with green stone to hold the harvest. The engines drink this. The people drink this. The boilers exhale it back out as steam, the steam becomes the fog ceiling, the fog settles, the combs strip it again. The town drinks the same water over and over, and the old-timers will tell you, with a straight face, that it is all the same water — that no water has entered or left this basin in living memory, only gone around.

Inside the crescent there is no space that is not used twice. Walls carry pipe-runs and fern-banks in the same course. Moss lawns roof the boiler halls because the exhaust keeps them green through the cold and their damp keeps the stacks from cracking. Stairwells are roosts; the rail cut is also the main drainage channel for melt-frost; a single block might hold a kiln, a loft of dew-farm racks, a cistern, and forty households, all interleaved, all mutually dependent. It is crowded the way a reef is crowded — not cluttered, but saturated, every surface alive with something and every something in service to something else. Small ferret-things live in the insulation and are tolerated for eating the worse vermin, mostly.

The quiet, when you first arrive, is the thing that undoes visitors. Machines are working everywhere — pumps, presses, the great slow layers out on the frontier — and yet the town hushes like a library. Part of it is engineering: padded gearing, steam exhausted through condensing mazes, bells forbidden. But most of it is the stone. Green stone — unhardened stone, stone still in its first years — drinks sound. The newest districts are the quietest places in the world; a shout there falls dead a meter from your lips, swallowed by ground too young to give anything back.

> *The ground is listening. Keep it smooth.*

That is the law, spoken half as physics and half as ethics. Noise in the green districts bubbles the setting stone — leaves flaws, voids, weak courses that will settle wrong in thirty years. So the frontier is worked in whispers and hand-signals, in lamp-flash and drummed code on boot-soles. Sound there is so rare that it is information: every tick, every clink, every soft crack of a pipe is noted, read, reported. A dropped wrench in the laying-fields causes a scandal.

The laying-fields are where the town makes the world. Out past the last hardened street, the long machines crawl through ground laid down in the past weeks — warm, dark, faintly sweating stone, steaming gently in the cold because fresh rock sets hot, exothermic, like the curing of some vast slow mortar. The machines lay it course by course from the stone-milk pumped up from the Old Floor — the ancient basin itself being drawn down, meter by meter, and rebuilt above as new land. The town eats the old world and stands on the new one. Where the milk is freshest the ground is still soft: press a thumb in and the print stays. The streets keep everything.
