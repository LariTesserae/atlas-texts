# The High Fen

From the lowlands it does not look like a place at all. It looks like a decision — a long green line standing above the edge of the world, flat the way a lake is flat when there is no wind, and wearing a ceiling of pearl that ends where the green ends, straight as a shelf. The rim people have a name for it: the High Fen. Those few who have crossed it keep fewer words for it. Words wear thin there.

**the veil**

The old country ends at the rim, in a cliff the width of a season's walk, and the cliff is not dry. It weeps. Water comes off it not in falls but in a single continuous seep — a wall of giving, every hand-span of it releasing its thread, bearded with moss to the waterline, polished and dripping. The sound of it reaches you a full day before the sight does: a vast held breath, let out slowly, forever. You climb for days in the spray-line, up green stairs the water has worn, and the whole climb is cold, colder the higher you go, until the lip.

Then the warmth takes you like a hand.

Not the sun's warmth — there is no sun here to speak of. The ground's. You have climbed out of the cold of the world into the warmest place in it, and the warmth rises up at you from below, the way warmth comes off a body sleeping under blankets. Highest place in the world, and warmest: the two facts are one fact. All this heat is the fen's own slow digestion — a compost the size of a country, oxidizing for epochs, never catching. Put your palm flat on the moss and it is the temperature of a sleeping animal, a little under blood.

**the mat**

What you stand on is alive.

That sentence is the whole plateau. The ground here is not ground; it is a mat of living moss over tens of fathoms of dead moss over drowned forest over black water over — sometimes, if you push a wand down and count heartbeats, a hundred, two hundred, and still no bottom — the roots of a world that is no longer there. With every step the land nods, a small agreement. In the hollows the skin thins to black water, and you can look down a well of years: below this year's surface-forest, a submerged forest; below that, a dark that is either deeper water or older time. You cannot find a hand-span of bare ground anywhere. The ground here is a crop.

Every finger-width down is a century. The fen does not bury its dead so much as file them — cool, black, whole. Things sink into it and are kept.

Three laws, and the fen keeps them perfectly. Whatever does not grow will sink. The rain that falls here stays here. Nothing hurries.

**the lid**

The sky is low. You could throw a stone at weather. The pearl ceiling sits close over the dwarf trees, and it is the fen's own sky, made of the fen: the whole continent drinks from below, breathes its water up through every stem, and the vapor gathers into this lid and rains back down warm. It drinks its own rain. Drought cannot find the plateau, and so nothing here has ever needed to hurry, and so nothing here does.

The Lamp shows through the pearl the way lamplight shows through horn — a paler patch that wanders, telling the hours without ever once being looked at directly. Nothing casts a shadow. Everything is its own faint shadow. The colors under this light are a dozen greens, the black of the pools, the bone-white of the lichens, and that is nearly all. At the dim hour the fog beads on every stem so the whole sward shines like a beast's hide — beaded, but warm.

Once in a great while the Lid parts. A rift, an hour, and a column of plain light stands on the green like something remembered. Everything holds — the herons, the walkers, the very rain seems to pause — and then the pearl closes and the fen tucks itself back in.

**water**

There are no rivers with banks here. Water moves as sheet and seep: a finger deep and a field wide, braided, patient, so slow you can stare at the falling rain and not tell which way the land drains, because the land barely drains. The pools lie in long strings along contours you cannot see, threaded like beads for miles. Every stone that breaks the mat — and there are a few — sits in a collar of its own weeping.

The year has two gestures. In the wet season the mat drinks and swells, and the whole continent rises a hand-span, inhaling; in the dry — a relative word — it exhales and settles. The plateau breathes about once a year. You will feel it only in your teeth, and only if you stand still long enough to be part of the measurement.

**the green**

The vegetation comes in floors, stacked, near-touching. The moss floor, which is the ground. Above it the sedge-lawns, which comb themselves flat when the long winds pass — and note that they pass in silence, because there is nothing here dry enough to rattle; the sedges move like seaweed, without a sound. Above them, the forest: a forest you step over. Each tree a thumb thick at the base and a hand high, and older than any tower in the lowlands — trunks that have taken three centuries to reach your waist, groaning rings you could read ten thousand years without kneeling. You could walk your whole life through this forest and never pass one that has died of age.

In wetter ground, the pitcher brakes: stands of green wells, tall as a man, each humming faintly with the small lives it keeps, each with its own drowned history inside. Sometimes a wing-beat sounds from within one. Then quiet. The fen drinks.

And here and there, rising above everything, the only tall verticals on the plateau: arcs of living wood, trained — persuaded — into arches over roads that are mostly sunk now, so that all that breaks the moss is the spine of each bridge, in a line, the way a drowned street keeps its doorsills. Read from above, the lines look like letters half-erased.

**the quiet ones**

The fauna is abundant and almost entirely silent, and it comes in floors too.

On the floor of the floor, the true multitude: worms and pale larvae, black minnows hanging in the window-pools, an entire lit world seen only by lying down and looking wrong-side-up through the water-skin. Above them, the hummocks. Every green mound on the fen is either asleep, or full, or patient. You will not learn which by looking. You learn to walk as if all of them are watching, and after a while this stops being fear and becomes a kind of etiquette.

The still-herons stand in the shallows for a day at a time; the day stands with them. The walkers — the great browsers, moss to the elbow on their shoulders — you will meet one and call it one, but it is a herd wearing horizons, the next member standing beyond sight, each keeping its own small weather. They move a slow mile a day, and their passage is the plateau's slow authorship: a wallow becomes a pool, a pool a string, a string a seep. The rivers here are written in footsteps. They do not flee you. They flow aside over hours, the way a tide considers a post.

And at the dim hour, the pools grow constellations. The frogs of the fen do not sing; they glow — one cold green wink per throat, thousands of them over the black water, each frog a single slow star. In a country that keeps its silence, light is cheaper than noise.

**the makers**

Someone was here once. They did not cut; they persuaded.

The roads — the firm ways — were never built, only agreed upon: a million footfalls over uncounted generations compacting the peat into dry-topped corduroys that hold firm when everything around quakes. The fauna uses them still; the herds thread them from stone to stone. And the stones: the Standers, set at intervals across the whole continent, each without a chisel-mark, rounded by the same rain that sanded the last tors, sunk already to their waists and sinking — give them ten thousand more years and only the basins will break the moss, still cupping rain, still full. They stand where the warmths rise: the slow chimneys of the fen, columns where the ground's heat escapes and the fog spirals upward and tender things bloom — the only strong flowers on the plateau, and the gliders hang above them all day, silent crosses, like kites with no one holding the string. The warmths are fixed. They are the plateau's stars. The makers marked them, and made roads between them, and the roads outlived the makers because everything the makers built was already alive.

Sometimes the slow erosion of a bank opens the archive, and a person looks out — leather-skinned, braided, hands curled softly around nothing. The next swelling files them away again. Whether they are the persuaders, the fen is not telling. The fen keeps what it is given.

**the long bloom**

The quiet has its calendar. Once a season, somewhere out of sight, a floating lawn tears its moorings — a rend, a sound like a door of the world — and then the hush closes over it as over water. Once a year, at the hatch, the fen allows itself noise: certain midges rise in continents of their own, and for a few days the silence hums. Then it closes again, complete.

And every eleventh summer, spring sets out to cross the plateau, and it takes three years, and it walks — a front of gold and bone-white the width of a storm, moving at the pace of a long funeral, the fauna following it, the hatches timed to it, visible from the rim as a bright band sliding across the dark green of the world. Then the color passes over the horizon of the fen, and the green closes behind it like water behind a swimmer.

The fen is old the way the sea floor is old. Mountains stood here once; the fen was more patient than the mountains, and won. Nothing is left of them but polished humps under green and the mineral crowns of a few tors, where a traveler can press a hand to the only cold, hard, not-alive fact on the entire plateau, and feel the difference like a memory. What you walk is not a ruin. It is a rest. It has already survived its own mountains, and settled into the shape it will keep.

Stand at a Stander at the dim hour, if you ever get there. The warmths show as pale pillars in the thinning fog. The lamp-throats open their constellations. The pitchers shine faintly from inside, each a small lure in the dark. And if you are lucky, once, the Lid parts, and a column of ordinary light stands on the green as if the world had remembered something — everything holds, the herons, the herons' moss, the patient hummocks, the water mid-thought — and then the pearl closes, and the fen goes back to its one long gesture.

In. Out. In.

The largest patience there is, breathing under its lid, keeping what it has been given, letting nothing go but breath.
