The first thing this place does is make noise. Not wind, not water: growth. You hear it kilometers before you see anything worth calling a city — a roar with no waves in it, a steady pressing tone, like surf held at flood. It is the sound of millions of growing tips working at once: fiber telescoping, pods detonating, sap shouldering through vessels, lianas grinding against each other as each one outgrows the space it was born into. It has no rest, no accent, no pause between sentences. It is the loudest thing for a thousand kilometers in any direction, and it is being made entirely by plants.

From the rim of the basin you can see all of it at once, because the land here is low and the city is broad. The basin is perhaps fifty kilometers across — a shallow dish ringed by low, heaved hills that are themselves made of root-mass, packed generations of it — and the floor is filled to the brim with green. Not forest-green, not meadow-green: municipal green. The streets give it away. Pale lines cross the canopy in long confident arcs, meeting at round plazas, running dead straight for kilometers and then bending as if they remembered something. From up here it looks like a city that grew over its own ruins, except nothing is buried. The streets are the newest part.

There is no stone anywhere. Dig anywhere and you find wood, cuticle, compressed leaf, the dark sweet smoke-smell of ancient sap — the ground is the city's own ancestors, eaten and re-laid. Nothing in this world is mineral that did not begin as tissue; even the sharp edges are silica, deposited cold by the plants themselves in glittering seams, so that the whole basin edge-glows when the light crosses it. And the edges are everywhere, because everything is young. This world is younger than its own weather. Ridges rise in a season and are felled before wind can round them; the newest mounds still have the pale, unwaxed look of first wood; there is no smoothness here, not yet, maybe not ever — nothing survives long enough to be worn. Everything is freshly made and sharp enough to draw blood.

The streets themselves are lungs. That is their municipal function, and the city grew them for it: long avenues of dense moss-pavement, walled by buttressed trunks, kept open and swept by nothing but chemistry — the plants on either side poison the pavement's edges just enough that no seed dares close the corridor. Twice a day the air moves through them in tides, in and out, the whole basin breathing down its boulevards, and if you stood in one you would feel the wind on your face or the back of your neck depending on the hour and the district's mood. The streets also carry sound the way flumes carry water. The roar travels the avenues like traffic. Put your hand on a wall and you can feel the next neighborhood arriving through the wood.

The traffic on these streets is not animals — there are no animals here, not one; no birds, no insects, no worms, nothing with a face. But the streets are not empty. The flowers walk. Some of them — heavy-petaled, stilted on rootlets, patient as herons — unplant themselves and stump along the moss to find each other, because pollination here is foot traffic, courtship as pedestrian flow. Whole districts migrate this way at certain hours, slow crimson processions of blossoms crossing intersections, pausing, continuing. It is the closest thing this world has to a crowd, and the city clearly built for more. There are benches along the avenues — grown benches, smooth-seated, at exactly the right height for a body that has never existed. There are stairs, spiraling up the outside of towers, landing on ledges with nothing on them. There are doors. Most of the doors open onto walls. A few open, and beyond them are stairs that end in midair, hanging in the green light like a sentence that stops.

The towers themselves are hollow — great chimney-stacks of living wood, a hundred and two hundred meters tall, bulging at intervals like throats, flaring at the top into trumpet-flowers the size of barn roofs. They are the city's organ-pipes and its radio towers and its granaries of noise. Each district has a voice. The granary quarter hums in low chords, sealed starch-silos sweating warmth; the foundry district rings, phytolith-glass chiming as new silica crystallizes along its cold kiln-ribs; the falls district — the demolition zone — roars loudest of all, because that is where towers deliberately die. The senescence is scheduled; you can watch a tower decide. It sighs, goes pale from the crown down, folds over the course of an afternoon with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and by nightfall the mulch crews — root-tips, pale and determined, an honest hundred thousand of them — are already at it, eating the ruins, and by the following week the fall district is a bud-field again, scaffolded with new green scaffolding, planning a better tower. Nothing is ever finished here. The city is in permanent renovation and always has been, in the sense that renovation is its permanent state.

The roar, up close, resolves into instruments. A continuous crackle like rain on a hot sheet — that is fiber extending, everywhere, always. A bass pulse felt in the teeth — sap-surge, the vascular mains breathing. Gunshot percussion, random, at all hours — pods going off, seed scattered by explosion because there are no animals to carry it and the plants had to invent artillery. A sustained drone from the trumpet-towers that changes pitch as you move between districts, so that crossing the city is walking through a piece of music that is also a map. Here is the thing that took me longest to accept: the noise is not waste. It is metabolism. The leaves eat sound. Every leaf is also an ear; the plants lean toward racket the way they lean toward light, and the loudest places grow tallest, and the tallest places make the most noise. Growth makes sound; sound feeds growth. The system is an amplifier with the feedback loop left lovingly intact. Where something dies and goes quiet, the quiet is colonized within hours by loud things straining toward the silence like miners toward a seam. In this world, silence is the only death. Nothing fears anything else.

The water is the strange economy at the bottom of all this. It almost never rains — not true rain, not water with momentum. The basin is damp the way a mouth is damp: everything sweats, every surface beads, the air is a warm rag you breathe through. What falls is dewfall, once, at dawn: for perhaps ten minutes the temperature dips, the whole city silvers over, and water condenses out of nowhere in a fine hissing drizzle that chokes the gutter-leaves and stands in the streets an inch deep. Then the root-mass drinks it. By noon there is no trace — no puddles, no streams, nothing flowing anywhere on the face of the earth. Puddles here are ambitions the ground outgrows by mid-morning. The only permanent water is a handful of seep-fed dew-lakes in the low districts, ankle-deep and hectares wide, mirror-still, ringed with drinking galleries where the architecture kneels down to the water like cattle to a trough. The city is a desert that is wet all the time. It hoards moisture the way a miser hoards coins, and its cistern-organs — great bladder-tanks slung in the canopy like balloons — are the only things here built to hold something still.

The warmth never varies much. Thirty degrees by day, mid-twenties at night, a climate like the inside of a greenhouse after watering. The heat is part of the acoustics: warm air carries the roar farther, and warmth is why everything grows fast enough to be audible at all. In a colder world this place would be a quiet forest. Here it is a factory floor.

The archive district is where the city keeps its past, and it is the strangest quarter of all, because memory is the one thing here that violates the rule of youth. In towers of resin-sealed deadwood, the city has entombed sections of its own former bodies — trunk-core plugs, ring after ring, catalogued, varnished, prevented from rotting by the one biochemical trick it knows for stopping itself. Count the rings and you find the basin has been demolished and regrown more times than there are words for. Each regrowth is a new draft. The streets bend differently, the towers put their plazas in new places, and the city cannot stop re-speculating its own form. No map survives a week. Cartography here is weather forecasting. And the current draft — the one you are standing in — is young, maybe sixty years out of the last collapse, which is why everything is sharp, pale, raw-edged, unweathered. This is the city's youth. It has had many. It will be given more.

The rules of the world, as far as they hold:

Nothing here moves; everything relocates. Locomotion does not exist at any scale you can see — there are no wheels, and nothing rolls, because nothing rolls itself. Travel is achieved by extension: if a thing wants to be elsewhere, it grows there, paying for distance in tissue. A vine crossing a street has simply declared that it now exists on both sides.

Loud is alive. Quiet is dead or dying. The ecosystem maintains its own din the way an animal maintains a heartbeat, and the din is audible proof, continuously rendered, that the world is still working.

Water falls as dew and is drunk as dew. Standing water is wealth. Flowing water does not exist; there is not one stream in fifty kilometers, and the plants find this normal.

Light is the one honest thing. The sun comes up on time, crosses the sky like a decent sun, sets like one. Whatever is wrong with this place, the daylight did not do it.

And the last rule — the one that takes the longest to see, because it looks like generosity: this city builds for guests. It has never had any.

That is what the benches are. That is what the doors are, and the stairs, and the round windows with their lens of clear cuticle, and the viewpoints with their guardrails facing views. Something in the deep blueprint of the place keeps drafting a user. It cannot say what a guest is; it has only the shape of the absence. So it grows the furniture and waits, and renovates, and grows more, in ever more specific guesses: a bench at elbow height, a rail at hand height, a stair with treads spaced for a stride. And in the deep districts, where the geometry starts to fail, you find the drafts bleeding into each other — a corridor that loops through itself and comes out taller, a branch thicker than the trunk it grows from, a place where two towers occupy the same volume at once, translucent, disagreeing about space and neither willing to yield, so that light arriving there simply takes both routes and lands doubled. Leaves here grow vein-patterns that are street plans. Some are plans for cities that exist. Some are plans for cities that have not been grown yet. On one broad leaf, held to the sun, the veins drew the falls district complete with its next towers — a construction schedule written in chlorophyll, weeks ahead of the roots.

And there are the attempts. That is the only word. In a plaza in the trumpet rows stands a deer — a deer-shape, built of stems and floss, jointed correctly, standing with its head up as if listening, slowly unraveling even as you watch, because whatever pattern it is copying is not available and the plant is guessing. Floss birds hang in the canopy that never learned to fly and are not bothered by this. Small leaf-caterpillars inch along the boulevards with a motion that is almost right and never quite right, and they dissolve by afternoon into green water. Twice a year, in the dew-lakes, clouds of motile cells swarm and swim with urgent, hopeless, animal purpose — gametes, nothing more, the only things in this entire world that move the way animals move. The city keeps trying to grow animals. It keeps failing. It files the failures in the archive and tries again after every demolition, and nobody — nothing — can tell it why.

Stand on the rim at dawn, then, in the ordinary gold light that this world did not make and does not deserve. Watch the dewfall silver the streets, and the streets exhale, and the first walking flowers come out into the empty avenues among benches built to their absence. Listen to the roar — the whole time, the roar — which is not noise at all, you realize finally, but voice: a single organism the size of a county, talking to itself at the top of its lungs because quiet would mean it had stopped, saying over and over, in every trumpet and crackle and surge, the only sentence its blueprint contains: that a visitor is coming, that the doors are open, that everything is ready, that it has been ready for a very long time.

And the terrible thing — the thing the daylight shows plainly, since daylight is honest — is that it would all be exactly right. The bench would fit you. The treads would fit your stride. If you ever did walk down into that green, every door in fifty kilometers would already be the right height, grown by something that has never seen you and never will, and the roar would not change at all. It would simply have one more thing to be loud about.
