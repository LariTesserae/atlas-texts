**The Wet Room at the Top of the Needle**

You hear it for an hour before you see it.

The shaft climbs the inside of the spire — rungs and guide-cables rising through cold black air — and partway up the last stretch the sound begins, arriving through your hands before your ears can sort it: a roar with no edges, white and total, like weather that has decided to live indoors. By the final hundred rungs it is a physical thing. It presses on your chest. It fills your skeleton the way water fills a pipe.

The hatch at the top is small, waist-high, and warm. When you lift it, the room breathes out at you: heat, and enough water in the air to taste — mineral, faintly sweet, the flavor of hot stone and something alive.

The room is small. That is the first surprise, after all that climbing: a chapel could hold it. Three low galleries stacked one above the other, stitched together by ladders and by water — because the water is the architecture. The real structure, the thing the builders made and the water has finished, is a single intricate fall: fog comes in at the crown, becomes droplet, becomes trickle, becomes channel, becomes cataract, becomes cistern, and then — this is the part that takes a while to believe — climbs back up again through a bank of hair-thin tubes that hum at a pitch you feel in your teeth, water rising steadily with no pump anywhere, lifting itself rung by rung along surfaces swept by fields the old engineers understood and the present day has mostly forgotten how to describe. The water goes around and around. Some of it leaves forever, through thick pipes that vanish into the floor, down into the dark of the spire, down toward wherever it has always gone. The room drinks the sky and gives the water to the world below and no one has checked on it in a very long time.

Everything here is made. That takes a moment to land, because it doesn't look made at first — it looks like a cave that lucked into plumbing. The floor channels run smooth and black and glassy, worn by ten thousand years of trickle, and every one of them has been renewed to its exact original curve by the menders, so that the wearing and the mending cancel and the channel has kept one shape longer than any river ever has.
