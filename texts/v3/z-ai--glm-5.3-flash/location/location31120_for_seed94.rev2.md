The last of the brown country gives out a half-day below the fog, and from there the climb is a climb into weather rather than toward it. The air thickens as you rise — not warmer, never warmer, but heavier, wetter, until your own breath hangs behind you like a slow animal. And your voice begins to fail. It still works, technically. It goes three meters and dies in the wool of the air. By the time you break through the fog deck onto the plateau proper, you have stopped trying to speak at all.

What you come up into is a second world, flat-lying and enormous, a country with weather in it rather than under it. There are no peaks here. The relief is all water-shaped: long swells of bog, ridges of dark stone standing like rails above the sponge, silver threads of flush seaming every hollow. The horizon is pools. From any rise you can see them by the thousand — shallow lenses of tea-black water set in green, each one reflecting a different piece of the sky, so the ground below the horizon looks like a sky that fell and stayed.

The ground itself is the first thing to understand. There is no bare earth anywhere. Not one patch. Moss — ankle-deep wool-moss, knee-high cushions, mats that shingle one over another for hundreds of years — covers everything, and under the moss is peat, and under the peat is water, and the water is at the surface everywhere, unhidden, unashamed. You walk on cushions that compress and pass your weight outward in rings, so that twenty feet away a pool puckers to announce you. Everything here touches everything else: roots one mattress, water one vein. The density is not crowding exactly. It is more like saturation — the landscape filled to the brim with itself.

The green is strange for the height and the cold, and the reason is simple: nothing here ever dries. Lushness through perpetual wet rather than perpetual sun. Fern brakes crowd the lee of every stone rail. Rosette plants stand shoulder-high in the sheltered flushes, each leaf axil holding its own cup of clear water, so that the plants are landscapes too — little reservoirs nested in the big one. Sundew fringes every pool margin with wet red teeth. The stunted trees — where they persist at all, bent under the wind in the gullies — are furred completely, moss on bark on lichen on moss, wood you could mistake for animal. The color of the whole place is a green with no shine in it. The green of things that are always cold and never thirsty.

And the quiet. This is the marish's deepest rule, and you learn it before you learn anything else: sound dies young here. The moss eats it. The fog eats it. The saturated ground will not carry a footfall, will not return an echo, will not let a splash survive past a few meters. A heron-thing strikes a fish in a pool beside you and the whole event is a soft *toc* and rings, and the rings are the louder part. Birds here do not sing. They ripple. Water-striders tap rhythms into the pond surfaces and the rhythms travel; you read the marish's conversation in surface tension, or you feel it faintly through your soles, a tremor arriving before any noise does. Wingbeats sound like breathing. The abundant life is not loud life. It has all agreed, over long ages, to speak at a volume the moss permits, and the moss permits almost nothing.

But the plateau is not still. Press your palm flat on a stone slab and hold it there, and you will feel it — the hum. Not a sound. A pressure, a purr in the sternum, faintly in the molars. The stains on the stones tell you why: orange bleeding of iron, green tears of copper, black lace of manganese down the seep-faces, where the groundwater weeps out of the whole mass of the plateau and leaves its chemistry written on the rock. Rain falls on the top of this sponge; black water percolates through centuries of peat and bodies of ore; and somewhere in the dark the water gives up its charge, electron by electron, redox front by redox front. The entire marish is a slow battery. Current moves through the saturated ground in a grid of its own invention, and the springs where the circuit grounds are the places the animals know. At dusk they glow — a cold green-white shimmer in the seep films, no heat in it, like light remembered from water — and the midges rise in columns above the glow, and the birds come to fish the columns, and the whole food web hangs off this quiet manufacture. Look closely at any root you pull: nodules of bog iron forming on it, cell by cell, dropping into the muck when the root dies. The marish refines ore and mints it into the mud, continuously, and no one has ever come to collect.

That is the other rule, and it explains why nothing stands here. The marish refuses artifacts. Not violently — patiently. Set an iron knife on a hummock and return in autumn: you will find a stain shaped like a knife and the moss already climbing over it. The ground digests iron in a season. Roots take boards within a year. Any wall you raised would be a brown softening in the peat within your lifetime. Even the marks of passage don't hold: your track closes behind you in hours, green and seamless, and bones — this is the part the animals seem to know — bones sink. The marish keeps no dead and no marks and no works. So it wears none. No cairns, no bridges, no ruins of any attempt. If anyone ever tried to build here, the plateau never let the attempt leave a face to remember it by. There is industry everywhere underfoot and not one artifact anywhere on the horizon. The engine that runs this place was never built. It grew.

The fauna comes in slow shapes, as everything must. The megafauna are mire-cattle — great smooth-skinned amphibians the size of oxen, hide patterned exactly like cushion-moss so that from a ridge you cannot tell herd from hillside until one of the hills drinks. They graze the moss all day in congregations of forty, silent, enormous throats working, and their passage opens lanes through the cushion-fields that take a week to close — one of the only movements large enough to read from far away. Ground-birds flock over them, picking insects from the cattle's wake, and these birds hum when they flush, a felt thing more than a heard one, a tremor in the air like a struck string two rooms away. Shrew-things hunt inside the moss mattress itself, and you track them only as tremors, small earthquakes of appetite. And in the quiet pools, some of the dark lids are not water. The predation here is all ambush and no announcement. A bird drinks at the wrong pool and there is no cry, no struggle worth the name — just rings going out, and then rings going out from nothing.

The weather is the marish's own, made and spent in a single day. Dawn is cold and startlingly clear: frost feathers on the pool margins that melt by midmorning, the fog deck draining off the rim like a tide going out. Then the plateau begins to breathe. By noon every pool is exhaling mist; the whole country lifts a grey breath into the thin bright air; by afternoon the breath has stacked into clouds with no ancestry but itself, and by evening it rains, the same water coming home, and the drips begin. All night the leaf-rain falls — condensation wrung out of the air by the cold, every fern fringe ticking, though no cloud is anywhere. That ticking is the clock of the place. And out across the interior, where the plateau stretches further than you will ever walk, you can watch its private weather standing over distant quarters like grey curtains, rain falling somewhere the size of a nation. When lightning flickers in those curtains there is no thunder. There is never thunder. The moss takes it before it can be born, and the storms are silent shows, light with the voice removed.

The scale takes days to believe. Rivers begin here in their thousands — none of them wide, all of them old, all of them balanced, neither cutting down nor silting up, giving away exactly as much water as the sponge can spare. The shape of the land is the shape it has agreed to, erosion settled into a long truce. In the interior the pools swell into inland seas, a still country you could cross in a week of wading; the stone rails run for days like causeways no one laid; and at the rim — you see it best from below, from the brown country, looking up — the whole escarpment weeps. Thousands of seeps along a cliff-line that runs out of sight both ways, each one a thin silver thread that never gathers into a waterfall, all of them together a hem of hanging mist half a horizon wide. The lowlanders call it rain coming off the roof of the world. It is the marish's only export.

At night the cold comes up off the water with nothing to stop it, and the hum grows more tactile as the day-sounds — the few there were — go out. If you lie back on a cushion-field you can feel the current passing, a tremble too faint to be called trembling. The nearest bright seep glows in the dark like a coin under black water. And once each dusk, somewhere out on the plateau, a single bird rings one pure bell of a note that carries impossibly far — the one sound the moss seems to allow, by whatever grace governs such things — and then the silence closes over it without a seam.

You are lying, you realize, on the roof of a water table the size of a country, inside the casing of an engine nobody built, an industry of rain and ore and moss that manufactures soil by day and current by night and has never once, in all its balanced ages, raised its voice. The silence here is not emptiness. It is the sound of everything already talking to everything else, at a volume you have to press your hand against the world to feel.
