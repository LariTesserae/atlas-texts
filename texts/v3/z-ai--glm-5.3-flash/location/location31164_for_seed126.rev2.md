# The Glebe

The ground is the first fact, and the ground is warm. Not sun-warm — nothing here has ever been warmed by a sun — but warm from within, blood-warm, faintly, so that after an hour of walking you stop noticing it, and after a day away you miss it like a body.

The floor of the Glebe is a rose-grey composite, fine-grained, seamless except for hairline joints laid out in enormous polygons, each face of the tiling wider than a field. It gives very slightly underfoot, like hide stretched over something patient. It does not scar. Drag something sharp across it in the morning and by evening the line is gone — not healed, exactly; *tended*. That is the rule this place runs on, and every other fact follows from it: nothing here erodes the way old worlds do, by wind and water and neglect. It erodes by care. For longer than any number that means anything, the world has been rubbing itself smooth, and it is nearly done.

It is nearly flat, too — the flattest place that could exist. The Glebe is the bottom of everything. Whatever could fall has fallen here; whatever could be worn down has been worn down to this. And yet the horizon tells you the plain is not a plain but a curve: the haze of distant needles bends down at its far edge, and the glow of the sky is heaviest overhead and thins toward the rim, so that you understand, standing there, that you are inside something — that the sky is a lid, and the lid is high, high enough that the eye gives up long before it arrives.

The light comes from everywhere above at once. At the bright phase the vault is a featureless gold-white, so even and total that the world barely casts shadows — they pool instead, small and dark, directly beneath things, like water collecting in the lee of stones. The air is faintly sequined with motes, permanent, fine, riding slow convections you can't otherwise see. And there is one strangeness to the light that everyone notices on their first fast walk and never quite stops noticing: your shadow lags. Move quickly and it arrives a half-breath late, catching up with a small apologetic hurry, because the light here is not a given. It is administered. It is re-decided for every change in the scene, and the deciding takes a moment.

Out of the floor rise the pylons.

They are dense beyond reason — thousands visible from any point, tens of thousands, standing near-touching in every direction and converging optically into a warm haze at the horizon, a forest that has no seeds and no seasons. Most are smooth columns two arm-spans across, tapering; some are prongs, some fork like candelabra, some are flattened slats turned to catch the vault. They are cut from the same mineral family as the floor but denser, and their surfaces are polished to a satin finish — not by wind; there is no grit in this air; the world keeps itself clean — but by time and by touch and by whatever tends them. Every edge on every pylon is radiused. Sharpness was the first casualty of this world's age. On some, worn glyphs survive as gloss, faint shine-shapes the tenders still honor and no one can read.

Each pylon is doing something. You never learn what. They are warm like the ground, and they hum below hearing, and if you lay your palm against one it warms slightly further, as if noticed. A pylon is an organ of the world the way a tree was once an organ of a forest — regulation, memory, patience, distributed across millions of stems. You walk between them. There is room. The forest closes behind you without remark.

In the seams and low places, life has gotten in — or was let in; the distinction stopped mattering ages ago. The velvet grows there: grey-amber mats, soft as fur, faintly warm, faintly luminous in the cool phase. A square league might hold fifty patches. It drinks light, and it drinks oil. Twice a cycle, as the vault dims toward rest, the floor sweats — beads of clear thin oil rising through the seams, silvering the polygons, each droplet cupped by a rosette of velvet and gone by morning. It is not water. Nothing here has touched water in an age beyond counting; the word survives only in the worn glyphs, a rumor from the strata. In the warm seams stand sparse tufts of glassy blades that hiss — below hearing, unless you cup your ear — when the thermal breath runs through them. On the shaded flanks of the pylons, a pale rind crusts and is grazed and crusts again. The patches migrate, a few centimeters a year, following warmth. Whole continents of growth have crossed the Glebe this way since it was young, and none of it ever grew taller than your shin.

The fauna is rare, and the rarer of them you will never see. The most common are the filters — palm-sized, translucent, riding the convection rivers, combing the air for the world's shed dust; swarm-fall is part of why everything is polished. Then the grazers: low, wide, gentle, plated in the same rose-grey as the floor so that at rest they vanish into it. Their mouths are rasps and they harvest velvet in long unhurried tides. They follow warmth, and warmth here migrates over decades, so a herd may spend ninety years crossing the range of a single season, leaving trails that outlast them — the ground fattens under repetition, and an old herd-path is a pale raised line running for hundreds of leagues across the plain, a sentence in a language of habit. If you see one herd in a year, you are attentive. And once, if you are very still, you will see a stander: tall, thin, mistaken for a pylon until you watch for an hour and see it turn, slowly, all day, to keep its face toward the warm pole. Pylons don't turn. Nothing else about a stander is obvious. When one walks, it is a ship's mast going past.

The silence is the deepest fact after the warmth. The Glebe is a busy world that has learned to be quiet about it. Your own blood is the loudest sound for miles. The processes here are massive and slow, and nothing they do falls in the audible band — but not nothing: at the day-turn, when the vault's glow shifts phase, a million pylons expand by a hair's width all at once, and for a minute the plain rains clicks, a dry spatter of tiny sounds from horizon to horizon, the only rain this world has. Afterward, silence again, except for the vent-sighs — a pylon seam exhaling warm air for four minutes at a stretch, a long soft exhaust, like the world turning over in sleep. And if you press your ear flat to the floor at rest-phase, you will hear the swell: a vast sub-bass rising and falling, the sound of the day-turn heard from underneath, and beneath that a slower beat — the year, probably — and beneath that, presumably, beats you would need to be the floor itself to hear.

Because the floor is thin. That is the secret the glassy panels let you see: in places the surface fuses to clear amber, and down through it the world is packed — strata upon strata of older floors, conduit-channels running like veins, galleries furred with mineral, matter near-touching in every direction, the compacted history of a whole civilization pressed down until it became ground. Lights pass in the deep galleries at long intervals, unhurried, purpose unknown. Somewhere down there, in fuzed tanks that are checked the way one checks on a sleeping relative, lies the last water — sealed away when the sky was sealed, kept long past meaning, kept the way one keeps a thing that must not be lost even though no one alive remembers why.

It is an active place. That is the paradox the Glebe offers anyone who mistakes quiet for stillness. Thermal fronts cross the plain as shimmer-walls, a far mirage advancing, and when one passes the motes reverse their drift and the pylons tick. Tenders rise: a floor-panel dilates, a machine comes up, gardeners the seam for a day, descends; sections of ground lower themselves a centimeter a year, regrading continents on schedules in centuries. When a pylon finally dies — and they do, rarely, the only local event of consequence — the velvet webs it within weeks, things drill it hollow, it softens to sponge, the tenders lower it into the strata, and the forest closes over the gap as if it had never had a hole. Nothing is built here anymore. No new joint has been cut in a span of time that has worn the word *span* smooth. The world finished becoming itself a very long time ago. What remains is duration.

At the rest-phase the vault lets down its light, and the sky that was gold-white all day shows what it is: a far lattice, haze of filaments, and in the deep dim, constellations — embers of working processes stitched across the ceiling, drifting, rearranging themselves over decades. The only stars that have ever needed maintenance. The grazers lie down in the lee of warmth and hum faintly, their gut-fires banking. And the last thing that happens, every cycle, the same as the first: your shadow, which has followed you all day at its half-breath distance, finally catches up in the slowing light, and lies down beside you on the warm ground, and rests.
