Long before there is anything to see, there is the chord.

You come down out of water so cold it has the temperature of a cellar, of stone in shade, of the hour before dawn — and the chord comes up to meet you through the dark. It is a low, broad hum, felt in the chest before it is heard, and threaded through it are small bright percussions: snaps, ticks, tiny cracks, scattered so evenly that they stop being events and become a texture, a grain in the sound the way sand is a grain in stone. Sound travels far in cold water, and this sound has been traveling longer than anything has been alive to be annoyed by it. It does not swell. It does not fade. It simply is, at one volume, forever.

You cannot see the mountain yet. There is no daylight down here to see it by — no dusk, no depth-shade, none of the gentle gradients by which eyes measure the world. The dark is not absence so much as a medium, thick and even, and you swim through it until the first lamps appear.

They do not flicker. Flickering is a kind of weather, and weather never got this deep.

The lamps hang at intervals in the black like a constellation that never sets, and each one is an animal — a polyp with a burned-glass brightness it has held at the same level since before your species learned fire. Where there is a lamp there is usually a presence: a station-keeper, a grazer, a wheel of small fish turning its slow circuit. Where there is no lamp there is nothing, and the nothing goes on for a long time. The place shows itself to you the way a night sky does — sparse points of certainty, profound dark between — and this is not accident but design. Light density is presence density here. The emptiness is load-bearing.

Then the mountain resolves out of the dark, and you understand what extreme means in a place like this.

It rises from an abyssal plain that is one of the flattest surfaces the world makes — flattened not by violence but by patience, silt laid down in an evenness that took geological ages to perfect — and it climbs four kilometers into the water column, a wall of architecture the whole way.
