# The Weft

Out in the old fen the mist lies flat until mid-morning, and in it, first of all, comes the sound of the town: not voices, and no bell — there is no bell here — but water, falling in small measured drops from a hundred places at once. Each drop has its own tempo. As you wade the last black channel and haul up onto the gravel, the drips sort themselves into an order: the slow one is the hood by the fish-racks, that quick double is the mouth with the bent lip, and so on, until the whole town has become a kind of clockwork you could hear with your eyes shut. The folk of the Weft say you can walk it blind and know every door by its drip. They say it because it is true.

When the mist thins, you see what the clockwork is hung on: a long low bar of gravel and sand, three hundred paces end to end, standing barely a man's height above the standing water — less, when the water is up. The crest gleams after rain like the back of a fish. Around it the world is flat in every direction: pools like hammered pewter, thin stands of reed going bronze, and far off, on clear days, the smoke-lines of two or three other ridges out in the fen, known by smudge and rumor only.

The bar is new. Two generations ago — the oldest people's grandmothers watched it happen — the great river that made this fen swung its course away in one monstrous winter, and its abandoned reach stood up out of itself, week by week: banks first, then bars, then this spine of gravel, rising into air that had been over it all their lives. People walked out onto ground that had been river-bottom and drove the first stakes while it was still draining. That is why everything here looks unfinished. The flanking gullies have edges still on them; after each rain they come back changed, one deeper, one rerouted, one gone. Sand sheets lie rippled where the falling water left them, like the marks in a wash-basin. The gravel at the rim is crisp-cornered, the waste of some enormous workshop, nothing yet blunted. The town's answer to all this rawness is work: the hood-keepers go along the seep-faces laying woven mats and sharpened stakes wherever a bank has begun to slump, and by evening the ground is stitched again, and by the next rain it is raw again, and nobody is surprised.

The name of the town is exact. Alder piles, driven in rows through the gravel, are the warp; over them, woven, the town — the weft. Everything else is water. The houses stand so close their eaves touch, each one raised on nine or fewer piles, knee-walled in peat-sod, wattle above, daubed with the gray clay, thatched steeply in reed. Bonfire-fired pots. Antler wedges, bone awls, cord of plaited reed and eel-skin. Fire lives on counted stones, and the town's two copper pins, traded from a far ridge, live in the Round and are spoken of the way weather is spoken of: seldom, and with respect. There are no wheels here, no roads, no nails. There are walkways — hurdles over brush, running spine and ribs through the packed roofs — and there is the standing work of the ground. The ground is hungry. Because nothing has rotted here yet, because the soil is still only gravel and dead reed, it compacts and goes down under any weight, and so the town feeds it: every few days reed bundles and brush are hauled up from the rim and pulled under the walkways, and the ground eats, and the walking surface stays where it is. The town is a meal the ground is always eating.

And this is where the Weft is strange, and its people know it is strange, and it is the deepest thing about them: *nothing here rots. It waits.* The wet keeps everything. Dig a cistern, drive a pile, sink a post — and up comes the earlier town, whole: a door with its hanging still stiff, a basket a grandmother wove, the first town's hearthstone, brown as apple skin and cold as the bottom of a jug. There are three towns under these floors, and none of them is old. So the town has rules about what goes into the ground. What the fire will take, burn. What the water may keep, give to the water. The dead go out past the rim on hurdle rafts, a stone at the breast. And everyone knows their depth: dig a hand past the keeping line and the hole fills within the hour, cold and patient, and takes your work with it.

The water itself is managed through the weep-mouths. Wherever a cut face weeps ground-water, there is a hood of flat stones, a wad of harvested moss, a pot set in a drip-hollow — one to a family, tended like a cow. Two mouths run rust, and their iron-tasting water is not for drinking; it tans the eel-leather and makes the red ochre with which door-posts are painted. At the crest, in the meeting-house called the Round, is the Cup: the great clay-lined cistern, hurdle-lidded, and in it the Reading-stake, notched at each hand's depth. At first light the water-crier walks the spine and calls the level. *"Coming up. Two notches."* Or: *"Slack. Holding. Cut your peat."* The whole day's work hangs from that voice. Etiquette is short and firm: take from a mouth, give back a hand of moss.

And the water keeps a calendar of its own. No tide reaches this fen; no moon explains it; yet every seep, every well, every pool, every pot — all the water in the ground, everywhere at once — rises and falls together, a slow breath of nine days: three rising, three full, three sinking. On the rise, the rim walkway stands a finger-depth under and the eels come up out of the deep fen, invisible plenty, and the traps are set. On the slack, the rim pans shrink to milk-puddles, the mud cracks into wide polygon plates, the children go out and take the shrimp — blind, white, cold things born in the dark under the bar, surfacing only now — and the peat face is cut, and the walkway boards go up on chocks. Why it breathes, nobody has settled. The criers say the water keeps its own calendar. The old ones say the far river pulls it like a rope through the ground. The children say the plain is breathing. The far ridges' smokes rise and dip in time with the Weft's, everyone knows that; no one has gone to test it.

Vegetation is young and thin. Moss in black-green felt on every seep face, cut for wiping and wicking and binding wounds; sparse silver grass on the driest crest; reed in patches, cut carefully, because the reed is as young as everything else. There are three willow withies in a tub of peat, and one tree — a young alder, a dozen years, thigh-thick, called Old Sister, the oldest living thing in the town, which tells you everything about how old things are here. One sprig of her is taken at each turning, for the peak of the Round. Fauna is mostly heard of rather than seen: the eels below, the shrimp in the pans, leeches in the wash-places, one red dragonfly working the slack pools, a single warm week of frogs. A gray heron-thing visits at the turnings and is read like weather. An otter takes its tithe from the traps and is not resented. Twice a year, skeins go over high, far up, and do not land. *"Too young,"* say the elders — meaning the ground. Even the birds can see it.

Light is honest. Midday burns the mist off and opens the fen silver and enormous, so that from the crest you can see every roof of the Weft at once and nothing else in any direction. The afternoon stacks its gray cloud-streets. The soft long rains come, an hour of whisper on thatch — the loudest weather the town knows — and afterward the Weft rains from itself for an hour more, every eave, every hood, the whole town weeping quietly onto its own walkways. Evenings go long and gold, the wet flats shining like beaten ore. Some mornings there are frost parings on the pans, thin as nail-parings, lifted whole by children.

And at night the Weft is a low raft of embers in a black field. The drips go on, slower. The timber ticks as it swells. Somewhere an eel noses through a stake-gap; somewhere the ground takes down a hair's breadth; in the dark of the Cup the water stands at a new notch on the stake, waiting for dawn and the crier's voice. The children here learn five things before they learn anything else: the water breathes on its own count; weight goes down, so build light and feed the ground; nothing here rots, so be careful what you put under your feet — it will come up again, whole, in some grandchild's time; take from a mouth, give back a hand; and know your depth.

Beyond that, there is only the counting. All night, all over the fen, all the water is counting.
