**The Hollow**

The plain does not announce it. A low country of pale stone, scoured and bright, going on in every direction under a white sky — and somewhere in it, a dimple no wider than a threshing floor. From above you would read it as a shadowless shade of white, a softness in the glare. You find it by leaning over the lip and seeing green: a thread of moss stitched along a crack, a fern's knuckle in a seam, and below that, opening like a held breath, the hollow itself.

It is a sink in the earth, maybe forty paces across, ringed by walls of rock slumped to their resting angle long ago — so long ago that the slump is no longer an event but a shape. Scree at the base has welded into one body under centuries of lichen. Nothing here looks ruined and nothing looks new. It looks *finished*. The erosion happened, all of it, and then stopped, the way a sentence ends.

You climb down by handholds worn to fit. And as you descend, three things happen at once.

The air thickens with damp — not rain, not mist, but a film. Water never falls in this place; it *condenses*. Every surface wears a skin of moisture so thin it cannot be seen, only felt, a coolness that transfers to your palms. The stone weeps at the scale of beads. The air itself seems to have a surface tension.

The cold closes around you — real cold, near the floor of what a body tolerates — but it is a still cold, without wind, without bite. The cold is not weather here. It is a substance, like the rock and the moss, and it has settled into its place.

And the sound dies.

That is the shock. One step down, and the world goes acoustically dead — not quiet, *absent*. The moss that upholsters every surface swallows noise the way sand swallows water. Your footfall happens and is gone within a hand's breadth. Your breath is the loudest thing you brought. You could open your mouth and speak and nothing would come of it; the words would be a movement of air without an event. Deeper in, you will learn that the people who live here talk with their hands — words traced on the back of a wrist, on a palm, language passed skin to skin like warmth — and you will understand why.

The light, meanwhile, is the strangest thing of all, and it takes a while to see because there is nothing to see. It is bright. Genuinely, generously bright — bright enough to read by, bright as an open noon. But it comes from everywhere. The white sky acts as one vast lamp, scattering itself through cold haze until no direction survives. There are no shadows in the hollow. Not faint ones — *none*. A stone sits in the moss with the same even light on every face of it. And because there are no shadows, there is no sun-position, and because there is no sun-position, there is no time of day. The light is noon that never leans. The dwellers have no word for *morning* or *evening*; when you ask — tracing the shapes on a palm — they offer you instead a word for the light itself, which translates roughly as *the flat white*.

The green, then. Lush is the honest word, lush the way sea-cliffs in cold oceanic countries are lush — not tropical, not tall, but total. No surface is bare. Moss to the depth of a mattress on every ledge; liverworts glossing the wet seeps; polypody ferns erupting from every horizontal crack; dwarf willow and cranberry matted over the shelves; sedge in the damper basins, sallow-bladed and dense. It is packed the way a nest is packed. You measure space here in finger-widths. And everything has grown *to completion* — nothing is mid-growth, nothing is sprouting, nothing is rotting. The vegetation stands at its own fullness the way a bell stands rung.

The town is threaded through all of it.

It is a real town — a true settlement, small as a fist but built with the full grammar of a town: houses, lanes, a square, a well. Thirty-odd cells of dry-stone, corbelled inward course by course, slabs fitted without mortar and roofed with sedge thatch that has since become moss-sod, so that the houses do not sit *in* the vegetation so much as continue it. Lanes are slots a body turns sideways in. Stairs are notches in the wall-face, each tread dished like a river stone by generations of the same feet — though *generations* may be the wrong word, in a place where the count does not move. The construction is layered three and four deep, houses stacked against the sink wall on ledges, ladders of antler rungs, door-hinges of hide, doors of woven willow with moss grown clean through the weave. No metal anywhere. Every tool is bone, antler, slate, birch, gut-cord. An antler pick leans at a doorway with the same patience as the wall it built.

The square is the pool. The spring rises under it — a slow seep, fed from somewhere in the rock at a trickle's pace — and fills a stone basin the size of a table. It never rises and never falls; what enters leaves by an invisible lip at exactly the rate it arrives. The water is so still and so cold it looks solid, a lens of slate, and it is lit so evenly from all sides that it reflects nothing at all. You look into it and there is no sky on it, only depth. Around its rim, the town gathers; the well is the hearth of the whole place, and its sound is the closest thing the hollow has to a voice.

Because the pool drips. One bead from the spring-lip, then another, at an interval so regular it stops being a sound and becomes a fact. It does not tell time — time here is not something that passes — it only *insists*. The dwellers do not hear it anymore. A visitor never stops hearing it.

As for whether anyone is home: you will think at first that the town is a ruin, because nothing moves. Then you notice the breath. Each house has a smoke-hole, and from each smoke-hole a thread of vapor rises — warm damp air from banked peat-fires meeting the cold, beading into faint ghosts above the roofs. Thirty hearths, thirty breaths. The fires do not flicker. Peat burns here at a constancy that seems wrong, flames steady as paint, giving their heat in a level line, and the whole town exhales its shared weather — the only weather the hollow has.

The people are short, quiet, and unhurried past any hurry you have a word for. They work, and the work is the strangest part: they work *at the rate of the place*. They cut sedge exactly as fast as it lengthens, no faster.
