# The Seedbed

Start with the light, because the light is the first thing that shouldn't be there.

This is the floor of the sea — a kilometer and a half down, where the water is five degrees cold and pressure would fold a lung like paper — and yet it lies under a broad, even, afternoon-colored glow. Not the thin blue starvation-light of a natural abyss. Something closer to an overcast noon in a northern harbor, dimmed and softened by distance but unmistakably daylight in character. It comes down from masts. Every few kilometers a tower rises from the plain, slender and braced, crowned with an array of lamps the size of houses, burning the sun's own spectrum downward into the dark. The glow-domes of the masts overlap hand to hand, so that nowhere in the basin is there true night. Between towers, where two lights falter, you can find faint seams of dimmer water, dusk-colored corridors — and here and there a mast stands dark, its lamps not yet installed, rising into the brightness like a column of missing tooth. The charts call this Basin Nine. The maintenance crews call it the Seedbed, and the masts are the reason: the light is not for visitors. It is for the plants.

Under this engineered noon, the floor stretches out.

It is made. All of it. There is no natural substrate anywhere in reach — no weathered bedrock, no ancient sediment spine. The plain is cast in enormous tessellated panels of dark artificial stone, seamed every hundred meters with expansion joints, each panel still bearing the faint mold-marks of its pouring, like fingerprints in clay. The relief — the hills and valleys of the place — was built, not worn: a constructed ridge-line runs off toward the east with scarps so fresh they look cut yesterday, corners crisp, no rubble fans at their feet, no slumps, no caves. Even the fake textures are crisp. Where the builders molded the panels to resemble old lava, the "ancient" rock has the stiffness of a costume still warm from its form. And over all of it lies the world's first sediment: a dusting of graded silt, millimeters deep, laid down deliberately at the opening and now, for the first time, being reworked by current into honest ripples. It is the only thing here that has begun to age on its own.

The burrows tell the story plainly. Small crabs — abundant, busy, everywhere — dig into the silt and strike the panel beneath after ten centimeters. Every burrow in this sea bottoms out on the floor of the world. Some of the crabs have learned to line their holes with shell fragments; most have not. It has not been long enough.

Rust is beginning, though. Orange blooms spread along the bolt-lines of junction boxes and the feet of the light masts — the first weathering this place has ever produced, arriving like baby teeth. Barnacles have found the pump housings and the cable runs that stitch the plain, armored conduits marching off toward the trench mouths, and they sit on the machinery with the innocent confidence of pioneers who don't know the ground is younger than they are.

The water itself is astonishingly empty. That is the next thing you notice — the clarity. Almost nothing hangs in it: no silt load, no murk, barely a drifting cell between you and the horizon of the plain. You can see thirty, forty kilometers down a current-lane before the water finally gives out into a blue that is depth rather than distance. The emptiness makes the lit water feel like a cathedral with the furniture removed — and then, very far off, something small and pale pulses through it. A jellyfish, alone, crossing a space the size of a country. It is the only moving thing for a mile in any direction, and it makes the vastness legible the way a single bird makes a sky legible.

The abundance, when you find it, is clumped hard against the machinery.

The outfalls are the oases. The circulation engines — and there are engines, this whole sea is stirred like a vat — exhale warm, mineral-rich, nutrient-laden water through diffuser arrays set along the trench channels, and the plumes rise from them like smoke, visible as shimmer-walls where warm water meets cold. Around each diffuser the builders planted kelp in ruler-straight rows, and around each outfall the kelp took — in patches, incompletely, a third of it thriving and the rest gone to stubble under grazing pressure. Snail-swarm hordes mow the algae films down faster than the films can grow. Above the thickest thickets, bait-balls of seeded fish gather in mile-wide, mile-deep schools, silver volumes that turn as one body when the predators come through — and the predators come through constantly, because nothing here has learned restraint yet. The ecosystem is in its pioneer phase, all throttle and no brake: booms, crashes, shoals in the wrong places, young sharks patrolling the seam-lines where prey funnels along the panel joints, squid rising on the thermal plumes like followers of smoke. The animals are abundant and naïve. They have no inherited map of this terrain — no grandmother eel ever showed them the way through this particular dark — so their migrations are improvised, sprawling, sometimes magnificent, sometimes doomed. They nest in the machinery. They spawn against the lamp masts. They are writing the first traditions of a place that has none.

The weather is hydraulic. The engines' waste heat rises in fat convection columns that braid and lean with the pump-cycle; thermocline fronts move across the plain like slow fronts of atmosphere, bending the lamp-light as they pass. Twice a day the great pumps in the trenches reverse, and the whole basin turns over — a tide made of schedule rather than moon, a wall of gentle current you can feel leaning on your back for an hour. The operators run the sea through programmed seasons: in "winter" the pumps slow and the masts dim to a dusk ration and the water drops another degree, and the fauna, who do not yet know that winter here is a setting on a console, respond with honest panic, moving, huddling, thinning. This is the Seedbed's first winter. Everything in it is first-generation.

And the sound — the sound never stops. The deep chord of the circulation engines underlies everything, a bass note felt in the chest rather than heard, and it carries absurdly far through the cold clear water: a pump station eighty kilometers away announces itself as a tectonic hum, steady as a horizon. Over it, the percussion of the place — hull-ticks of metal warming and cooling, the far grind of a silt-dredger working a channel, the scheduled thunder of a maintenance crawler dragging its lights along a causeway like a small dawn on treads. And over that, the life: the crackling sizzle of snapping shrimp colonizing every panel seam, a static-hiss of tiny pistol-shots so dense it becomes a texture; the moans and knocks of fish choruses under the masts; the high pinging of steward buoys marking the nutrient lines. Loud, all of it, layered and constant. Silence does not exist here. Even in the empty stretches, the water carries the hum the way air carries wind.

Here and there, in the flanks of the built ridges, pressure-doored galleries hold pockets of true air — maintenance bells where crews surface between shifts — so the world is submerged but not wall-to-wall liquid; there are rooms in it where a person can stand and hear the sea only as pressure on the walls. From outside, they read as faint echoing bladders in the rock. The crews are the only human presence, and they are thin on the ground: a pinger, a crawler, a service submersible running its inspection lights down a cable run, small as a firefly against the plain. The works imply a plan of centuries. The plan is on year thirty.

That is the true character of the place, the thing that gathers all its strangeness into one word: it is new. Not alien, not impossible — an abyss that behaves exactly as an abyss should, cold and lit and humming, full of the ordinary violence of eating and being eaten — but young, younger than any sea has been since the world cooled. The seams will not always show. The silt will thicken to meters; burrows will stop hitting bottom; rust will finish its argument with the bolt-lines and lose; the kelp will close its gaps; the fauna will inherit maps from grandmothers; the dark masts will light; the mold-marks will vanish under life, and no one will be able to say where the made ends and the grown begins. The machinery will go on turning the water over underneath everything, unseen, unheard — or heard the way you hear your own blood.

But that is later. Now the plain lies bright and bare under its lamps, sharp-cornered and faintly mold-scented with newness, crackling with shrimp, trembling with pumps, its first millimeter of history rippling in the current. An ocean at the beginning of learning to be one.
