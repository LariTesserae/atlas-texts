You would never find the Dint on purpose. The plain above it is a stone table under a hazy sky, featureless for days in every direction, dim enough that noon looks like late evening anywhere else. The only sign of the hollow is a seam of warm air crossing the ground, barely a breath, rising out of a crack you could step over without noticing. The folk who live below say the plain keeps the Dint the way a closed hand keeps a coin.

The way down is the Stair: a spiral cut into the rim rock, its steps dished by feet and by the slow scouring of glass-dust wind, worn so smooth that whole generations of walking seem pressed into it like fossil rain — not that anyone here has ever seen rain.
