# The Shelf

There is no ground beneath this place in the way ground is usually meant. The Shelf hangs at the top of the world — a limestone terrace perhaps four hundred paces across, clinging to the shoulder of a peak so high that the air itself is thin and glassy and cold. Below it, cloud-sea. Above it, more cloud, and the dim violet ceiling of the upper sky. The Shelf is a room in the sky, and everything in it has learned to live in one room.

The name is accurate: it is small. You can walk its length in a quarter hour, if you walk carefully. Its edges are its boundaries — crumbled old rim-wall, moss-furred, dripping — and beyond them the world simply drops away into cloud and wind.

**Water is the master here.** The cliff face behind the terrace weeps constantly. A hundred seeps bead out of the rock and run down in threads, gathering into streams, and the streams have worn the limestone for — who knows how long — into a settled, balanced network of channels, terracettes, and little falls, none taller than a man. Pools sit in hollows like bowls of pewter. Mist hangs in the air most of the time, condensing on everything, so that every stone, every stem, every strand of spider-silk carries a rim of water. The whole place drips. It never stops dripping.

**The sound is enormous.** This is the paradox of the Shelf: it is tiny and nearly empty of things, and yet it is one of the loudest places imaginable. Wind comes off the cloud-sea below and over the rim in long rhythmic surges, and the terrace acts as a sounding board — hollows in the rock boom softly, the mist-filled air thrums, and the hundred waterfalls rattle and splash over each other in a continuous hissing chord. Locals — the birds, and the few people who have ever lived here — say the Shelf *speaks*. Sometimes the wind finds a resonance in a fissure and holds a single low note for a minute at a time, like a pipe organ practicing in an empty cathedral.

**Life is abundant but sparse-looking** — density near nothing, fauna everywhere. The flora is lush in the wet zones: deep mosses, sphagnum cushions, saxifrages with wet-black leaves, stunted willow scrub, small white flowers with heads that never quite close in the drizzle. But between the green patches the bare wet rock stretches out, so the whole thing reads as sparse — islands of thick growth in a wash of stone. The animals, by contrast, are everywhere: a dense little ecology compressed into four hundred paces. Flightless midges swarm in the lee of boulders. Amphibian-like frogs — cold-adapted, pale, the size of thumbs — cling to the seep walls and chorus at dusk in a sound like pebbles applauding. Small marmot-things graze the moss lawns and are hunted by a pair of slate-feathered raptors that nest on the rim and ride the updrafts in long, screaming circles. In the pools: translucent shrimp-like things, and one species of small flat fish found nowhere else on the mountain, or anywhere at all, probably.

**The people — there were people** — left only scattered marks. A low shelter of dry-stacked slabs, half-fallen, its roof long gone, on the driest knoll. A channel cut deliberately in the rock to route one seep toward the shelter — the only straight line on the Shelf, everything else being curved by water and weather. Simple tools: a stone hammer-head lodged in a crack as if set down mid-work and simply never picked up again. Whatever craft lived here was hand-tool, rope-and-slab work, and it has been quiet a long time. The moss has opinions about the paths and is slowly undoing them.

**Weather here is a cycle of hours, not days.** The cloud-sea rises and floods the lower terrace with fog, then withdraws. Cold rain comes sidelong off the void, works itself out in ten minutes, and the sun — a normal, decent, high-altitude sun — breaks through and turns the whole dripping landscape into falling diamonds. Cool always; never warm; the water so cold it aches. And always, through everything, the wind and the water and the wind-and-water, in their long established duet.

It is not a strange place. It is only a small one, hung at the edge of the sky, wet and loud and busy in its own tight way — a grove-sized world that has been in balance so long that everything in it, moss and frog and falcon and abandoned hammer, seems less like it *lives* here and more like it *is* here, the way the dripping is here, permanent and unhurried.
