# The Cloudstead, Fourth Week

The first thing to understand is that the ground is new. Not cleared, not claimed — new. Every surface here was made, recently, by someone, and most of it was made within living memory of the plants now trying to cover it. You notice it underfoot before you notice anything else: the floor is cast, plated, seamed. Great tongue-and-groove terraces of manufactured stone, the joints sealed with dark pitch that still smells faintly of the vat. And the floor is warm. Not sun-warm — warm from below, the way a loaf is warm from the middle. The rock this whole platform sits on is still cooling from its own making, and it breathes its heat up through the seams into your boots.

The whole place is small. That is the second thing. From any point on the Cloudstead you can see nearly all of it: three stacked terraces, a garden deck, the pump-house, the condenser tower, the rail at the edge. A person could walk its full length in twenty minutes and has, many times, pacing off the irrigation lines. There are boundaries everywhere — rails, lips, gutters, a fence of pale struts — and beyond the boundaries, cloud. Not distant cloud. Cloud as the medium the place floats in, close enough to touch, beading on your sleeve. Nothing here has ever seen a horizon.

The air is saturated. That is the permanent condition and the engine of almost everything. Warmth plus altitude plus water-in-everything means the atmosphere itself is always condensing: droplets gather on handrails, on leaf-tips, on the housings of cables, on the inside of the pump-house windows. Along the windward edge run the mist-combs — acres of fine vertical mesh, silver with beaded water, combing the cloud as it drifts through, the harvested moisture running down in threads into milled channels, into gutters, into cisterns you can hear sloshing beneath the garden deck. Rain comes on a cycle nobody quite controls: the cloud thickens, a warm drizzle falls for an hour, the cloud thins. The rain is warm the way bathwater is warm. Everything, always, has the quality of having just been rained on.

And into this wet warmth, someone has planted. The beds are made loam — dark, sweet-smelling, engineered soil laid down in felted mats and mounded slopes — and the growth is astonishing, because growth here has no history of restraint. These are all first-generation plants: fast, tender, huge-leaved, the botanical equivalent of morning. Vines put on a hand-span a day and the keepers trim them every morning; there are neat brass collars on the pipes where the vines are permitted to cross and nowhere else. Moss has found every seam in the manufactured stone and filled it. The green runs three layers deep wherever you look — rail, vine, mist; pipe, fern, drip; trellis, bean, fog — nowhere on the Cloudstead can you perceive just one thing at a time. The first fruit is forming on the south deck, small and hard and green, and someone has tied a tag to it with a date.

The machinery is quiet, and it is quiet on purpose. At the heart of the middle terrace sits the pump-engine, a squat cathedral of a machine that lifts water from the cisterns and pushes it up through the walls, and it does not roar — it breathes. A slow hum you feel in your molars more than your ears, a steam-vent that sighs at intervals, white plumes that rise off the condensers and vanish upward into the same cloud they came from. There is a rule, posted and also simply understood, that you keep your voice down near the young stone. Nobody knows for certain whether noise genuinely startles the rock while it sets. Everyone whispers anyway.

Because the rock *is* still setting. Past the last rail, the built world ends and the raw world begins, and the raw world is unfinished. New stone rises out there in slow domes and faceted slabs — glassy in patches, veined, faintly steaming, ticking softly as it cools and expands. Survey stakes stand among it with dates chalked on them. A crack measured last week is a fold this week. The map of the Cloudstead is redrawn monthly because the mountain it sits on is still deciding its shape, millimeter by millimeter, and dig anywhere — anywhere at all — and there is nothing underneath but more newness. No strata. No fossils. No past. The oldest thing on the plateau is a marker stone at the pump-house labeled *Day One*, and it is thirty-four days old.

Fauna, by contrast, can be counted. Two swifts arrived uninvited in the second week and now nest inside the condenser tower, and their cries are enormous here, flung loud across a landscape built for quiet. A few pale moths live in the pumphouse and are tolerated as pollinators of last resort. The worm census stands at forty, distributed across the garden beds by hand, and someone knows exactly where each one was released. There is one fish, in one tank, against the east wall. On still evenings, far out in the cloud, a lamp blinks — someone else's platform, perhaps, or something else entirely — and no lamp here answers it.

Twice a week comes the pollination fog. The keepers run it at dawn: a gold-colored mist released along the upper terraces, drift and settle, drift and settle, dusting every open flower and every closed one, the whole plateau briefly sweet and amber-lit. It is weather, manufactured. It is one of six or seven weathers the place cycles through. The day has a shape you learn quickly: mist front at gray morning, arriving as a single long breath of wind and then gone; warm drizzle; then the noon-open, when the cloud thins for one fierce hour and true sunlight comes straight down and the entire Cloudstead glitters — every drip lit, every mesh screen burning silver, steam rising visibly off the warm young stone — and then the mist closes again and the drip-hours resume.

The sound of the place is a floor, not an event. Staggered drips from a hundred leaf-tips, arrhythmic, patient. The pump's slow breathing. The tick of cooling stone out past the rail. The paper-rustle of enormous leaves leaning against each other. The swifts, outrageously loud. Footsteps are audible for a long way and the small crew — you rarely see them; you see trimmed vines, a watering schedule chalked on a board, boot-prints half-erased by moss within days — they move through it quietly too, out of habit or out of respect for the growing rock.

Stand at the rail in a drip-hour and the whole project becomes visible at once, which is only possible because the project is small. Below you: no ground, only the bright underside of cloud. Around you: a garden outrunning its own mountain. Beneath you: heat, water, and something tectonic stretching in its sleep. The plants will cover the terraces within the season; the stone will take decades to finish deciding what it is; the swifts will raise broods in machinery that is younger than they are. Everything here is a seedling, including the ground, including the light, including — if the chalk calendar in the pump-house is to be believed, and it is the oldest document on the plateau — time itself.

Tomorrow is Day Thirty-Five. The drip goes on all night. Things grow.
