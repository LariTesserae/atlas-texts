Begin with the water, because the water is the world.

It is not the water of a shore, which belongs to a coast and answers to weather. It is water that has outrun every coast, every basin, every thing that could have held it — a single liquid substance lying over the whole of a small and drowned planet, cold as held breath, three or four kilometers of it stacked above the plain I want to show you. Down here the water does not behave like a fluid you have ever swum in. It is packed. Its own weight has pressed it dense; it carries pressure the way stone carries load; a push on one side of this region is felt on the other as a nudge, a slow announcement. Nothing moves quickly here, and nothing that moves is ever entirely still.

And beneath it, at the bottom of everything, is the plain.

Not a floor — a pavement. That is the first thing to understand about this place, though nothing here is understood quickly. The seafloor for as far as anything could see — and nothing sees far — is paved with nodules: lumps of dark iron and manganese oxide, potato-sized, fist-sized, rounded as river stones, lying shoulder to shoulder across the mud in their uncountable billions. They did not wash here. They grew here, molecule by molecule out of the seawater itself, and they grow at the width of a fingernail per million years. Each one is a calendar. Crack a nodule — and one will be cracked, later in this account — and inside you find onion-bands of mineral, faint rings of rust and black, the chemical memory of ages laid down one over another. Some of these stones were already old when the youngest mountains of the upper world were young, and the mountains are gone. The sea kept the stones.

So the pavement is the plain's ground-cover, and it is absolute: no room between presences. Where the mud shows through, it shows only as thin seams, and even the seams are stitched with burrows and trails and the pale lace of things that lived and were smoothed away. The whole surface has the finish of something handled for a very long time. That is what deep time does when nothing interrupts it: it does not carve. It sands. Every ridge is rounded, every hollow eased, every edge softened toward the shape the water prefers. The great erosion of this world is finished. The mountains gave up before there were eyes to watch them go; the coastlines dissolved; the whole land sank into its solvent and became seawater's memory. What remains is the oldest stable state, the final form. Nothing here is being built. Everything is being kept.

And yet the place is not still.

This is the paradox the plain holds without strain, and you should sit with it a while: maximum motion, minimum change. Far overhead — a kilometer up, two, it hardly matters, distance has gone soft in this medium — there is a storm. There has always been a storm. At the surface of this world-ocean, wind and sea have been in conflict since before the pavement began to grow, a churning that hurls the top of the sea against itself, wall after wall of driven water, a chaos so old it is simply climate. But none of that violence reaches here intact. It arrives transformed, filtered down through kilometers of stratified cold as internal waves: long, slow undulations of the water itself, invisible bellows hundreds of meters tall, passing over the plain the way swells pass over a drowned wreck. They make no crash. They make a pulse. The pulse is felt in the fog as a breathing.

The fog. The lowermost water of this region is saturated — a nepheloid haze, kilometers long and only tens of meters thick, so dense with suspended silt that visibility is a matter of a few body-lengths in any direction. The water here is not clear and will never be clear. It is grey-white going on black, thick as thin broth, and everything that lives on the plain lives inside this murk like fish inside a held cloud. The internal waves breathe it: with the slow tide the fog lifts and thins by a hand's width, then settles and thickens again. Inhale. Exhale. The tide is law down here. It comes and goes in the dark, pumping the storm's leftover energy through the region, and the sediment rises and sinks in answer, grain by grain, and each grain, settling, finds its old place in the pavement — or a new place, its old one having been taken during its fall. That is the rate of change on the plain. A grain at a time. Every now and then, once in a generation perhaps, a pulse of colder, saltier water comes sliding down some distant slope and pours across the plain as a benthic storm — a flood with no surface, no spray, no sound, only a current strong enough to comb the ripple-marks flat and rebuild them into new ones over a sleepless day. The animal that lives here knows these. It anchors itself in the lee of a nodule boulder, pulls its stones close, and waits out the hour when the world washes its face.

The animal. Let me bring you to it, because the plain, so densely packed with mineral and murk, is sparsely inhabited in the way that matters. You could drift a day through this valley of fog and meet nothing larger than an amphipod nosing the seams. The life here concentrates where the rock leaks.

There is a field of seeps toward the middle of the region: cracks in the old crust where gas and mineral-rich water ease out into the cold, rising in columns of beads so slow they look solid, like grey syrup pouring upward. Around each vent spreads a mat of bacteria, white-grey, faintly trembling, the closest thing this world has to meadow — sparse, small, each mat no wider than a table, separated by long empty stretches of pavement. Along the margins of the mats stand thin thickets of tube-worms and small sea-lilies, feathers of stone-pale animal raised into the current, and the current, courteous, moves them in unison, back and back and back, a forest of perhaps forty individuals where a forest used to mean a billion trees. Nearby lies the brine pool: a lake set into the lake, a hollow ringed with mussel crust where water four times saltier than the sea lies unmixed, glassy, colder than the cold. The water above it cannot dissolve into the water around it; the boundary is a shining, wobbling line, and things that cross it — an unwary crab, a sinking leaf of detritus — stop, pickle, and are kept. The plain's rule, everywhere, in everything: what sinks is kept. Nothing here is wasted, because nothing here leaves.

The animal lives at the edge of the largest seep mat. It is an octopus-kind thing, though not one you would recognize — pale, long-armed, its skin the color of the fog it sits in, its two great eyes wide and unreadable in the dark. It is rare because its kind is rare; the deep has supported perhaps a handful of its lineage, spread across this drowned world like punctuation. And it does something almost nothing down here does.

It keeps tools.

Three nodules, selected. One flat — the anvil — set into a shallow scoop it has worn in the mud beside its mat. One heavy — the hammer — held in two arms, polished along its working face from use. One spare, tucked beneath its mantle, carried always. It hunts the spiral-shelled grazers that creep the bacterial mats, and when it catches one it does not bite through the shell; it sets the shell on the anvil and strikes. Tick. A pause, the fog resettling. Tick-tick. The sound carries farther than anything else in this region — ten meters, twenty, the only regular noise in the valley, a small stone percussion marking out the afternoon in the eternal dark. It is the loudest thing in the world, here, and it is barely a sound at all.

There is almost nothing else to hear, and it is worth inventories to say so. The seeps hiss, faintly, a whisper like breath held close to the ear. The internal waves make no sound at all, only a pressure you feel in the soft parts of the body, a slow rolling of the deep. Once in a long while a thump rolls through the water from somewhere out in the fog — some large unseen thing calling across the valley, a signal with no answer, arriving as a felt wave that stirs the nearest worms. And when the rare pulse-currents come, they are silent too. The dense medium muffled its own turbulence eons ago. There is no room in this world to be loud in. The storm above has raged itself down, kilometer by kilometer, into a tap-tap of stone on stone.

The darkness is nearly total. What light exists is made by the things that need it. The animal has photophores under its arms — small cold-blue lenses it switches on while it works, so that from a few meters away the sight is: a fogbank, a pale trembling mat, and a ring of dim blue light low to the ground, and inside the light two arms rising and falling, patient, unhurried. When it hunts in the open it goes dark. Occasionally the murk flashes white around a startle — some shrimp detonating its little charge of light and spending it all at once, a flashbulb in a warehouse — and then the fog closes back over the afterimage, which hangs for a moment like a ghost deciding not to be one.

Scavengers pass. That is the other form of life here, and it is properly rare: once in a great while a shape the size of a chapel comes sliding out of the murk, some vast slow elder of the open dark, and crosses the region without stopping, its wake stirring the fog long after its tail has gone. When such things die — and they do, somewhere far up in the water column, and fall — the fall is an event of the age. There is one on the far side of the seep field: a skeleton, ribs like a fallen arcade, colonized now by sparse crinoids that raise their stone feathers into the slow current and wave, and wave. The flesh was gone within a decade. The frame will stand for centuries. It is, in its way, the only architecture the region has ever had, and nothing built it, and nothing will live in it but what already lives everywhere: mats, worms, patience.

Time here does not feel like time above. It feels like thickness. The pavement thickens. The bands in the nodules add a ring to ten thousand rings. The storm overhead rages on without beginning or intention, and the plain below receives it and converts all of it — all that fury, all that eternal wind and driven water — into a fingernail's width of mineral, laid down in perfect quiet, in the dark, with no witness but the tapping thing, who looks up sometimes when the internal waves pass and its light shivers off the fog, and then goes back to its shell, its anvil, its tick in the silence.

Nothing here is being made. Nothing here is being lost. The world is finished, and the finishing never stops: the fog breathes, the tide sweeps, a grain finds its place, a nodule gains its ring, and stone taps stone, softly, in the packed and ancient water, and it is enough.
