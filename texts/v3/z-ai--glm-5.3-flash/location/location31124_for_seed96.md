# The Drinking Mountain

**First sight**

You see it before you feel it — the thin bright air up here does not soften anything. A grey massif stands out of a plain of pale crust, and across its face, from rim to rim, run white lines: thousands of them, horizontal, stacked, branching. Stairs. Channels. Copings. The whole mountainside is ruled like a page.

From the high rim you can look across the entire extent of it in one turn of the head — a day's walk, no more, and every feature of it named and known: the Long Stair climbing the south face, the cistern courts, the great quarry scar now dressed and dignified into a terrace, the Rim gallery with its low stone seat-stones facing outward over the haze. It is not a vast country. It is a complete one. Nothing in it is provisional, and nothing in it is far.

The light is hard and vertical. The air above the plateau has been wrung of its dust, and the sun comes down with an almost metallic clarity, so that every wet surface throws light back up at you and you walk, all day, lit from above and below at once.

**No ground that is not floor**

There is no soil anywhere. There is no ground that has not been made into ground. Where the massif's grey stone broke the ancient waterline and lifted itself into the air, hands followed it, and everything the hands did not build has since been dressed: the natural benches are floors now, the natural fissures are drains, the raw quarry scar is a stepped amphitheater with a channel cut along its lowest lip so the rock itself weeps on schedule.

Look closely at any surface and it is in use. Every wall carries niches — for lamps, for tools, for sitting out of the drip. Every wall carries marks: tallies of water, instructions, names. Roofs are stairs to further roofs. The space between structures is not empty space; it is stair, or channel, or drying-court, or the shallow trenches of standing water they use as levels. The plateau is less like a settlement than like a single instrument, fully strung, every part of it touched by someone each day.

The edges tell you the age of everything. Nothing is rounded. Arrises are crisp enough to sting a fingertip; the stair treads still show their tool-furrows under the thin white skin that is beginning to grow over them. This is young country. It has not had time to be worn down — and, as you will understand slowly, it is not being worn down at all.

**The water**

The sky almost never rains here. The sky gives dew: each night a glowing fog condenses out of the cold upper air and sits on the terraces like lit milk, and by mid-morning it has lifted and everything drips. But the dew is only the mountain's breath. Its drinking is done underground.

The massif is saturated. It lifted itself out of deep water and never emptied; the water table stands close beneath every floor, and the whole mountain sweats. Water does not fall here — it *arrives*, beading along seep-lines, welling from weep-holes drilled at intervals, sliding down channel-walls in sheets thin as breath. The builders long ago accepted this as the local law of nature: water comes out of things, and your work is to catch it.

So the terraces are really a circulatory system. Every drip has a channel. Every channel has a weir-stone set to hold it back. Every channel ends in a cistern — small, lidded, cool-mouthed, its rim carved with the tally of what it gathered this season and the name of its keeper. Some cisterns hold a bucketful; the Night Cisterns near the rim hold enough to glitter at dusk like sunk sky. The water itself is heavy with mineral, cool as a cheek, and slightly sweet where it has passed through the wet stairs.

And here is the quiet cleverness of the place: they build *with* the water, not only around it. Their most precise instruments are shallow trenches filled to a standing level — a spirit level made of the same substance the mountain is made of. When a new gallery must be true, they cut the trench first, let the water find itself, and build to the surface of it.

**The making**

The tools are simple and beautiful: chisels of soft native copper that comes up in the mountain's own veins, mauls of river-smooth stone, cords, wooden frames, plumb-bobs. Nothing cuts fast. Everything cuts patiently, and the patience is the point, because the mountain is still rising.

A hand's breadth each year — that is the measure they set the survey stones by. Every spring the tenders walk the terraces resetting the lines, because last year's true level is this year's slope. The Long Stair is not a finished thing; it is a *practice*, extended terrace by terrace as the massif lifts, begun by people now entombed in sinter and continued by their grandchildren's grandchildren. Work here is measured in lifetimes the way work elsewhere is measured in days.

And the mountain answers them in kind. The mineral-heavy water deposits a white skin — sinter — over everything it touches, a finger's width per human generation. Walls fatten. Floors rise in gentle glide. Doorways that were proud recessions grow shallower year by year, as if the stone were slowly closing its eyes. Every surface you walk on is younger than the thing beneath it.

Which is why the names matter so much. Every wall is inscribed, and the sinter is always coming for the inscriptions. So each generation, the tenders re-cut the names of their dead — going along the walls with fine chisels, tracing letters a hand's width deeper into the stone before the white skin seals them over. Some walls carry names cut twenty, thirty times, the same letters descending through strata of the mountain's own making, like footprints going down through snow.

**The bloom**

Nothing grows here in any way you would call a plant. But the wet stone is not bare. It carries a film — the tenders call it the bloom — a dark, faintly iridescent skin that lives on seep-lines and stair-shadows, drinking light and mineral in equal measure. It is not fungus, not moss, not lichen; it is simpler than all of those and stranger, and it is the only harvest the mountain gives.

The tenders garden it. They encourage it along the wet stairs with little weirs and shade-cuts, and scrape it in slow ribbons, pressing and drying it into dense dark cakes — the only food the place produces, eaten with mineral salt crusted from the evaporation-pans, and deep water drunk warm from jars set against the living stone. The bloom, passing through the water, eats the iron taste out of it; a cistern downstream of a bloom-stair fills sweet and clear, and the keepers can read the history of a terrace in the rings of the film the way readers elsewhere read rings in trees — fat dark years of wet, thin pale years of dew.

Where the bloom does not grow, the mountain shows its metals: weeps of copper green down grey faces, threads of red ochre, and once in a great while a pale vein of the tool-metal itself, which is treated less as treasure than as lumber — marked, claimed by a future project, and left for whoever cuts it, in whatever century.

There are no animals. Nothing flies, nothing flees, nothing hunts or is hunted. The only motion that is not water, hands, or the slow lift of the land is the cisterns' slow chemistry — drifting pale motes in the deepest pools, which are not creatures but the water remembering its deep origin, and which the tenders watch without naming.

**The tenders**

The place is fully occupied, and you will meet almost no one. This is not a contradiction; it is the local style. The fabric is dense with *evidence* of people — swept stairs, filled tallies, trimmed weirs, a lamp-niche freshly scraped — while the people themselves are sparse and slow and mostly indoors against the warm walls. The mountain is faintly alive below, and the stone a hand-span in from the surface stays a few degrees kinder than the air; the tenders know every warm wall on the mountain the way other people know the sunny side of a street.

They speak little and unhurriedly, in a language that seems to have been trimmed for silence — most of its volume spent on names, water-tallies, and the vocabulary of cuts. A child's first task is to carve one letter of their own name. An elder's last task, almost always, is to re-cut the names of the dead. Between those two acts lies the whole career the place asks for: keeping water, keeping level, keeping letters ahead of the sinter.

**The sound**

The mountain keeps time in water. That is most of what you hear. Not patter — drips. Counted, irregular, patient: *tick* … *tick-tick* … a long nothing … *tick*. Each cistern has its own rhythm and its own resonance, and the tenders can tell them apart with their eyes closed; there are listening-stones set at certain junctions where you press your ear to the rock and hear, far down, the long swallow of the mountain drinking itself.

Over this, occasionally, a chisel: one clean tap, a silence, the tap again — someone, somewhere, extending a line that will take their lifetime and three more. And under everything, so low it may be your own blood, the seep-hush of the whole massif exhaling.

Wind there is, but the built walls baffle it into a rumor. No leaves to shake, no lungs but yours. On a still afternoon the loudest thing on the mountain is the light.

**Evening, at the Rim**

At dusk the condensation fog rises again from the warm stone into the cooling air and hangs over the terraces in slow luminous shelves, and every drip-lit channel catches the last of the sun so the white lines burn briefly copper. The tenders draw their lids over the cisterns, tally the day, and go in against the warm walls.

Then the sound changes — not louder, just different: the drips continue in the dark at their own unhurried pace, the mountain counting through the night. Below, on the pale plain, the crust gleams under a sky dense with cold stars; the whole bright apparatus of the terraces goes quiet except for its one long sentence, which it has been carving for centuries and will be carving still:

*the land rises, and we rise with it, one hand's breadth at a time.*
