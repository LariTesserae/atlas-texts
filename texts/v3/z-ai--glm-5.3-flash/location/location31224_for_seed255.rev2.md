## The Work

Call it the Work, because that is what everyone calls it, and no other name has ever been needed. It is a country with a lid. A plain as wide as a valley — you could cross it in a long day's walk, if there were such a thing as a day — and every part of it made. Every floor was laid by someone's hands: packed clay, kiln brick, slabs of cast stone, mortar that still smells faintly of burnt lime. Every wall was raised, every gallery stacked above another gallery, every stair counted and re-counted. And over everything, the Lid: a ceiling of slabs and daub running farther than any lamp has ever reached, low in places enough that a person on a scaffold can press a palm flat against it, high elsewhere enough to swallow the kiln smoke and keep it.

Under the Lid there is no sky. There is only, in every direction, more made thing. The Work does not sit on the world. As far as anyone has ever established, the Work *is* the world.

It is cool here. Not cold — the clay holds a chill like a held breath, and in the deep halls your breath shows faintly, a ghost you make and unmake with each step — but cool, and damp at the edges of things. Warmth exists in only two grades: the temperature of hands, and the temperature of kilns. Whole districts migrate seasonally toward the kiln-quarters the way animals might migrate toward a sun, except that here the sun is a craft, stoked in shifts.

Light is a ration. Lamps are small, wicks spun from grey felt, and there is a discipline to them: you light what you are working on, not what you are walking past. So the vast interior of the Work lives in a permanent brown dusk, and the eye learns to do what the ear does — to reach out, to test, to map. At the raw edge of the construction the kilns burn openly, and their glow is the nearest thing this world has to weather in the sky: a low orange horizon that swells and fades with the firing schedule. People say you can tell the state of the Work by the color of the dark between the kilns.

For most of its history the Work was dry. That is the thing the old cords record, and the young ones find hard to believe. The Lid wept nothing. The air was dust and lime. Then — no one can say exactly when, it was before the current water-wards were apprentices — the seams began to weep.

It does not rain here. It *weeps*. Moisture beads on the underside of the Lid in the cool hours, gathers, and lets go — a soft, intermittent percussion, drop by drop, sometimes for an hour, sometimes for a night, sometimes not at all for weeks. Along certain seams it comes as a thin steady line, seam-rain, falling in one long thread like a rope of cold light. The makers had cut grooves in the floors long ago — they say the grooves were for carrying whispers, though no living crew uses them for that — and now the water has claimed the grooves, running along them in visible silver threads toward the cisterns. The cisterns are called mouths. Each mouth has a tally-cord beside it, and a knot is tied for every measured depth of drip. Water is counted the way other worlds count gold, or breath.

And where the weep falls on floors rather than grooves, something is happening that has no ordinance, no precedent, no word in the old verses: the water is cutting.

Gullies. Thin at first, no deeper than a finger joint, running downhill across brick that was laid flat and true and meant to stay that way. The water-wards go out to them with lamps and kneel and read them, because everyone has slowly agreed that this is reading. The water writes on the floors. A gully that forks cleanly is recorded as favorable. A gully that heads toward planned foundations is reported, and there is argument — because by custom, a gutter older than one season gains *gutter-right* and may not be paved over, so the water has, in effect, begun to veto construction. Crews reroute around the writing. Brick trains — long sled-trains hauled by rope and muscle — detour in the dark around channels of damp that did not exist last year, that did not exist *ever*, that are the newest things in the world.

The Work is loud, and always has been, but its loudness has changed key. There was always the hammering: the raw edge rings with it, chisel and maul and the tap-tap-tap of alignment checks, and the knuckle-code moving between districts, brisk patterns knocked on wall-stones so that a crew at the raw edge can ask a crew at the mouths whether the drip has slackened, and get an answer before the echo of the question has died. There were always the kilns, roaring like held breath. What is new is the rain-noise: the water-wards have set out listening-boards — flat stretched sheets of hide and clay — under the known weeps, and they measure the seams by their drumming, drops counted per breath. The whole place is turning, slowly, into an instrument being played by weather.

The Listenmen press their ears to the walls the way others press them to bellies. It is their job to know the sound of the Work as a single body, and to notice what is missing.

Because something in the Work is quiet.

They call it the Hush. It has never been seen clearly and no one wants to see it clearly. It is known the way a hole is known: when it moves through a district, the noise does not stop — it *steps aside*. Hammering continues at the raw edge, but a lane of hush moves through the middle distance, and people in its path stand still and listen to themselves breathing, and afterward there is usually a milk-cricket missing from a tally, sometimes a lampmoths' roost emptied, once — the cords keep it — an apprentice who was found at the edge of the hush-lane, sitting, unharmed, unable to say what he had heard. Which was, he said, nothing. For a long time. Everything alive in the Work came through the seams with the Wet, and the Hush came through too.

The life is sparse and has to be looked for, and that is part of how it is loved. Milk-crickets: pale, blind, soft-bodied, singing in the damp cells, counted individually by the cricket-tenders, who know the good singers by name. Lampmoths, grey and small, that kiss the lamps and leave smuts on the glass. In the mouths, white worms no longer than a fingernail, threading the sediment — the only creatures that eat the writing, which is to say, the only ones eating what the water has worn away. And in the wettest cells, far from the kilns, the palecap farms: terraces of standing damp where a fungus is tended blind, by touch and smell, harvested by workers who read the caps with their fingertips like the Listenmen read walls. From the oldest damp walls grows the grey felt — a crust, barely alive, more weather than creature — and it is stripped and spun for wicks. It is worth saying plainly: the only green the Work has ever had is grey. There are no plants here. There is mineral, and fire, and craft, and this thin felt of damp.

The strangeness lives mostly in the Ordinances. The verses of instruction are inherited verbatim, knot for knot, generation to generation, and they are long, and exact, and occasionally they say things like: *Break the wall where the floor is colder than your hands. Behind it you will find a stair. Count the risers aloud.* And the crew breaks the wall, and the stair is there, dry and finished and lamp-ready, and the risers number exactly what the verse says. These are called built-ahead chambers, and there are many, and their existence is simply recorded and never, in any cord anyone admits to reading, explained. No one says the word *before*. Sometimes, at shift change, a knock-code returns from a far district with one extra beat — a rhythm belonging to no registered crew — and the crews mark it in their tallies with a knot of different fiber and go on working. The answer-knock is not discussed. The answer-knock is counted.

At the raw edge, the Work is still becoming itself. Sleds hiss past with pans of clay slurry; the brick trains grind; kilns stand like low suns in a row, and apprentices sweat in the cool air and learn the exact weight of a hammer head by hand. The scaffolds here are laced together so densely that the edge districts seem woven rather than built — walls sharing walls, galleries stacked on galleries, stores hanging from beams overhead, rope everywhere like veins. The scale is the strange part: you can stand at the edge in the lamplight and see farther than sight works, a whole dark country of made floors going out under the Lid, crowded to its last inch and utterly quiet at any distance, except for the code-knocks ticking from wall to wall like rain.

Here is what the water-wards have begun to say quietly, among themselves, in the last few seasons. The Work was made to be finished. Every verse points toward a finished thing. But the Wet has introduced a second idea, which no ordinance contains: that things can *wear*. In the oldest quarter, at the bottom of the bowl where the mouths stand fullest, the gullies converge, and at the convergence a dark paste has begun to gather — ground brick, and cricket-shell, and grey felt, and lime-fine silt, carried by the threads of weep and left in still water as the first sediment the Work has ever produced. The old ones have a word for it. They say it carefully, the way you name a thing that might hear you.

They call it earth. It is the first earth, and it is being made by accident, and the crews have been forbidden — informally, by the unspoken law that governs everything new — from paving it.
