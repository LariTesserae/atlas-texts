# The Driplands

You come up out of the funicular and the first thing you understand is that nothing here ever finished happening, and nothing here will ever change again.

The plateau stretches to every horizon at once — a high cold shelf of country, so vast it makes its own weather, though "weather" is the wrong word, because the weather does not move. It hangs. Cloud has condensed out of the air and stays condensed, a permanent grey low ceiling that sweats rather than rains: fat slow drops gathering on nothing in particular and falling from an altitude that gives each one time to think on the way down. The rocks weep. The whole escarpment weeps — a young landscape, barely emerged, its cliffs still sharp-edged and unsoftened by age, and every new-cut face of it beading with water like something freshly born and still damp from the making. Water stands everywhere the ground allows: sheet-shallow marsh lakes, black and perfectly still, their edges lush with cold-happy sedge and moss and low seeding things that grow green in the near-dark and do not grow further.

There are no animals. The silence would be total except —

It is loud. That's the paradox of the place. The city is loud.

The city: an industrial town grown to continental ambition, laced across the marsh-edge plateau in tiered grids and viaducted causeways, its works built right down into the young rock. Engines. The engines never stop and they never started, in a sense — the place is in a kind of frozen thermodynamic noon, an equilibrium that engineers elsewhere would call impossible. The great condenser-towers pull water out of the sky and the sky replenishes instantly, at the same rate, forever. The dynamos turn and the marsh stays still. Steam rises from the stacks to a fixed height and stops, sheared off flat, as if the clouds above were a lid.

Every sound the machines make has a twin and no echo, because the sound hits the cloud-lid and the still water and comes back exactly, so the whole plateau rings like the inside of a bell. Hammer-sounds, flywheel-thrum, the periodic great *whump* of the pressure-relief valves — all of it returning to you a half-second late, perfectly preserved, forever.

The workers are long gone, or were never there in the way you'd mean it. The foundries keep running. Lights burn at a steady ordinary daylight-brightness behind frosted industrial glass, neither brightening nor dimming. Moss creeps halfway up a stair-rail and stops at an exact line. A bucket, half-full of rainwater, sits where it was set down; the water in it never evaporates, never overflows.

You can walk for a day between structures — the density of the place is so low, the spacing so wasteful and grand — and the whole time the engines are in your ears, near and far at once, and the drops keep falling on the still black lakes, each impact a small glass note the world then repeats back to you.

And you understand, gradually, that you are the only gradient left. You breathe, and the condensation beads on your coat. You are warm. Somewhere behind you your footprints fill with water at exactly the rate the water level dictates, and then they are not footprints anymore, and the plateau goes on ringing, finished and unfinished, until you leave or become part of the equilibrium too.
