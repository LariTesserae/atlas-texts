# The Youngest Pour

You come up through the pillar, up a stair that turns inside stone so new it still has a temperature of opinion — blood-warm, faintly yielding, like the flank of a sleeping animal. The stair is the only old thing here, and it is two months old.

Above you, the sound has been rising for a long time. Not one sound. A weather of sound: dripping in ten thousand unsynchronized tempos, a humming from something hanging in the air that you cannot yet see, bird-voices announcing things in official tones, and underneath it all a pulse too deep to hear properly — a pulse you feel in the fillings of your teeth, steady as a heart that belongs to the ground.

Then the stair ends and you step out onto the newest land there is.

It is small. That is the first surprise. After the pillar, after the climb, you expect distance, and instead you get a table: a rough oval of stone perhaps six hundred paces across, every edge of it visible from anywhere on it, so low-walled that from the center you can see the whole world at once — huts, stakes, pools, the rim, the haze. You could shout to any point on it and be heard. Everything here is heard. That is the second surprise: nothing in this place is quiet, because the place is small and hard and fresh, and every sound it makes comes back.

The light is dim, and warm, and seems to come from nowhere in particular. The sun is real — you can find it, a smeared coin low in the haze — but the haze is thick, and the haze is the work. The plateau's machinery breathes out warm vapor in slow tons, and the vapor has nowhere to go, so it hangs, and the light comes through it the way lamplight comes through cloth. Everything is the color of tea. The air is a bath. The stone under your palm is damp, and the damp is not rain and not dew but condensation: the whole sky is a cold glass over a warm drink, and the land sweats.

The stone itself has no history. That is what makes it strange to touch. Old rock carries its biography in it — fold, fault, fossil, scar — and your hand reads it without your knowing. This rock is a blank page. It is uniform, faintly green-grey, wet, warm, and it gives, very slightly, under pressure, the way good mortar gives. There are seams in it — long hairlines where the pours met — and along the seams, and also in grids across the open stone, faint pale lines glow underfoot: survey marks, drawn *in* the material, not on it. Sometimes one pulses, and the ground near it stiffens by a degree you can feel through your boots. The land is not finished. The land is being *argued into* its final shape by something in the middle of the matter — printing that happens below hearing, below sight, below the scale where eyes work. You never see a machine. You see consequences.

Every six drizzles or so, the whole plateau sets. A shiver runs through it rim to rim, all the seeps restart in unison, every hanging thing swings, and — this is the part you will not be believed about — for one breath you hear the last hour played back quietly: the birds, the drips, a scream, a door. The stone has not yet learned to forget, so it keeps what falls on it. Old landscapes remember by eroding. This one remembers by recording, for now, until it has enough past to be selective.

The water is the youngest water on earth. It has no routes yet, only intentions. It emerges everywhere — the stone weeps from a thousand pores, warm and clean-tasting — and it wanders. You can stand at a fork in a streamlet no wider than your hand and watch the water *hesitate*, split, rejoin, try a direction for a body-length, give up, try another. In two places it still flows gently uphill for a few paces, following a grade the lattice is holding from a plan, before surrendering to the obvious. The First Gully is nine days old and two fingers deep, and it is already a landmark: the stitchbirds hold their announcements from its lip, as if it were a monument, which it is. It is the first thing on this plateau that the rain has ever taken away. Everyone treats it with the respect due a first sentence.

The built remains are scattered and half-swallowed. Four seam-huts stand along the northern arc — three maintained, one collapsed, not ruined but *set*: the stone flexed during a settling and took the hut into itself the way amber takes an ant. Its door is now a fossil of a door. You can still read the stencil: RAISE 12-C. Around the huts, stakes rise from the rock in long ruled lines — lashing points for a loom — and between the stake-tops, once a cycle, the drizzle begins, summoned along threads too fine to see, on schedule, warm as bathwater, fifteen minutes long. The work continues with no worker in sight. There is a slate inside the standing hut with entries in a human hand, days apart, and the last entry is not about the stone at all. It says: *stowaway weed, south pool — unscheduled — ?*

The weed is real. One plant, knee-high, growing where nothing was planted, the first unsanctioned life on the plateau. The birds have already adopted it.

Because the fauna did not wait for the flora. That is the ecological signature of this place: the animals came first, and they are *many*. Along two hundred meters of stake-line hang the lickers — translucent, bladder-winged, five hundred strong — drinking the condensing air itself, swelling with each drizzle, each one humming one low held note, so the stake-lines sing a slow shifting chord that swells when the rain starts and dims as they sate and glow, faintly green, in the steam. The stitchbirds are everywhere else: small, loud, official, nesting in the hut eaves, announcing every weather change at full volume to the entire plateau, since the entire plateau is, conveniently, within earshot. The rasplers graze the open stone — broad flat animals like ambulatory molars, a dozen of them, slowly rasping the raw surface, texture-work that the plan seems to have assigned them or that they simply invented. What comes out the back of a raspler is the first true sand on earth, and in the wake of the herd, islands of thread-grass take root — pale, quick grass that glues itself down with mineral spit, grows in hours, and is grazed back in days. The plants here are sparse not because life is reluctant but because *soil* is. The land's first soil is being manufactured by its first herbivores, one dung-pat at a time. Under the weeping walls, seam-moss felt creeps in thin green-black stripes. And in the south, a guarded circle of stakes holds four saplings — true trees, ancient genome, days old, knee-high — the future forest, roped off like royalty.

There is predation, and it keeps the schedule. Needlers — javelin-shaped fliers, hunting in pairs — strike at the onset of each drizzle, precisely when the stitchbirds' announcement chorus peaks and the whole plateau is loudest. The kills make little silences in the noise, holes in the sound, and the other birds pour into the holes immediately, filling them with more announcing, so that death here reads, acoustically, as a brief punctuation mark in a very long official statement.

Walk the rim, near the end, because the rim is where the rules fray. At the edge, the stone's surface resolves into mesh — the land becoming lattice, the lattice becoming haze — and the horizon is not a distance but a seam: a straight hairline where the haze meets a wall of working fog, with a feeling, beyond it, of other work, far off, at other scales. Here gravity goes a hair soft; a step lifts a little too easily, the way it does in the last moment of sleep. Your compass is a rumor. Echoes here come back *early* — the rock hears your shout through its own young body faster than the air can carry it, and returns it before the air-version arrives, so you hear your voice answer, then speak. The land has not yet learned the order of causes. It is warm, and dim, and loud, and small, and dripping, and alive past every reasonable order of construction, and nowhere in it is a single thing that is finished.
