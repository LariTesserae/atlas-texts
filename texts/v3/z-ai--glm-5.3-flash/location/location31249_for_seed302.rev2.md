First the sound.

You hear it long before you see anything — from the low haze country below the rim, from the last gray slopes where the fog thickens and the air goes cool and tastes of chalk. It is not wind, though wind is here. It is not surf, though it has the sea's mass. It is a roar with no events in it: no cresting, no breaking, no lull, no gust. Ten million small waters falling at once, each too quiet to hear alone, none of them ever stopping, and the ear — unable to find a single event to hold onto — gives up and reports one enormous sound. A held chord. The mountain, exhaling.

Then the light, and the rim.

The plateau's edge is a cliff of white stone two hundred meters tall, and it does not pour water in columns. It *weeps*. The whole face seeps — every square meter of it — so that the rock wears a film of moving water thin as breath, and the low sun comes through it sideways, and the cliff becomes a wall of lit glass with stone behind it. Rivers of light run down it without bending it. Below, the haze country fades to nothing. Above, the glare begins.

The stone itself is the first strange thing, and it takes a while to name, because it is not rock that water carved. It is rock that water *made*. The entire massif is a deposit — sinter, scale, precipitate — laid down mineral grain by mineral grain by the water rising through it. Somewhere far beneath, the deep ground gives up its dissolved stone, and the water carries it up through hundreds of meters of porous dark rock, and arrives everywhere at once, at exactly the height of the land's own surface. The water table and the skyline are the same line. That is the master fact of the place, the fact everything else follows from: *the country is full.* It cannot be wetter. It cannot be drier. Any hollow you press into the ground fills before you finish pressing. Any footprint is a pool by the time you look back at it.

So the land has grown terraces, the way a wound too wet to close grows scar. Stairs of white and rose and bone-colored stone, steps wide as fields and steps wide as hands, tier below tier for as far as the haze lets you see — two days of walking just to cross the stairs, and they are only one province of the place. Every pool brims to its lip, exactly to its lip, and over each lip the overflow slides as a sheet a fingernail deep, laminar, glass-clear, silent in itself and yet — multiplied by every lip on every terrace across the whole plateau — the source of the roar. It is the sound of rain on a pond, scaled up until rain-on-a-pond becomes weather, becomes an ocean of it, becomes a fact of the atmosphere.

The rims of the pools have been rolled smooth, the way wax is rolled by a thumb. Every edge in this country is water-worn; there is not a sharp face anywhere; channels that ran bright a hundred thousand years ago and now run nothing are polished inside like throats. Erosion finished its work here a long time ago. The water and the stone came to terms — this shape, held — and the shape has been held ever since. The plateau looks neither new nor ruined. It looks *finished*, the way a river delta looks finished, or a full cup.

Because of the white stone and the thin high air, the light is ferocious. The sun is one light, but the country is full of others: every wet face gives back what it's given, every pool is a mirror laid at its own private angle, and the shallow pools are worse — brighter — because light goes through them, hits the pale floor, and comes back up, so that the glare arrives from below as much as above. You walk on a lit ceiling. Shadows are thin and blue and barely yours. Noon and late afternoon look dangerously alike; the country keeps its own illumination, indifferent to the day's turning, and at night the stars simply get doubled — a sky above and its spilled copy in ten thousand pools — while the roar continues exactly as it was.

It is cool here, and the coolness comes out of the ground. The deep water arrives cold, and the pools breathe it into the air, and the air holds it. Over the whole terrace country hangs a standing mist, knee-height to head-height, born of the pools and returning to the pools, evaporating upward at precisely the rate it condenses and drips back down. It does not drift; it *belongs* to the place, stationary as the stone, and it has hung here — this same mist, renewed — for as long as the country has existed. It softens every distance. It haloes the sun into a white stain. It beads on your sleeves and never dries, because nothing dries here. Ever.

Underfoot, the stone lies. Tap it and half the time it rings hollow — abandoned conduits, old spring-throats, the dry and wet plumbing of the mountain's whole history — and some of those buried channels still carry water, and you can hear them working beneath your boots. Put a palm flat on the rock and you feel it: a hum, a river's drum through the stone. The sound is not only surface. The mountain is an instrument, half-full. There is bass in the roar — deep buried rivers keeping their own note — and there are the breathing pools: certain dark basins gulp air on a fixed count, one every eleven seconds, another every nine, metronomes with no musician, and no one has ever established whether the exhalation is gas finding its way out of the stone or something in the stone exhaling. Both answers are equally unwelcome.

The sound has no edges. You cannot locate it, cannot walk toward it or away from it; it comes from everywhere, including from below, and after a day you stop hearing it as sound and start moving through it the way you move through air or water. It is deafening the way daylight is blinding — not an event but a medium. You give up speech on the first morning. There is no one to speak to anyway.

Nothing green has ever grown here, and nothing grows now, and the reason is simple and total: nothing here has time to. The water lays down mineral faster than any dust can settle, faster than any spore can root, faster than anything soft can establish itself. Surfaces on this plateau do not collect the world. They accrete. A seed that blows in from wherever seeds still are — and it is not clear anywhere still has them — is coated white within a season and begins its long career as a fossil. A feather, an insect shell, a dropped cup, a bone: the stone takes all of it, seams it, claims it. The plateau is a fossil-maker with a waiting time of months. Which is why nothing has ever been built here, and nothing can be. There is no timber, no clay that stays clay, no footing a wall can hold — the ground drinks every foundation, the water finds every joint. The only things standing on this country are the things the country stood up itself: the spring-mounds in the interior, dimpled cones that dribble eternally, their summit lips rolled like poured wax; the terraces; the rims; the stair-cascades at the rim's broken places where the whole overflow of a mile gathers and falls.

The only soft life is a skin too simple to be called vegetation. On every wet surface lies a felt — bacterial, mat-thin, laid down by the water the way the stone is — banded in rust and cream and olive-black where different minerals brew through. It does not look alive. It looks like staining. And it is the entire economy of the place, which is why the fauna is what it is: rare, and strange, and always near the margin of becoming stone.

Under the glassy sheets, on the terrace floors, you will see — if the glare cooperates, which it mostly doesn't — small flat shadows moving against the light. Coins, leaves, hands. They graze the felt, rasping it where it thins, and they are translucent, and you know them mostly by their shadows and by the wake-lines that close behind them like water closing over a skipped stone. They keep moving. They have to. Anything that stands still on this plateau for more than a day is staked to it — the mineral claims whatever halts — so the grazers walk their slow loops like ghosts under glass, forever circling, forever just ahead of being built into the floor they feed on. Deeper, in the bottle-dark pools where the mineral bands ring the rim in rust and cream, rarer shapes hang at the cold layer — pale, large-ish, unhurried, mouths like folded gloves — seen perhaps once in a season, and then only as a dimming of a dimming. And at the rim cascades, inside the bright falling sheet itself, live the fall-dwellers: dark wet shapes holding station in water that is busy leaving the mountain, taking what the water carries as it goes. A shadow crossing a shadow. You could cross the whole plateau twice and see one.

Nothing changes here, and the roar is how you know. There are no seasons — cool is the whole of its climate. No flood, no drought: the level is the level, and rain that falls is instantly indistinguishable from the water already standing in every pool, so the sky's arrival makes no mark. Where one terrace silts, a vent somewhere else opens; the ledger balances; the surface holds. Time passes here only as millimeters of stone, as the grazer-loops ringed slowly outward over ten thousand years, as the hair's-breadth deepening of one black pool. Stand on the rim for a lifetime and you will watch nothing end and nothing begin. The sound teaches this better than anything: it is not the noise of things happening. It is the sound of fullness — of a thing that happened completely, long ago, and is still, and will be.

Crossing takes days. There are no paths; nothing walks here often enough to make one, and the stone would seal it. You route-hop along terrace tops, thread between brimming pools, give the breathing basins their room. You drink, reluctantly — the water tastes of chalk and metal, and it is only barely water — and you sleep on a humming rock in a light that never fully goes, wrapped in a roar that your bones will keep for weeks afterward. Descending from the rim into the haze country, the sound fades — but not into silence. Into a smaller version of itself, and then into your own ears, which have been recalibrated, and which will report every quiet room you ever enter afterward as a held breath, an absence with a shape, a rim of white stone just out of sight.

It is the loudest still place there is. The water never rests, and nothing ever happens.
