You meet it as a shape inside a light.

The lowlands are gone — not dark, just dissolved. Two days' walk of salt pan and dried gravel lie somewhere beneath an amber blindness that begins at the knees and never quite ends, and you take them on faith, because the haze has eaten the horizon whole. The air down here smells of old sea and no sea: salt, hot mineral, a faint sweetness of something fermenting far away in the glare. Once this basin held water. Now all that remains of it is in the air, making its rounds — a ghost-of-water economy, a sea that lifted itself into the sky and never agreed to come all the way down.

And out of that stands the mountain. Or what the mountain has become. It is no longer a mountain; it is a stump — the last root of a range that wore itself away before there was anyone to remember the range. Nothing sharp survives on it. No ridge, no spire, no fresh scar. Every edge has been rounded off by wind and by time beyond counting, until the whole landform reads less like rock than like the shoulders and flanks of enormous sleeping animals, grained like sugar-weathered bone, colored rust and ash and dry honey. It has arrived at its final shape. Rock falls from it the way dust falls from a finished carving: technically possible, essentially done. This is the oldest stable thing for a thousand miles in any direction, and it behaves like it.

The climb takes half a day, and for most of it the mountain gives you nothing. Bare flanks. Warm stone under your palms, warm even through the soles of your boots, because this world's air is deep and heavy and heat rises and pools around high places the way water once pooled in basins — the peaks are warm islands in a warm sea of air, which is why a place this high can be this mild. Noon here wears the amber of some other day's late afternoon. Shadows are rumors. You pass runnels cut into the rock — beautiful drainage, engineered by nothing, and bone-dry, and dry for so long that moss has colonized the cracks of channels that never, in any living memory, ran. Higher up, the wind finds holes worn into the stone and the mountain begins, quietly, to play itself: long low notes from the blowholes, sustained, changing only when the wind changes, a sound so constant you will stop hearing it in an hour and only miss it later.

There are beetles. There is a crust-lichen the color of old brick, patient as geology. There is a bird that nests inside the flutes and rides the note out at dawn. Below a certain line, that is the entire census of the mountain.

Then the line.

You would call it a treeline, except it comes at one contour, clean as a drawn border — you could walk it in an hour, a slow ring around the crown, and on one side of your footprints there would be bone-colored stone, and on the other side, everything.

This is the beard: a green cap maybe three hundred paces across at its widest, the only lushness in the visible world, and it lives entirely on fog. Every warm day the haze-sea below breathes upward, carrying not just vapor but dust — mineral lifted off the drylands, salt and silica and the fine powder of everything the basins used to be. At night the stone at the crown cools by just enough, and the fog condenses into it: drip, and drip, and a slow beard-soak. The dust comes down with the drip. Dust and drip are the mountain's entire agriculture. The desert below is, quite literally, this garden's soil.

The trees here have learned the rules. Fog-woods with trunks swollen like stored water — because they are; the wood is soft and pale and half of it is reserve. Leaves wide, thin, held nearly flat, built to comb a faint light and a passing cloud at the same time. Moss hangs in ropes thick as a forearm. Ferns stand in every vertical crack. Orchids — or something doing orchids' work — flare white in the half-dark under the canopy, and tank-plants cup themselves at every joint, and each cup holds, at dawn, a few mouthfuls of clear water: this is the only standing water in the world, and it is guarded. Thumbnail frogs live their whole lives in the tanks of single plants, tadpole to grandfather without ever leaving the cup they were laid in. Beetles climb the stems before dawn and stand on their heads and let the fog bead on their backs and run down into their own mouths.

And into the beard, at exactly the line, no higher than it can help, no lower than it dares, is the town.

At a distance it looks like rubble — which is the first wrong impression the mountain gives you, and it will spend days correcting it. Closer, the rubble walks, and laughs, and is calling to itself in a language of pure voice. Three hundred souls, maybe, stacked in dry stone along two hundred paces of summit shelf and spilling down the green flank in terraces. Stair-streets, because nothing on a crown this small can be flat. Houses of the mountain's own worn blocks, fitted without mortar, rounded like river stones though no river ever touched them. Roofs of flat slabs, and gardens on every roof — the town grows upward into the fog the same way the trees do, so that from below, on the right night, town and beard are one continuous dark-green animal with fires in its hide.

Everything here is built around the drip. Nets of wool and hair and fog-grass are strung from ridge to ridge, scaffolded like vineyards, and at first light the combers go out into the murk — visibility is a dozen paces, so they work by call, a whole grammar of hoots and rising thirds — hauling the nets in heavy and wringing them, net by net, into stone mouths. Below every net-field run drip-channels cut into living rock, and below those channels are older channels, and below those, older still; the town digs a foundation and finds another town's floor, another town's cistern, another town's door, because this has been the place to be for as long as there has been a beard, and the beards, like the mountain, do not hurry. The cisterns are the treasury: sealed with clay and wool, lids tied down, wardens sworn, theft of a drip the only crime anyone here takes personally. The language has a word for *river* that survives only in an old song, sung as if it were the name of a god nobody worships anymore.

The town is primitive by its own cheerful accounting — hammerstone and fire, bone needles, wool, clay, and a little soft hammered copper, worked into bells and one crown's worth of ornaments that have passed through every family on the summit and will pass through all the rest. No wheel has ever been useful here. What they have instead is craft: looms clicking all day in doorways, hand-mills turning in the same rhythm century after century, and the fog-calls — some of which are not for the town at all. On the right nights, when the fog lies thick below like a second, luminous ground, the wardens climb to the bald crown above the beard and call outward, long notes shaped for distance, and wait. And somewhere across the amber void, faint as something remembered, another crown answers. There are other stumps out there, then. Other beards. The world is a drowned—no: a dried archipelago, and the calls go peak to peak at the pace of weather, a conversation that has been in no hurry for a very long time.

It is not a quiet place. Nothing about the census of this crown is quiet. The day runs on a shimmer — katydids in the millions, a stridulation so continuous it becomes a texture, an airflow, something you feel on the skin like warmth. Dove-throated boomers start before light and their calls fall down the flutes of the blowholes and come out of the mountain several notes lower, so that the dawn sounds like the mountain itself waking in two voices. Swifts pour out of the flues at midmorning to hawk the insects that ride the warm updrafts off the dead lands — the lowlands feed this crown through its birds as surely as through its dust, and the guano terraces below the nest-holes are mined like a second quarry. Goats — small, dry, quick-eyed — come up the bare flank to graze the beard's outer fringe at dawn and are gone before the fog lifts. Moths rise at dusk in such numbers that the air goes soft with wings; bats work the fog margin; and when true dark comes the fireflies take over the understory, thousands of slow green sparks drifting among the tank-plants, so that on fog-nights the beard glows from inside like a banked ember.

But the loudest thing on the mountain is the arrival of the fog itself. It comes the way weather comes in old worlds — slowly, with warnings, the boomers going silent one ridge at a time — and then the front pours over the crown and the whole summit *hisses*: condensation on ten thousand leaves at once, a rising white noise, and then the ticking, and then the dripping, every channel finding its voice, the town's cistern-mouths beginning their long slow swallowing. It is the sound of rain. It is not rain. It is the closest thing to rain that exists, and when it happens, work stops. People come out and stand in the stair-streets with their faces up. Children catch drips on their tongues. It is, everyone agrees, the most beautiful sound there is — and once, a few times in a life, actual water falls from above, a true rain, a whole afternoon of it, and the tanks overflow and the runnels — the ancient ones, the ones that never run — run for a day. Those days are named. People tell you where they were, forty years on, the way people elsewhere tell you where they were at a birth.

Nights are warm and thick. The haze below holds the day's heat like a lamp's oil, and even the stars are compromised by it — the sky is a deep dim glow, the moon a blurred coin, no sharp points anywhere; the world keeps its light the way it keeps its water, close and diffuse and old. Fires are small, out of respect for dryness. The stone under everyone stays warm. The frogs sing their tiny territorial claims from inside their individual cups, a sound like fingers on wet glass, and the flutes play whatever the night wind gives them, and somewhere far off, if it is that kind of night, another mountain is calling into the dark and being answered.

The rules here are few and absolute, and everyone learns them before they learn letters, if they ever learn letters at all. Nothing flows; what falls, falls as fog, and what moves, moves by wing or by foot. The mountain is finished and does not break — all stone is inherited; the only new building material the world provides is dust, arriving at the rate of a season per century. Height is law: a hand's width of altitude decides between garden and bone, and the town lives exactly on the line, and no one has ever gotten an argument out of that line yet. And the dead are laid into the rock in dry stone, and a tank-plant is set above the grave, so that each generation adds its few cups of standing water to the hill — the closest thing this world has to a river, one plant at a time, watering no one, holding everything.

Stand on the Shelf at dusk, at the town's one open plaza, when the calls are going out to the sister stumps and the first fireflies are finding their places and the flutes have warmed to their evening register. Below you, a mile of luminous nothing. Above you, a hundred paces of bald, finished stone and then a sky like the inside of a shell. And around you, pressed into the last green band the world was willing to pay for, three hundred lives, stacked and netted and mill-clacking and loud — a whole dense civilization balanced on the crown of the oldest patient thing in sight, which stands there in the warm dark, wearing its green beard, drinking the sky.
