**The Foundling Sea**

The sound arrives first. It always does, here — because sound arrives early in this place, ahead of its causes, the way letters arrive before the letter is written. You feel the roar in your sternum before you see anything at all: a pressure that has texture, that ripples the water in fine concentric stitches wherever it stands upright, which is more often than it should.

Then the light, late and dim, sifting down through a low ceiling of vapor — bronze-green, directionless, the glow of a foundry seen through smoked glass. The vapor lid hangs perhaps a hundred spans up, fed by a thousand funnels and vents and hairline cracks in the world, and somewhere above it, there is presumably a sun. It is a rumor here. Light comes sifted, secondhand, arriving — you will eventually notice — after the objects it belongs to. Shadows lag. You see a reed bow, and then the reed, and then, belatedly, its shadow hurrying to catch up.

The water is warm. Not seasonably warm — bathwater warm, blood-adjacent, the warmth of something still running underneath. It is the color of steeped tea over iron, thick with silt and spore and sugar, and it is not one water. It comes in layers that decline to mix: a warm skin, a warmer heart, cold veins that arrive without warning against your wrist like a stranger's hand. A lagoon that reads knee-deep from above will drop away at your fingertips into a darkness with a machinery-glow in it, far down, like windows. Depth here is an opinion the water holds loosely.

And beneath the water, still running, there are the Works.

Nobody is sure how long they have been drowned — the flood came recently, that much is agreed, the water keeps bad records — but nobody ever turned the engines off. Pump cathedrals stand on the bottom with their lit windows full of green murk, gauges still sweeping, needles still faithful to readings no one will ever take. Turbine halls exhale steady streams of fat vapor that climb through the sea, break the surface in rolling columns, and build the ceiling of the sky. Cables run everywhere underfoot along the bottom like the tendons of something asleep. Warmth is their exhaust. The sea is warm because the machines do not know they are drowned, and because they do not know, they have never stopped, and because they have never stopped, the world here has never finished being born.

Which is the other thing you learn quickly: nothing in this place is old, and nothing is smooth.

New land rises constantly — like bread, like bruising — islands surfacing with edges still knife-sharp, still steaming, silt and ash and matter you don't have a word for, pushed up by pressures the Works either cause or merely fail to prevent. The young islands are crisp as broken ceramic. There is no weathered stone anywhere in the Foundling Sea, no worn anything; paint on the drowned machines is still legible; the reeds come up in unnervingly straight rows, like hair on a newborn. By evening, an island born at dawn is already furred with green.

Sometimes the green is already old. There is an island three days old with a tree on it that has ring-counts you could check, if there were anyone left to check them, and the reflection in the still water beside it shows a grove that does not exist yet — reflections here show schedules, not presents. In low places the vapor comes down instead of up, and the rain falls upward in slow vertical threads, sea to sky, returning what was borrowed.

The vegetation does not so much cover this place as constitute it.

Ribbon-forests anchored to drowned cranes and valve-heaps, waving in the engine-currents like the hair of submerged giants. Reed ramparts ringing every new island, so thick and so packed you could step from one islet to the next almost all the way to the horizon — the world is building a floor here and has not noticed it is building a road. Floating mats of stitched vegetation thick enough to bear a person, blooming in spirals that trace the warm currents the machines make, the algae glowing faintly along the flow-lines like tracer in a vein. Moss furs every pipe, every ladder, every floating hull. A reed will gain a knuckle's height while you stand deciding whether to trust it. Growth here does the work that appetite does elsewhere — and this matters, because—

There are no animals.

No fish shadow under the boat. No insects stitching the warm air. No birds, no feathers anywhere, no burrows, no Eyes in the reed-walls reflecting your lamp. A lush, sweet, crowded, warm sea, and nothing in it that moves except plants, machines, and water. The absence has a weight. Your eye keeps going to the empty spaces where an animal should be, and the empty spaces keep being full of something else: more green, more vapor, more noise.

Because the ecology didn't collapse when the animals left — or were never admitted — it reorganized. The roar carries the pollen. The great pressure-waves rolling off the Works and off the new-risen land pump through the ribbon-forests and the floating mats, and the blossoms open to it the way flowers elsewhere open to bees. Sound is the courier here, the mover, the matchmaker. And nothing eats. Nothing is eaten. Nothing, strictly speaking, rots. Things get overgrown — swallowed whole by green, adopted, incorporated — which is this world's version of burial, and it is not a bad one.

The deafness has structure. Stand still and take the roar apart: the bass heartbeat of the engines, endless and tidal; the surf-hiss of vapor vents; the crack and groan of new land being born out on the rim, arriving at your ear before the plume of its rising comes into view. Between these, the strangenesses: echoes that arrive from no wall, standing waves you can lean on for half a second before they step aside, and — rare, warm, a few paces wide — quiet cells, bubbles of almost-silence drifting like oil on the noise. The world listens inside them. You feel addressed. The roar occasionally organizes itself into something that is not words but is unmistakably grammar, and you understand, without being able to say why, that it is not speaking to you. Yet.

The built things are few, and scattered, and mostly wrong. A stilt-shack standing on a three-day-old island, built in a style older than its own ground — it was somewhere else, apparently, and arrived here already aged. A survey mast whose anemometer cups still turn, measuring a wind that does not exist. A rowboat, oarless, crossing a lagoon at a patient walking pace with nobody in it. A ladder rising out of open water to a door at head-height; the door opens on sea. One sign, legible still, unaccountably: KEEP — and then either rust or unmaking. And in one drowned window of the Works, a light that no machine needs, that flickers in a rhythm too patient to be panic and too irregular to be a mechanism, which may mean someone is home, and may mean the building dreams.

You could cross this whole sea in two days, if there were anywhere to walk — and nearly there is; the region is a valley's width of crowded water, and on the rare moments when the vapor thins you can see all of it at once, end to end: the drowned Works glowing in the middle distance, the reed-ramparts and mat-meadows packed between, and out at the rim, right now, as you watch, another island shouldering up through the surface, sharp and steaming, and the green already moving toward it like a sentence eager to be finished.

The Foundling Sea. Warm as a body, dim as a held lamp, loud as a nursery with the cradle rocking. Everything in it is younger than the noise. Nothing in it eats or dies; things are only adopted. Sound comes early, light comes late, and the still water is a mirror with opinions.

It has been running its engines and rehearsing its pollen and building its floors for as long as it has existed — preparing, with the unconscious patience of a warm room, for whatever will be its first animal. When it comes, it will find the water ready, the ground three days old, and a world that has been calling into the dark this whole time, slightly ahead of itself, waiting for something to finally arrive late.
