On the few days each year when the low clouds thin enough to show the sky's foundations, people in the valleys stop what they're doing and point up. There — a hairline, plumb against the blue, faintly green at the top like a stem that has flowered. It doesn't look built, from down there. It looks drawn. It takes a while to understand what the line is standing *in*: no ridge, no shoulder of rock, no neighbor. Just weather below it and clean sky above, and the thing itself, upright in all that nothing, holding its little crown of leaves.

Whether there was ever a mountain under it is a question the keepers have stopped answering. The tower's foot is a masonry plinth wide as a threshing floor, and below the plinth the surveys simply end — cloud, then rumor. What matters is that everything you can stand on is made. Deck plates, trusses, ladders, gutters, duckboards, handrails, cistern walls: no natural substrate anywhere in the place, not a fistful of native soil. The gardens grow in engineered beds. The moss grows on painted steel. Even the tree — and there is a tree — grows out of masonry it has slowly swallowed.

That tree tells you the oldest thing about the place. It roots on the south flank and its roots have taken the central shaft in a grip a century long, fused with brick and rod until no surveyor can say where the made ends and the grown begins. The tower was built total, everything artificial, and then it was lived in so long that living became a kind of construction, and construction a kind of growth. The keepers argue about other things. About this they are silent.

The crown terrace is small — sixty paces across, hamlet-sized, holding perhaps twenty souls — but the tower is tall the way a bell is tall, and its volume is mostly air. Stand at the crown's rail and you can see every other person alive at once, each of them tiny, each separated from you by open trusswork and kilometers of clearance. Nothing touches nothing. The interior shaft — the keepers call it the Well — drops away through the middle of everything, and a stone dropped into it ticks off guide-rods for a while and then is eaten by the hum, and no one has ever heard one land.

The hum comes first, always. It is the tower's engine note, a low organ tone that makes your chest a sounding board, and it never stops, because the tower never stops working. The place is deafening in the way a bell is deafening when you live inside the bell: not violent, just total. Wind plays the open truss cells like a stopped organ, chords shifting with the wind's quarter; certain edges have been filed into whistles over the generations, tuned on purpose, because the keepers decided long ago that if they had to live inside a sound it would be one they'd chosen. On top of the hum the swift colony adds its glittering band — several thousand birds screaming in a high bright register that cuts through everything, audible the way light is visible. Human voices are useless here. Everyone signs. To speak, you put your hand on the same rail as the other person and knock, and the rail carries the words bone to bone. Children's first sentences are knocks. Some of the old tenders have conversations of tremendous subtlety through a water pipe.

The water, though. The water is made, never found. Nothing up here is wet by nature; the sky is too cold and thin to give anything away. So the tower sweats it into being. Condenser ribs, glycol-cold, stand open to the trade winds like combs, and the fog that brushes them gives up its moisture in beads; the beads run into gutters — half pipe, half living root, braided together over so many years that the plumbing has a cambium — and the gutters feed the cistern at the tower's heart, where pale blind-curious fish rise to the surface like coins whenever a shadow crosses the hatch. From the cistern, drippers, and from drippers, everything green. Rain itself is an event: once in a while the tower threads an actual cloud, and for half an hour warm rain falls on the crown, and the keepers come out of the engine house and stand in it with their faces up, because rain that falls on you from the sky is the rarest weather they have. Water here is condensation first, ration always, rain only by luck or schedule.

The warmth is the engine's gift, and it stops at the deck's edge like a tide-line.
