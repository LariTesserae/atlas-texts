# Coldharbour

## The Shore

Above Coldharbour, the plain is dry for many miles — marl, wind, stunted thorn — and the road runs straight across it because there is nothing to go around. Then the road dips, the wind dies, and cold air comes up to meet you over the lip of a shallow bowl, like breath from a cellar door. Ten paces and it is five degrees cooler. You will see your own breath in the Dish some mornings as late as June.

From the rim you can see all of Coldharbour at once, because there is almost nothing in it to hide anything else: a green dish a mile across, sunk thirty feet into the bed of a lake that dried before anyone here kept records. You could walk across it inside an hour and count everything in it before you started — every roof, every pond, all three Wells. The bowl is the lake's leftover. Its old shoreline still reads as a low terrace all the way round, and in the north rim there is a notch where the water used to escape. Water left through that notch once. Fog comes in through it now, most nights, and the town lies open to it like a net.

The green has a limit, and the limit is a line. All down the south shore it runs straight as a canal: living felt on one side, grey cracked clay on the other, no fade, no half-green. The keepers cut that edge back to the old shoreline every autumn. The straightness is theirs. The line itself, they say, was agreed to long before any of them.

## The Green

Call it moss and you will be corrected — gently, by people who have been correcting visitors for three hundred years. It is a felt: liverwort over rootmat over older felt, compressed toward peat at depth, a foot or two of it, sprung like a mattress and utterly silent underfoot. Sound goes into it and does not come out.

It is one thing. Any piece grafted onto any other takes hold, and the keepers graft freely — mended paths, patched lawns — because every square inch is already joined to every other. They speak of it as a tenant of enormous age. The founders, the Lease says, came to this bowl because the Green was here first, and were hired to keep it. To keep it small. The Lease is read aloud every year at the Shore-Cutting, half in jest and half in the other thing.

It drinks the sky. Rain goes straight in; dew vanishes upward by midmorning; nothing flows away, ever, which is why the old lakebed stayed dry enough to build on. Cuts heal in days. It tolerates no other plant — every seedling is weeded out by the mowers, a patient endless war of preference — and it does not like depth, which is why everything built here stands on shallow stone. In frost weeks the ground stays soft beneath a skin of white. Decay at the root of the felt makes a little heat, and the Dish spends its winters sleeping on it.

Walking on it is what changes people. No footfall, no scrape, no crackle. The mowers wear felt boots. Loud walking is considered rude, and after a week you agree.

## Water

There are no streams. Rain arrives on most third days, stands on the felt in beads, and is gone by afternoon — drunk, tallied, remembered. Open water exists in eleven places, all of them made: dew ponds lined with wax and clay, each with a name (the Cradle, Long Pond, the Wash), each patrolled by a pond-ward with a long blade, scraping back the green that advances on every bank, every day, forever. This is the only predation in Coldharbour: the ground itself against its ponds. It has been a stalemate for centuries and neither side is winning.

The three tallest things for miles are trees, and they are infrastructure. The keepers call them the Wells. Their crowns have been grafted and trained for so long they read more like slate roofs than leaves — dark, dense, angled at the sky — and every night they drip, down channels spiralling their trunks in gutters worn glassy by centuries of water, into cisterns at the root. A stair has been grafted into each trunk, and it is by these stairs that the tank-keepers climb at dawn to open the valves. The Wells are the town's water towers, its watch posts, its landmarks. Children are told which Wells they were born under.

At dawn, if you crouch, you can watch dew run — visibly run — along the felt's capillary seams toward the nearest Well. The keepers say the Green feeds its towers first. It looks like water deciding to go uphill.

And at night, when the air is still, the fog comes through the Breach like something with a purpose: a river of it, thigh-deep, flowing down the old outfall channel and spreading across the felt in a slow flood. The lamplighter marks its arrival the way a harbour marks tide. By the smallest hours the whole Dish is full of it, silver, beaded, and beginning to drink.

## The Town

Coldharbour is smaller than it was. A turf-fuel boom two centuries back raised a stone arcade, granaries, a proper square; the plains found coal; the boom drained away; and the Green, patient, took the empty quarter up to the lease line and stopped. It has never taken a kept door. One door was not kept, and now that house is a tree with a scar for a front door, and children dare each other to knock on it. The arcade — the Arches — stands out in the sward still, its lower storey swallowed to the windowsills, its upper arches used as drying racks. Nobody is troubled by it. It is considered tidy, as ruins go.

The living town strings along one causeway of dished stone from the Shoreyard to the engine hall — forty-odd houses at intervals of an acre, wide swards of mown lawn between. The houses are grown: hedge walls pleached over frames, then hollowed and lined with wax and clay; roofs of living sedge that must themselves be mown twice a summer; gutters of split culm feeding downpipes of gourd-vine grafted to copper. Every house is three ages in cross-section — a stone footing from someone's great-great-grandmother's time, the living middle, and the youngest layer of glass, wire, and iron brackets bolted on top. The dead go back to the Green: a felt shroud, a hand's depth of cover, a mound that greens over in a season. The rises stand far out in the empty lawns, mown around forever.

The public buildings stand on lifted brick. The Exchange has dry floors, scales, the wax hall, and the Beast Book. The compost yards steam all winter — the warmest place in the Dish, where the old people go to nap and mushrooms are raised in dark sheds among the pale slugs, one of which, palm-long, is called the Deacon and may be older than the Lease. The Shoreyard sits at the rim on honest grey clay: a crane, a scale house, drying floors. Beyond it the hard road runs for the plains, and once a week a wagon comes in with grain, salt, and iron bar, and leaves with wax, resin, medicine, and turf-bricks, which burn cool and clean and are much liked by people who have never heard what the Green does to a sound. The town eats the Green too, of course — pressed curds of the mowings, yard mushrooms — but grain comes from outside, and is counted.

A single rail cart, the Snail, runs Shoreyard to Exchange at noon on stored cable wound up by the flywheel, its wheels shod in felt. It rings a small bell, and the bell is one of the loudest regular things in Coldharbour. The lamplighter is the only person whose footsteps you can hear anywhere in town, because he alone walks everywhere on stone.

## The Inhale

At the head of the Street stands the engine hall: iron ribs, glass, and black slate harps — condenser arrays fanned open to the sky and tilted toward the Breach. Their work happens at night. The harps give up their heat to the stars, fall below the temperature of the arriving fog, and the fog, breaking over them, comes down as water. By the smallest hours the whole array is running: millions of drops, ticking into culm-troughs, gathered into the loft-tank above the wheel.

At first bell the sluice opens. A night's fog, weighed in water, falls through the wheel, and the flywheel — thirteen feet of oak and iron, turning in a felted pit — takes up the day. Line-shafts run from it along the spine of the causeway, out to the sickle-carts in the mow-fields, to the turf press, to the lathe house. A small boiler at the resin works burns turf-bricks under stone, and its stills sigh out white steam that smells of camphor and honey. The dynamo gives light to the Inhale, the Exchange, and a line of dim culm-post lamps down the Street. The town is electrified the way a farm cat is domesticated: nominally, and not to be relied on past ten.

The hours are marked by the drip-bell, a small bronze thing rung by counted drops from the loft-tank. Everything else is damped — felt gaskets, wax-packed bearings, culm-bushed linkages — and the machinery of Coldharbour runs quieter than its own footsteps. The loudest sound in the Dish is dripping.

## The Kept Rules

There are few, and they are old. Trim your doorstep; let no green take a kept door. Walk soft. Burn nothing that grew here except under stone. Log every beast. Waste no water; the word is the worst one they have. The dead lie shallow, because the Green does not like depth and takes them anyway.

## The Beasts

The Beast Book is three centuries deep, and entries are years apart. *Grey wagtail, the Cradle's rim, Year 412, 14th of the Hush; stood through two bells; left at the changeover. (Miller, and the Widgery girl.)* In the margin, in a dead keeper's hand: *well-year.*

What a beast finds in Coldharbour is nothing — no seed set, no nectar, nothing to drink that has not been tallied and sipped first, nowhere to hide, since the Green allows no tangle and no depth. And yet they come, a bird down through the fog perhaps once a season, and they stand head-cocked, listening, sometimes for hours, to something under the felt. Then they leave without eating or drinking, as if embarrassed. The margins of the Beast Book hold the town's theories about what they hear. The theories do not agree.

The resident fauna is this: the white moth of the wax works, which lives nowhere else; the slugs of the compost yards; and, on Yellowing mornings, whoever is passing.

## The Year

The light should be mentioned first because it is the least remarkable thing here. Mornings are milky; the fog burns off to a plain blue; the Dish keeps a faint softened haze, like a clean window not quite clean. Nothing in the sky of Coldharbour is strange. It is everything under the sky that is.

The days have a shape. Fog in at dark. Dripping till dawn. The wheel at first bell. Mowing through the middle of the day — the mowers work by regrowth, a mown square ready again when its cut has healed to velvet, and since the felt is the clock, the rota moves round the Dish like a slow hand, a full circuit each season, never resting. Some third day, rain. In late summer, a drought week — the Hush — when the Inhale rests, the ponds are measured, rations are posted, and the town goes to bed at dusk. The Hush is the quietest quiet in a quiet place.

Then the Yellowing: one morning between summer and autumn, for about an hour, the entire felt flushes straw-gold and the air goes sweet, and then it is over and the Dish is green again for another year. The mowers take the day. More beasts are logged on Yellowing mornings than all other days together.

Then the Shore-Cutting: every keeper to the rim with long blades, the green edge trimmed back to the old shoreline, the cut edge weeping for a day and crusting pale. It is not called a victory. The Lease is read; the founders' request is repeated — *stay small* — and the cutting is framed as asking, politely, again.

And some still nights, when the fog river has filled the Dish, comes the changeover. For the length of a held breath, everything pauses: drips hang, the flywheel seems to hold, the felt — people differ on the felt. The Inhale's log records it without comment. *C.O. — 3 min past 2.*

## Rest

Nothing in Coldharbour erodes anymore. That is the thing newcomers cannot put a name to. The stones are worn — dished by feet, rounded like river cobbles — but the wearing is finished. The ditches hold their shapes. The boom is two hundred years gone, the Arches are a drying rack, the Green stopped where it was told, and every process here is answered by another: growth by cutting, arrival by tally, fog by the wheel, the wheel by the fog. Nothing is building up. Nothing is running down. The Dish is not wild and it is not dead; it is kept, in the oldest sense of the word — attended, in balance, at rest.

Which is why, if you shout in Coldharbour, no one hears you at the next house.

And why, if you stand still on the felt at the hour the fog first comes in — quiet boots, hands still — you can hear the ground drink.
