The climb takes most of a morning, and for the last hour there is nothing left to see. The spire rises out of hot dry country where the last trees gave up two thousand feet below, and its skin — bronze over ceramic, seamed like old sailcloth — wears a strange shame: beads of water standing on every surface, in a country that has none. The air outside is thin and warm and tastes of dust. The tower weeps anyway. Condensation runs down its flanks in threads, gathers at the seams, falls as fat slow drops into a place that never learned what to do with rain. The keepers say the tower is dreaming of the chamber inside it. The chamber is why the tower exists at all.

You feel the place before you reach it. Through the rungs of the service ladder, through the soles of your hands, a hum arrives — low, patient, enormous — and by the time you reach the hatch your palms are buzzing like struck bells. The hatch itself is painted over with a signed alphabet, glyphs cut deep into the paint and repainted for generations, cut deep so they can be read by fingertip in the dark. Nobody speaks inside. Speech is not a thing the chamber permits. You open the hatch and the sound comes up through the gap like water through a torn seam, and then the warmth comes, and it is a wet warmth, body-temperature, the warmth of an open mouth, and within a minute of stepping through you have stopped being a person who dries.

Inside: one room. That is the first shock, after the climb — that the whole apparatus of noise and heat and life is twelve strides across and three storeys tall, a barn-loft of layered works packed until nothing is out of reach. Pipes run over pipes. Gantries cross above sumps. Cables and drip-lines and hollow resonating struts fill every volume the machinery left open, and the machinery left very little. You could not fall in here without catching a handhold. The floor is grating over grown-mat; the "soil," where there is any, is engineered felt. Nothing is natural. Nothing was ever natural. The room was made, and then everything that lives here arrived by wing or by intention, and settled into the made thing the way silt settles in a harbor.

And it is dim. That is the second shock. The only light comes from below the knee — dull amber service lamps along the walkway edges — and from the walls themselves, which are veined with a faint blue-green glow that moves. The veins brighten where the sound is loudest. This is the voice-moss: an algal film, bred centuries ago, that fluoresces under pressure. The chamber's lighting is its noise made visible. The walls are gauges. You can read the machine's mood off the plaster the way you'd read loudness off a needle, and you can tell where the quiet pools are, because they show as dark country on the wall, and you keep your hands out of the dark country, because that is where the gliders roost.

What the chamber does is simple and impossible. It swallows clouds. The spire stands at cloud-height in a warm sky, and when the haze comes through, intake membranes along the roof-frame open and ingest it — a whole cloud, drawn down into the room in slow spirals — and then the swallow begins. The resonance engines spin up. This is the roar you heard through the hatch: standing waves laid down through the hollow struts and drumhead floor-sections until the air itself is squeezed like a fist, and water that was fog is water no longer — it condenses out of the vibrating air in sheets, runs the weeping housings, feeds the drip-lines, fills the sumps. Every drop is booked. The condensate channels are inscribed with tallies, and the tallies say where the water goes: down the spine of the spire, to the dry country below, where they drink the sky secondhand and have mostly forgotten this is how. The country below has stories about the hum. They call it thunder that learned to stand still.

During the swallow — the throat-shift — speech would be pointless at any volume. The roar flattens thought. The floor drums under your boots. The ceiling, which is otherwise unknown country, shows itself for the only time, lit from beneath in ragged continents of blue-green, and if you look up during those minutes you can see the gliders hanging in the dark up there, folded, patient, watching the lit walls the way owls watch a lit field. Overflow rain falls inside: for six minutes the perforated ceiling weeps in earnest over the sumps, on schedule, booked like everything else. Then the engines spin down, the roar drops to the standing hum, and the room exhales into its long digestion.

This is when the chamber's true work becomes audible, because the chamber has staff.

No one planned the staff, exactly. The first tenants were seeded — or simply arrived, the histories disagree — but the balance now is load-bearing and centuries old. The chamber keeps animals the way an orchard keeps bees. Without them the condensation membranes film over within a season and the harvest fails; with them, the surfaces stay licked clean, the films stay cropped, the sumps stay living. The water ledger even carries their line item. They are budgeted. They are staff.

The clock-crickets live in the hollow struts, ten thousand of them, and the struts amplify them into the machine's own register, so their song rides the hum like stitching on a drumhead. Their intervals are the schedule. The pump timings are set by ear. The bell-frogs crouch bronze-backed at the sump rims, hundreds strong, and they have solved the problem of the roar with a trick that took no one's help: they sing at frequencies set just off the hum, so that the difference-tone surfaces above the noise — small struck-glass notes, ringing clear on top of the avalanche. A room full of frogs sounds, from the gantry, like a bell choir performing inside a waterfall. In the sumps below them, shoals of skimmers — shrimp-things, translucent, paddling in their thousands — keep the water moving and the films turned. On the membranes themselves patrol the grazer-beetles, blunt and patient, working the condensation surfaces vein by vein, and their work is the reason any of this works.

The moths breed in the sump-films, in clouds. Their wings have come to carry markings shaped like the chamber's status-runes — little false ember-marks glowing along their wings — so that hunting gliders strike the painted walls instead of the flying ones. It works often enough. The gliders hunt anyway, at spin-down, in the turbulence gap between roar and hum, when the moths tumble and the light-veins flare across the ceiling and the whole room becomes one brief lit battlefield: wing-claps, falling moths, frogs rising to the knocked-down ones, crickets marking the interval. Then it settles. The gliders fold back into the ceiling dark. The frogs resume their difference-tones. The hum takes everything back.

Nothing in here is new and nothing is failing. That is the third thing you understand, after the noise and the warmth, and it takes longest. The gantries wear dripstone — calcite grown column by column, drip by booked drip, decades of scheduled water laying stone on manufactured anchors, so that even the geology here is made, or at least commissioned. The sumps are terraced with mineral shelves, smooth as glaze. The handholds are polished bright by generations of gloved hands. Tool marks sit fresh on top of patina, which sits on older tool marks. The feed-hopper by the east sump was topped up recently — yeast-dust, the base of the whole food web, poured in by someone who then climbed back down two thousand feet. The signed alphabet by the hatch was repainted within the year. Somebody tends this room. Somebody has always tended this room. The room has repaid them by refusing, for centuries, to either improve or decay — it has simply settled, the way a river settles, into being exactly what it is.

The days here have a shape: dry bell, throat-shift, spin-down, digestion, the crickets marking it all in stitchwork. The years have a shape too — the slow mineral thickening of the dripstone, the drift of species balances, the keepers' ledgers reconciled against the water tallies each season. And underneath both, one longer process that no ledger accounts for: the engines are drifting. A quarter-tone, over generations, toward the pitch of the frog chorus. It shows up in the tuning logs as a recurring correction, something that keeps having to be un-made — and then, some decades back, the notations change, and the corrections stop being made. The last engineer to touch it left a single line in the margin: it sounds better this way. No one has overruled them since. The machine is drifting toward its frogs the way a river drifts toward the sea — not because anything forces it, but because the room has had so long to become itself that it has started, gently, to prefer things.

From the dry country at night you can see the spire against the warm sky, and near its crown there is one window that glows — amber, low, the color of a lit hand — and on still evenings, when the wind lies down, the country below can hear it: a hum with small struck-glass notes moving on top of it, ringing clear above the roar, like something enormous keeping water in its mouth and singing to what lives there.
