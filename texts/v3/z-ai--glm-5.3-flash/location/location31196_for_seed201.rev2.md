Before dawn the Hollow is a bowl of grey glass.

It sits high — higher than anything that has a right to be green — a cirque carved into the shoulder of a young mountain, four hundred paces across, walled on three sides by a headwall still scalloped from the ice that left it within living memory. The way in and the way down is a single notch on the east side, and through that notch the world simply ends: a drop of ridges and faces falling away for a day's walk in every direction, all of it bare. No water, no green, no shelter, for a day's walk in any direction along the stone. Everything that lives at this height lives in this bowl, or commutes to it.

The ice has been gone only a short while, and the hollow wears its recentness openly. The floor is a apron of fresh till — moraine ridges heaped like crude dry-stone walls, erratics sitting on the surface like dropped tools, scree cones with faces still the color of the inside of things, unweathered, sharp-edged. Nothing here is smooth. The headwall's polish, where the glacier rode for centuries, is only now being colonized: rings of black and orange lichen spreading outward from the lowest scars, mapping the retreat year by year. You could read the ice's departure in color if you knew how long a lichen takes to cross a hand-span. The mountain is still growing, too — quietly, in small tidal shrugs — and everything in the Hollow has arranged its life around that fact.

Frost comes up every night like a second geology. By first light, rime feathers stand on every stone facing the wind, grown in crystal blades a finger long, and the seven tarns wear skins of ice thin enough to see the dark water and the doubled walls beneath. Then the sun comes over the notch and the day's whole economy begins.

The light here is not like lower light. The air is thin enough that the sky overhead is not blue but a deep bruised violet, nearly black at the zenith, and the sun is a white coin without mercy. At noon, two or three patient stars are visible straight up, faint as breath on glass. The light warms nothing unless it touches you directly — step out of the sun and the cold closes over you like water, instantly, no gradient. Shadows are colder than shade. The glare off the tarns and the one surviving snowfield is so total that squinting is a permanent condition; anyone who works up here for long does it through slit-bone goggles, and there are such goggles in the hut, carved from sheep bone, lenses of nothing but two horizontal cuts.

Melt starts at the toe of the snowfield mid-morning, and a stream is born — a real stream, talking to itself over the till, running the length of the floor — and then, by mid-afternoon, it dies. It has a lifespan of hours. Everything green here lives in the seams between its arrivals: seep-lines where meltwater never entirely finishes draining, kept dark and wet under mats and snow year-round. In those seams the green is startling. Moss deep enough to swallow a boot. Cushion plants grown dense as pincushions, each one a fist-sized dome that took thirty years to build, each one a small warm building — inside a cushion at noon it can be fifteen degrees warmer than the air, and the insects know it; they move in like tenants. Saxifrage splits the fresh rock with patient acid. Lichen paints the polish. And once a season, when a few dozen hours pass without a hard frost, everything flowers at once — the whole bloom compressed into a single day, the floor suddenly speckled loud as a festival, gone by the next frost.

The fauna arrives in layers, following the warmth the way the bowl focuses it. For two hours after noon the hollow is the warmest place for fifty miles, a dish catching light, and everything with wings knows the hour. Midges hatch in the tarns in black standing columns that hold their shape in wind. Small spiders run the moss. Choughs — black, fluting, absurdly acrobatic — ride the updrafts off the sunlit wall, cache crumbs in the scree, and are the only voices the Hollow has for most of the day. Round haymakers work the grass tussocks on the old moraine, cutting and curing stacks between stones, squeaking their thin alarms. Ash-grey climbers come down the headwall at dusk, near-invisible until they move, to drink at the tarns on hooves like knapped flint. And above it all, rarely, a hunter: known mostly as a shadow that crosses the snow, and by the instant silence it brings down over everything before the shadow arrives.

At noon the stone begins to tick. The sun on the fresh rock makes the whole headwall crackle with small thermal shots — the sound of a young mountain adjusting, a slow dry clock. Once or twice a day there is more than a tick: a tremor, a note felt in the teeth before it's heard, a thread of dust sliding off a ledge. When it comes, the strangest thing in the Hollow happens. Everything stops. Midges hold station in the air. The haymakers stand upright over their stacks. The choughs land and fold. The pause lasts exactly as long as the ground hums — no longer, no shorter — and then every creature resumes as if nothing had occurred, as if the stillness is a courtesy paid downward, to the deep thing that is still lifting this place into the sky.

In the afternoon the weather comes from below, as it always does here. The cloud-sea that fills the lower world heats, rises, and pours up through the notch — a white flood moving in silence, spilling over the eastern rim and down the inside of the bowl until the tarns, the gardens, the hut, are all inside a cloud. The mist beads on every surface: rime-needles, cushion leaves, the quartz caps of the cairns. This is the Hollow's other water, the moisture it drinks from the sky itself, and by evening, when the sea withdraws back down the notch, everything is wet, and the wet freezes, and the whole hollow goes glazed — every stone lacquered, every grass blade cased in ice that catches the last red light like blown glass.

Sound up here is a short list. The air is too thin to carry much; a voice dies at ten paces. What you hear, all day: the tick and click of stone. Drip from melting rime. The midge hum, and the way it vanishes when a cloud covers the sun. The choughs' fluting. Wind, which doesn't howl here — it shoves, arriving as pressure and cold on the skin while staying nearly soundless. A distant rockfall arrives as a click. At night, if the frost-growth is heavy, there is the faintest sugar-creak across the tarns, the sound of ice crystals forming, which some say you can hear and some say you imagine.

The built things are few and far between, and from above the hollow you'd miss most of them. A line of cairns walks a safe path across the moraine and through the notch — wrist-to-knee-high, dry-stone, each capped with a chunk of white quartz that catches the low sun, so at dawn and dusk the route home lights itself. In the lee of a fallen block sits a hut no bigger than a cart: dry-stone, roofed with sod weighted down by rocks, invisible from above. Inside, a blackened hearthstone, coils of grass rope, horn tools, a single cold-hammered copper pin — the only metal in the place — and a niche in the wall holding three objects someone carried up here on foot through a day of empty stone: a carved antler, a knapped blade, and a bleached seashell, from an ocean no one who lives in these mountains has ever seen. On the lintel, notches cut in a row — one for each melt-season someone came — thirty-nine of them, and the fortieth half-cut.

The keeper is not here now. Whoever it is arrives with the melt, stays some weeks, and goes down before first hard frost; there's a stone-lined basin below the big seep that refills overnight with the frost-melt, kept clean, kept ready. A set of knucklebones on the hearth suggests the waiting hours. The animals tolerate the visits the way they tolerate weather.

Night, when it settles, is total in a way the day never suggests. Fire behaves badly here — burns small, blue-flamed, unenthusiastic, as if embarrassed. Water boiled barely cooks. Scent is gone; the thin air carries almost nothing, so the whole world is transacted through eyes and ears. Sleep comes down heavy, and the dreams that ride it are famously vivid. Outside, the tarns seal over one by one, each pool closing like an eye, and the stars — doubled now, in the ice and above it — crowd a sky with no atmosphere left to soften them. Overhead, the faintest stars are out; below, their reflections lie in the black glass of the tarns; and between the two sets, the headwall stands sharp and frost-feathered against the violet dark.

That is the Hollow's whole secret, if it has one: it is the exact place where water and light still agree. Above the notch, the cushions fail within a stone's throw; the line runs right there, through the gap, clean as a ruled edge — below it, green and water and swarm; above it, stone and starlight. The life of the sky has climbed as far as intermittent melt and brutal brightness will carry it, and it has stopped precisely here, and for a few furious weeks each summer this bowl holds everything: the bloom in a day, the stream with a lifespan of hours, the communal pause before each tremor, the fortieth notch half-cut and waiting.

Then the frost takes the bowl back for the year. But the mountain keeps rising underneath it, a little each year, lifting the last garden slowly toward the light it came for.
