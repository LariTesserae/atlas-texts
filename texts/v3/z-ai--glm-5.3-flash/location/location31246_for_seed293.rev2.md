The sound arrives before the place does — a mile out, across the flat: a low choric hum with a two-note pulse underneath, like a choir humming through a heartbeat. The herons don't look up. They commute.

The plain around it is the oldest kind of flat. A turf so wet and springy that every step is a small negotiation; water stands in your footprint before you've lifted the other foot. No hills, because nothing here has needed hills for an age. The land finished its argument with water a very long time ago and settled into a truce measured in inches — and there are no inches to be had. It is a landscape that has stopped becoming anything. Except in one spot, where it clearly has not.

Out of the flat rises a low green dome, maybe four hundred paces across, like a hill buried up to its ears. Except that it sweats. A haze stands over it even at noon, exhaling in the warm hours, drawing mist back in at dusk. And there is the sound, closer now: not wind, not water. Industry, of a kind.

Marsh-folk call the hollow and the thing that lives in it by one name, *the Pith*, as if the land had a marrow. Some travelers say the Bowl. The herons, who nest on the rim and carry no opinions, have no name for it at all.

The rim is a ring of springs. Each one has spent an uncountable span building itself a terrace of pale stone — step over step of deposited lime, warm water slipping over edges it polished before any living memory. The stone is warm to the palm. Roots braid through the terraces like dark stitching, drinking at every seam. The air here smells of wet bark, yeast, crushed green — the smell of the hour after rain, held permanently, as if the place keeps post-rain as its only tense. It is a degree or two warmer than the plain in every season. You can feel the metabolism on your face.

There is no gate. There is a mouth.

The Pith grows a lintel where people tend to enter, and the opening is a shutter of woven roots that dilates — slowly, unhurriedly, the way an anemone decides. Inside, the light changes to green-gold, the light of being underwater without the drowning. And the sound closes over you. Everything here is wood and membrane and hollow stem, and everything resonates. A dropped cup gets an answer from three directions. The whole bowl is an instrument being played from inside.

The floor is not soil. It is callus — generations of grown floors fused together, polished where feet go, shaggy where they don't. Push a stick two fingers into it and the stick comes up wet; that is the water table, trembling just under the surface, and the Pith drinks from it constantly and sweats it back out constantly. By mid-afternoon the walls weep visibly. Gutters of curled leaf collect the condensation and hand it to channels of grown flume — hard, smooth, porcelain-smooth interiors, warm as skin. In an emergency, the walls are potable. Nobody here has an emergency.

Look closely at any wall and you can read it like timber, except the strata are civic. Thin dark bands are lean years. Wide pale bands are expansions. There are seams where two lineages fused, knots that are the sealed doorframes of older chambers, floors stacked on floors: canopy hamlets above, gallery districts at mid-depth, root-warrens below — a town of maybe a thousand souls laid out vertically in a footprint the size of a hamlet, because the Pith builds up and into itself rather than out. The whole city is mostly room. It keeps a third of itself deliberately empty — great fallow halls, aired, unfurnished, held in reserve the way a prudent household keeps a spare room. The emptiness is not absence. It is inventory.

Streets here are vessels, and traffic is their only tenure. A passage that carries traffic stays open and slowly grows to fit its crowd; a passage abandoned thickens into solid wood and becomes wall. You cannot own a wall. You can borrow one. Rooms exist because someone goes through them, and the plant is always watching to see who goes where.

That is the part that takes a day to accept: the Pith is paying attention.

There is a lattice through its body — fungal thread and conductive tissue braided into the wood like nervous tissue — and signals run it constantly, cool flickers you can sometimes feel as faint static if you press your ear to a trunk. Deeper in, at the floor's center, is the Thinking Floor: a broad warm hall that hums, whose temperature rises measurably when the Pith is working a hard problem. Fauna nap there for the warmth. Its foresight is the thing no one has fully explained — it seeds its own dew, opens and closes streets before congestion forms, grows a bridge two days before anyone needs one. Its light-pipes conduct daylight into windowless depths with an efficiency that no fiber anyone can spin should allow, filling deep halls with a clean, sourceless noon: you can find its color but never the sun in it. Whatever was given to its lineage, long ago, it has since iterated past its givers.

Which is not to say it lacks a memory of them. In a niche off the Thinking Floor there is a shrine: a first schema, sealed in living amber, hair-fine silver wire threading through resin that the Pith renews annually with disturbing precision. Nobody alive can read it. Possibly the Pith can't either — in the way you cannot read yourself — but it maintains the shrine exactly, annually, the way you keep a heart beating.

The inhabitants, meanwhile, run the daily business. Gleaners graze the algae felt off the interior walls — small warm-blooded things the plant herds to its dirty corners on gradients of scent, gardeners who don't know they're employed. Loppers with beaked faces prune the overgrowth for pay in sugary galls. Lantern-moths drone through the nectar market in clouds so dense they set its pitch; the whole market tunes to them. Wallfish ripple inside the weeping walls like eels in jelly, filtering. Courier birds wear grown scent-collars and carry messages between districts faster than the plant can push them through air. And everywhere, doing the fiddly work, are the clerks: thumb-sized green bodies the Pith grows each morning and reabsorbs each night — neither fauna nor flora, just hands it grows and takes back. They will adjust a gutter, sweep a sill, gesture you along a corridor. If you sleep here, you will find, by morning, a small side-chamber beside your bed, grown to your proportions, refit nightly. A fitting room. No one knows why. The plant makes models. The plant is always measuring.

The dead do not linger. Carrion beetles ferry what ends to root-mouths at the fallows' edge — grown mouths that ingest, unhurried. There is no refuse heap because there is no *away*. Everything circulates so fast that the city is visibly mid-renovation at any hour: a hall dissolving overnight, its residents scent-herded to a new one grown three streets off and already furnished; walls swelling; streets hollowing; the pistol-crack of fast growth and long green creaks threading under the moth-drone and the drip-clock and the two-note pulse of the hearts — audible everywhere, in everything, faster at midday. Roughly every nine days it surges, prunes, and consolidates. Some hot afternoons it over-charges its own air: fog stacks to the roof, condenses, and it rains *indoors* — warm, hard, twenty minutes of it, everyone slick and laughing, gutters singing — and then the Pith drinks the sky it just made, and the light comes back glass-clear.

Here is the strange arithmetic of the place, the thing you only understand on leaving. Nothing in it is old. Every molecule has been replaced many times over; the clerks you saw were not there yesterday and will not be there tomorrow. And yet the Pith is ancient — older than the marsh, older than the terraces, which are merely the fossil record of its thirst. Its core heartwood is stone-dense; its taproot goes down past the water table into the dark of the aquifer and drinks there. What is old is not its material but its *shape*: it long ago settled into its final form, and that final form is a state of constant renovation. It is not preserved. It is renewed so thoroughly that its age has become structural. A lineage, not a relic. The plain around it reached its end-state by stillness; the Pith reached its end-state by refusing, at maximum effort, forever, to be any different.

Walk out at dusk, when the plant circulates its sleep-hormones and the whole city naps in a soft wave, and look back. The dome is just a green mound exhaling, with one seam of light-pipe glow along its flank like a banked coal. The hum carries a mile and then the plain closes over it, flat and finished and wet, hiding the one place on it that never stops moving — and somewhere behind you, in a small green chamber, something is already rehearsing your return.
