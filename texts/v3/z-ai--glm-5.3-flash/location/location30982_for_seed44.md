You come down into the bowl on the old road, through the Rim Wood, and the descent takes longer than the walk across the top had led you to believe, because the rim is farther off than it looks and the bowl is deeper, though not deep. Everything here is low. The basin is a green amphitheater maybe half an hour's stroll across, walled by old slopes grown thick and patient, and in its floor sits the town — if *sits* is the word for a thing so intergrown. From the last bend of the road you can see all of it at once, which is the first thing to understand about this place: it is small, it is bounded, and it is entirely green. Not a town in a forest. A town inside a plant, the way a heart sits inside an animal. Thin columns of steam rise from it and vanish into the underside of the leaves. The roofs are living mats, the smoke is white and sweetish, and the whole bowl exhales a humid warmth you can feel on your face from the rim, like standing over a rising dough.

Before you see anything clearly you hear it, and what you hear is a drone — a note below music, more felt in the teeth and sternum than heard — and layered on top of it the surf-sound of an enormous canopy of leaves moving in no wind in particular, just breathing against itself. Beneath both, water. Always water, going somewhere. Later you will learn the drone's name: it is the screws. But at first it seems to come from the ground itself, as if the bowl were humming to keep its contents warm.

The ground, when you reach it, is not exactly ground. It is a mat — root, peat, old fiber, decades of fallen leaf pressed into a dark springy substrate that gives under your boots like a thrown rug over a mattress. Water stands a hand's depth below it everywhere. Dig anywhere and you hit it in a minute; the wells are barely wells, more like polite holes. So the town's first law is **pump**: the water must be lifted, always lifted, out of the ditches into the canals, out of the canals into the ring, out of the ring through the cut in the rim and away. And its second law is **prune**: in a warm wet bowl where everything grows with enthusiasm, the green would close every channel, bud every window, and swallow every plank-walk within a season if it were allowed. The town obeys both laws at once, constantly, gently, and has done for so long that the obedience looks like stillness. Nothing here is finished. Everything here is maintained.

The heart of it is the Screw Hall, a long low building on the First Dike — the ancient ring-walk, now canopied by the oldest trees in the bowl, trunks wide as doorways. Inside, three great Archimedean screws turn at a walking pace, wooden flights shod with iron, each one lifting the ditch-water two lazy meters to the next stage. They are driven by a waterwheel on the main canal, and the same wheel, by belt, drives a dynamo that lights the hall and the lamps down the Dike: warm bulbs under green shade, always beaded with condensation. The boilers fire on dried press-cake and charcoal brought down from the rim kilns, and their iron is painted in resin-varnish because ordinary paint gives up here. Everything metal in this town weeps. Brass fittings wear permanent dew. Moss colonizes the boiler jackets in velvet patches, gets scorched brown on the hot side, regrows on the cool side, a war fought at the pace of lichens, and nobody minds.

One machine you should see. The old Number Two engine, the founding screw's drive, still turns — and it lives inside a tree. Two lifetimes ago the engine house was new iron and brick; the trees around it were young and ambitious; the trees won slowly, the way they win everything here, growing around the housing year by year while the engine kept turning inside.
