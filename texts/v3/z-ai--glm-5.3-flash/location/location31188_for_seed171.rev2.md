You hear it before there is anything to see. There is no quiet approach to this country; the noise has geography of its own, a second landscape laid over the first, rising and falling like terrain. From the last bare ridge you feel it through your boots before you can name it — a bass hum coming up out of the green, continuous, patient — and threaded through it, a sharp vocabulary of work: hiss, drip, creak, and every few minutes, from some unseen quarter, a flat cracking report like a rifle shot, followed by the boom of falling stone that never quite lands.

The green goes out past every horizon. From the ridge it reads as a single unbroken surface, dark and wet-looking, slightly domed, like a lid fitted over the world — and in a sense it is. Nothing interrupts it. No road, no clearing, no gray thread of river, no naked outcrop, no herd trail, no smoke. Only green, and above it a low, even ceiling of cloud the same gray-green as the light underneath, so that the whole country seems lit from within rather than above. The light is dim and has a color you associate with deep water or with photographs of aquariums: noon here is a green-gray murmur, diffuse, directionless, spent by the time it reaches the ground.

You go down into it and the lid closes over your head.

Underneath, the world is packed. There are no gaps — not gaps of ground, not gaps of air. Trunk to trunk, tier under tier, shrub in the elbow of every root, moss riding the moss, vines binding tier to tier so thoroughly that distance stops being something you cross and becomes something you climb through. Everything is wet. Every surface beading, every stem cool to the touch, the air itself at its dew point so that your breath is a small visible event and your passage through a fern-frond wrings an actual rain onto your shoulders. The air near the ground is warmer than the air above — the groves hold their heat like glasshouses under a cold lid — and where that warm wet air meets the cold it makes a standing mist, knee-deep, that softens every edge.

And the ground is not ground. It is a woven lid of root laid over rock, giving slightly underfoot like the deck of a ship, and when you pull your boot out of a footprint, water rises into the print behind it. There is no soil here. Not thin soil, not poor soil — none. Soil is a thing erosion takes centuries to make, and this place has not granted erosion a single century. The rock under everything is appallingly young: faces like freshly skinned stone, edges unrounded, minerals glittering raw with no patina of lichen, no weather-stain, no moss older than a season. A mountain that was uplifted yesterday, as mountains measure things. The first dirt in this world is a black paste you find packed at the root-tips of the great trees, millimeters deep, and it is not dirt at all. It is crushed mountain. It is being manufactured, right now, everywhere, by the roots.

That is what the noise is, mostly. Work.

Consider the quarry-trees. They lean against the cliffs by the thousands, and they quarry. A trunk swells a water chamber at its base with a sound like a held breath, then snaps the chamber — a cavitation crack, focused, aimed — and a plate of granite the size of a door spalls off the rockface with a rifle-report that echoes off the raw stone. The plate never reaches the ground. It lands in a net of woven stems that flex, catch, hold, and by the following season it is gone, taken apart molecule by molecule, because this forest does not wait for the mountain to weather. It eats the mountain directly. Its roots secrete acids that unlock fresh rock; its tissues build themselves around fibers of magnetite and silica pulled out of the stone — hold a compass anywhere in this country and it spins uselessly, because north here is grown, not found, and every direction is faintly a tree. Some of the energy that runs the green doesn't come from the veiled sun at all. It comes from chemistry: the slow burning of fresh minerals, olivine and iron still hungry from the mantle, oxidizing in the wet dark. The mountain is on the menu.

Put your palm flat on any trunk and you can feel the hydraulics: a rushing hiss, a water-main under tension, the tree lifting the spring uphill. Every river in this country is vertical. Rain does not gather into streams here; sheetflow dies within meters, intercepted, drunk, routed up into the canopy and back down through stems and leaves. There are no banks because there are no channels, because nothing has had time to carve and nothing is allowed to. In hollows of the bedrock sit pools, kettle-cold, mirror-still — and the stillness is the eeriest thing about them, because no animal has ever come down to drink. The only ripples are rain. Around every fracture line in the rock hangs a beard of wet moss and a slow weep; some of the weeps run warm, the young massif still holding heat in its bones, and where the warm seeps meet the cool air the mist thickens until the understory is a soft white dimness with green shapes in it.

By afternoon the whole country breathes out. The transpiration of a million square kilometers of leaf has filled the low ceiling of cloud until it can hold nothing more, and it rains — a warm, directionless, canopy-filtered rain that is partly the forest's own exhalation coming home. By night the rain falls without any cloud at all: every leaf-edge beaded, guttating, dripping, so that darkness under the trees is a sound as much as a sight — a trillion drips on a trillion leaves, tick and splash and tick, layered into a roar that never resolves into silence. Twice a day the pressure swings enough to feel in your ears. The weather of this place is born here, lives here, and dies here, hundreds of kilometers from any ocean; the forest makes its own sky and then stands under it.

Much of the rest of the noise is the forest reading the mountain. It sings into the rock — long pulses of infrasound you feel in the sternum rather than hear — and listens to what comes back, mapping ore bodies and fracture planes and hidden water the way a bat maps a room. The rifle-cracks are both quarrying and survey. And at certain hours, most reliably in the black hour before dawn, a single low wave passes through everything at once: every root system on the horizon firing in synchrony, a pulse that takes whole minutes to cross a region, felt in the chest like a second heartbeat. The green is deciding something. Where the next crack goes. Where the water is dammed and where it is released. It thinks slowly, continentally, in the dark.

Because there is no one else. No artifact anywhere — no cut stone, no nail, no cairn, no char, no ditch with square corners. Nothing made, nothing dead lying about either: no driftwood, no bones, no leaf-rot heaps, because a fallen stem is taken apart and re-ingrown within days and nothing lies wasted. And yet from that ridge, looking back, you saw the arrangement: ranks and arcs in the planting, kilometer-wide curves too regular for accident, groves staged in shade-graduations, sheetwater guided by levees that are roots grown into walls. Nothing was built. Everything was planned. The plan is the artifact, and it runs in the genome, without any builder present — perhaps never present, perhaps long gone — executing itself with the patience of a thing that cannot be bored. If anyone upstream has a name for this country, it is probably a number.

The absence is precise once you notice it: the noise contains no voices. Nothing calls, because there is nothing to call to. No wingbeats break the drip, no birdsong, no insect whine, no splash of anything swimming. The forest is loud the way a factory is loud, not the way a marsh is loud. Once, in a footprint-pool, you watch thin white threads draw back from your shadow — protists, almost-animals, too simple to be called creatures and too purposeful to be called weather. That is the whole of the fauna here: a shadow's worth of almost-life in the water film, and pollen drifting in veils with nowhere to go but back into its own kind.

It doesn't matter. Nothing here is edible in the old sense — no soft fruit, no flesh, no nectar — because the food web runs on rock and light and closes on itself, plants feeding their own dead back into their own roots. Nothing has arrived to eat anyone, and nothing has evolved to flee. The only events are constructive ones. Growth is fast enough to watch: a torn stem calluses in days, a rockface greens over in weeks, slope-shrubs migrate by leaning and rooting and leaning again, a few meters a season. There are failures — an over-ambitious grove on a steep wet sheet of stone lets go and slumps — and the failures are harvested, taken apart, redeployed. Nothing is wasted, including mistakes.

Walk far enough — a lifetime, and the walking is climbing — and the green ends. Not in dieback. In a line: straight as a surveyor's chord, green on one side and raw, steaming, freshly uplifted rock on the other, the mountain's new skin waiting under a rare moment of open sky. And the line moves. Meters a season, at the pace of something that has already won. Beyond it lies the next nursery: bare stone, warm weeps, air being prepared.

Stand still and the country simply takes you into account. Your footprints fill with root and water. The pulse goes through you on its slow rounds. Somewhere near, a trunk swells its held breath and fires, and a plate of the mountain comes loose and is caught, gently, by everything below.
