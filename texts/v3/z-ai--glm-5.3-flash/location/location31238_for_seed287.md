**The Stillroom**

It is a chamber, first of all. Not a cave in the wild sense — a cave implies vastness, implies unexplored dark stretching beyond lamplight. This place has boundaries you could walk in twenty minutes. A low-vaulted hollow beneath the world's skin, maybe eighty paces long, ribbed with pale stone that has settled into its final shape so long ago that the settling itself has become the shape. Nothing here is eroding anymore. Everything has finished eroding. The walls are worn the way a river stone is worn: completely, patiently, to the exact form the water intended, and then the water moved on and left the form standing like a finished sentence.

Except the water hasn't moved on. It never moves on here.

Moisture is the chamber's rhythm. Not rain, not flow — *condensation*. Heat rises from somewhere deep and unseen beneath the floor, hot enough that the air shimmers faintly at knee height, and this heat draws water out of the stone itself. It beads on every surface. It runs in slow, deliberate channels — grooves in the floor, worn by millennia of droplets taking the same path — down to a handful of shallow pools that never grow and never dry. The pools are mirror-flat, mineral-dark, faintly warm to the touch like bathwater left standing. The whole chamber breathes once a day, perhaps: a slow swell of humid warmth toward the ceiling, a slow release of condensation down the walls, drip by drip by drip. Drip. The sound never entirely stops.

And in this heat and this damp, the vegetation has gone lush past reason. Without animals to browse it, without frost to check it, without seasons to discipline it, the green has simply *continued*. Mats of moss thick as upholstery. Ferns unfurling from cracks in the masonry, their fronds overlapping like roof tiles. Vines that have grown so long and so slowly that they have become structural — cables of growth thick as a wrist, draped across the vault, stitched from wall to wall, some of them older than anything built in the world above. The chamber is dense with it, layered, near-touching: fern over moss over stone over root, every surface green or being claimed by green, the air itself thick with spore-fog and green sweetness. In the pools, pale filament-threads sway like hair.

But here is the strangeness, and it takes a while to notice, because your eye keeps insisting it must be a garden, a ruin, a forgotten conservatory.

*The machines are still running.*

Scattered through the undergrowth — half-swallowed, mostly — are the remains of an industrial presence that never entirely left. A pump. A small, squat, riveted pump, green-bronzed with age, and it is *clicking*. Slowly. Once every few seconds, a sound like a patient clock. Rusted pipes run along the walls and disappear into the stone, and if you press your palm to them, some are warm. Somewhere down there, something old and mechanical is still doing its work, at a pace so reduced it might be a kind of sleep. A flywheel in a vine-wrapped alcove turns one rotation an hour. A gauge on a bracket shows a needle that has moved, in living memory, perhaps a single degree.

Nobody knows who built them. There are no bones, no tracks, no droppings — no fauna at all, not an insect, not a worm, nothing to scuttle in the leaf litter. The green lives alone here, and the machines live beside it, and neither seems to require the other, and yet the pipes sweat in time with the moss.

Light comes from below. Cracks in the floor, fissures along one wall, emit a dim, sourceless amber glow — heat-light, furnace-light, or perhaps something stranger, some slow chemical patience of the deep stone. It is enough to see by, barely. Enough to make every fern frond cast a soft black silhouette upward onto the vaulted ceiling, so that the ceiling is a forest of shadows growing in the wrong direction. In the pools the light shudders faintly with each distant *click* of the pump.

Loud, actually, when you stop to listen. Not loud the way a surface is loud. Loud the way a stomach is loud, up close: the drip, the tick, the creak of vines expanding by fractions of a millimeter, the low sub-audible hum rising through the floor, the whisper of air moving through the vents in the stone. The chamber is a body at rest, and it is never silent.

The rules of the place, as far as anything can tell:

Nothing here hurries, and nothing here stops.
Everything cycles — water up, water down; heat in, heat out; growth out, decay in — in loops so slow a human lifetime samples only a fraction of a turn.
The machines are not dying and not alive. They are *enduring*, at minimum power, forever.
And the green does not mind. The green has all the time there is.

If you sat in the Stillroom long enough — and you would grow warm, and drowsy, and strangely calm — you might notice, once a day or once a year, hard to say, one of the pipes shudder and exhale a long slow breath of steam into the ferns. And the ferns would drink it. And the pump would click.

And the world above would go on not knowing.
