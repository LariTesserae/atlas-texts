**The Kettle**

High on the mountain's flank, above where the trees give up, there is a ledge, and in the ledge a slot of blackness no wider than a door. The night outside is thin and cold enough to bite; stars stand close in the rarefied air; frost glitters on the scree. And out of that black slot, a warmth breathes against your face like the flank of a sleeping animal. The locals call the cave the Kettle, and the name is exact. You climb into it the way you'd climb into something alive.

The river that carved this place died long before anything now living was born. You can still read its passage in the stone: a smooth-walled gallery worn by water, potholes like cupped hands, a channel curving down through the mountain and then out — but the channel is bone-dry, has been dry for ages, and the cave it left behind was inherited, empty and warm-hearted with geothermal heat, by the things that fly.

They came in the millions. They are here still.

The main chamber is small, as such things go — forty paces across, a low dome, a choked passage at the back — and yet in the dark it has no far side. The dark is not empty. The dark is full. Put a hand to the wall and the wall is not stone under your palm but bodies: a shifting carpet of cave crickets, silent, long-legged, packed shoulder to shoulder up the rock in their thousands, and beyond them on the ceiling the bats hang in such dense clusters that the roof of the chamber looks furred, pelted, a surface of folded wings. The floor gives underfoot like deep snow, and it is not snow — it is guano, meters of it, stratified like sedimentary rock, the compacted history of a hundred thousand years of departures and returns. You could cut a block from it and read the past in its layers: dark bands and pale bands, ash from some ancient fire that no one remembers, a lens of tiny bones.

Walk ten steps and you have not disturbed the place so much as entered its bloodstream. Beetles the size of thumbnails move over the floor in slow herds, domed and dark, their shells clicking glassily as they jostle. Springtails and mites work the deeper strata in numbers that do not bear counting. Moths flutter up from the walls in soft Coca-Cola-less silence — no, in soft *smoke*-like silence, moths that live on hair and feather and the leather of what was once carried in. Spiders run sheet-webs across the narrower passages, and near the mouth, where the dusk air carries in the first flying insects of evening, the webs hang heavy. And from the ceiling of the inner dome, threads of silk descend, beaded with moisture, each one anchored to a small cold star of light — larvae that fish the air with glowing snares, harvesting the cave's only rain, which is condensation, which is breath. Ten thousand animals exhaling; the moisture climbing to cold rock; the rock sweating it back down. That is the water here. That is all of it. The colony drinks its own exhalation and the larvae string lights with what falls.

For there is no water in the Kettle and no green thing has ever grown in it. It eats the outside. Every dusk the bats pour up the entrance shaft in a column of bodies like smoke rising in reverse — a river of wings going out over the cold high country, down the mountain's dark flank to the far lowlands where rivers still run, where moths swarm above the reeds. They eat the world and bring it home in their blood. By dawn they are funneling back down the shaft in the thousands, folding into the pelt of the ceiling, and the chamber swells faintly with their warmth, a tide coming in. The cave inhales the lowlands nightly and excretes a mountain of the night's work on its floor, and the floor's herd of beetles converts that into more beetles, and the spiders and the crickets and the larvae convert beetles into themselves, and everything is eaten, and everything eats. No room between presences. Even the air has bodies in it — a haze of fine dust, a drifting of gnats — and the smell is ammoniac and musky and mineral-sweet, the smell of a place that is digesting.

And yet it is nearly silent. That is the discipline of the dry: every sound costs water, and nothing here can afford one. So the whole overwhelming population keeps a kind of mutual vow of quiet, and what you hear instead is the texture beneath the vow — the tick of falling guano grains, constant, like a clock made of sand; the papery fidget of a wing adjusted; the clink of beetle meeting beetle; once in a long while a single bat, somewhere deep in a passage, gives one thin shriek, and the sound rolls away down the stone throat of the old dead river and takes ten full seconds to die.

Someone found the salt here, once. Old guano weeps niter, white efflorescent blooms on the banks and walls, and long ago people came with wooden scrapers and cut channels in the pale floor that are still there, straight and deliberate among all the organic wandering. A ladder rotted into the entrance gallery; its stubs are black stalactites of wood now. Soot stains the dome where their fire burned — the only fire this place has ever hosted. On the wall near the mouth, where the last grey light reached, there are charcoal tally marks, hundreds of them, and a handprint pressed flat. The moths found the handprint generations ago; it is fainter every year, licked of its sweat-salts by small grey wings. The cave is eating them too, at its own pace, and in a few hundred more years the marks will be a smudge, and then not even that. There is no other technology here. No device, no nail. The people came with wood and fire and patience and left with bags of salt, and the Kettle has filed them under weather.

At the threshold, in the colder air, lives the other predator: a pale owl that hunts the exodus each evening, taking bats from the smoke-column on the wing, and beneath its ledge the pellet-middens have built up into a soft grey stratum of their own, thousands of pellets, thousands of tiny skulls, a bone-shingle beach marking the boundary between the cave's jurisdiction and the night's. Deeper in, past the second chamber, the heat pools — the gallery narrows and the rock is blood-warm and the beetle herds thin out and stop, all at once, as if at a border. Along that wall lie shed skins, translucent, some of them long. What owns them you will not see. You will see instead that this last stretch of floor is utterly clean, scoured of everything, and that the silence there is a different silence — not the dry-thrift quiet of the colony but an attentive one. Even the ticking stops. Whatever lives in the warm deep has made a clearing around itself the way big things do, and everything smaller has agreed.

Stand in the main chamber long enough and your eyes adjust to nothing, and the place becomes what it is: one body in the dark. A gut with its slow herds; a lung, breathing out its warm moisture up the shaft; a heart in the deep gallery, keeping its own counsel; a skin of wings on the ceiling, tensing at dusk. It is warm because it is full, full because it is warm, and it sits high on the mountain like a held breath of the lowlands, converting the distant wet world into beetles and bone and heat, quietly, without a single green thing, without a drop of rain, night after night after night.
