**The Proof Garden**

You come up through the middle of the world to reach it. The lift is a riveted cage on a counterweight chain, and it climbs a shaft whose walls are still chalked with floor numbers and survey fractions — Level 40, CONCRETE CURE 60 DAYS; Level 55, DO NOT STACK — past floors of nothing yet, empty rooms with fresh-scaled floors, the smell of setting stone. The air on the way up is thin and cold and slightly wrong, the air of a sky that has only recently been switched on. Your ears adjust twice. Then the last door, a double-hung thing with a porthole window, opens, and the world finishes.

Warmth first. Wet warmth, green-smelling, pressing on your face like a held breath released. Then the light: ordinary daylight, afternoon-colored, falling from above — and only above, because the sky in here is a ceiling, a vast lens of slightly rippled glass that renders a plausible sun somewhere past its zenith. Then the green, everywhere, in every direction at once, filling a room you can cross in forty paces.

That is the second thing this place tells you: it is small. Forty paces long, twenty wide, three terraces and a stair, a cistern pool at the bottom, a glass sky maybe ten meters overhead. The whole horizon of the garden is forty feet away. You can hear every drip in it individually. This is not a landscape that contains you; it is a landscape you could pick up, if your hands were big enough, and it has been arranged with exactly that awareness — the concentration of a whole intended world, folded into the footprint of a chapel.

The third thing, once you look closely at the ground under the green: none of it is old. The "rock" of the terraces is cast aggregate, pale as unbaked bread, with the faint grain of formwork still visible where nothing has grown over it yet. Seams run straight. Bolt-lines are legible. The walls weep — warm water seeping from capillary channels the builders cast inside them, designed to sweat, to stay damp, to offer moss a living — but the weeping has not yet stained. There are no mineral streaks, no darkened drip-lines, no green furrow worn down the stone face. The channels that drain each terrace were cut dead straight, ruler-true, because no water has yet disagreed with them. Water here is still young. It hasn't learned to wander.

Even the soil is made: a dark manufactured loam, laid in beds like upholstery, heated from below by pipes so that the ground underfoot has a faint animal warmth, the temperature of a sleeping flank. The spring at the top terrace is a bronze spout on a valve. The pebbles edging the pool were tumbled in a drum to look like river stones. Everything in this room is built, down to the weather — but built so recently that the build is the most legible thing about it. This is a world one or two years into its own existence
