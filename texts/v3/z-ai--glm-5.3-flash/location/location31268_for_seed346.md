# The Condensary

## The Descent

You reach it by the Founders' Stair, though the founders cut nothing. They found the spiral already templated in the rim of the hollow and waited four generations for the stone to resolve around it. The steps are firm now, mostly. Here and there one still carries the softness of a thing printed last century, and your boot leaves a print that fills itself in over the following hour, patiently, the way water fills a hollow — except that what fills it is stone remembering how to be unbroken.

The hollow is the lowest place for a day's walk in every direction: a bowl some three hundred paces across, walled by a surface too regular to be rock. This is the Selvage — a sheet of machined constants, hair-fine lines laid in hatchings, cool to the touch and slightly warm to the *attention*, if that makes sense, and in this place it does. Above, the bowl is lidded with young stone that never quite closed. One seam runs across it, and through the seam comes a column of ordinary daylight — overcast-noon light, the kind that makes no promises — leaning and sweeping slowly, as if the sun outside still remembers to move.

You can see the whole city from the stair. You could shout across it, in fact, in a few seconds of voice. You must not, but you could.

Nothing here has ever been dry. That is the first thing your skin reports, before your eyes finish their report. The air is cellar-cold and beaded; every surface carries its condensation; the stone sweats at its seams the way a pitcher sweats on a summer table, except that there has been no summer here, only this one long cold season of arriving.

## The Fall

It rains the way a thought arrives.

At what the residents call morning, the lid begins to sweat. A fine drizzle falls for the length of a bell — the Morning Fall — and it is cold, mineral, faintly sweet and then faintly of iron, and whether it is *water* is a question the residents have formally stopped litigating. Caught in a bowl, it stays water, more or less, and is carried to the cisterns and eventually to the gardens. Landed on a templated surface, it becomes: slate, glass, brick, an alloy that grows in coral branches, a porcelain with a grain in it like wood where some forest template misfired two centuries ago and was never corrected, because correcting is not among the options.

The residents call it the dew of things. It is the raw form of nascent matter, and in this place matter condenses out of the air the way frost condenses on a window — except that the window is the world, and the frost is a city.

## The Print

They do not call it a city. They call it the Print, and it behaves like one: a few hundred structures — a hamlet's count, a city's behavior — packed at print-density onto the hollow floor. Streets a shoulder wide. Eaves touching, or nearly, and getting nearer.
