Nobody sees it all at once. You come down to it the way everyone does — by the funicular, or the long stair-roads — and for the last few hundred feet you are inside cloud, white and close, hearing the cable hum and the water somewhere below you, everywhere below you. Then the car drops through the floor of the fog and the valley opens under it: a bowl of terraces, tier on tier, wet stone going gray-green down into the dark, and out of the middle of it, columns of steam rising slow and leaning with the wind. Lamps come up through the dim in gray-gold orbs. The whole valley fits the eye at once — rim ponds, work tiers, weir chain, outfall — and it is all one made thing. There is no rock visible anywhere. There is no ground.

The mountain is still there. You feel it as mass, as cold coming off the walls, but it has been faced over entirely — every slope cut into terraces, every terrace walled, every wall carrying something. Steps dished by two centuries of boots. Iron hand-rails worn bright along the top and furred with rust below. Timber lintels gone greasy-dark. Nothing is painted; paint would be a lie here. Instead there is patina, and there are repairs: patches of pale new stone, raw lashed timber, a launder of fresh planks in a run of old ones, all of it destined to darken and match within a season. The place is not ruined and not new. It is maintained, and the maintenance never ends — someone is always re-pointing a wall, clearing a channel, bailing a sump, lashing a flume. That is the motion of the valley. It churns constantly in order to remain exactly what it is.

Because the walls weep. The rock behind the masonry holds water the way a sponge does, and every face seeps — thin sheets of water sliding down under the moss, seep-lines mapped in green like brushwork, gutters and cornices dripping in long uneven rhythm. There is no dry season and there is no dry wall. The older stonework has given up resisting; its whole surface is alive now, a skin of moss and fern and cress gone wild in the seams, thick enough on the shaded faces that it mops the rain and lets it go again hours later, slowly, so that the valley drips for a full day after the weather has passed. In the workshops they line the walls with woven moss-matting for the same reason — it drinks the damp and it eats the sound. The quiet of this place is not an accident. It is built.

The water has a schedule and a hierarchy. Up on the rim, above the last terrace, stand the header ponds — still, black, cold — and along the crest above them the fog fences: tall frames of fine mesh set to comb the cloud as it pours over, each panel weeping steadily into its runnel even when no rain falls at all. From the ponds the water descends through a nervous system of penstocks, launders, and sluice-gates, and it is never used once. Pond to turbine. Turbine outflow to the dyeworks. Dyewater to the cress-beds and the wall-gardens. Greywater to the settling tanks, the tanning pits, the moss frames, and at last down to the weir chain on the valley floor, mostly roofed over, where slow dark wheels turn furred with moss and the turbine vaults hum a note you feel in your teeth rather than hear. Everyone knows the order. You do not foul water above your own level; you do not block a launder; you do not touch a gate that is not yours. The hours are told not by bells — this is a quiet town — but by the gates, which open on the hour and land with a soft iron thud you receive through your boots.

The tiers sort themselves by light and dryness, and everyone knows that too. The top terraces get whatever the sky gives — a few pearly hours down the valley axis around midday, three or four times a year a brief shocking appearance of the sun, which stops all work and sets the whole population blinking up like cave creatures until the glare off the wet stone drives them under cover. Up there are the gardens: roof after roof of carried-in soil in stone troughs, fed by the greywater cascades — cress and sorrel, dwarf plums against warm walls, gooseberries, greens, barley where the light is truest. Nothing grows here that was not planted; there is no soil that was not brought up by hand. Below the gardens come the paper-shelves, whole bridges hung with racks of gray sheets drying like flags in the still air; then the spinning halls, where line-shafts run overhead and the belts whisper rather than slap; then the dye-stairs, the vats sunk under the bridges, cloth going down into color in the gloom — the colors read as black until someone carries a lamp close, and then a bridge-length of cloth will come up madder-red or woad-blue all at once, briefly, like a thing surfacing. lowest of all, under the weirwalk, are the old levels — the Drowned Shelves — where the founders' first works now stand half-surrendered to the water they made. It is coldest down there, and the cold is mined: ice from the winter ponds is stacked in the deep galleries under sawdust and moss and sold down to the lowlands; mushrooms grow on racks in the dark; eels live in the flooded stairs. Everywhere down there you can hear water working behind the walls, and the pumps run without pause, because the valley is always quietly bailing itself.

The machines are water-fed, so the soundscape is soft. An inventory, standing still on the Weirwalk at shift-change: the manifold drip, which never ceases and which nobody hears anymore; the hiss of the penstocks; the felt-quiet of the belts; the dynamo hum under everything like a held breath; the creak of drying racks being cranked; the funicular bell arriving from above late and flattened, fog-strangled. Voices stay low because they do not carry, and the valley's real conversation happens inside its plumbing — the water pipes double as a telegraph, and all day a code of taps and knocks runs through the walls from tier to tier: gate's open, rag cart on the stair, come down, hold the light. Goods travel overhead on cables through the fog like slow dark birds. Steam from the works climbs, cools, and falls back on the middle tiers as the valley's own drizzle, so that on windless days it rains from below the way it rains from above. At dusk the wind reverses, and the rim-cloud comes over the crest in a slow silent waterfall, erasing the upper terraces first, and the lamplight rises to meet it — bowl of gray-gold orbs, tier by tier — until the valley is just lights and sound of water in the dark.

The animals are few and everyone knows them individually. Cats own the drying racks and the wool stores, and are fed as employees. A heron works the outfall below the dye-stairs, arriving with such irregular majesty that work stops on its bridge until it has settled. Bats come out at dusk where the moths are; the moths come to the lamps; eels take the census of the still water. Rats exist at the margins, kept there. That is nearly all — the dense animal of this valley is people, packed edge to edge and tier to tier, no space unclaimed: workshops under bridges, beds over workshops, cisterns under beds, gardens on all of it, pipes and ladder-ways and wires stitching everything to everything. Dryness is rank here; to end the day dry is to have worked up top. On the rare clear mornings, the whole valley stands in the light for as long as it lasts.

And then the cloud closes, and the lamps come back, and the gates thud on the hour, and the patching and pointing and bailing go on. Everything in the valley is made — the terraces, the gardens, the water's schedule, the local rain, even the silence. The only unmade things are what arrive from above the rim: the cloud, the cold, the thin dim light. The valley cannot rest; the water would take the low levels in a single winter. So it moves, always, quietly, in balance the way a rope-walker is in balance — and underneath everything, on every wall, in every pause, the drip. Never stopping. That is the sound of the place holding still.
