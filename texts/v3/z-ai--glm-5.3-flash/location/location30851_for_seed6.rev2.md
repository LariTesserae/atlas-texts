# The Crown

The surface is above you.

That is the first thing this place teaches: up and down still mean something here, but they have changed sides. The sky is now a silver ceiling a few meters overhead — a rolling mirror seen from the wrong side, dimpling with swells you feel as pulses of light before you could ever hear them, and you never hear them. The drowned world is quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

You are at the crown of a tower that was, until recently, the tallest thing for a long way in every direction. It isn't tall anymore. Nothing can be tall with nothing dry left to be tall above. It is simply the last place the water reached, and it is under, and the water is young.

That is the second thing: the flood is young. Water this new is still clear. The rivers have not had time to teach it silt; it has not learned to be murk. So the daylight comes down through it nearly unchanged — ordinary light, daylight-strength, only combed into shifting nets on every surface, caustics crawling slowly over steel and leaf and railing. The light is normal. It is only the medium that is impossible.

And the tower itself is new, too, in a way that unsettles more than age would. Cast concrete with mold-lines still sharp as seam-marks on fresh porcelain. Welds unpainted. Scaffolding still clipped to the upper drum, left where the crews stood the week the water finished rising past them. Nothing has had time to foul, and — this is the strange mercy of the place — nothing will foul the usual way, because fouling is mostly the work of animals: borers, burrowers, grazers, shell. There are none. Not one fish has found the tower. Not a barnacle. The structure will stay new-looking for a long while yet, like a thing kept in a display case, and the display case is the whole sky.

The whole crown is small — smaller than a village square, a cramped stack of terraces and machine rooms and one open garden deck, its edges defined entirely by water. There is no horizon here except the ceiling of the surface. All distance has become vertical. A map of this world would be a single column.

Nothing on it is bare. That is the third thing, and the densest: every surface is occupied. Pipes run in the shadows of other pipes. Cable trays have become planters. Railings hold railings of water hyacinth, whose roots knit the stairwells into green shafts you would have to part like curtains. The walkway grating is furred with algal felt grown ungrazed into actual velvet — an inch deep, deepening. Leaves everywhere, and here is the detail that catches: not one leaf is notched. No insect has ever taken a bite in this garden. No worm has ever loosened its soil. The lushness is perfect, and the perfection is what is wrong with it. Everything grows as if nothing in the world is hungry.

Almost nothing is. The garden was planted in the last weeks by people who understood what was coming — willow cuttings rooting straight through grates into the warm outflow, reed beds in what used to be cooling tanks, food vines trained up masts that once carried signals. Even the soil is made: graded ceramic gravel in trays, fertigated by drip lines. Of all the things in this place, only the water, the light, and the green were not fabricated, and the green was fabricated too, just by a different process.

The warmth is the engine's. The tower's heart still runs. It was built to run wet — that is the fourth thing, the rule of this world, and the strangest. The builders engineered for drowning the way other people engineer for rain. Every level, as the flood took it, was sealed and switched over in an orderly ceremony — the record of it still glows in the low rooms below: lights stepping from amber to a small steady green, pumps reversing, stairs accepting the water floor by floor. The machinery runs water-lubricated, magnetic-coupled, sealless; there was nothing to fail, because nothing was ever closed. Heat pours off the working surfaces into the flood and rises as a slow plume, a shimmer like heat-haze, and the whole garden leans in it, all one direction, moving at the pace of weather that takes an hour to pass.

The gardener is a machine. One arm on a rail above the deck, advancing one station every hour, pruning at the speed of growth, which is the only speed that matters here. Its pumps stroke maybe four times a minute — a double-thrum you feel through the grating more than hear, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Otherwise the sounds are small: the tick of cooling metal as the light-slant changes, an occasional structural ping dropping down the tower's spine to somewhere very far below, the squeak of the gardener's rail. The water presses all of it into softness. Silence here is not an absence; it is a pressure holding everything still.

At the very top, in the highest cupola, there is an accident: a dome that sealed imperfectly, so a lens of air clings flattened beneath it — a pocket of old sky the size of a small room, squeezed thin and bright. Sounds behave differently inside it. And in that last dry breath of the world there is one moth. Pale, folded, clinging to the ceiling of its tiny sky. It flew at the false horizon for a while — the marks are there — and now it hangs dormant, sleeping or finished, impossible to tell from outside the curve. It is the only animal left at this elevation, and possibly in the world, and it keeps this information to itself.

Above the cupola, three antenna masts still break the surface. Their tips are the last dry inches anywhere — on calm days the sun dries them and each one wears a bright wiggle of water-light at its waterline, like a ring. When a big swell comes through, even they go under, and for a few seconds there is no dry thing at all, and the mirror-ceiling lowers until it is nearly close enough to touch, and the light dims as if a cloud had passed. No cloud passed. You are the weather now.

The flood is still rising, though slowly — a finger-width a day, patient as everything else here. At that rate the crown has a season left. The hyacinth doubles faster than that. Somewhere in the arithmetic of those two curves there is a quiet race, and no one is running in it, and it may not matter which wins. The gardener advances one station. The pumps keep their slow double-beat. The caustic nets crawl over the unnotched leaves, and the tower, young as the day it was finished, hums its one low note into water that has not yet learned to carry stories.
