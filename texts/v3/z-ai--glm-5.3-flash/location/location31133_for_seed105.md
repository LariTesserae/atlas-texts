# The Undermoor

Come up out of the deep. Below you the abyssal plain runs flat and starless for leagues in every direction, and then the water begins to climb under you — not the water, the ground — and a country rises out of the dark like a held breath let slowly down. A drowned highland. Once these were fells above a young cold sea; then the sea rose, or the land grew tired and sank — the stone keeps no account of which — and now the hills stand under a thousand fathoms of near-freezing water, wearing their old shapes like clothes that no longer fit but were too well-made to fall apart.

This is the thing to understand first: the water is not the landscape here. The water is the *sky*. It has weather. It has seasons. The highland lives beneath a climate of pressure and current the way other places live under cloud.

The light, what there is of it, comes from far above — a rumor of a sun, a gray haze of almost-dawn that only reaches the summits. The tallest ridge of the undermoor wears this faintness like frost. Stand on it (swim above it, rather; nothing stands here) and you can almost believe in the sky. Descend into the valley between the hills and the last of that ghost-light thins away, and then it is true dark, the kind that has weight, and the only lamps in the world are the ones that grow here.

---

The gardens are the moor's great secret, and its great wealth.

Where the ground is bare — and most of it is bare, dark stone plains running empty between the hills, floored with a skin of black starving film — nothing lives but the film itself, a lichen-thin nothing that has given up on everything except persisting. Swim a league across such a plain and you will believe the whole world is finished. Then you round a shoulder of hill and a valley basin opens below you *lit from within*: a lampfield, tiered and shaggy and furiously alive, a hanging garden of cold blue-green fire pouring up the drowned slope like spilled luminous paint. Lush is not a strong enough word. The gardens here are cathedral-lush, dense with shawls and fronds and velvet under-turf, every strand of it glowing, and the light moves when the water moves, so the whole hillside seems to breathe.

They grow this way in darkness because they have given up on light. Life here learned, long ago, to eat sound instead.

The moor is a loud place, and that is not an accident; the loudness is the *farming*. Every groan and tick and slow percussion of this drowned country is a meal. The internal tide — a river of colder water that slides over the rim every six hours and fills the valley like a bowl — makes a deep sliding note as it comes. The bowl of the highland seiches after it, the whole valley of water sloshing once, slowly, like a struck bell. Stone shifts on stone. Currents comb through the ruins and play them. All of it is food, and the plants have grown instruments to catch it.

The lampreeds are the humblest: reeds with pale cold lights at their tips, dim when the water is quiet, flaring when a sound rolls through — you can watch a noise arrive as a wave of brightening crossing the field. The tollstems are stranger: tall leathery kelp whose upper fronds end in hollow bulbs. Young bulbs are soft and knock dully. Old bulbs mineralize — the moor's dead turn slowly to stone; hold that thought — and so the oldest tollstems carry bells of actual rock on their shoulders, and when the current works them they *toll*, each on its own slow irregular schedule, a congregation of bells that never quite agree on the hour.

And there are the pilgrims. The herd. Once a season — a water-season, a length of time measured in slosh and chill rather than sun — the rollweeds come across the plain: great drifting tumble-forms, ball-shaped masses of braided weed as large as cottages, rolling with the tide like sea-thistles, dozens of them strung out across the dark in a slow procession. They graze on sound. They follow the loudest weather. When a herd passes through a lampfield the whole garden brightens to greeting, and the rollweeds hum as they go — a low contented chord that fades down the valley for an hour afterward, going from loud to less loud but never, in this country, to silence.

Nothing flees from them. Nothing flees from anything here. That is the second thing to understand.

---

There is no animal life on the undermoor. None. No fish in the water column, no worms working the silt, no crabs in the ruins, no drifting clouds of small hungry lives. The sediment lies in untouched layers, page after unturned page. Nothing darts away from your hand when you reach into a hollow. Nothing has eyes anywhere in this dark, and so nothing has ever needed to hide.

The absence has a texture. You notice it most in the middistance: a healthy sea has *flicker* in it, small motions at the edge of vision, a hundred small decisions being made every second by small nervous things. Here there is no flicker. The only movement is the movement of water and the movement of plants, and both move the way continents move — patient, total, unbothered. If anything notices you as you pass through a lampfield, it is the garden itself: the lamps bend, very slowly, toward the thud of your heart and the thunder of your moving body. You are the loudest creature this country has held in a thousand years, and you are made of food. The light follows you like slow fire. You cannot sneak across the undermoor. You can only try to be quiet, and fail, and be adored for it.

The vines, at least, do not eat you. But they eat each other. In the gardens there are stranglers — pale cords that bind tollstem to tollstem, patient wars conducted over centuries, one grove slowly swallowing a neighboring one while the swallowed grove goes on glowing, unbothered, bright to the end. Predation exists here; it simply runs at the speed of stone.

---

Which brings you to the builders.

They were primitive — hammer and fire and stacked stone, no metal worth mentioning — and they are gone, and the water keeps their stone the way a cold attic keeps a trunk. Their valley was the loudest place on the moor, though they never knew why that mattered: their hammering rang off their walls for generations, their wells and weirs and water-stairs played the currents like instruments, and so their terraces grew the greenest pastures the fells ever held. Then the water came, and the people took what they could carry and went up and over the rim and out of the world — and the sound-farms kept their shape without them, like ghosts made of noise.

Now the ruins are the gardens. Drystone hut-walls furred with shawls of glowing weed, door-holes glowing like lit doorways into lit rooms, hanging gardens pouring from the wall-heads. A field-wall walks the spine of a ridge, lamp-lit along its whole length, the brightest road on the moor. A stone weir — a comb of pillars driven across a drowned stream-cut — stands furred white with tollstems, and tolls. On the valley floor there is a ring of standing slabs, a choir, and inside the ring the turf of lamps grows thicker than anywhere else, a velvet of cold fire, because at every turn of the tide the choir stones hum in harmony with the seiche and the garden inside feeds on the chord.

One more thing, and it is the strangest. Near the choir stands a single taller stone, cup-marked by hands, and around it a circle of bare ground. Nothing roots there. Not the gardens, not the pilgrims' path, not even the black film — the ground around that stone is the only truly clean stone on the moor. The plants refuse it the way they refuse silence. No one is left to say why. And at the outer rim of the highland, where the plateau drops away toward the abyssal plain, a stair of twenty dressed steps goes down out of the lamplight into the deeper dark — and continues, by all accounts, beyond anyone's swimming. It is the only structure on the moor that leads nowhere the gardens will follow.

---

Stay for the turn of the tide. Everything on the undermoor is worth seeing, but the tide-turn is worth *hearing*.

It begins at the rim, with ticking, stone on stone, as the cold mass starts over the lip. Then the choir hums. Then, one by one, every stone-belled tollstem in every garden on every hill strikes — no two together, an unwillingness of bells — and the sound rolls outward and multiplies, and for a quarter of an hour the whole drowned country is one low rolling chord, and in the ringing every lampfield flares. Three hills and a valley, strung with fire, ringing. The sound goes out over the plain for leagues, diminishing but never ending, and herds of pilgrims far out in the dark turn toward it and begin to roll home.

Then it ebbs. The lamps sink back to embers. The cold closes its hand. The water remembers its patience.

And the moor goes on being what it has always been: a country where everything that grows must sing, and everything that sings is, in time, absorbed — for nothing rots here, nothing is eaten, nothing is carried away. The dead gardens simply harden, generation over generation, into the plain itself. The ground you drift over is the churchyard of its own forests, stratum under stratum, and the sea takes a grain of it and gives a grain back, and the balance never tips.

Everything that grows here must sing. Everything that sings will, in time, be stone.
