## The Terraces of Unfinished Green

You arrive on a stairway that has not decided to be stone.

That is the first thing to understand about this place: nothing here is raw, and nothing here is finished. The terrain is a vast series of high terraced shelves — a valley of plateaus stacked like a half-built amphitheater — and every surface of it is *made*, but the making is not masonry or metal. It is grown. Living plant-matter, trained and grafted into walls, floors, bridges, gutters, doorframes. The built parameter is total: there is no soil exposed anywhere, no naked rock, no natural substrate at all. Yet the tech is nothing. No tools, no devices, no fastenings. Everything that stands was persuaded into standing — woven, grafted, pleached — by hands or by something that works like hands.

The construction is embryonic. Erosion has barely touched anything; edges are soft, unresolved, fringed with new pale growth like the stubble of a world only days old. Whole sections of terrace are just lattice — an open ribwork of living boughs with daylight showing through the gaps — waiting, apparently, to be filled in. Some of the filling has begun: green foam of moss pushing through the weave, waxy leaves sealing the gaps from the inside. You can walk through districts that are architecture, and districts that are only the *idea* of architecture, still skeletal, still breathing.

And it is wet here — persistently, structurally wet. Water is close to the surface everywhere, though you rarely see it running freely. Instead: weeping. The living walls seep. Condensation beads on every leaf-ceiling and drips in slow percussion from the eaves of grafted roofs. Between the terraces, channels lined with reed-concrete carry dark, slow water from one shelf down to the next, and where a channel ends before its destination — as several do, mid-slope, still under construction — the water simply pours off the edge and becomes a veil of fine mist that rises again into the warm air. The atmosphere is thick with it. The whole valley breathes humidity. You are never dry; the landscape has decided that and you live within its decision.

The vegetation is not *in* the landscape. The vegetation **is** the landscape. There is no distinction between the plant and the thing it constitutes. A house is a tree grown into the shape of a house. A bridge is a root arched where a bridge is wanted. Moss serves as plaster, grass as carpet, lichen as paint. Walk far enough and you stop being able to say whether you are outdoors or indoors — you are inside something green either way.

The scale is generous but not infinite: this is a region, a valley-kingdom of terraces perhaps a few days' walk across, ringed by higher living ramparts that were clearly begun with ambition and never completed. But within it, the density is strikingly low. Whole plateaus stand empty — single structures spaced far apart, a lone spiral tower on one shelf, a long empty colonnade on another, nothing between them but breathing green and dripping air. The emptiness is not abandoned-feeling. It feels *reserved*, as though the builders intend to return. Perhaps they do. Perhaps they are returning always, slowly.

Because here is the strangeness at the center of the place: **nothing changes.** The mist rises and does not drift. The drips fall at the same intervals, metronomic, patient. The half-built channels have been half-built for — there is no way to know, but the quality of the light and the stillness suggest a very long time — and yet the pale new growth at every raw edge never advances. It is a construction site in perfect equilibrium: mid-formation, forever. The work is not abandoned and not progressing. It is *held*, the way a held breath is held. The warm air presses on everything equally. There are no seasons here, no weather in the meaningful sense, no gradients of decay or growth. The valley is a chord sustained.

Fauna is rare, and what lives here seems to belong to the architecture rather than to move through it. Pale, slow moths the size of a hand that rest on walls in the exact pattern of water stains. Something eel-like in the channels that you glimpse once and never again, and which you suspect may be part of the plumbing. In the emptiest plateaus, birdsong — but the same three notes, in the same order, whenever you hear it. You begin to suspect the birds are also grown. You begin to suspect everything here is grown, including perhaps the silence between the sounds.

And it is loud, for all that. Not noisy — resonant. The drips keep their rhythm. The wet leaves shush in a wind that exists only as sound, since nothing visibly moves. Deep in the terraces, the wood itself groans — long, low, tectonic notes, like a ship or a cathedral settling, except it never settles, it just groans and groans, the same groan, the valley clearing its throat eternally and never speaking. Water everywhere amplifies everything; the whole valley is an instrument resonating with its own unfinishedness.

The light is ordinary — a plain, warm, overcast-bright daylight, sourceless or sourced somewhere far above the mist, the kind of light in which every color is truthful and nothing casts a dramatic shadow. It gives the place an almost domestic quality, which the strangeness then quietly undermines. A kitchen garden here. A chair made of one living bough there. A staircase spiraling up into fog. A doorway that opens onto a wall of fresh weave, walled up, waiting.

If there are people — and there are signs of intention everywhere, which is not quite the same as signs of people — you never see them. But the work feels attended. Somewhere, always, it feels as though a gardener has just stepped around the corner of the last unfinished wall, and will be back in a moment to continue, and has been about to return for as long as the valley has existed.

Walk to the highest terrace, at the rim. Look back across the whole green amphitheater, mist rising from every shelf, drips keeping time, the deep groan rising from below. It is a city that was being born, and will always be being born, wet and warm and patient, holding its breath in the shape of a place where someone meant to live.
