# The Drowned Foundry

It sits in a hollow of the seafloor, and it has been sitting there so long that the sea has stopped thinking of it as an intrusion. That is the first thing to understand: this is not a wreck. Wrecks are accidents. This is a *settlement* — a town the size of a generous churchyard, walled in by its own machinery, fully submerged, fully occupied, and entirely at peace with its condition.

The water here is warm. Not sun-warmed — nothing reaches this far down — but warmed from within, by pipes, by engines, by the slow metabolic heat of ten thousand moving parts that have never fully stopped. The water carries that warmth the way a bath carries it: evenly, patiently, without variation. There are no currents strong enough to matter. There are barely currents at all. A dropped object falls in slow motion and lands where you can see it land. A hand drawn through the water leaves a seam that closes like a mouth.

The town is packed. There is no plaza, no empty lot, no breathing room between structures — the buildings lean against each other, grow out of each other, share walls and roofs and plumbing. Walkways of rusted gratting thread through the interior at three levels, then five, then seven, until you lose count. Everything is metal at its base — iron ribs, riveted plates, valves the size of dinner plates — and everything metal is furled in life. This is where the lushness lives: a garden that no one planted and no one tends. Kelp-forests of brown frond rise from every horizontal surface. Coral has claimed the smokestacks, and strange soft anemone-things have claimed the coral. Sponges push up between floor tiles like moss between cobblestones. The whole structure is a reef now, but a reef with a floor plan.

The light is dim and comes from three sources. First: the sodium-amber glow of working machinery, leaking through portholes and split seams in irregular warm patches. Second: the biological — clusters of pale lantern-creatures that have colonized the lamp housings left behind, glowing where the electricity once did, doing a passable imitation. Third: the faintest residual luminescence of the water itself, a blue-grey nothing-light that only tells you which way is down.

The sound is the strangest part. You would expect a drowned industrial town to roar, and it does — but quietly, the way a beehive roars. The engines still turn, somewhere below, driving something no one remembers the purpose of, and their sound arrives filtered through fathoms of water and coral and hull-steel as a hum. A held note. A drone with harmonics. Standing in the central gallery, you feel it in your teeth and sternum more than you hear it. Above that: the tick of cooling metal. The whisper of fronds against fronds. Once every few minutes, the deep groan of a pressure joint adjusting itself — a sound like the town clearing its throat in its sleep.

The fauna is rare but not absent, and what lives here has committed. A pale octopus the size of a cat tends the kelp on the eastern walkway — not eating it, *arranging* it, a gardener with no reason to garden. Eels sleep coiled in the boiler housings, warm as hot-water bottles. Once, in the far gallery, a shape moved that was too large and too deliberate to be a fish, and it did not come back, and the town did not seem surprised.

And the engines — this is where the strangeness thickens. The machinery is not abandoned. It is *maintained*. Valves are turned by hands, or by things like hands — eight-fingered, barnacled, patient. There is a population here, or the remnant of one, or the descendants of one, who live inside the flooded rooms the way their ancestors did, except now they swim through doorways and breathe through gills or gear or faith, it is impossible to say. They keep the pumps running. The pumps keep the water warm. The warm water keeps the garden growing. The garden keeps the fish, and the fish keep the keepers. A closed loop, in balance, refined over centuries of slow adjustment. Nothing rushes. Nothing needs to.

The whole place changes on one timescale: slow. Rust blooms and is eaten. Fronds grow a hand-width a year. The engines lose a revolution per decade and nobody minds. Somewhere, very gradually, the town is becoming more reef and less foundry, and the transition is scheduled to take another thousand years, and everyone involved seems fine with the deadline.

If you visit — drifting in through the broken loading bay, past the coral-crusted crane arm — you will find it dim, warm, humming, green, and utterly, deeply calm. A factory that the sea adopted. A garden with a heartbeat. A small town at the bottom of everything, still running its engines, still warm, still home.
