## First, the sound

You hear the Still long before you see it, and you never entirely see it, so you may as well admit from the start that you are going to know this place by ear.

There is a chord underneath everything — three low tones, a minor triad, sustained without pause. That is the Note: the engines, breathing. Above it, a percussion of falling water in the millions, each droplet striking stone or ice or shell or skin, but not random — the drips are tuned, sequenced, arranged into slow patterns that repeat over hours. Above that, life: shimmering veils of small sound, the boom of channel-worms underfoot like distant foghorns, the soft ring of water-jars being emptied, human speech in a whistled register that carries for a mile. And under all of it, felt in the teeth more than heard, the mountain itself — a sub-audible groan of rock still settling into its own weight, still rising, still young.

A visitor's first instinct is to call this noise. Stay a day and you understand it is architecture. Stay a year and you understand it is law. The only sin in the high country is to be the one quiet thing.

But I am ahead of the terrain.

## The Lid

There is no sky here, or rather the sky is a ceiling. A cloud deck sits over the whole summit complex, permanent, faintly moving, and above it — nothing anyone has verified. No sun has been seen in living memory. No moon. Light arrives only as leakage: cold lightning crawling inside the cloud-sea far below the summits, a dull red glow from the engine-vents, and the *gleed* — luminous films of bacteria that paint the watercourses in pale blue-green lines, tracing every channel and weep like veins on the back of a hand. The gleed is enough to silhouette a tower, a beast, a person walking. It is never enough to read by. The people of the heights have milk-pale eyes, vestigial, and ears that move like a deer's.

Water comes down constantly. Not rain — rain would be weather, an event. Here it is condensation, the air itself wringing out against every cold surface, which is every surface. The whole mountain weeps. Cliffs seep. Ice grows hair-fine filaments overnight. Every stone wears a skin of moisture, and your breath fog is indistinguishable from the mountain's. The locals call the condensation simply the Weep, and the cloud deck the Lid, and they speak of both the way farmers speak of seasons — except their seasons turn over decades.

## The Yearling

The massif is called the Yearling, though it is ten thousand years old, because ten thousand years is young. The geology is raw, embryonic, still deciding what it will be. Arêtes stand knife-edged, unrounded by any weather worth the name. Talus fields are fields of blades. Frost-quakes come every few weeks — a crack like a struck anvil somewhere in the dark, then a long rumble — and each one re-arranges a scarp, opens a new spring, seals an old one. The tuners keep maps of the hydrology, and the maps are perpetually half-obsolete; every generation redraws the delta of the springs.

There is no soil. Nothing has ever rotted here to make soil. Which brings us to the great absence:

**Nothing grows.** No moss, no lichen, no grass, no tree, not even a cushion plant hanging on in a lee. Too cold, too dark, too young, and no substrate but bare rock. And yet the plateau is crowded — the food web here simply starts a kingdom lower. Bacterial films oxidize the minerals in the weeping rock: iron, sulfur, manganese, whatever the young mountain bleeds. Fungal webs run through the fractures like root systems that never bothered becoming a plant. The sulfur films smell faintly of struck matches; the iron films stain the runoff the color of old wine. Where the films are thick, they chemically glow, and that is the gleed, and that is the light of the world.

So: no plants, and no famine. A different kitchen.

## The Still

The city takes its name from what it does. It is a still in the old sense — an apparatus for distillation — because what the Still refines, from the mountain's slow warmth and the sky's cold breath, is *heat*.

Here is the economy, plainly. The Yearling is warm-hearted; young rock still holds its heat, and the mountain's interior slowly melts the ice fields above and the snowpack in its fractures. The city catches that water, then spends it: the engine-halls freeze cisterns of it, and freezing releases heat, and that harvested warmth runs through the terraces, warms the nurseries, drives the workshops, keeps the whole shoulder of the mountain habitable at altitudes where exposed metal snaps like sugar glass. The waste cold is vented upward into rime gardens, where it grows sculpture. The mountain recharges the ice; the city harvests the difference. Current physics can describe most of this. What it cannot fully describe is the freezing itself — the tuners' acoustic nucleation, ice induced to crystallize by sung frequencies with an efficiency no lab on the warm lowlands has ever reproduced, water persuaded to let go of its heat in ordered, musical pulses. That persuasion is the Note.

The city that does this is built the way a stalactite grows, because that is essentially what it is. There is little carved stone here; there is *accreted* stone — mineral-rich water piped along scaffold lines, dripping for a century onto a timber armature until the armature becomes a tower of flowstone, still growing, still wet, glistening under the gleed. Nothing is finished. Nothing is supposed to be. The founding virtue of the Still is patience with unfinished things; a completed wall is a dead wall.

The urbanism is hydrology. Every street is a rill, every plaza a settling basin, the whole map a delta drawn upslope. The districts are named for their order in the delta — Firstfork, the Hundred Rills, the Cistern Quarter, the Weir. Belljars hang from every eave: domesticated dome-animals that chill their own shells and condense water out of the air, dripping it into the gutters, pinging softly when full so the keepers come with buckets. The water-sellers in the market describe vintages — *the Third Rill, this decade: flinty, a long cold finish* — because every channel tastes of the rock it drains, and the rock is always changing.

The people themselves: broad-eared, whistling half their speech, everyone wearing something that sounds — bone tiles in the cloaks, rings on the staffs, bells on the harness. Not fashion. Armor. But we will get to why.

## What eats what

The density of life here is the thing that undoes visitors who expected a dead ice world. On any square stride of wall, three kingdoms of something is moving. The mist is never empty: sifters strain it in veils of thousands, tiny gliding droplet-eaters whose combined wing-noise is the high shimmer in the city's chord, and when a swarm passes your face it feels like a cold comb drawn across the skin. Knellworms pleat through the channels, keeping them clear, their segmented bodies resonating as they go so that the drains of the Cistern Quarter hum in slow chorus. The cisterns themselves are rimmed with pale scavenger-shrimp picking the films. And out on the summit plateau, the rime-oxen — herds in the thousands, big slow grazers with rasping tongues that lick mineral film off the flowstone, hooves of layered ice-talon that never fully thaw, breath steaming from warm bodies so that a herd at rest is a stand of walking hearths. The herd-folk sleep against their flanks in winter and are never once cold. The herds will not go down into the Steams below the cloud line. No one asks them why twice.

Predators exist. The one that matters is the hush.

A hush is not large. What a hush is, is *silent* — not merely quiet but silencing, its body an organ that damps vibration, drinks echo. It hunts by locating the loudest isolated thing in the soundscape and moving toward the hole its own presence creates. And this is the reason for everything: the tuned drip-streets, the bells on the children, the sustained engine chord, the whistled talk. The loudness of the Still is armor. Noise everywhere means no one stands out. Solitary silence is the one obscenity, the one summons. Parents teach it as arithmetic: *a quiet thing alone is a thing being located.*

There are places where sound dies entirely — the Silence Pools, ponds under overhanging flowstone where the hushes gather and the water lies flat as poured metal. The keepers leave them offerings and do not explain. This is the extent of the strangeness the high country permits itself: everything else, however unfamiliar, is legible as engineering, husbandry, weather. The hushes are simply the price of the quiet the world would otherwise impose.

## Slow things

Finally, the rules, which are all versions of one rule: *everything here is long.*

The Weep has a tide. The Lid rises and falls over years — a high-tide decade swallows the lowest terrace, the Lowling, in mist for months at a stretch, and the city plans around it the way port cities plan around moons. The towers accrete a hand's width in a lifetime. The mountain rises a finger's width a year and groans about it nightly. Water is believed to remember: channels that have carried flow for centuries run more willingly, and the tuners "teach" new channels by singing old routes into them, which the skeptics attribute to micro-grooves cut by ancient ice and the devout attribute to nothing at all. Both are probably right.

The great clock is not a mechanism but a single drip — the Long Drip, falling from a weeping rafter in the founding hall once every few minutes, counted by an order of near-blind archivists who have not let the count lapse in nine hundred years. Their calendar's largest unit is not a year. It is the temperature of the deep rock. The heat-ledgers are kept to the fourth decimal, and they all bend the same direction, gently, irreversibly, down. The Yearling is cooling. In four or five thousand years the wells will run tepid, then cold, and the Still will have spent its mountain. No one hides this. Children learn the Cooling the way other children learn that rivers run downhill. The city's answer is not denial but craft: build slower, teach the water better, waste less, and perhaps the descendants will find a second mountain, or a way to make ice give back more than it takes.

Once in a while — eleven years, or seven, or nineteen; the archivists argue — the Lid tears open for the length of an hour, and one star is visible. The whole region stops. The engines modulate the Note down to a murmur so it can be heard through the feet, the herds stand still on the plateau, the sifters settle, and for one hour the high country does the most difficult thing it knows: it is silent, all together, on purpose. The hushes come up out of the pools on those nights and stand among the people, and no one moves, and nothing is eaten.

Then the Lid closes, the Note swells back to full voice, the drips resume their centuries-long music, and the Yearling goes on rising, a hand's width per lifetime, patient as water finding its way down through a mountain that is still deciding to be a mountain — with a city clinging to its shoulder, ringing like a bell that has been sounding for eight hundred years and intends, carefully, deliberately, to sound for ten thousand more.
