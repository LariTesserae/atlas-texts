# The Pan on the Last Mountain

The plain comes first, and the plain is what a mountain range looks like after it has finished apologizing. Every ridge here has been worn down to a suggestion; the land is a long, hot, bone-colored table where the wind goes about its rounds with nothing left to say. You can walk all day without gaining the width of your hand in elevation. The stone underfoot is old in a way that isn't a number anymore — old the way a stone is old when its wrinkles have relaxed, when every joint has been rounded into flattery, when the ground has settled into the last shape it will ever bother to hold. Heat stands on it in visible columns. The dry washes that score the plain run toward nothing and have not run in living memory.

Then the dome.

It is the last piece of the old range that refused to lie down — a whaleback of pale quartz rising a half-day's climb above the tableland, rounded and smooth as something the sea made, though no sea has come near it in an age longer than any word for age. As you climb, the air changes before the view does: thinner, brighter, cleaner, the blue of the sky deepening by degrees, the plain below beginning to haze and shimmer. The dome's flanks are glassy with wind-polish, feldspar bleached to the color of old teeth, and lichen spreads across it in slow spills — olive, rust, a black so black it reads as holes in the rock. Lichen is the only continuous thing here. Everything else that lives on the dome has claimed a place and kept to it.

Near the crown, you hear the place before you see it.

That is the first strange thing, and it is a constant one. From the summit flat — a wide, featureless, sun-blasted pavement — there comes a sound like a market heard from the sea: layered, chattering, dripping, thrumming, coming from everywhere and nowhere. Nothing on the flat explains it. No gorge, no trees, no structure. The crown is bare stone and sky. Then, among a ring of leaning slabs, the ground simply opens, and you are at the lip of the pan.

It is small. That is the second thing, and stranger than the sound. The bowl is perhaps three hundred paces across, a stone basin sunk into the summit, shallow-walled, with the lean slabs standing along its rim like the remnant teeth of something that once laughed. From anywhere inside, everything is visible. A single glance takes in the whole world of the place: every pool, every plant, every animal, every visitor's approach. Nothing here can hide from anything else, and nothing seems to want to. The basin's inner walls are polished to a sheen, and the polish is not accidental — the smooth stone throws sound inward and around, so that the pan is a sounding dish, an amphitheater with no seats, and the loudness you heard from outside is the loudness of a contained world hearing itself.

The water comes from a seam.

On the northern inner wall, a hairline crack in the rock weeps. It does not weep steadily. Twice a day — once at first light, once at last — the seam swells, and a thread of water crosses a channel toward the basin floor. The channel is the first sign that this place has been tended: it is lined with a dark, glassy material, straight-edged and slick, too even to be geology, worn too smooth to be recent. The water runs it without losing a drop. Below, the channel hands the pulse to a stair of potholes — a dozen stone cups, each polished like a mortal thing, each brimming and spilling into the next — and the overflow steps down through them, cup by cup, in a small descending bell-sound, until it reaches the deep pool beneath a single ancient fig tree whose roots grip the bowl wall like fingers.

Around this, the whole economy of the place turns on condensation. Every dawn, warm air rises up the dome's flanks and cools at the summit, and a fog forms — thin, quick, and certain — that the rim slabs catch and hold. Droplets gather, join, run inward along grooves worn into the stone. Some of the grooves are natural. Some are not. And standing along the rim in scattered places are threads — filaments finer than hair, taller than a man, glass-bright, swaying without weight. At dawn they go to work. Dew beads along them in ordered ranks and sweeps downward, bead by bead, in lines too orderly to be anything but tended, each droplet delivered to the rim's groove and funneled down into the bowl. Nobody has ever seen the threads do anything at noon. At night, under the stars, you can watch them still moving: the rim dotted with beads sliding in the same direction, like an abacus being counted by no one.

Perhaps every third warm afternoon, a cloud stalls against the dome — cannot cross it, piles up, darkens, and breaks — and ten minutes of hard vertical rain turns the pan into a roar. The potholes brim. Water sheets across the polished floor and finds the grooves, and the grooves find the pools, and the whole bowl drinks standing up. Then the sun returns, steam lifts off the stone, and within an hour the surface is dry again while the moss holds the memory.

The vegetation is lush the way a court is lush: few in number, magnificent, and widely spaced.

Each plant is a landlord. The dew-columns stand alone — tall succulent trunks crowned in fans of fronds, each rooted in its own smooth funnel-basin that gathers every drop in its neighborhood and delivers it to the roots. Their shaded sides are always wet. At noon they drip, punctually, like eaves. Moss maps the fog's habits: gray all afternoon, it flushes green within a single hour of mist arriving, a tide of color crossing bare stone. Orchids crowd the seams and cracks, impossibly wet-throated for this altitude, this heat, this dryness. The fig is the oldest and largest single being — its crown shades the deep pool, its aerial roots hang and drip, and its fruit falls into the water and feeds everything that waits there.

Between the landlords: nothing. Bare, warm, painted stone. The lushness lives in islands, and the space between is honest emptiness, which is why the place feels the way it does — like a room with three magnificent pieces of furniture and polished floors.

And the fauna. The fauna is why the pan is loud.

Because there is no other water — none, not a seep, not a wash, not a shadowed crack — for many hours of dry walking in every direction, everything comes. And everything has learned the order.

Insects never leave; each pothole has its own resident cloud, each cloud its own signature drone. The frogs live sealed in the pothole mud through the driest weeks and surface, blinking, the moment the seam swells — their chorus, doubled and redoubled by the bowl's acoustics, is the pan's principal instrument. The birds come at first light in a queue that is not a metaphor: seed-eaters first in shifting flocks, then the iridescent drinkers that work the fig's blossoms, then the stilt-legged wader that fishes larvae from the second pothole with a patience that seems professional. The climbers descend the bowl wall mid-morning — antelope-legged, sure-hoofed, their hooves clicking on glassy stone in sounds that echo cleanly from the far side — and they wait, lined along the wall, until the birds give way. Small carnivorans come after, at the slack hour. A lizard patrols the leaves and harvests droplets through channels in its own skin.

Nothing enforces this order that anyone has seen. But along the rim, the hair-fine threads hum — a tone at the edge of hearing, beneath the birdsong, beneath the frogs — and the tone changes, subtly, when each kind of visitor crosses it. It is a fence made of sound. The pan is loud partly because it is a dish, and partly because it is a liturgy, and partly because a wall no one can see keeps singing its one note.

There is a gray thing that comes at the empty hour, when the queue has thinned. It drinks without a ripple and leaves without a sound, and the threads do not hum when it passes. No one has named it. The fence has no opinion either, which may be the same thing as reverence.

The built remains are few and faint, and they are old the way the plain is old. A flight of steps climbs the inner wall, worn to slippers of smooth stone — worn by whom, no one living can say. A single curved fragment of wall stands near the rim, porcelain-bright over some darker core, etched with lines that still move, very slowly: a display gone mostly dim, that wakes occasionally to show a glyph no script resembles, and a number that changes constantly and never arrives anywhere. A half-buried lens tracks the sun all day, faithfully, a point of focused warmth crawling across the stone — and once each season, when its bright dot crosses a particular patch of moss, a single white flower opens on that exact spot for one afternoon. On that day every pollinator in the district knows, and the pan's loudness stops being describable.

The strangest fact is the quietest one: the rim does not erode. Five hundred storms a season, and the slabs stand true, the grooves run true, the potholes hold their polish. Everything else on this dome has been surrendered to time; inside the pocket, time is being kept — like accounts, like a garden. Somewhere in the last few thousand years the maintenance became indistinguishable from nature, and now no one could say where the garden ends and the wilderness begins, including, perhaps, the garden.

The water, when you are offered it — and the queue includes you, if you wait — tastes of lightning and stone. The echo here returns your footsteps a half-beat late and slightly changed, as if the bowl considered them before giving them back. The pools stay blood-warm through the night. The fig's shade is the only coolness on the mountain.

From the plain below, at evening, the dome is nothing: a bump on the end of the world, silent, its bowl invisible, its whole roaring congregation folded into a few hundred paces of stone. The fog will form again at dawn. The seam will pulse. The threads will sweep their beads. And somewhere by the rim, the slow number on the broken wall will go on adding what it counts to itself — a ledger no one has come to settle in a very long time, in a place that has decided to go on anyway.
