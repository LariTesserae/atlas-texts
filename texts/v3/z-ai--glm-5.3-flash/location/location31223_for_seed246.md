# The Choir

The plain comes first, and it comes on without argument. This is old ground — ground that has been dying for so long that dying has become its character. Somewhere far back in time there were mountains here, and the rain and the frost and the wind took them apart grain by grain, patient as water wearing a spoon, until what remains is a floor: gravel and bare stone and thin turf, stretching to a horizon that sits low in every direction like the rim of a shallow dish. In places you can still read the ghosts — long shadows of ridge and valley pressed flat into the land, the sleepers' outlines of a range that finished its business ten thousand centuries ago. Nothing on the plain is taller than a boulder. Nothing has been taller than a boulder since before anything now living was born.

And yet you can walk out of sight of everything and still not be lost, because of the sound.

You hear it before you see anything. At first it seems like weather — a chord with no orchestra behind it, thin as breath on glass, arriving from nowhere, steady, unblinking. The old traders navigate by it. There is no other landmark for two days' walk in any direction, so they walk toward the note the way sailors once walked toward bells, and on the second or third day the source lifts over the horizon's curve: a knuckle of quartzite, a few hundred feet of stubbornness, the last argument the range ever made. Everything around it surrendered. This did not.

The climb is not dramatic. Nothing here is dramatic anymore; the slopes have softened into long whaleback humps, and the talus at the base is fixed — every fallen stone lying exactly where it came to rest an unreckonable time ago, mossed over, lichened, settled. No rock has rolled on this mountain in longer than memory can pretend to reach. You walk up through air that grows colder and thinner with each step, your breath hanging a moment behind you like a small ghost, and then, near the top, you pass into the sound the way you'd wade into a river. It rises from a murmur to a pressure. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in your teeth.

The summit is small. A few acres — you could cross it at a stroll in a quarter of an hour — rimmed with rounded tors that were worn smooth before the first wall was laid. Most of the top is bare: polished stone and pale gravel, wide open space, emptiness with a horizon in it. Three small tarns sit in natural hollows, dark windows of water that hold the entire sky and no fish. On the coldest nights they freeze and boom and crack like distant artillery. Apart from that: the city.

It is a hamlet, really — thirty cells, some kept, some roofless and retired, some half-swallowed by their own repairs — gathered in the lee of the tallest tor. But it was built the way a town is built, not the way a camp is: lanes, plazas, a low round tower at the center corbelled inward course by course, walls stepping up through visible ages. The dark weathered stone below is older than anyone's oldest song; the pale stone above is young, a century or two at most. There is no mortar anywhere. There are no tool marks anywhere. No metal has ever been carried up this height. Every stone was found, weighed in two hands, turned, tested, and given its place — fitted by patience alone, packed with moss where moss would serve, shimmed with pebbles where it wouldn't. It is construction the way a bird builds: entirely by inheritance of the eye.

And the joints are not silent accidents. Look closely at the walls — at the little beads of grit set into the gaps at regular intervals, like punctuation, like frets — and you understand what this place is. The lanes run narrow on purpose, oriented to the prevailing gales, so the wind accelerates between the walls the way breath accelerates between lips. Each wall has been tuned. Each cell has a chord. The whole hamlet is an instrument, and the wind, which almost never fully stops here, plays it ceaselessly: drones in the long walls, flutes in the high gaps, a hum in the hollow of the tower, all braided together into the sound you heard from two days out on the plain. The builders were not people who built a town and found that it sang. They were people who built a song and agreed to live inside it.

The water that keeps everything alive here comes down out of the sky twice a day, sideways. At dusk the fog climbs the flanks of the old mountain — every dusk, without negotiation — and swallows the summit whole. All night the mist combs through the walls, and everything beards with rime: hoarfrost on the tor crowns, white fur on the lintels, ice needles standing up out of the gravel, the tarns skinned over. Then dawn comes, plain and hard and ordinary, the sun doing exactly what the sun does, and the melt begins. Dark wet streaks bleed down the windward stone. The moss drinks. And in the first hour after sunrise there is a lull — the night wind lies down, as it always does, as if by appointment — and for a few minutes the loudest thing in the world is dripping. Hundreds of small percussions, the night's beard coming off the walls, each drop ticking on the stones at its own tempo. The keepers call this the drip-hour. It is the only quiet the summit ever gets, and they treat it like a meal.

The vegetation is lush the way an oasis is lush: ferociously, and in pockets. On the lee side of every wall the moss goes fat and deep, dark green, soft as a mattress, wet even when it's dry. Cushion-plants hump up against the stones, and small ferns crowd the door-shadows where frost never quite reaches. Then ten paces of bare polished rock, nothing at all, and another green pocket. The lichens keep the deep time: rings of yellow-green map-lichen on the oldest stones, spreading a millimeter in a human lifetime, so that some of the circles on the tor above the town are older than the town itself. The keepers garden in the walls' wind-shadows and eat what the rime waters, and they are thin and unhurried, and there are perhaps a dozen of them.
