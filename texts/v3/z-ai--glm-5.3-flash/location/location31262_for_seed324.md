## The Weeping Deck

You reach it the way you reach anything up here: by steel. A stair wound inside one of the tower's hollow legs, then a cage ladder, then forty rungs of vertical shaft with the roar growing in the rails beneath your gloves — felt first, a tremor, then a hum, then a sound. At the top there is a hatch with a wheel-handle and a stenciled warning: **CONDENSER GALLERY 3 — HEARING PROTECTION BEYOND THIS POINT.** You turn the wheel. The hinge gives. And the sound does not enter the room. It is the room.

It takes the ears a long time to admit this. The roar is not coming from anywhere. It has no direction, no edges. It is a medium, like the air itself, and you are submerged in it. Twenty minutes in, your hearing gives up trying to parse it and simply folds: fan-blade chord underneath, plenum hiss over that, the water somewhere inside it all, your own blood joining the last band. After that, the gallery is in a strange way silent. No small sound can exist here. Nothing in this room has been audible since the day it opened, eleven weeks ago.

The gallery is small. Twelve paces long, four wide, ceiling just above your head. It is a pocket stitched into the upper works of the plant — one room among many, the newest deck, still on its shake-down runs. The floor is open grating; through it, a shallow concrete channel runs the length of the room, and in the channel the water slides fast and glassy toward a black sump at the far end. One wall is the plenum: a blank face of louvered slats, trembling, with the fans behind it. The other wall is the condenser: ranks of upright fins, chilled, silver-grey, and *weeping* — there is no better word, and the crews use it, which is how the deck got its name. Between the two walls there is barely room for a person, a walkway of two strides with the roar on one side and the cold on the other.

Everything is wet because everything is supposed to be. This is a room for wringing cloud. The fans haul outside air through the louvers by the ton, the air is pressed against the chilled fins, and the water lets go of it — beads, swells, lets go — and runs. The channels underfoot carry it off. The sump gathers it. Every few minutes the pump beneath the sump fires: you never hear it start, you feel it, a deep knock coming up through the floorplates into your shins, and then the black water pulls down into an eddy and the discharge pipe in the wall thrums like a plucked string, sending the harvest down the mountain. Condensed water is nearly distilled water. The sky does the cleaning; the plant does the lifting; the country below does the drinking.

Because that is why any of this exists. The ridge combs the sea's weather into cloud most days of the year, and the lowlands are dry, and somebody did the arithmetic. The whole station went up in a single short season — haul roads, helicopters, a town of temporary huts lower down — and this gallery is the newest room in the newest building on a young mountain. You can feel the newness everywhere, because eleven weeks is nothing. The concrete still carries the ghost-prints of its formwork, snap-tie holes plugged with bright plastic caps. The paint smells faintly when the lamps warm it. Hazard striping unscuffed, decals crisp, handrails unworn by any hand. The only erosion in the room is the first erosion: pale mineral tears beginning below each bolt, weeks old, faint as breath on glass. One freckle of rust at a hinge — days old. The water has only just started its long work here, and it is working at the speed of decades, patient, and the room is going to be old someday, but not yet.

The cold is the condenser's, and it is a working cold, around eight degrees, radiating off the fins in a wall you feel with your whole near side. Your breath fogs faintly when you lean toward the plates. The water in the channel is colder than that — four, five degrees, snowmelt temperature, though it has never been snow. Your hands stiffen on the rails within minutes. And the air is at the very edge of what it can hold, so anything colder than the dew point receives a skin of water instantly, including you. Droplets gather on your sleeves, run off your chin. A notebook goes limp; ink blooms. At knee height a layer of mist drifts the length of the deck, stirred by the drafts, slow as smoke, and in it the lamplight becomes visible — amber caged bulbs every five paces or so, few and dim, each one wearing a small pale crown of haze.
