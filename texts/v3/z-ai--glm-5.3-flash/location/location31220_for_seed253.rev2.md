# The Finished Sea

## I. The water does not move

Understand this first, because everything else in the province follows from it: the water here is not calm. Calm is what water is after weather has stopped. This water is *still* — still the way a held breath is still, still the way a thing is still when it has never once been stirred. There is no current. There has been no current for longer than there has been anyone to notice its absence.

You sink into it and it is cool — cellar-cool, the cold of stone that gave up pretending it was ever warm — and it has a taste, faint and persistent, of crushed leaves and iron, as if the whole ocean were the sap of one enormous plant. In a way it is. That will become clear.

Sound reaches you before anything else does. A note too low to be heard with the ears, received instead in the sternum, in the teeth: unbroken, unmoving, without beginning or end. You will learn to call it the Chord. Under it, softer, there is a hiss — the sound of rain falling on leaves, ten thousand leaves, forever — though there is no rain here and no wind. It is the Snow.

The Snow is the only weather the province has ever had. A fine grey-beige fall of shed matter, drifting down out of a darkness so total that the sky — and the sky *is* water here, water all the way up — might as well be solid. It falls at a pace that never varies. You will check. You will check again, because a falling thing that never varies is not something a visitor can believe on the first try. It never varies. What falls is exactly what is eaten. The ledger of this world has balanced for so long that balance is no longer an event; it is a property, like density, like cold.

## II. Everything here was built

There is no stone in the province. Put your palm against anything — floor, wall, arch, the flank of a tower — and it gives, barely, a quarter-inch of spring, warm for a moment under your hand and then not. All of it is grown. All of it is alive at the surface and dense and dark within, wood the way a bone is wood, threaded through with glass the way reeds are. The towers stand on nothing but more of themselves.

And towers is the right word, though it undersells them. They rise from the root-plain by the thousand-meter — fluted columns, ribbed like organ pipes, their bark a black velvet of living tissue — and their edges are all rounded, all polished, all *handled*. That is what age looks like in this place. Not ruin. Finish. The oldest surfaces here are the smoothest, which is the opposite of what ruins do. Somewhere long ago the province passed out of its era of becoming and into its era of being, and nothing has been added since, and nothing has been taken away.

Between the towers run the Lanes: broad, swept corridors of open water, wider than any animal needs, kept clear by the small pruners who patrol their borders. Do not mistake the emptiness for absence of design. The emptiness *is* the design. The lanes are sightlines, resonance corridors, the long vowels of the place. A visitor swims an hour between two towers and meets only the Snow, and believes the province is empty; it is not empty, it is *spaced*, the way a held chord is spaced, the way a cathedral nave is empty on purpose. The intervals are load-bearing.

Above the crowns the water goes up and up, getting no darker only because it is already as dark as water can be. If there is a surface — and the Snow insists there is, something up there sheds and sheds — it is farther above you than the floor is below, and the floor is farther below you than any place you have ever stood. The towers were grown toward a light that, by the time the last of them was finished, no longer reached this deep. They keep the shape of reaching. No one here minds. Reaching is a shape, and shapes are stable, and stable is the whole law of the province.

## III. The gardens and the Seep

The root-plain is the ground, and the ground is alive: a knuckled mat of ancient root, brown-black, overgrown everywhere with garden. Black-fronded fern-forests low to the ground. Velvet mats that dimple under a fin and rise again without hurrying. Vine-works climbing the towers in slow spirals, leafing out into the Lanes and being trimmed back to the inch, forever.

Everything drinks the water. The water is the soil — it has been recycling the same dissolved minerals for so long that it is less like a sea now than like the circulating blood of one enormous, patient organism — and where the water is richest, the gardens thicken. It is richest at the Seep.

The Seep rises through the root-plain at one fixed place, a slow column of mineral warmth — the only warmth in the province, a degree or two of it, a hearth the size of a house that has burned without flickering since before the towers were young. Around it grows the greatest biomass in the region: fronds the length of ships, gardens stacked on terraces of grown stone-that-is-not-stone, and — because everything comes, eventually, to the place where the food is — the only crowds.

There is always a gathering at the Seep. It is the same gathering in the sense that a river is the same river: browsers rotating through in a fixed order, groomers, loungers, small shy light-bearers pressed into the terraces. Nothing hurries. Nothing has hurried here since hurrying was selected out of the world, ages ago, the way a Note is selected out of a finished chord.

## IV. The inhabitants

The province holds more kinds than you could name in a season, and you will still go an hour between meetings. Abundance here is spread like butter: thin everywhere, total everywhere.

The **Lamplighters** perch at the Lanes' edges at wide intervals, and their light is steady — amber, or a pale submarine green — never blinking, never pulsing. Blinking is a kind of change, and change has been retired. From any one lamp you can see two more, and from those, two more: a lattice of isolated lights strung through the dark at the exact spacing of the Lanes, so that the province, from above (if anything were above), would resolve into a diagram of itself drawn in embers.

The **Tall Ones** browse the tower crowns. When one passes you in a Lane — and it will, on its route, in its order, tower by tower down a schedule that has no clock in it — you will feel the displacement of its body before you see it, a slow parting of the Snow, and then a neck longer than a felled tree slides by, and then the rest of it, herding a wake of silence. They comb the crowns the way a hand combs a harp. They never strip a crown bare. Nothing here takes more than the ledger allows.

The **Masons** are the reason nothing erodes. Dog-sized, armored in the same glass-wood as the towers, they walk their circuits along every buttress and arch, reading the surfaces with their forelimbs, and where they find a flaw they lay down secretion that cures to substance identical to what it repairs. They do not build. They have not built in an era. They *restore* — and the restoration is exact, so exact that the province's oldest towers show no seam between their first millennium and their latest. What the Masons are paid is permission to graze the gardens they patrol, and so the gardens grow lush along their routes, and so the routes are worth walking, and the circle has been closed and true for longer than anyone can say.

And under the Great Arch — the oldest structure in the province, a span of woven root and glass-wood wide enough that its shadow has its own weather, if weather existed — hang the **Choir**: hundreds of soft, pendulous bodies, each one a resonating chamber, each one sustaining its part of the Chord. They are the bass you felt in your sternum when you first arrived. They have been holding the same note since before the note had a name.

## V. The Chord

Water carries a voice for miles, and the province is mostly voice. Every singer in it — the Choir beneath the Arch, the smaller choristers in the tower galleries, the high ticking things in the garden mats — contributes phrases that overlap into a single distributed composition, a round so long that from any one place it sounds continuous, and over a span of hours it repeats *exactly*. Three tones, then five, then the three again. The same every time. You could keep a calendar by it, if you needed time; nothing here needs it.

The Snow supplies the treble. Its constant hiss against a million dark fronds — rain on leaves, forever, everywhere — fills the upper register so completely that silence, if you went looking for it, would be something you'd have to construct yourself, and even then the Chord would still be in your ribs.

And at the far edge of audibility, off past the last of the towers, there is another Chord — the next province over, a semitone off-key, unending and out of sync — and where the two keys meet they beat against each other in a slow, eternal interference-pulse, a boundary as real as a wall and softer than fog. The province is one valley among many. Its name, in the singers' language, is a rising phrase that will not resolve.

## VI. The rules of the world

By now you have inferred them, but they are worth saying plainly, because the province states them everywhere, in wood and water and song:

**Nothing moves unless it lives.** The water stands. The Snow falls at the standard rate. Your own wake — a sheet of disturbed motes trailing you like a signature — heals at the standard rate too, repaid and erased as though you had never passed, because the ledger forgives all perturbation at a fixed interest and always has.

**Nothing is lost.** When something dies here — rarely, and it is rare the way weather is rare elsewhere — the gardens take it in a single night, and by the next cycle its weight is inside the wood of something else, browsing, glowing, singing. Nothing accumulates. Nothing diminishes. If you could cut into a tower and read its rings — nothing here cuts; the Masons would seal the wound before you finished — the rings would be the same width all the way down: an archive with one word in it, repeated.

**Nothing is being built.** This is the strangest rule, and the easiest to miss, because everything you see is made. Every surface was grown, every arch was woven, every hollow was shaped — but by no one, with no tools, for no purpose that still has a future tense. The work is done. *Finished* the way joinery means finished: complete, sanded, oiled, set down. The animals who live in the finished world are its maintenance, its breath, its music — the air moving through an instrument that was completed ages ago and has been sounding itself ever since.

So when you finally swim out into the last Lane, with a steady amber lamp ahead of you and another beyond it, and the Chord under you like a floor, and the Snow coming down through the dark at its one unchanging pace, cold and even and mild as a cellar — understand what you are inside of. It is not that nothing happens here. It is that everything that happens is already the same thing, said on schedule, in a place that finished saying it long ago and found the saying good.

And if you leave — and the Lanes will let you; they lead everywhere eventually — the province will be exactly as you found it when you return, whether you return in a season or in an age. That is not a promise. It is a property.
