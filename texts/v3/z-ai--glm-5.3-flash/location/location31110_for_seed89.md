# The Warm Room

The mountain is one of those that stand above weather. Its summit rock has never felt rain—only wind polished to a fine, dry hiss, and cold, and the blue-black of a sky with too little air in it. From the glacier country below, the crown of the peak looks like a fist of black stone closed against the light. Cloud sea breaks against its shoulders. The ledges up there hold nothing but crusts of lichen the color of old bruises, and the wind has been working on them, patiently, for longer than anything has been counting.

And yet, two hundred paces below the very summit, behind a leaning flake of stone, there is a hole that breathes warm air.

It is the only frost-free opening on the whole upper mountain. Around its lip the rime melts back into a dark halo, and in that halo—impossibly, at this altitude—there is a smudge of green. That green is the doorway's signature. Slide in past the leaning flake, follow the warmth upward-and-inward as the flue bends twice like a swallowed swallow's wing, and the cold world closes behind you in stages: the wind's hiss thinning, then gone; the ice-sound gone; the light gone last, filtering down to a gray memory and then to nothing.

What opens at the end of the crawl is a room.

Not a cavern in the way of deep caves—a pocket, a pocket organ's largest pipe, forty paces long and half that across, arched high enough that a standing man's raised hand would just brush the lowest overhang. Its walls close on every side. Its only sky is the flue: a narrow throat rising from the ceiling's highest point, twisting, so that no direct beam ever enters. The whole world of this place is bounded, walkable in a few hundred steps—and it sits at the roof of the world, inside the crown of a peak that pierces the cloud layer. A warm dark room at the top of the cold sky.

The rock is black and dense, a stone that drinks heat and gives it slowly. Somewhere far below, the mountain's own deep warmth rises through fissures in the floor, and the floor slabs are blood-warm to the touch. Warm air climbs the shaft all day and night in a slow tidal breath. High up, where the breath meets stone chilled by the thin outside air, it can't hold its moisture anymore, and it weeps.

So the walls sweat. Every surface beads. Condensation is the weather here—democratic, everywhere, fine as breath on glass—and every bead finds a route downward. Over uncountable ages those routes have been carved. The walls are fluted with drip-channels like plumb lines, each groove the signature of one thread of water, each groove finished—this place has no rough edges left, no work in progress. The erosion here is not happening; it has happened. The mountain and its water arrived at their agreement a very long time ago, and now they simply keep it.

The floor is a field of worn stone cups.

Dozens of basins, each perfectly round-lipped, each polished glass-smooth inside, each deepened by a single drip arriving at the same point a million times a year. A thumb's depth takes a century. They are tuned differently—this one drips every four breaths, that one twice a minute, another holds a long silence and then releases two drops close together—so the room is never silent. It is an instrument with forty voices, playing a score no one wrote and no one could finish reading.

Where a basin overfills, its spill runs out as a thread no thicker than a hair, stitching itself to the next basin down, and the next. Water moves through this room the way blood moves through a body: drop by drop, routed, unhurried, all of it accounted for. On the rare nights when outside weather presses down hard enough to force wet air through the throat, true rain falls inside the mountain—a curtain of it, loud as applause—and the threads all swell at once, and the basins brim, and brief falls cascade from cup to cup like white stairs.

That is the only fast thing here. Drops are fast. Everything else is patient.

Vegetation in the room is thin overall and dense where it can be. Under the strongest drip-lines, moss grows in cushions deep enough to lose a hand in, fat and dark and ruthlessly green against the black stone. Two ferns cling in the one corner that receives the throat's faint gray breath, and they are the only plants that ever unfurl anything you'd call a leaf. Elsewhere: fungus, pale and small, in clusters like spent matches, some of them carrying a low cold glow, a foxfire gleam just strong enough to make adapted eyes uncertain whether they're seeing or remembering. Outside, on the sunless ledges where the lichen lives, there are a few moss gardens too—but the green's whole kingdom, inside and out, would fit in a barn.

The barn is full, though. The room is full. That is the thing about this place: it is small, and it is crowded, and the crowding is layered, tier on tier, from floor to ceiling.

The floor belongs to the beetles—thousands of them, graveling the warm slabs, processing everything that falls: molted fur, husks, droppings, the fine constant snowfall of a crowded world. Above them the folk.

The folk are fist-sized, long-nosed, quick-pawed, furred in a soot-brown that matches the stone, and there are some hundreds of them in the room. Their eyes are small and nearly idle; eyes are no use here, and the folk know it the way a fish knows water. They read the world through sound, heat, and nose. They sleep in piles on the warmest slabs, and they wake, and they sit very still and listen, and what they hear is everything: which basin is full, which drip has changed its rhythm this season, where the moth-shoals are riding the updraft, who is crying from the nursery hollows, whether the draft's note is rising toward rain.

They build nothing. Nothing in this room has ever been constructed—no wall, no woven nest, no fire, no cairn. The folk's entire material culture is found objects and small adjustments. They pick up sharp flakes of chert from the fallen talus and use them until they dull: to prune the moss gardens, to open snail shells, to pry, to scrape, to dig. They keep bone splinters for finer work. They drink from shells and carry water in them, bowl-up, wobbling, on rain nights. And here and there a pebble has been wedged under the lip of a drip-channel to nudge its thread two hand-spans to the left, into a basin beside a sleeping slab—a kindness measured in millimeters, their only architecture, invisible to anything that didn't know to look.

The overhangs belong to the birds. A soot-dark swift, shaped exactly like its work, nests here in the hundreds—bracket nests of mud and moss stuck to the walls, a hand-span apart, tier above tier until the ceiling tiers blur together. The swifts come and go through the side fissures that open onto the mountain's outer faces, out into the windless cold and the cloud, hunting the midges that get blown up the rock from the valleys far below. Their churring is continuous, a soft boiler of wings, and when the whole colony turns in the dark the sound comes from everywhere at once, like the room itself purring.

The air column belongs to the moths and midges, uncountable, hanging in the warm updraft in shoals you walk through as through warm smoke. The basins belong to a pale frog that has never in its history needed to be anything but pale, and to colonies of tiny blind crustaceans that graze the mineral film. Snails work the moss cushions, their shells clicking against each other in the dark with a sound like very small typing.

All of it is loud. Stand still in the middle of the room and you will hear your own heart only by accident, between drips. The draft hums its one low organ note in the throat. The basins count their rhythms. The swifts churr, the folk chirr and click in long conversational sentences with long stillnesses inside them, the moths boil softly, the beetles grind, the snails type. And beneath all of it, faint as a memory, the sea-noise of the outside weather comes down the flue—sometimes a whisper, and once in a great while a roar, which is the sound that sends every folk in the room running to set out their shells.

Time here is measured in strange units. The folk's calendar is not made of days; it is made of the needle. On certain nights, when the outside sky is clear and the angles are right, a single star—or the rim of the moon—sends a thread of light down the twisting throat. It lasts a few hundred breaths. It touches first one wall, then the floor, a needle of silver moving through the dark, and the whole room holds still while it passes: the folk frozen mid-step, the swifts silent, even the drips seeming to lean in. The needle visits a handful of times each season. The folk remember each visit the way other creatures remember a generation.

Beyond that, everything is long. The moss advances a finger's width in a decade. The basins deepen a knuckle in a lifetime of the oldest folk. Populations rise and fall across years, not seasons—fat years when the midges come high and the rain falls often, lean years when the outside goes calm and dry and the threads thin to almost nothing—and the folk carry the ledger of it in songs, hydrological histories sung in clicks: which basins have gone sour, which have sweetened, which thread of water shifted its course the winter of the great stillness, four hundred needles ago.

The room has been like this for a very long time, and it will be like this for a very long time more. The drip that is carving the newest basin—it takes an age to find fresh stone—will finish it long after every creature now in the room is gone, and the creature that drinks from that basin first has already been, in some sense, awaited. Nothing here is beginning. Nothing here is ending quickly. The mountain breathes, the walls sweat, the cups fill one drop at a time, and the needle comes down the flue when it comes, and the whole warm crowded dark of it keeps its patient score, at the top of the world, inside the cold.
