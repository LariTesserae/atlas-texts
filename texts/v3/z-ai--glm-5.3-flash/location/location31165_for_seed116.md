# The Fathom-Works

You hear it before you find it. The mountain hums. Shepherds on the slopes above say the rock has a heartbeat — a slow, deep *whum… whum…* that comes up through their boot-soles and makes the grass shiver in rings around their flocks. They have never seen the door. The door is a milled bronze hatch set in a fold of the mountainside, worn so smooth by hands and weather that its warning-engraving is now just a gloss of letters, a shine where words used to be.

Inside, the hatch opens on a small round room of air — the Mouth — and the sound stops being a hum and becomes a *place*. Pumps. Flywheels the size of millstones turning with the patience of planets. Piston-rams groaning on their long slow stroke, up and down, up and down: the machinery of a tide. Copper coils sweat beads of water that fall with a hiss into the dark below the grate under your feet. The whole room is warm as a bakery. The air tastes of steam and oiled iron and, faintly, of salt.

And through a grating in the floor, when you look down: the sea.

## The Surface — the first fathom

The sea is not large. That is the first thing you must accept, because everything after it will try to make you disbelieve it. The water below the grate fills a shaft a little wider than a threshing floor — you could stand at one wall of the Mouth, spit, and hit the far wall of the sea. You could skip a stone across the entire ocean, rim to rim. From the outside it is one room, one room inside a mountain, with a door.

But it is a sea. It has a surface with a tide on it — the piston-rams lift the whole water-column the width of a hand and set it back down, twice a day, breath by breath, and the machined shoreline ring rises and falls through its seals with a sound like slow swallowing. It has weather. It has a sun. The shaft's mouth is open to the sky above the mountain crown, and true daylight falls down the bore in a wide column of gold, and where the daylight thins, ring after ring of installed lamps takes over — matched so perfectly to the sun that the seam between sky's jurisdiction and the Works' is undetectable. The light is ordinary. That is the strangest part: an afternoon, everywhere, at every depth, warm and even and mild as a bright overcast day.

Things live in it that shouldn't fit in a room. Periodically something breaches: a *shearer* — a long-bodied warm-water swimmer with stiff wing-fins — launches itself out of the sea into the air of the Mouth, arcs across the machinery, and knifes back in with a slap you feel in your teeth. They shear back and forth between the water and the air all day, the only citizens who commute between the two countries. When the lamps dim for maintenance-dark, the shift changes: the shearers rest, hanging in the shallows in loose rows like coats on hooks, and other things come out.

## The Orchards — the warm hundred

Descend the keeper's ladder — brass rungs, each one burnished mirror-bright by eons of grips — and the water closes over you at bath temperature. Nothing here is cold. Cold is not permitted. The Works have never once let the water forget summer; the heat-exchangers see to that, exhaling warmth through the whole column, and so the sea is tropical to its bottommost fathom, which is a wrongness you only notice later, when you think about what deep water is supposed to be.

The walls are not rock. Nothing here is natural and nothing ever was — that is the deepest rule of the place. The walls are poured stone faced with cast plates, and on the plates, in milled sockets, grows everything green. Terraces spiral down the upper hundred fathoms: kelp-hedges trimmed by current into soft grey-green waterfalls, sponge-orchards in fruiting rows, lawns of moss climbing the lamp-rings themselves. A vine grows *along* the power conduits, hugging the copper, and the locals swear it drinks the current — it flowers brightest on the cables, dimly on the struts. The planting is lush but spaced with a strange formality: wide bare panels of plate between every bed, clearances kept for sight-lines no instrument requires anymore. The builders planted in thoughts, not in crowds, and the garden still obeys their sense of spacing, two hundred generations after the last gardener.

The abundance here is a census of kinds, not of crowds. A dozen species of grazer, but never a school of them — each one holds a huge solitary territory along the terraces, a finch on a hedge, alone. Clams with spiral auger-shells bore gently along the plate-seams and *replant* things: they carry fragments of kelp-holdfast in their mantles and press them into old sockets, gardeners by instinct, the last keepers of a contract they were born knowing. Nothing that lives here is native. Everything is either installed or descended from the installed.

## The Drop — the middle water

Then the terraces end, and there is the Drop.

Here is the secret the smallness hides: the shaft is a plaza wide and deeper than any trench the honest ocean ever dug. Past four thousand fathoms, in a column you could ring with a hundred easy kicks of the legs. The elevation is extreme the way a needle is long — all of it in one dimension.
