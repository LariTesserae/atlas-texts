# Sill

The first thing you notice, coming down off the rim road, is that your footsteps have stopped mattering. The path here is grown thick with fallmoss — shaggy, gray-green, deeper than your boot — and the moss does not so much absorb sound as refuse to return it. You say something to your companion and hear it twice: once leaving your mouth, once arriving, flattened and intimate, as if the air itself has agreed to carry only what is necessary. Behind you the wind is still working the rim, but down here the wind has been combed quiet. The whole basin works like that. It takes noise the way a wool coat takes rain.

Then the light does its one trick, which is no trick at all: the fog that filled the basin overnight pours out over the rim in a slow silent flood, going the direction you came, and the sun — low, cold, ordinary — finds a floor of stone below you that seems to be sorted by age. Near the rim everything is white-green, the color of frost on a window. Toward the middle the pale gives way to blue-gray, then to a deep slate that holds the city: Sill, the oldest ground, the first-cured stone, laid out in streets whose names are dates. You can read the basin's history in its tints. The newest land is the whitest. The past is simply darker.

Here is how the ground is made. Every night, the cold air pools in the basin the way cold air does, and the high mineral-hungry airstream coming off the young range to the north has no choice but to let go of what it carries. It lets go as frost. Not ice — stone. A hoarfrost of rock, fine-grained and damp, settling at the rate of a fingernail's growth on ordinary nights, a hand's width when the outflow runs strong. You can hear it if the world lets you: a tick, a tick, a tick, like dust settling on paper, like the world's slowest clock. The locals call it the fall, and they call the stone it leaves fallstone, and the stone is soft. Soft as pressed cheese for its first weeks. You can press your thumbprint into it. Children do. The stone cures over a season — hardens, darkens, keeps — and the thumbprints cure with it. Everything marked here stays marked. The basin has an excellent memory and no voice.

The strangest places are the warmings. Where the fall runs fast, the new stone comes down *tepid* — the act of its arriving gives off heat, faintly, the way condensation does — and so a hillside in active growth steams in the cold air like a horse after work. These warm veins are where the basin keeps its lushness. Cold as it is, the ground here is never idle, and the plants have responded with a fanaticism that would look tropical if it didn't look so small: fallmoss running a finger-length a day, stonecrop shouldering through the fresh rime within hours of its settling, lace-lichen sheeting the newest rock so quickly that harvest crews mark their stakes at dusk and find them half-swallowed by dawn. Ferns crowd the warmings in brakes you can barely push through. What the basin has is not the lushness of rain. It is the lushness of opportunity — of endless newly-empty ground meeting endless eager pioneers — an ecosystem locked in permanent early spring, forever arriving, never allowed to grow up. There are no old oaks because no ground has been allowed to be old for long enough, except at Sill's core, where a ring of black pines stands on the basin's first floor and everyone pretends not to be a little afraid of them.

Nothing bare lasts. That is the law of the ground before it is the law of the city: land that arrives and is not used does not stay. Unclaimed fallstone thins again over the following weeks, smoking faintly away like breath off glass, returning to the air that brought it. So the settlement that grew up here — Sill, low on the oldest floor — is organized around a single civic urgency: the staking. Every night crews go out with lanterns onto ground that did not exist at supper, and they score it, stake it, and moss it before morning, because rooted ground stays and roofed ground stays and everything else is a lease the sky is free to end. The result is a density that visitors find hard to breathe in: no vacant lots anywhere, not because of greed but because vacancy is a physical impossibility here. Space, unoccupied, un-occupies itself. Every inch of Sill is either stone being cured, moss being grown, or something being built. The addresses are vintages. You can ask a woman where she lives and she will say, *the ground of the fourteenth of Deepfall, third terrace,* and know exactly what she means.

The water, oddly, comes out of the walls. Fallstone falls hydrous — it holds its moisture the way clay does — and as it cures it weeps. A new district of Sill is built with gutters under its own foundations, and for the first year the buildings sweat out their water content into cisterns, cold and faintly mineral, a little bitter, entirely drinkable. The city drinks from its own masonry. Beyond the walls, out in the moss-meadows, dew ponds sit in old cured hollows and hold what little free water the basin permits, rimmed with the only open mud for miles, and every creature knows them. Twice or three times a year, a warm front forces its way over the rim and it *rains* — real rain, loud rain, the loudest thing that ever happens here — and the whole basin stops. Work is forbidden. Every pan and cure-trough and curled snail shell goes outside. The moss flushes green in a day and the ferns in the warmings stand up like something remembered, and for a few days the whole region blooms at once. They are the best days of the year, rain-days. They are also the dangerous ones, because rain on soft stone carves, and a hard wet night can cut a channel through a young district that will be there forever. Sill's oldest gorge — the one the children dare each other along — is a work of two storms, cut through stone that was minutes old when the water found it.

The industry is quieter than you expect an industry to be, because it has to be. Fresh fallstone takes its cure from stillness; vibration leaves flaws in it — clinker-lines, the masons call them, weak gray seams invisible until the wall cracks — and so the harvest crews work in silence and the mills run on the steady hush of the outflow wind, which blows down off the rim every night of the year and turns the great quiet windmills on the ridge. The sawing of fallstone is the gentlest heavy work in the world: the stone is so soft in its first weeks that it can be planed like green wood, and it cures into masonry that will outlast everyone involved. Kilns speed the curing where speed is wanted, and their smoke — here is the joke the basin plays on itself — seeds the air for more stone. Sill's chimneys help make the weather of stone that Sill harvests. The city grows its own ground, and its ground, curing, grows the city. Nobody has decided how to feel about this. The ledger-keepers of the Survey have graphs going back a century showing the fall thickening year over year, and the graphs have the shape of the city's own growth, and everyone in Sill can do that arithmetic in their head.

The quiet, then, is three things stacked. It is the moss and the soft stone and the cold dense air, which swallow sound the way a snowfield does. It is discipline: noise is a defect in the merchandise, and a crew that laughs over a curing slab will answer for the clinker-line. And it is older than both of those, older than the city: the fauna of the basin does not call. Abundance here is silent abundance. The hush-ox herds that move out onto fresh pasture every dusk — broad-footed, patient, two hundred animals crossing a meadow without one sound above the munching — learned quietness the way everything here did, because the loud are heard. The hush-hunters see to that: pale, long-eared, owl-faced things that sit at the edge of the warmings and hear a quarriesnail rasping stone half a mile off, and hunt the way the basin taught them, in perfect hush. Their ears are enormous. So are the quarriesnails, some of them, big as dogs, grazing the new stone with radulas that rasp it to a fine pale powder — you can find their 论ed trails cured into the rock, record of a meal eaten before the eater's grandchildren were born. In summer the warmings fill with candlemoths, drinking the tepid condensation off growing stone, so many of them that a hillside in active growth will look, from the rim at dusk, like it is burning very slightly.

The fauna keep up or get lifted — that is the other rule of the ground. The cliff-nesters build on stone that gains a hand's width a week, and their nests rise, and their chicks fledge higher than their eggs were laid, riding the growth of the world itself. Nests on too-fast stone simply ascend out of reach and are abandoned. Nothing here is permanent except what cures, and even the permanent is only a few generations deep. Sill itself, the ancient heart, sits on stone a hundred and fifty years old, which the residents will tell you gravely, as if it were deep time, and outside their walls it basically is.

The maps are redrawn weekly. That is the whole character of the place: turbulent, but turbulently *quiet* — a churn with the volume off. A warming will run for a month and put a hill where a meadow was; an outflow surge will drop a foot of fresh ground in a night and the stakers will work until their hands seize; a cold calm season will starve the fall and the edge-districts will thin and lift, going back to vapor with a softness that witnesses describe as the worst sound in the world, which is to say, no sound at all — a street of houses becoming weather. When it happens they ring the bell. One bell, old cured bronze, hung in the slate core of the city, rung only for lifts and rain-days, and its single stroke goes out over the moss and the meadows and the silent herds like a crack in the world, because everything that absorbs sound has been absorbing it for so long that the bell comes through the hush intact, unswallowed, the way nothing else does. People stop walking. Hush-ox lift their heads. The moths scatter off the warmings.

Then the bell fades, and the tick resumes — tick, tick, tick, patient as ever — and the basin goes back to the only work it has ever done, which is quietly making more of itself, one cold white night at a time.
