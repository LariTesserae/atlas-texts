The first thing to understand is that this is not a forest.

From the rim of the high shelf — after the long climb, when the air goes thin and the morning sun comes down clean and ordinary and hot — the plateau opens up in every direction at once: a plain the color of old hide, tiled with light and shadow, running flat to a horizon that curves faintly with distance. What reads at first as forest is not forest. It is columns. Single trunks, gray-green, fluted like organ pipes, rising clear and branchless for a hundred feet before they break into small crowns of paddle-shaped leaves. They stand a quarter mile apart, or half a mile, or grouped in loose groves with whole sunlit distances between them, and between them the ground is not soil at all but a woven, tawny, root-felt mat that gives slightly underfoot and hums.

The hum is the second thing to understand.

Put your palm flat on that mat and you will feel it: a deep, distributed vibration, as if the ground were a struck bell that never finished ringing. It is not the ground. It is the columns. Each trunk is hollow — a chimney, a flue, a well — and the hot plateau air pulls a steady draft up through every one of them, and the draft makes them sing. Each column has its own note, pitched by the length of its hollow, so every district of the plateau is a chord, and walking the aisles you pass in and out of standing waves, dead spots of uncanny quiet, then nodes where the sound closes over you like water and you cannot hear your own voice.

Every column is joined to every other one. The root-mat is a single body; where one trunk's buttresses dive under the aisle they graft with the roots of its neighbors, and the grafts graft onward, shelf-wide, horizon to horizon. The whole plateau — a land so large it keeps its own weather, a land you could walk for forty days and not cross — is one organism. One growth, expressed in widely spaced parts. The people who live here have no plural for it.

They are few, and they come later in this account. First: the water, because the water explains everything else.

The plateau is hot and nearly rainless. Midday in the aisles the light shudders, the root-mat crackles underfoot, and the only shade is the narrow shade of the columns themselves, which swings around each trunk like a slow dark blade. No river crosses the shelf. In the deep seams where the mat dips there are brackish field-pools that last a few weeks after rain and then are drunk. At one broken rim, far from anything, an aquifer leaks in a single thin bright thread that drops off the edge of the world — the only running water, and it never changes.

What the plateau has instead of rain is the night.

At this elevation the days scorch but the nights go cold, and the bark of the columns radiates its heat fast, so that by the small hours every trunk is the coldest thing in the landscape, colder than the air, and the dew comes down on it in quantity. The bark is fluted — those organ-pipe grooves run the full height of every trunk — and the grooves are channels. All night the condensation beads and gathers; before dawn the channels are running; and the water in them goes, groove by groove, seam by seam, down and down to the swollen base of the trunk, which is not wood at all at its core but a cistern. The trunks drink first. What overflows seeps out at the buttress joints in slow, bright dribbles, and it is there — at the overflow, never the source — that everything on the plateau drinks.

So the water economy of this world is a salary and a windfall. The dew is the salary: small, nightly, reliable, rationed by surface area. The windfall is the Ring.
