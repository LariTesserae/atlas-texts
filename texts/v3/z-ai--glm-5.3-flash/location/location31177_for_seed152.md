You would walk right over it and never know.

The surface is a low black plain of young lava, cracked into plates, warm through the soles of your feet, breathing heat from the seams between them. Rain that falls here does not gather into streams — there are no streams yet, not one in miles — it thins into a thousand fissures and vanishes, swallowed. A few rough-barked pioneer trees lean out of the cracks. Otherwise: stone, sky, heat-shimmer, and a silence so flat it feels pressed down. The land looks finished only from a distance. Up close it is raw, brand-new, a landscape still wet from its making, its edges sharp, its textures legible — you can read in the floor the record of the fire that poured it, like handwriting no wind or water has yet begun to smudge.

Then a circle of green in the black: a rim of ferns, a column of faint mist standing in the air, and a hole in the ground with light falling into it.

This is a skylight — a place where the roof of the tube gave way, or was opened on purpose, long enough ago that life found the edge and hung on. Stone steps fit snugly against the shaft wall, each one shaped to the rock it sits on. Halfway down the air changes. Warmer. Thicker. It smells of wet basalt and green things and something faintly mineral, like a struck coin. By the bottom, the daylight has softened to a permanent late afternoon.

**The tube**

It is a corridor of poured stone, ten paces across, never taller than a two-story house, running a few hundred paces before it narrows into dark. The floor is smooth coils and braided sheets of rock — rope that cooled mid-twist, so fresh that even the fine corduroy of the flow's last motion is intact. The ceiling hangs with glassy stalactites, razor-tipped, undulled. You can lay a palm flat on the ground and feel it: warm, distinctly warm, like the flank of a sleeping animal. The tube is decades old and geologically minutes old. Nothing here has been worn, rounded, softened. The whole place has the self-conscious rawness of a thing still deciding what it will be.

And it is, in fact, still deciding. Every so often, far off down the corridor, there is a single dry click — the rock settling as it cools, a stone easing against a stone. That is the loudest sound the tube makes on its own, and it makes it perhaps once an hour.

**Water**

The tube drinks from its own breath. Warm air rises off the geothermal floor, meets the cooler vault overhead, beads, runs, and falls back. The drips are the clock of this place: one drop every few seconds in the wet rooms, one every few minutes in the dry ones, each falling the length of the ceiling into a pool and ringing there, a soft bell-note with a long stone echo. Rain above finds the fissures and seeps down through the rock for days afterward, arriving as cold threads in the seep walls. There are pockets of stillness in all this dampness too — long galleries of bare, dry, gleaming stone between the wet rooms — so that water here is never a presence but a series of visits.

At the lowest end, where the tube's toe dips near the coast's water table, there is a room with a mirror-still pool, a finger deep, faintly brackish. Snails graze its edges in slow orbits. Nothing ripples unless something moves it.

**The green**

Vegetation gathers where the light and water agree, and refuses everything else. Beneath each skylight stands a column of green — ferns in deep crowns, moss laid on like velvet, a small rough tree whose roots went through the ceiling crack years ago and have not stopped: they descend as living stalactites, pale-tipped, still growing, currently ending a hand-span above the floor. Under construction. The seep walls wear hanging gardens the way walls elsewhere wear curtains. The pool rooms have beds of a starchy corm and broad-leaved greens planted in stone basins.

But between the green rooms, the tube is empty — genuinely, luxuriously empty. Minutes of walking on bare glassy stone, warm underfoot, nothing but your own reflection in the floor. This is what makes the lushness read as lush: each garden is an island, and the black silence around it is the ocean. The place is mostly space. The green is what defines the space.

**The village**

Someone lives here. You understand this slowly, because nothing announces itself.

A dozen small rooms are set along the dry stretches: walls of fitted stone without mortar, roofs unnecessary — the tube is the roof — floors of pebble mosaic laid flat so water can pass. Fiber cords hang from ceiling to urns like long hair; overnight they bead with condensation and wick it down, and in the mornings the urns are full. Channels of split, hollowed stalks carry seep-water from terrace to terrace, garden to garden. There is no metal, no glass, no fire — fires are not burned here, because all the air is shared, and the tenders hoard it. The tools of this settlement are fiber, stone, patience, and time.

And yet it is organized like a town, without irony. It has a square — the pool room, where everyone's paths cross. It has streets — pebble paths that step across the damp places on laid stones. It has districts: the garden terraces at the foot of each skylight, the dry storerooms, the salt room by the brackish pool, and a boundary at the far end — the dark end — which belongs to the bats and to the foxfire, the pale green glow of rotting root-mass, the only lamp anyone uses down here. It is the smallest city in the world, and its population speaks at a murmur, because the tube repeats everything you say, softly, a beat later, forever.

**The animals**

The abundance here is quiet abundance. Swiftlets nest in the cracks of the shaft walls and commute up and down the columns of light all day, navigating by small soft clicks that come back to them as terrain. Pale moths the color of paper scraps hang in the still air. Long-antenned crickets move through the leaf litter making, at most, a tick. Springtails glint on the water surfaces like dust. Shrimp ghost through the pools; small pink fish, eyeless, drift in the deepest one. Geckos lie flat on the warm walls, absorbing the floor's heat, perfectly still, shadow-colored. At the dark end, bats. And everywhere, descending from above, the roots — each one a living stalactite, each one still traveling.

**The clocks**

Several kinds of time run in this place at once, and you can feel all of them if you stand still. The drip-clock: seconds. The fern-clock: fiddleheads unrolling over days. The root-clock: months per meter, downward, forever. The mineral-clock: the seep walls are growing white and ochre blooms and thin stone curtains, a centimeter a year, slow as fingernails. And the rock-clock: the tube is cooling, generation by generation, the warmth in the floor a diminishing inheritance from the fire above.

The tenders work across all of them at once. They carry leaf-fall down to make the first soil this land has ever had — actual soil, being built by hand, in basins
