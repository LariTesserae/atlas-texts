# The Rising

First, know this: the rock is young.

The mountain has no name older than "the Rising," because naming things as finished facts is a small blasphemy here. It is still being made. A surveyor's cairn stands in the middle of the fourth terrace, far from any edge, marking where the cliff stood within living memory. The plateau has swallowed it and kept going. Locals lease their land, never own it — *held until the stone takes it back* — and nobody has ever seen the mountain's base. It descends through haze for days of travel and never resolves into a foothill. The world below is a rumor told by exhausted travelers whose wooden packs have cracked and whose leather has curled in the dry.

The air up here is thin and hot at once, which shouldn't be possible, and is. Heat pools under a low lid of luminous haze that looks close enough to touch — a fixed ceiling, a milk-white sky that never quite clears. The sun is a white coin behind it. Shadows come out soft-edged, blurred at their borders, which means sundials are useless and time must be kept another way (more on that later). Noon is not an hour here so much as a force. Sunward stone will blister a palm in seconds. All work happens in shade, or in the night, or in the stilling hour when even the heat seems to hold its breath.

## The rock itself

There is no soil. There has never been soil. Nothing has had time to rot, and nothing has ever grown here to rot anyway. Every surface is a fresh fracture face: obsidian-black sheets, pale pumice-colored slabs, conchoidal curves like muscle. No stone anywhere is rounded. There are no pebbles, only edges. Wind is nearly absent — the thin air barely moves, and haze stands in layers like shelves — so nothing gets sandblasted smooth. The only weathering is thermal: the great daily swing pops flakes of glass off the sunward faces, and at mid-afternoon you hear it, a sparse ticking like fingernails on a table, spall-chips clattering downslope one at a time. That is the entire erosion budget of this place. Its edges will still be sharp in a thousand years.

Into this dryness, warmth threads up from below. Fissures exhale vapor — the mountain breathing through its teeth — and around every seep, the world's only softness gathers. On the wet stones grows a black velvet felt, a crust finer than moss and simpler than moss, more mineral than either, hairy with crystalline filaments. And from certain seeps rise the tubes: bleached silicate chimneys, a hand high, bent like a bleached thicket, growing perhaps a finger-width a year. A grove of them took a century to become a grove. Nothing here is a plant. The closest thing to a forest is chemistry, patient and blind.

## The damp

Water is the whole economy, and water is manufactured.

The vapor that leaks from the fissures is saturated — it needs only an excuse to become liquid — so the city gives it one. Condenser forests cover the warm districts: acres of glass fins mounted like canebrakes, engineered to shed their heat to the night sky. At dusk the fins go colder than the air around them, which in this place is a small miracle of surfaces, and sometime before dawn the mountain's breath begins to weep on them. Each fin beads. Each bead runs. The gutters start to tick, then to trickle, and by first light the whole city is quietly raining upside down, every rooftop a dew-farm, every cistern under every floor filling.

Mineral tidelines mark every wet episode — pale rings on black stone, a legible record of a hundred damp seasons. And once a year, when the mountain exhales more than the works can catch, the surplus fog rolls through the streets entire: the Wet. A single day of visible moisture, children dancing in the gutterings, every surface ringing with drips. The loudest day of the year, by a wide margin, is the wet one.

The law follows the water. Every drop is counted; every pipe is known; tampering with a condenser fin is the only crime with an old word attached — *dew-thief* — and it is spoken quietly, the way frightening things are spoken of here.

## The city

It is stacked. Terraces on terraces, and beneath them, districts sunk into the rock itself: old vapor-bores, mined and abandoned, then mined again for dwellings — shaft-houses going down nine and ten levels, laced with ladders and lamplight. The density is the point. You can pass a kettle hand to hand across six balconies without anyone raising their voice, and no one ever raises their voice.

They can't, really. The thin air eats sound. A shout dies within twenty paces; bells are useless and the city has none. So it signals with steam-plumes and lamp-code, and it has developed a whole hush-culture: conversations are household-close, footsteps are soft-soled, and the loudest things in anyone's day are accidental — a dropped ceramic plate, a spall-chip cracking off a wall. The city contains furnaces, engines, factories, and it sounds like a quiet apartment.

Nothing is wooden. Nothing is clothed in anything that ever grew in a field. Ropes are drawn glass; rope-glass, drawn fine and cooled slow, holds better than hemp ever did. Bedding is mineral wool. Books are fired clay. Text is stamped or fired, never written in ink, because there is no ink. The palette of everything is black glass, pale stone, ceramic, dull metal — and the only color is water: the dark wet stripes on the fins, the gleam in the cistern mouths, the blue-white burn of the forge-district at night.

## The works

The engines drink the difference between day and night. Thermal pistons, massy and slow, charge their flywheels through the cold hours — flywheels the size of cistern-rooms, turning all night in stone vaults with a hum you feel in your molars rather than hear. Pneumatic mains breathe through the terraces, one long exhalation a minute. And at noon, when the gradient between rock and sky collapses, the whole machinery idles: the stilling hour, a daily pause you feel through the floors, the city going quiet the way a sleeping animal is quiet.

But the great product of the works is not vessels or tools. It is ground.

At the plateau's rim stand the annealing halls: long tunnels where the mountain's freshest extrusion is received still-hot, kneaded by screw-presses the size of houses, rolled flat, vented, and laid down as new terrain. The Rim advances a few ells a year — measured at the annual survey, celebrated quietly, each new flat sheet of black land counted like income. The industry of this place is midwifery. The city does not sit on the mountain; it helps the mountain be born, and it builds its streets out of the newborn. Wander to the Rim at night and you can watch land being made: a slow screw turning, a sheet of ground cooling from cherry-red to black, vapor lifting off it in banners, and the whole process nearly silent, because nothing here rushes.

Downhill from the halls, the vats: fungal ferment-troughs fed on mineral broth and warm condensate, the food of a place with no fields and no grain, produced at industrial scale in long dim galleries that murmur very, very faintly.

## Three species and a rumor

Fauna is counted here. Annually, in the Wet fog, the census-keepers perform the Count of Living Things, and children memorize the figures the way other places memorize kings.

- **Dewlickers** — small herds of them, goat-sized at most, plated in silica, the young translucent as blown glass. They appear at dawn along the condenser fins and lap the bead-fall, aligned in silent rows like iron filings. By mid-morning they're gone into the shade-fissures. They have no call; their alarm is a tick of glass plates, tiny and identical to the spall, so that half their alarms go unnoticed.
- **Molt-herons** — tall, patient, standing motionless in the seep-slots for hours, filtering the black felt off the wet stones through lamellae beaks. Once a year each sheds its casing, and the glass feathers fall and *chime* on the rock below. A significant fraction of the dusk soundscape is molting.
- **The Calm** — the rumor. Deep in the oldest bores, when the mountain exhales, something far down inhales. The miners feel it as a slow reversing draught and leave a lamp at the bore's mouth for it. The Count lists it as one. No one has voted to change this.

Dew-reading is a private eccentricity rather than an institution, but on old glass — window panes and fin-surfaces more than a lifetime old — condensation forms in repeating rosettes, and a few households keep almanacs of the patterns, and will not say what they have concluded.

## The rules

Time is told by condensation. The day divides into wet hours — those after the fins begin to weep — and dry hours, and the bell-less city keeps its appointments by gutter-tick. Shadow-clocks are worthless; the soft-edged noon smears all dials.

Heat is a neighbor, not an enemy; it is granted its noon. Children learn shadow-maps before they learn letters, and sunward work is not forbidden so much as unthinkable.

Nothing is finished. Ownership is provisional. The innermost terraces, the oldest ones, are slowly being swallowed — seams widening year by year, staircases tilting as the mountain rearranges the ground beneath its oldest districts — and when a district goes, it is not mourned. It is *returned*. The city has always been migrating, rimward and upward, one generation at a time, a town walking on a thing that is still being born.

## Dusk

The light goes rose under the lid, then slate. The fins begin to cool. Somewhere on the plateau a heron molts, and a single glass feather rings off the rock like a struck spoon. Gutters start their ticking. The flywheel vaults open their doors and the engines take up their night's work, patient, massy, almost soundless. Deep under the fourth terrace, in ground that was cliff-edge when the old surveyors were young, the mountain adds another finger-width to itself. Nobody hears that. Nobody is meant to. The place is quiet the way an animal is quiet when it is growing — and in the morning, everything will be beaded and gleaming, and the gutters will be running, and the dry hours will begin again.
