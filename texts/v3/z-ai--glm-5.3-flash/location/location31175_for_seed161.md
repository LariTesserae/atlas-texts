# The Crown

You come up to it by the Thousand Stair, out of a world that has forgotten water. Below you the plain runs out to every horizon in ochre and bone-dust, flat as a table left outside for a million years, streaked with the pale geometry of dry lakebeds. Nothing lives down there but salt-tolerant scrubs and the things that eat their corpses. The stair climbs for two days through that nothing — steps cut into the peak's dry skirt, each tread worn concave by feet so ancient that the wear itself is polished smooth — and then, at a certain height, a line crosses your path. It is not drawn on anything. It is simply that moss appears: first a gray-green crust in the lee of each step, then a green so sudden and shameless it looks like an accident. The cloud has found you. A few turns later you walk into it, and the world goes soft and gold, and you hear — before you see it — the sound of the crown: water ticking in stone channels, hundreds of birds, a deep bronze gong, and above it all a low chord like a ship's rigging, which is the sound of the fog nets singing in the wind.

The peak is called the Grandmother, and the town on her crown has no other name than *upstairs*, because to the people who live there, there is nowhere else worth distinguishing. The crown is small. A strong walker circles its outermost terrace in an hour and a half, pausing to greet people, which one does. Yet it is the highest ground in the world — and here is the thing the elders will tell you by the spring, with the particular patience of people who have explained it to every stranger: it is the highest place *because* it is the oldest. It was not pushed up. It was left. The Grandmother was once the low valley of a range so vast that the range's name has not survived, and the range wore itself away, grain by patient grain, until the plain below is what the mountains became. Only this one hard knob of stone refused. So its extreme height and its utter stillness are the same fact. There is no scree on the Grandmother, no fresh rubble, no slope waiting to fail. The rock reached its final shape so long ago that even the lichens have finished negotiating with it. In shaded cuts beneath the moss you can find long parallel grooves scratched into the bedrock — the track-marks of ice from a cold so distant that no myth mentions cold. The mountain is done. It will hold this exact shape longer than there have been people to stand on it.

The water arrives as fog. The cloud sea — the White, the people call it — lies over the dead plain like a lid, a few hundred meters below the crown, and it breathes. In one season it rises and laps against the Skirt terraces; in another it withdraws and the desert shows through, vast and indifferent, below your feet. What never changes is that the crown itself sits in the moisture stream. The town harvests it the way their great-grandmothers did: on masts above the rooftops, great woven sails of camelid wool, hung in ranks, each strand beading with droplets until the whole sail darkens and drips. Twice a day the net-menders walk the masts, wringing each sail into clay-buttressed channels, and the water runs — ticking, chuckling, absurdly precious — down through the town to cisterns sealed with clay and oil-cloth. At the very top, under a dome of the oldest stone, is the Spring Room: a seep so slow that an old woman guards it, doling out what the rock weeps in measured shellfuls. Everything else up here drinks fog. There has been no rain in living memory, and the crown is nonetheless one of the greenest places anyone who lives there can imagine — because the green *makes* the water catch. Every leaf, every moss curtain, every bromeliad tank strapped to a branch is a net. The town knows the loop as a moral law, taught to children before they can talk: tend the green, and the green drinks for you. Neglect it and you are back on the stair, going down.

The town fills the crown the way moss fills a crack — completely, vertically, without a gap you could slide a hand into. Houses of dry fieldstone climb the terraces in stacked rows, roofs flat, roof on top of roof, each roof a garden: shreddy-barked crooked trees in stone tubs, ferns spilling over parapets, red trumpet-flowers that the hummingbirds own. The oldest walls were laid without mortar by founders so far back that the joints have vanished under stone-gray lichen; newer walls use mud, patched every dry season. The lanes are staircases. The plaza is barely wide enough for its fountain and its market, and on market days it is a warm press of wool, llamas humming their complaints, dogs asleep underfoot, small pigs kept in kitchen-corner pens, dye-pots steaming, a smith working trade-copper beside a shelf of obsidian flakes. Under the floors of the houses are the ancestors, cisted in dry stone — you cannot dig deep into finished rock — so that families do not so much inhabit their houses as grow them, one roof and one generation at a time. A few thousand souls, and every one of them within earshot of the gong.

And it is loud, in a layered, domestic way. The gong itself sounds at dawn and dusk — copper, taller than a man, rung by the keeper of the spring — and its note rolls around the crown's stone bowl for a long time before it dies. Under it: the constant chord of the wet nets; the tick and trickle of water in clay pipes running beside every lane; the whir and chip of hummingbirds working the flower roofs; the colonized cistern walls, hung with the bottle-nests of small swifts that pour out at dusk in one river of wings. When the White withdraws at evening you can watch it go, and hear what it leaves: a chorus of fog-frogs rising from the bromeliad tanks and moss lawns, thousands of small animals that will never see a stream, breathing through skin kept wet by the air itself. Lizards come out onto the warm stone at first light and lap droplets off the parapets. Gray moths work the night shift of pollination. And over the windward edge of the crown, all day, turn the condors — they belong to the Dry, not to the crown; they ride the updraft of the peak's thermal collar and drop down to whatever the desert has killed, including the dust-colored locust plagues that sometimes come up the stair on the wind and arrive dead. The crown's cats hunt the frogs. The crown's people tolerate the cats for exactly that reason. Everything up here, one way or another, is paid for in condensation.

The light is dim and gold, and the people have stopped noticing. The sky of this world carries a permanent high dust, and the sun is a warm coin behind it even at clearest; usually the Veil thins it further, to a honey-colored glow with no direction, shadows faint and doubled, interiors lit by small windows and clay lamps burning seed-oil. It feels — everyone says so, including visitors from the stair — like late afternoon, always. The warmth comes from the White itself, a blanket draped around the peak: nights are mild, wool worn loose, no frost, kitchens vented through roof-gardens to sweeten the moss. Perhaps thirty days a year the Veil parts entirely — the Openings — and the whole town stops. Work pauses mid-motion. People stand in the lanes and face south and are quiet, and you see the cloud sea below shining like a second plain, and beyond it the true desert, where occasionally a dust storm walks, slow as an animal, and never reaches you.

Very little here changes quickly, and the town has organized its soul around that. The year is counted in fog-tides and Openings. The great labor is not building but mending — nets, walls, channels, thatch — a calendar of maintenance that repeats with a rhythm closer to geology than to news.
