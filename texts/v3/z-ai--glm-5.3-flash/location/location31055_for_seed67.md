**The Weeping Plain**

The first thing to understand is that it has just rained. The second thing is that it is always about to.

The whole world sits in that state — the low bright minutes after a shower, air thick enough to drink, every surface beaded, every puddle lit from below like a lamp left on under water. But the rain never quite arrives and the wetness never quite leaves. The sky hangs high and pearl-colored, lit from some source that never moves, and the light it gives is mid-morning light: it has been ten past ten here for as long as anyone has thought to check. Shadows lie at the same angle they lay yesterday. They never lengthen. They pool under things like paint.

The land itself is low and goes on forever. No mountains break the horizon — nothing rises that wasn't raised — just a soft continental plain of pale stone, ranked with terraces, levees, shallow lakes held at their set lines, and in the far distance columns and organ-pipe towers standing up out of the haze like the pipes of some instrument the size of a country. The air is warm the way an exhale is warm. The stone is warm too, blood-warm; lie down on it and it feels like the flank of a sleeping animal, except that nothing here sleeps for long, and nothing here is an animal's flank. Everything is made. Dig anywhere and you find the same story: grown mineral, dense and fine-grained, then service channels, then deeper, more world.

The stone is young. That is the strangest ordinary thing about it. Edges everywhere are crisp — trowel-crisp, kiln-crisp — seams straight as scribing, corners unrounded, the whole terrain looking like it was finished that morning. And in a sense it was, every morning, because there is no morning. The world renews itself. Crawler units the size of houses move along the seams at glacier pace, laying down fresh substrate in slow beads, troweling it glassy, and by the time any edge could think about wearing, it has already been replaced with a younger copy of itself. Nothing corrodes. Nothing accumulates. No dust, no sediment, no litter, no ruins — the plain refuses history the way a held breath refuses speech.

And it weeps. The walls weep deliberately. Water is kept pressurized just beneath the surface everywhere, oozing through micropores in slow veils, so that whole cliffsides glisten and drip in long rows, and the sound of the place — one of the few sounds it permits itself — is a wide, sparse ticking of drops into channels. A lattice of weirs and rills carries the water nowhere in particular and back again. Puddles never dry and never deepen. Rain comes on rounds, warm as bathwater, in scheduled squares of the map; you can watch a gray curtain cross a district exactly when it crossed yesterday, watering the same terraces, then lifting. Even the wind has a shift schedule.

You expect an industrial world to roar. This one whispers. Every engine down there runs on fluid drives and magnetic bearings, and every surface is micro-slotted, padded, tuned to swallow its own noise — the builders, whoever they were, wanted a quiet nursery, and they engineered the hush into the material itself. Somewhere under the plain a foundry exhales fresh substrate in slow geyser-columns of paste; you stand a hundred meters away and hear nothing at all. A pump inhales and you cannot tell whether it inhaled or exhaled. What survives of the soundscape is small: the drip-toll of the weeping walls, a soft chink as a feeding arm presents a mineral block, the low rumble of a herd like a train that never arrives, and under everything a purr in the floor of the world, so constant you stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own blood.

The animals are everywhere. That is the point of the place, as far as anyone can tell — it is a garden with no gardeners visible, built for its beasts. There is not a plant in it, not a blade, not a leaf, and no green anywhere except nowhere; all the color in the world belongs to the animals. Herds of broad-backed browsers with wet hides the color of ochre move across the fresh trowel-marks, cropping the chalky rime that the fabricators exude — a frost of living mineral, the closest thing this world has to grass, and it regrows behind their teeth overnight. Their grazing leaves bright scrape-moons on the new stone, and by the time the marks could weather, the stone has healed. Fliers wheel in fixed circuits overhead — the same dark chevrons, the same bank over the weirs at the same interval, as if flown on rails — layered above the herds, while below everything the ground is riddled with burrow-galleries like reef rock, and the whole plain is vertically crowded: diggers underfoot, browsers on it, flocks above, skimmers rowing the channels on still wings.

The animals are not afraid. Nothing here has ever needed to be. Feeding arms descend among them like patient herons; crane-machines stand knee-deep in the shallows beside real waders, and for a moment the two are hard to tell apart — the whole infrastructure mimics the fauna, tortoise-crawlers and lung-pumps and trowel-beaked graders, two versions of everything, one born and one built, and occasionally you genuinely cannot say which is which. The count of animals is known to the last individual; the census is taken constantly, and it always comes back the same number. The individuals churn — some are born, some are taken gently in the night by machines that leave no mess — but the number does not move. Nothing is permitted to lie still long enough to become a fact. There are no bones in this world. There are no graveyards, no growth rings, no old scars, no remains of anything. Death happens; it just doesn't accumulate.

Stand still too long and something will come to see whether you are broken. A slim maintenance arm, bird-skulled and unhurried, will walk over on its cable, scan you, find nothing to repair, and withdraw — and that is the closest thing to an opinion this world will ever offer about you. It does not ask where you came from. It does not ask what year it is. Nothing here remembers, because remembering is a kind of sediment, and sediment is not allowed.

So the place is old the way a fact is old, not the way a thing is old. Everything in it changes constantly and nothing in it ever becomes different. The herds are the same herds. The rain falls on the same squares. The flocks turn at their appointed moment over the weirs, ten past ten, forever, and the weeping walls go on ticking into their channels like a clock that tells you nothing except that it is still working. Either someone is at the controls and is very good at being unseen, or no one is, and the instructions are simply running, and will run, wet and warm and newly-made, past every reason they were ever written.

If you wait, a drop will gather at the lip of a wall — gather slowly, trembling, heavier than it should be able to bear — and fall, and toll once, softly, into the water below.

Then another gathers.
