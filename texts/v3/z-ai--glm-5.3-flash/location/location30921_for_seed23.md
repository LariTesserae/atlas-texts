Come to the rim first, before you go down.

The bowl sits high, in the hard thin air above the general weather — some days the only cloud in the world is the one in the cup. From the rim-walk you look down into it the way you look into a bowl of milk with a lamp lit underneath: a half-mile of glowing fog, held in black stone walls a few hundred feet tall, ice-toothed along the top and streaked white where generations of birds have done their generations of work. The fog tops out below the rim. It stays in. Roofs break its surface here and there like islands — a hundred and some roofs, countable, which is to say the whole town is countable, which is to say the town is small. From up here you can see every channel-glint, every fish-rack, every pen and stair and terrace. You could cross it on foot in a quarter of an hour and pass every kind of thing the place has.

And it is silent from up here. That's the first wrongness. A town that busy should hum.

The warmth is older than anyone's records. Down at the bottom of the bowl, water comes up through a scattering of spring-eyes — pale vents in the black stone floor, blood-warm, steady, unexplained. Nobody knows what burns below. The water has always done this; the water will always do this; that certainty is as much a part of the place as the stone. The warm water spreads out in terraced pools and cools as it goes, and the moment it meets the air — air cold enough to make spit crack, cold that aches in the teeth, the true cold of a high place — it steams. All of it, always. The bowl is a distillery the size of a quarry: warm water rising and evaporating below, condensing on the cold heights above, freezing there, calving, melting, returning.
