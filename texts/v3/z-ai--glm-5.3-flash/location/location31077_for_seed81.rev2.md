You reach it by climbing. That is the first thing to understand: whatever road brings you here — the switchback duct-road, the long outer stair with its rope handlines — it climbs for a day and a half through air that thickens as you rise, until your ears have popped twice and nothing on you is dry. High places are supposed to be cold. This one is not. The mountain is an old body that never finished cooling, and every current of air that crosses its stone comes away heavier, warmer, wetter. By the time you see the city you are drinking the landscape just by breathing.

The glow comes first: an amber stain in the grey-brown murk, too diffuse to have edges. Then the shape resolves — a ring of terraces riding the rim of an ancient caldera, hanging over an interior filled to a depth of hundreds of feet with slow, luminous fog. The locals call that fog the Low Sea. It moves like something alive, silvering where the lamplight catches it, and the vapor ceiling far above — the Roof, they call it — hangs low and permanent, never breaking, never thinning more than it must. The city's formal name is Calder, though almost nobody uses it. The name that has stuck, and it suits, is the Weep.

Because everything weeps here. Not as metaphor. Every face of stone in the city exudes a film of water that gathers into beads, beads into runnels, runnels into the gutters that vein the whole place like a circulatory system someone drew in bronze. The rock itself is in on it: springs break out at every elevation, warm and cold only a few paces apart, and no one has ever satisfactorily explained why two weeping walls standing side by side will sweat hot water and cool. The air is so saturated that sweat does not dry on skin. Everyone shines.

*

The Weep is a machine for drinking weather, and its emptiness is not decay — it is design. The city is enormous for what it holds: avenues wide as plowed fields, towers hollow as chimneys, whole districts that are nothing but volume. This is because the fog needs room to move and the water needs somewhere cool and dark to fall. The great hollow towers — the Harrows — are condensation engines, sheathed in fins of blackened copper driven deep into the mountain, fins that stay cooler than the air around them, so the fog gives up its burden on contact. On the windward terraces these combs stand in fields, hundreds of them, and on certain mornings you can hear the harvest: not a roar, but a tick and patter, the sound of a sky being wrung out. Strings of condensate gather on the fins and hang there, trembling, humming at a pitch felt in the molars more than in the ears. The harrowers time their shifts by that hum. Nobody has fully explained why the beads do what they do, why on some copper lines the droplets creep uphill overnight against every law the engineers were taught. The pipes are simply graded for it. The mountain is old enough that its habits have become the rules.

The harvest runs down through the gutters, through the aqueducts, and out — gravity does the exporting. Over the outer rim the main flumes fling the water into the world below in three long white falls, and beneath each spout the dissolved minerals have built white terraces over the centuries, growing stone, milkstone, step after step as if the mountain were growing new teeth. The city drinks the sky and pays the lowlands in water and in salt, raked from the evaporation pans by hand.

Down at the caldera's heart is the Boiler Quarter, and this is the heat's own country. Two great boilers — the Hearts — breathe in long, slow strokes while the third rests, and riveted mains climb the inner wall in switchbacks, feeding pumps that stroke with the patience of things that have never once been hurried. The heat there is near-scalding; the workers wear almost nothing and move in the rhythm of people who have learned that haste in hot water is how accidents happen. What the distillation stacks leave behind is mineral in its purest Performances: salt gardens, crystalline flats, terraces of deposit that from a distance look like snow that never melts.

*

And yet — quiet. This is the second thing visitors cannot get over. The fog eats sound. It is dense, mineralized, heavy with vapor, and it swallows a voice within a stone's throw, swallows a whistle, swallows the enormous continuous industry of the Quarter down to nothing but pressure: a sub-bass you feel in your soles and your teeth, a heartbeat under the paving, and otherwise nothing. The Boiler Quarter could be a mile away or behind your shoulder; the ears cannot say. So the city talks with its hands. On the long stairs and along the gutters, the people sign to each other in a fluid, economical cant developed over generations, and the spoken word is saved for arm's length. What you do hear is near and small: the tick of cooling metal, the hiss of a fin shedding its load, your own pulse, and everywhere, always, the drip.

Deep in the great hollow cisterns — the Hollows, where the harvest pools in black mirrors — there are stillrooms: pockets where even the drip is absent. The silence there has weight, like standing inside a held breath. People descend to them deliberately, to hear their own thinking. Some stay too long. The vent-wards keep a ledger.

The population would not fill one of its own avenues. A rim village, a Boiler shift, the harrowers, the vent-wards in their slow rounds between the great doors — a few hundred, perhaps, in a city built for the ambitions of whoever raised those drowned streets below. The older residents say the emptiness is load-bearing now. Fog needs corridor space; condensation needs cool dark volume; the vast gaps between the city's presences are working parts. Whether or not that is comfort, it is true that the city does not seem neglected. It seems held — worn to a state like river stone, every handrail polished bronze-smooth by centuries of palms, every edge rounded by runoff, the verdigris on the mains as stable as paint. Nothing rusts away here; everything is worn into agreement. The city and the water reached their terms a long time ago.

*

Life is scarce and simple, as if the world here never got past its first drafts. No plants — nothing that could be called a plant — only felts and films: rust-colored mats around the warm seeps, black velvet crusts on the vent-stones, white salt-fur on the drying pans, all of it creeping at the pace of a fingernail. Grazer-things the size of fists shell the rim terraces so slowly you could watch one for an hour and swear it hadn't moved. The lampmoths are the city's small delight — pale soft-winged things that orbit the amber street-globes and feed on the condensation beading the hot glass, gathering in veering clouds after each overboil.

Every few years a deep spring surges; the Low Sea climbs two street-tiers in a day, goes white and hot, and the fog thickens until lamps are suggestions. Doors close. The city waits it out. Then the tides swing back, and at low tide the Sea withdraws far enough to expose the Drowned Tiers — older streets down on the caldera floor, older lamps that still hold a faint sour glow, doors of an earlier city that was built here before this one and is only mostly gone. People walk them with lanterns, in groups, in season. They are part of the same long construction: the Weep is layered like sediment, and its builders clearly intended it to be added to forever by whoever came next.

Out in the Sea itself, at overboil, long soft shapes have been seen to surface — rounded backs, unhurried, gone before a lantern can be raised. The vent-wards note them in the ledger and say nothing. Once in a generation someone swears to a pale glassy eel in a warm gutter, thick as a wrist. The city's fauna is mostly rumor, and the city seems to prefer it that way.

*

Day and night barely differ under the Roof. Night is when the drip cools and the copper smell sharpens; day is when the furnace-glow from the Quarter tints the fog from below, so that the whole city hangs lit between two faint lightings, amber above and ember-red below, everything in halos. The one weather the Weep has is the chimney-day: rare mornings when the Roof thins for an hour and a dim grey disc appears — the sun, as a rumor of itself. Children are taken to the rim to see it. It is the closest thing this world has to an eclipse, a festival, a miracle.

And then the routine resumes, and it is a beautiful routine: the mountain sweating itself into streets, the streets funneling into the Sea, the Sea breathing up into the Roof, the Roof leaning back down as fog against the copper fins, all of it one slow circulation with almost nothing standing in its way. Stand at the rim on a quiet evening, after the lamps come up and the hands of the harrowers go still, and you will feel the whole mechanism turn beneath you as a pressure rather than a noise — and a single warm bead of water will find the back of your wrist and follow it down, unhurried, as if the place had already counted you among the things it was going to condense.
