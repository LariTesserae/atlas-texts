Come at it the long way, because there is no short one.

The plain below is itself a high country — red-black, drained, dry as a kiln floor — and the spire rises out of it by better than two miles, a tooth forced up through the floor of an older world sometime recent enough that the event still counts as news. From the plain you cannot see the city at all. You can barely see the summit; it stands in thin air, in a sky that is nearly vacuum with ambitions, under a sun too remote to be called a lamp — a small hard coin that crosses low for a few hours and gives light the way memory gives light: thin, bluish, no comfort in it. Everything else up here is the mountain's own glow, deep in its sealed courts: a black-red ember light, like a forge remembering.

The last half mile is stair, cut into a face of unweathered volcanic glass so fresh it still carries the conchoidal scars of its own fracture — smooth in the way of a fresh break, not the way of age. Nothing here is old enough to be smooth. The cliff remembers being one piece. Fracture mirrors throw back the coin-sun as brief white slaps in the general dimness, so the wall appears to be winking at you, slowly, a blink every few hundred steps. Dust exists but has not had time to become anything; the ledges wear it like a dusting of knives.

Then the notch.

It is a room the wind forgot: a fold near the summit, walled on three sides by the spire's own mass, open on the fourth to a drop that does not end so much as give up being describable. Forty paces across. You could shout across it and the thin air would carry only half the shout. And folded into this pocket, using its walls as a trellis, is a city.

Not a city of walls — a city of edges. Rails, ribs, trestles, strings, stair-thread, catwalk-lace. You can see through it the way you can see through a ship's rigging: its mass is mostly air, its density almost a politeness, voids between presences so generous that from certain angles the whole settlement reads as a single drawn line across the stone. Every window faces inward, toward the rock. Every seat faces the stone. The city has turned its back on the view entirely, and this is its camouflage — not hiding, but intimacy. It watches the mountain. It is not interested in the world.

The registers in its oldest chamber, if you could read them, call the place a word that translates roughly as *the Cradle*, and that is the whole of the mystery resolved and the whole of it deepened: this is a station for the keeping of a newborn mountain. The spire is still making itself. It is doing the work loudly in the only language heat has — it glows, it creaks, it clicks. Stand still in a quiet cell and you will hear it counting itself down to cold: a tick, a long wait, two ticks, like a cooling engine the size of a county. The air in the notch is hot — dangerously so at the height of the coin's crossing; bare skin on open stone will take a memory away with it. But that is the second rule of this place, after *nothing here is old enough to be smooth*: **cold is the road, and heat is the country.**

The paths a body may safely use are laid in cold. Rails and threads run through the city at waist height and ankle height, and along them the rock's heat has been forbid to pass — you walk the settlement as you would walk a reef at low tide, from wet dark line to wet dark line, and the country on either side of the road seethes silently at a temperature that would end you. The city's founders did not build streets. They built a lacework of exceptions through a furnace, and called the lacework home.

Water here is not a feature of the land. It is an appointment.

The sky gives almost nothing — at this height, moisture does not linger; it sublimates and leaves like a guest who has seen the room. So the city makes its own weather, on a schedule, in a volume you could hold. Above the open court hang the rain-hooks: slow-turning lenses of spun filament, thin as breath, wide as wagon wheels, that comb the passing haze and wring it. On the appointed hour — every so often, metronomic, unhurried — one of them shakes out its catch, and a cloud the size of a courtyard drifts across the notch. A private drizzle. A procession. It crosses the court like a slow parade, and for those few minutes the city is loud: rain on openwork metal is a brazen roar, a bell being played from inside, and the walls drink the sound the way they drink the water. Then it is gone, the silence closes like a door, and the strings begin their work.

The strings are the city's rivers, and the only river in this place runs up. Water that escapes the cisterns is caught on the dew-lines — filaments strung everywhere, faintly luminous where vapor rides them, thin silver-blue light in the general darkness, so that at the ember hours the whole city shows as a constellation in the shape of plumbing. The beads walk the lines upward against every instinct gravity has, pausing at junctions as if reading signs, and are delivered back to the keep. The tech involved is beyond any patent you could file; the effect is simple to describe and eerie to watch: water that behaves like something with somewhere to be.

And some of the water is not for the city at all. This is the Cradle's real work, the reason for the rounds. The mountain must cool evenly or it will crack its own summit off, and evenness must be administered. So on schedule the keep injects measured dew down into the deep fissures — quench by dropper — and lets the court-drizzle soak the walls, and the whole settlement performs a slow, patient doctoring of the stone it lives in. The founders were water-keepers. They finished their term some generations ago, when the mountain passed a threshold its instruments marked as *setting*, and they went down the long stair and did not come back. The city has not been told. It keeps the rounds. It sets the tables — flake-glass surfaces in the empty cells, laid each cycle by threads thinner than hair, wiped and reset, prepared for staff who are not coming.

Nothing here is alive, and yet the place has an ecology; it just has traffic instead of life. Vapor traffic. Heat traffic. A mineral skin — call it the varnish — creeping across the wettest stone at the pace of paint drying, a black lacquer that is not a plant and not an animal and not quite a mere mineral either, the closest thing the summit has to a lawn, gaining a centimeter a century and losing most arguments with dryness so slowly the losing looks like style. The varnish grows only where dew sleeps. It is, in a sense, the city's shadow: the record of everywhere the water has been allowed to rest.

The cycles run concurrently, unbothered, mostly silent: the coin-sun's low crossing and its long shadow-times; the tick of the summit contracting; the drizzle procession and the roar; the strings' work, a faint held note like a wet finger on glass; the rain-hooks pivoting by fractions to face the haze-lanes; and underneath everything, in sealed chambers you cannot enter, the glass *creeps* — the rock, still warm at depth, flowing millimeters a year, and the city's foundations sheared and jointed to slide with it rather than fight, which is why the first city is rubble on ledges far below — you can sometimes see a shard of it, white as chalk, a thousand meters down — and the third city is not.
