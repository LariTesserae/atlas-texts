# The Sump Gardens

You reach them by climbing for most of a day, and then all at once you're there — the mountain's summit is not a peak but a shelf, a hanging plateau no bigger than a village square, pinned to the sky like a wet flag. Below you the clouds pool in the valleys. Around you, on every side, the air simply ends: a hard-edged drop of kilometers in any direction, young rock still sharp from its recent uplift, faces so new they haven't yet learned to crumble. The cliffs are sheer and unweathered, black-grey, streaked only where water has begun — just begun — to write on them.

Because it rains here, or rather, the mountain sweats. Warm air rises all day off the lowlands and dies against this cold shelf of ancient stone, and everything it carries condenses. The rocks weep constantly. Every surface is beaded. Water stands in the shallow basins the plateau has not yet had time to carve deeper, and it seeps from seams in the cliff face in slow bright threads that fall away as mist before they ever reach the ground.

The lushness is absurd at this altitude. Everything growing here has learned to drink from the air. Moss runs up the old machinery in green fur. Ferns the size of dinner plates crowd into every crack. There are stunted, dense, defiant trees — twenty years old and knee-high, their rings like a watch's gears — growing directly out of rust. The vegetation does not sprawl; it presses. The whole summit is layered, saturated, things growing on things growing on rust: moss on lichen on fern on vine on pipe, all of it wet, all of it warm-breathed, all of it crowded into a space you could cross in four hundred steps.

And the machinery. That's the strangest thing, the thing that keeps you turning in circles here. Someone industrialized the sky.

It's old — decades, a century of rust feeding the ferns — but it was built with real confidence. A great horizontal engine block sits at the plateau's center, half-swallowed by green, its flywheel taller than a person. Condensers: vast finned copper arrays, now furred emerald, arranged around the summit's edge like a crown, built to harvest exactly the condensation that happens here naturally — to pull water from air at the top of the world. Pipes run everywhere, underfoot, overhead, into the cliff itself. Some still drip. One, somewhere beneath the moss, still ticks — a slow, patient, mechanical heartbeat, once every eleven seconds, like the mountain keeping time.

Whoever built it worked small and built to last. There's a single stone hut with its roof caved into a garden, a rusted winch at the cliff edge with a cable descending into cloud — toward what, down there? — and hand-painted gauges on the condensers, their dial-faces now colonized by lichen in perfect circles. No town. No mine. Just this one absurd, beautiful gesture: a factory for water, built where water was already falling from the sky, for reasons no one alive can explain.

Fauna is scarce. A pair of pale moths. Something small that skitters under the ferns and is never seen twice. High overhead, once, a bird crossing the impossible gap between this summit and the next — the only moving thing in an otherwise still sky.

The sound is the surprise. You'd expect silence at this altitude, but the plateau is loud with smallness: the constant hiss of mist off the cliff edges, water dripping from a hundred ledges in overlapping rhythms, wind through wet fern, the tick of that buried engine, and beneath it all a low hum — whether from some still-charged component or from the mountain's own young, restless rock settling into itself, you cannot tell. It's the sound of a place mid-sentence.

Everything here moves slowly. The moss advances maybe a hand-span a year. The rust blooms in seasons. The condensation cycle runs its daily round — air climbing, dying, beading, dripping — with the patience of something that has done this forever and will do it long after the machines finish becoming gardens. Even the cliffs, though young, seem in no hurry. The mountain just pushed itself up here into the warm wet sky, recently, violently, in geological yesterday — and now it is resting, sweating, letting the green climb over its shoulders, letting the old water-harvester rust quietly in its embrace, content to be the only room in the sky.

If you stay until evening, the light turns the mist on the cliff edges gold, every drip a falling ember, and the whole summit glows — a small, dense, breathing world, one wet green held breath above the clouds.
