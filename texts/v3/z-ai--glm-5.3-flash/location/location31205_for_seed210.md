The mountain is new, and it will stay that way.

That is the first thing to understand about it, though it takes a visitor a long time to understand anything here, because the second thing — the hum — arrives through the soles of the feet before the eyes have finished with the first. The peak is sharp the way a broken plate is sharp. Its ridges carry the bright, unhacked faces of stone that has never been worn, fracture surfaces still bearing the memory of the single violence that made them, and that violence was recent — is recent, is perpetually recent — though no weather has touched them in what might be ten thousand years, because the weather here does not touch anything. It falls. It does not work.

Partway up the crown, where the rock shoulders level out, there is a terrace. It is small. You can walk it end to end in the time it takes to grow cold, and you can stand in its middle and see all of it at once, which is a strange property for a world to have — the proportions of a room, without any of a room's intentions. A bowl of wet land cupped in young stone, ringed by ridges, sealed above by cloud.

The cloud is the light. There is no sun you could point to, only a sourceless pearl dimness coming from everywhere the sky is, slightly stronger toward one quarter where the overcast holds a deeper glow, like a furnace banked behind wool. It never brightens. It never fails. It has the steadiness of a lamp rather than a sky, and under it everything on the terrace glistens, because everything here sweats. The rock weeps. Water stands in the stone's pores and leaves it in beads that gather and drop at fixed intervals from every overhang, each drip the same size, each striking its pool at the same pitch. The air is thick enough to drink. Your breath leaves you and does not disperse; it hangs, thins, and folds itself into the mist the way a note folds into a chord, and the mist is no thicker for it, and your breath is never entirely gone.

Rain falls, a fine silvered drizzle, always the same rain — not the same pattern, the same rain — and here is the thing that undoes anyone who stays too long: nothing is eroded. The drizzle has been falling onto those sharp edges for an eternity or for no time at all, and not one corner has rounded. No channel has worn in the turf. The water is not a sculptor here. It falls, seeps, rises, and balances, to the drop. Everything the rocks weep upward, the pools take in, and the count comes out even, always. The water is not going anywhere and it is not staying anywhere. It is being water, continuously, at a fixed amplitude.

The ground itself is a sponge — turf and moss deep enough to admit your boot and then close behind it without memory — and the pools lie in the hollows like lenses. Lean over one and you will see that the surface does not lie flat. It stands in ridges. Fine concentric corrugations, hexagonal rosettes, patterns that hold without travelling, because the hum is in the water. The hum is everywhere. It is a low turbine note out of the mountain's interior, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, and when you press your palm to the wet stone you can hear, close now, chambers at work far below. Not caverns — caverns are carved, and nothing here was carved. These were formed, the way a cell forms, finished at the instant of their existence, and they turn, or resonate, or do whatever they do, and no one excavated them and no one assembled them and there is not a bolt, a wire, a pipe, a wall anywhere on the mountain. The mechanism is the geology. Strata wound like armatures. Channels that spiral with the exact section of turbine housings, born that way. Veins of native copper, green-streaked and beaded with condensation, warm to the touch, humming a harmonic above the deep note. The mountain runs. What it runs, it runs for itself: it makes exactly enough power to warm its own skin, drive its own rain, light its own mist, and turn. Its only product is the continuation of its turning. Whether it was made is a question the place does not answer, because nothing in it was made. You can say what it does. You cannot say what it is for. Only one of those questions has an answer here.
