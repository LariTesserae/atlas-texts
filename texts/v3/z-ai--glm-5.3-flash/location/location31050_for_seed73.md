# The Condenser Stair

You reach it by climbing through cloud, and then the cloud stops — not because the air ends, but because something has *ordered* it to stop.

The Stair is a highland the size of a small country, terraced in immense shelves of dark stone that step upward for what the maps (few, old, contested) say is four thousand meters, then five, then more. The air up here should be thin and cold. It is neither. It is hot the way a mouth is hot — wet, thick, breathing — because the whole plateau runs warm from below. Geothermal, the old surveys guessed, before they stopped guessing. The rock sweats. Every cliff face weeps in slow curtains, water beading out of the stone itself as if the mountain were a jar left out in summer.

And it is summer here always. The parameters of the place were set, someone says, long ago, and have held.

The terraces are gardens gone feral — or gardens that were never tended and simply *obeyed*. Lush is a weak word. Moss and fern grow on the vertical faces; hanging vines loop terrace to terrace like rigging on an anchored fleet; groves of broad-leaved trees stand in the warm standing water of the shelf-pans, their roots in water black as tea. Every surface that can hold green does. The air smells of crushed leaves and hot mineral steam and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit — the smell of decomposition moving fast, everything growing and rotting in the same generous heat.

The water is everywhere near the surface. Pools lie between terrace and terrace, connected by trickles that spill over the shelf-edges in long silver thread-lines, thousands of them, so that from above — no one has been above, but the theory goes — the whole Stair must look like a sheet of falling beadwork. Hot springs vent in quiet places, sliding pale mineral scars down the rock. The marsh-pans at each terrace's low side breathe out a haze that never quite lifts, and above the highest visible shelf hangs a permanent soft ceiling of condensed vapor, glowing a pearly, even gray-white. The light here is ordinary daylight, but filtered, doubled, coming from the sun and from the fog at once, so shadows have soft gray twins.

## The Machines

This is the part the surveys struggle with: the place is *maintained*.

Small structures dot the terraces — scattered, sparse, alone. Square housings the size of chests, or of chapels, built of a dark material that is neither metal nor stone, warm to the touch, veined with channels where something moves in slow pulses. Some have sprung hairline fissures from which steam leaks with a sound like a long inhale. They are condensers, the theory says. Atmosphere-harvesters. Nothing so crude as a pump — more like a throat, a lung, an organ. Whatever civilization built them built them to last and then either left or went inside, and the machines continue, patient, on some cycle so long that the terraces themselves have grown around them like bark around a nail. Moss has learned to grow thickest on the warm ones. Vines avoid the cold ones.

Occasionally you find marks of the builders: a step cut into bare rock with no path leading to or from it; a wall six stones long; a ring of standing posts grown through with lianas, purpose unrecoverable. No roads. No roofs. The builders built *systems*, not houses — or their houses were the kind you leave.

## The Fauna

Life is abundant and mostly unhurried. Amphibian-shaped things — some are amphibians, some merely agree with the shape — cluster in the warm pans, broad-mouthed, motionless for hours, then taking one enormous deliberate hop. Birds exist in fewer varieties than the greenness would suggest, but the ones that live here are large, pale, and silent; they ride the updrafts off the hot rock in long slow spirals, and you can watch one circle a single terrace all afternoon. In the deepest marsh-pools, shapes move under the black water with the confidence of things that have never had a predator. Insects rise in humming columns above the warm vents at certain hours, and the birds descend on the columns, and both events happen like clockwork nobody winds.

## The Sound

The Stair is loud the way a rainforest at night is loud, but stretched and slowed: water dripping in ten thousand rhythms that never quite sync; the trickle-spill at every terrace edge, a constant wash like distant applause; steam hissing from a fissure somewhere, always somewhere, always just out of sight; the low chorusing of the marsh-things at dusk, a sound like wet thumbs on glass, rising and falling on some tide of heat you can't feel a gradient of. Underneath it all, if you press your palm to a terrace housing, a hum. Below hearing, more a warmth in the bones. The mountain's engine note.

## The Rules

Everything here is slow and patient, and that is not idleness — it is *calibration*. The Stair holds an exact temperature, an exact humidity, an exact green, and it has held them for longer than the terraces have been eroded into their settled, weathered, comfortable shapes — which is to say, a very long time. The machines do not fail visibly. The seasons do not arrive. Nothing rushes and nothing dies badly; things grow, fruit, drop, rot, and re-grow in the same warm loop, and the loop's only visible motion is water, always water, moving down the Stair in its ten thousand silver threads, going somewhere below the cloud line to be lifted again.

Walk it long enough and you stop trying to get anywhere. You find a warm stone near a spill-thread, you eat something the vines offer, and you listen to the slow loud wet breathing of the highest garden in the world — a place that was engineered to be a paradise for someone, and which has, in their long absence, simply continued, generously, without them.
