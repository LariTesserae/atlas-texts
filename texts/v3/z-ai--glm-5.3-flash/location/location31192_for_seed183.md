## The Weeping Saddle

There is no way down that is not a way up. That is the first thing the place teaches you, and it is wrong, and you will learn to live with it.

The saddle sits between two peaks so high that the air itself has given up on behaving. Below — far below, a kilometer of falling light — the clouds hang in a flat silver sheet like a flooded floor, and everything above that floor belongs to this place: a plateau of black stone slick with water that has no source. It simply *arrives*. The atmosphere here is saturated, condensing constantly, so that the whole world is beaded with moisture — every surface weeps. The cliffs drip. The boulders sweat. Great glassy slabs of obsidian-like rock, fractured and re-fractured over ages, run with a permanent thin film of water, and the light comes down so bright and so white from a sky that has no visible sun — just brightness, everywhere, sourceless — that the wet stone burns to look at.

The rock is the main event. No soil anywhere. No green thing has ever grown here, or if it did, it was so long ago that even the stone has forgotten the chemistry. Instead: mineral life, or something wearing mineral's clothes. The wet basalt terraces are colonized by crusts — black, blue-black, faintly violet — that look like lichen from a distance but are actually something else, something that is not alive the way you'd check for alive. It doesn't breathe. It doesn't grow, exactly. It *accumulates*. It has the slow patience of geology and a geometry that bothers the eye: when you stare at a crust for too long, the pattern in it starts to look deliberate. Fringed. Like script. The locals — and there were locals, once — chipped their tools from ridges where this crust grew hardest, and there's a theory, carved into a shelter wall in careful strokes, that the tools were not really tools.

The built remains are scattered and few, as promised. A shelter here: a low wall of fitted stone, hand-stacked, unmortared, half-collapsed, its interior lined with slabs set on edge to shed the condensation. A cairn there, on a ridge nose, one of a chain of cairns that marches across the saddle and then, disconcertingly, marches *back the other way*, doubled, as if the builders walked the route in both directions and wanted both walks remembered. Broken flakes of glassy stone lie where they were struck off, fanned on the ground like shed petals, undisturbed. Whoever worked here used stone and only stone — no metal anywhere, no cut timber, no fire-blackened rock — and they left centuries or millennia ago, and the wet hasn't rusted or rotted anything because there is nothing here to rust or rot. Their work has simply been *rained on* for all that time, polished at the edges, settled into its own state. The erosion here isn't violent. It's mature. The landscape has finished arguing with itself and reached its shape; the waterfalls have worn their channels to a comfortable depth; the piles of talus at the cliff bases sit in long-term truce with the slopes above them. Everything is in balance. The balance is just a strange one.

Because the water does not always fall.

Mostly it falls — that's the norm, the baseline, the rain-that-never-ended, a fine drifting mist-rain that never commits to being weather. But in places, in the low basins between terrace steps, the water collects in sheets and then climbs. It goes over the rim of its pool and travels uphill in a thin gleaming sheet, silently, against everything, pooling again at the next level, and the next, ascending the saddle in flat silver stairsteps toward the crest. Nobody knows where it goes from the crest. Standing at the top of one of these climbs, watching the water arrive from below and slide past your boots and over the edge of the world, you understand that the water is not confused. It is *going somewhere*. The saddle is on one of its routes. The weeping of the rocks, the constant condensation, the mist — it may all be the same thing: a circulation, a vast slow loop of water passing through this altitude on its way to a place that is not, strictly speaking, downhill of anything.

The sound is enormous and continuous. Loudness without an obvious source: a deep layered roar that comes partly from the waterfalls, partly from wind that moves through the saddle in standing waves, partly from — this is the part that takes adjustment — the *light*. Brightness this total, this sourceless, seems to have an acoustic component here, a white hiss underlying everything, the sound of radiance under pressure. And the echoes are wrong. Shout at the cliff wall and the echo returns before your shout has finished leaving you — arrives early, or arrives in your own voice pitched slightly wrong, or arrives as the sound of dripping, no matter what you said. The stone walls seem to hold *prior* sounds. Lean close to the shelter wall and put your ear against it, and you can hear, faintly, the rhythm of a hand striking stone against stone. Chip. Chip. Chip. Still going. Maybe it never stopped. Maybe the sound of the work soaked into the rock the way water soaks into it, and this place, which lets nothing leave — the water loops, the light loops, the sound loops — keeps everything.

The weirdness has a texture rather than a list. Distances misbehave at the saddle's edges: walk toward a prominent spire and it recedes at a rate slightly out of proportion to your steps, so that you arrive at it later than geometry allows, or earlier. Shadows fall in mild disagreement with whatever brightness casts them — yours can trail you by half a step, or point the wrong way, and you learn not to look down at your feet too often. Twice a day, on no schedule anyone could call regular, the whole plateau *shivers* — not an earthquake, nothing moves — a shimmer passes through the air like heat-haze in a cold place, and for the duration of it, perhaps a breath, perhaps ten seconds, the saddle is replaced by a version of itself that is almost identical but not quite: the cairns are a little taller, or the shelter stands whole, or the pool on the third terrace is on the left instead of the right. Then it shivers again and you are back, and the only proof is the feeling of having briefly been two places, and the way the crust-lights on the rock have rearranged, just slightly, like a sentence someone re-worded while you blinked.

And through all of it: the water. The water is the constant. It is everywhere, at a temperature that never quite warms — cool, not cold, the chill of a cellar rather than a glacier — and it is *busy*. The plateau is a machine of water at rest: mist condensing, sheets drifting, channels running, pools climbing, the great cliff-faces weeping in a thousand threaded streams that braid and unbraid down the black stone. The dynamic here isn't biological — nothing eats anything, nothing grows toward light — it's hydraulic and luminous and possibly ceremonial. The place is in motion the way a clock is in motion. Processes. Cycles. The shimmer passes. The light brightens and dims on some tide of its own. The water completes another lap of its impossible ascent.

Whoever the toolmakers were, they came to the highest wet bright place in the world, at the edge of what holds together, and they worked stone beside the climbing water, and they stacked their cairns in both directions, and then they left — or became the script in the crust, or the sound in the wall — and the saddle kept everything of them, because that is what this place does. It keeps. It circulates. It weeps and shines and roars, quietly going about its one long errand, and the clouds below it hold their silver floor, and nothing falls that does not rise again.

If you stand very still at the crest, at the top of one of the climbing sheets, and watch the water go over — it goes over. You can watch it. It does not come back down the other side.

It comes back up behind you.
