**The Glasshouse**

The plain above it gives nothing away — hard, pale ground, heat-shimmer, a scatter of stones. Then a hole, narrow as a well, and sixty meters down a widening, and then the crack that lets you sideways into the one room under the world. You understand the name you'll give it before you're fully inside, in the moment the shaft's light finds you: the air is thick, bright, and hot the way an opened oven is hot, and every surface is wet and shining, and it is all, unmistakably, glass.

Not glass the material — glass the behavior. The chamber is perhaps fifty paces across, domed, its floor a shallow bowl of fresh rubble. The rock is young enough that it hasn't learned to be subtle: black-blue basalt broken in conchoidal faces like bottle glass, edges that could draw blood, fracture scars still dusted with powder. Nothing here is rounded. Nothing has begun to slump or soften. Whatever opened this room did it recently — a season ago, a year — and the room has spent its whole short life getting shinier.

The inventory is short. A shaft of daylight, three meters wide, lying on the floor like a dropped coin. Three pools of different sizes, set well apart in fracture basins, each holding its own water with its own faint chemistry. A single seep wall where the mountain weeps — ten meters of dark stone beaded with moisture, a marsh stood on end. Two young cones of calcite growing from the floor, glazed like poured sugar, still sharp. One fallen slab. One steam vent, whispering. Everything else is space and glare, and the space between presences is the larger part of the room.

The light is the first thing that doesn't add up — in a good way. One hole in the roof should mean one bright spot and a great darkness. Instead the whole dome works. The chalcedony glaze that skins the ceiling — thin where it's new, thicker where the seeps have fed it — carries light sideways, edge-lit, so the ceiling itself glows a hot milk-white and throws it down everywhere. There are almost no shadows in this room, only gradations of glare. The single beam moves through the day like a slow searchlight: coin-shaped on the gravel at mid-morning, crawling up the seep wall by afternoon, a column of lit mist at the hour when evaporation is thickest. It is, if you let yourself think it, a lamp with one wick — and the wick is the sun.

The heat is a body. Fifty degrees at floor level by noon, hotter along the seep wall where the water comes up warm, hottest at the vent, whose breath fogs your glasses and hisses at the volume of a whisper. The air shimmers over the pools. And the hot spots move. Not fast — a pace a week, perhaps — but they migrate, circling the floor like slow animals, so that no single place is ever scoured for long. You can find yesterday's scald ring still faintly warm, and today's a few meters off, and between them the thin film of life on the stone has survived both. It always survives. Just.

The water is the room's whole argument. Warm air climbs off the pools and gravel, touches the cooler dome, beads, merges, and falls back as a fine drizzle most afternoons — the room drinking its own weather, rising as vapor in the morning, returning as rain by four. The pools rise a finger's width by evening. By morning the seeps and the evaporation have traded it all back. Nothing leaves; nothing floods. The seep wall is stitched with hairline channels, spaced too evenly, like sutures, and each channel drips on its own metronome — this one every three seconds, that one every seven, another at eleven — steady as clocks. When one slows, its neighbor thickens, and the sum, you discover over an afternoon of watching, holds. It always holds.

That's the second thing that doesn't add up. A crack that begins to open gets a mineral cast within weeks; the old micro-fractures in the glaze are all sutured, all of them, in a pattern with no randomness in it. The pools each keep their own level and their own dissolved minerals, in air hot enough that salts should crust every margin white — and they never crust, or they crust and are re-dissolved in the night without your hearing it. Where a flake fell from the dome recently, taking a piece of glaze with it, the scar is already regrown, smooth, edgeless — in a room that is otherwise all edges. And the mist, when it forms, hangs in layers at set heights, like shelves.

None of this is machinery. Walk the whole chamber and you will not find one part you can point to — no seam of workmanship, no tool mark, no hinge, no artifact, not a bolt or a bead of anything made. But the whole behaves the way mechanisms behave. It holds its settings. It shares out its heat, keeps its sums, heals its damage, feeds its thin life exactly enough. And it is new — freshly broken open, sharp-featured, weeks from its own birth — and it is already, completely, the thing it intends to remain. Young and final. Those are not supposed to go together. Whatever set the settings did not stay, and did not leave so much as a fingerprint-shaped grain of dust out of place.

So there is no plant in this glasshouse, and never was. The only life is simpler and older than gardening: films. A rust-orange scum where the water runs hottest, a gray-green sheen where the beam lingers longest, a near-black margin at the vent's lip — all of it breath-thin, slippery, less a carpet than a stain that learned to eat light. Grazing on the films are pale pinhead creatures, amphipod-shaped, translucent enough that you see their dark holds before you see them. They are rare the way furniture in a large house is sparse: wait an hour by a pool, and you'll be shown two. And once a day, around noon, the visitors come down the shaft — small rust-dark flies, a handful, no more, dropping out of the bright pipe of air from the world above. They stand on the warm wet stone with their wings half-open like a gesture of cooling, they drink from the film, they lay nothing that you can find, and they are always gone before the beam withdraws. They are the only thing that enters this room from outside and leaves again. The room does not seem to mind them. It also does not seem, in any way you could testify to, to need them.

The quiet is engineered too, or behaves as if it were. The sounds here are small and spaced: the tick of a drop reaching a pool; the drier double-tap of a drop on bare glaze; the vent's whisper; the metronomes, three and seven and eleven, holding their sums. When weather crosses the plain above, the chimney exhales a warm slow wind and every drip interval shifts by half a second, all together, and returns — the room's one barometer. In storm season the shaft sounds a single low note, one tone only, and stops. The dome eats echo; shout, and the sound comes back flat and close, without a tail, as if the room had intercepted it and considered it. The afternoon drizzle, when it comes, is rain on leaves with the leaves removed — a hush of a thousand small impacts that never once gets loud.

Toward evening the beam climbs the seep wall and, for a few minutes, the whole fan of weeping water lights up like struck metal, every channel a burning thread, the beads a spill of coins. Then the sun slides past the shaft's mouth and the room dims to a hot gray gloom, and the temperature begins its long relaxation toward the stone's own warmth, and nothing in the behavior changes at all. The metronomes go on — three, seven, eleven. Somewhere above, the mist is condensing in its shelves. The films are fed. The sums hold.

You stand sweating in the one bright room under the plain, listening to water count, and you understand that this is what the place is: not a cave, not a spring, not a ruin of anything. A kept thing, keeping itself, with no keeper in evidence — young, exact, and patient as a clock — waiting under sixty meters of ordinary rock for a world that may never check on it again.
