# The Sough

The flats come first, and they come on for a long time.

They are green-grey and soaked and level to the point of insult — a mat of rooted vegetation so old and so uniformly trampled by its own history that it no longer rises, dips, or surprises. Nothing grows tall out here. Nothing dies in a way that leaves a mark. You could walk the flats in any direction for a season and the scenery would not change so much as continue. The only event the plain permits itself is a low ring on the horizon, pale as a scar, which is where you are going.

The ring is the rim of a basin, and the basin is the whole world's one exception.

It is small. Six hundred paces across, maybe less, and the rim never rises higher than your shoulder — a circle of rootstone, which is what the ground makes when it wants to keep its water: limestone excreted over eons by roots, braided through with fossil channels like handwriting no one reads anymore. The stone is warm and sweating. Lay your palm on it and it comes away damp, and the damp smells faintly of everything that has ever leaned here.

Inside the rim, the air changes before the view does. It thickens. It goes warm — blood-warm, breath-warm — and sweet with transpiration, and you understand that you are no longer breathing weather. You are breathing the town's exhalation.

Sound arrives next: a chord with no instrument in it, low and woody and continuously shifting key. This is the sough itself — wind moving over the hollow throats of the pump-towers, each throat tuned by its moisture content, the whole basin humming like a struck bowl that never fully settles. The tenders say the town was named for its noise, and that *sough* means both *a drain* and *a sigh*, depending which elder you ask. Both are correct.

Then you see it, and the first thing you see is that it is airy.

---

There is no soil in the Sink. That bears repeating, because your eyes will insist otherwise. What looks like meadow is the felt: a matted tissue of moss over moss over root over root, knitted past the point where one plant ends and another begins, compressed by its own dead into a living peat that has been the geology here for longer than stone bothers to be. Step on it and it firms to meet you — not springy, not yielding, but *attentive*, the way a sleeping animal firms when you rest a hand on its flank. Kneel and it will hold the shape of your knees until the next rain. Walk the same line every day for a season and it will lean into your habit and give you a smooth ribbon of path, because the felt is not intelligent but it is suggestible, and it has opinions about where you should go.

Dig a handspan anywhere and water stands in the print before you've straightened up, already inhabited by skaters the size of commas. The whole floor of the basin is a sponge that finished learning how to be ground several million years ago and has simply kept the lesson.

Water shows itself everywhere and pools almost nowhere. The rim stones weep. Liana gutters — grown along the eaves of the town's houses — drip in tuned rhythms, some of them deliberately musical, carillons you could fix with shears. At the center lies the Lens, a shallow mere no deeper than a bath, mirror-skimmed by hand every dawn with long boards, because a still surface here is not a given but a maintenance. Dig anywhere else and you find canals — patient, narrow, rerouted seasonally, each one a decision the water and the town came to together.

And the water is old in the personal sense. The basin is a closed condenser: the towers drink from the table, transpire at their crowns, the fog condenses under the ordinary milky light, and it rains — briefly, warmly, on schedule — and seeps back down, and climbs again. The tenders do not think of this as a cycle. They think of it as a household. The water in your tea has been through the lungs of every mayor this town has ever had, and most of its plumbers.

---

The town itself is scattered the way stars are scattered — placed, but with room to breathe.

There are perhaps three hundred structures, and calling them structures is already a lie of category. Nothing here was built. Everything was *grown awake*, which is different, and the difference is the civilization. The houses are galls: the tenders irritate chosen trees in patterns held secret by guild law, and the trees swell in response, and the swellings are harvested as dwellings — smooth-walled, iron-hard, warm to the touch, fitted with door-sphincters that relax open to a knock-code.
