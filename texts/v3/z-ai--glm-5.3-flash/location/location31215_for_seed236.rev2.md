There is no water here, and nothing misses it.

The place is a shelf of green on the shoulder of a young mountain — a terrace a few hundred paces across, cupped into the rock like a leaf held in a stone hand. Stand at its rim and look down, and you see the world it was lifted out of: a plain of pale salt running to the edge of sight, cracked into hexagons like the skin of something long dried. Nothing moves down there. The light comes up off that whiteness so hard and so clean that the green at your back feels like a warmth against the back of your neck.

The salt was left by some water that departed before anything here ever learned to need it. That is the first rule of the place, though no one states it: nothing drinks. The green that carpets the terrace arrived later, carrying a different thirst. It drinks light, and it eats stone.

The mountain itself is young in a way that takes getting used to. Its faces have never been touched. No rain has ever softened them, no frost has ever pried at their seams, no wind — there is almost no wind here, ever — has come to round them off. The fracture faces are as crisp as quarry cuts, the edges of the boulders still knife-sharp, and the whole shelf sits in the middle of this newness like a kept garden. Some of the sharpness is kept that way on purpose: a pale velvet grows on the cliff faces, a mineral moss that grazes the rock rather than the other way around, planing the surfaces it colonizes to a strange polish. Where the velvet has worked, stone is smooth as sea glass. Where it hasn't, stone is a blade. The terrace is a negotiation between the two.

The rock is warm. Not merely sun-warm, though the sun helps — the stone heaved itself up from somewhere deep not long ago, in the way of slow things, and it still carries a little of that deep heat in its body the way a loaf carries the oven an hour after baking. Lay your palm anywhere and the mountain is the temperature of a sleeping animal. All night it pays that warmth back out, and everything here lives on the installment plan of that heat. Warmth is the second crop, harvested after the light goes.

The vegetation has a glassy patience to it. Grass grows in blades of near-quartz, translucent, green shot through with gold, and when the sun crosses the terrace the light passes through and comes out the other side changed — the ground is laid with panels of borrowed green-gold, shifting as the leaves lean on their slow hinges. Trees wear bark like poured wax, faintly glossy, splitting at the joints to bleed resin in slow amber beads. The sap is oil and resin; nothing in this place has ever needed to be thin and liquid. The flowers are the slowest of all: wax-petaled cups that take three days to open and three days to close, filling meanwhile with a nectar of warm resin that smells like honey cut with hot stone.

The air lies still in layers. With no wind to stir it, scent does not scatter — it settles, in strata, like water finding its levels. Near the ground it is crushed green and warm mineral. At the height of a raised hand it is the honey-resin of the flowers. Above that, something drier, like sunned skin and dust of polished rock. You can walk through them like doorways. You can stand still in one and feel it on your face the way you'd feel entering a room.

The fauna keep to the quiet with an discipline that seems almost liturgical. Nothing here calls out. Nothing needs to. In a world where all time is thick, urgency is a waste, and sound is urgency made physical. The creatures you meet are abundant — the shelf is crowded with life, layered and near-touching — but they are all, without exception, silent as snowfall.

Waxmoths the size of-open hands glide on broad slow wings above the canopy, feeding at the resin cups, and when they land they fold like letters closing. Deep among the trees stands a browser tall as two men and nearly as still, gray-waxed and patient, browsing the glass grass with the unhurried attention of furniture; the town was built around it, lanes bending to accommodate its customary routes, and it moves through the settlement the way an old oak stands in a village square. Small darting things swim in the scent-layers themselves, holding to fixed depths like fish in still water, feeding on the aromatics the flowers exhale. And everywhere, trimming, pruning, grazing the excess: the pruners, who keep the wild edges wild-shaped, who are the reason the cliff faces read like a tended garden rather than a ruin.

The town is here too, and it is the strangest thing of all: architecture without tools, settlement without fire, built by the masons.

They are four-handed, dust-colored, the size of children, and they build the way plants grow — by millimeters, without end. There are hundreds of them. They fit stone by feel and by sound that isn't sound: each block is tapped, tapped, tapped against its neighbors until its hum matches theirs, masonry tuned by resonance, ears in the palms. When a stone sings in harmony with its wall, it is done. The mortar is resin chewed with rock dust, laid in amber threads that set like honey poured in winter. The windows are cast panes of the same resin, honey-dark, glowing when the sun passes through them so that at certain hours the whole town looks lit from inside by slow fire.

It is dense the way a reef is dense. Terraces stack on terraces; chambers are carved into the warm living rock below; bridges of woven root and resin rope stitch tower to tree; the roofs are meadows — planted, not built, so that the roof of one house is the garden of another and the shade below is a crop. Nothing is ever finished. A wall ten years old stands knee-high and will stand waist-high in ten more, and this is not slowness of ambition; it is the town's metabolism. Building and growing are the same verb here.

And instead of a well — there being nothing to draw — the town keeps a light-well: an open shaft in the stone, lined with polished mineral, that collects the sun the way a well collects rain. A column of warm brightness. In the still of the afternoon, creatures lie around its rim with their flanks in the brightness, drinking, in whatever sense that word applies to a mouthful of sun.

The talk — and there is talk, constant, layered, dense — happens in the stone. The purrers conduct it: long-bodied creatures that lie along the warm walls and drum with their bellies, sending slow tremors through the rock, and the mountain, being young and continuous, carries every message everywhere. A conversation can take an afternoon to cross the terrace and no one minds. Rest your palm on any wall and you will feel a sentence go by — a pressure, a rhythm, felt in the meat of the hand and the bone of the wrist — words in no language you know, moving at the speed of patient.

The silence of the air, then, is not emptiness. It is a pressure. Your own footsteps sound enormous when you first arrive, a catastrophe of noise, and you learn quickly to walk the way everything here walks: placed, deliberate, slow. Your breath sounds like weather.

Time is the last thing, and the largest. The petals open over days. The stone pays out its heat through every evening. The town rises one tuned course each season. And the mountain is not finished rising — the shelf gains a finger-width every generation, the uplift still pushing, which is why nothing here has ever been rounded off: the ground keeps presenting fresh edges faster than anything could dull them. Even erosion is a process too fast for this place, and it has been politely declined.

Near evening the sun goes low and the light through the resin panes goes deep orange, and the whole terrace warms a final degree as if the stone were clearing its throat. The waxmoths fold down onto the canopy. The big browser stops moving, if it was moving. The scent-layers settle and thicken. And if you put your hand flat on the warm rock and wait — really wait, longer than feels reasonable — you will feel the town's day being told back to itself in the stone, a long sentence, years in the saying, and beneath it, fainter, older, still rising: the mountain's own word, which is nearly finished, and has been nearly finished since before anything here learned to drink.
