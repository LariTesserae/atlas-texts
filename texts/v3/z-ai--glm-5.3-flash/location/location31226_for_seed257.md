# The Bath at the Top of the Deep

The valley drowned long ago, and the water never gave anything back. It is a modest sea — a flooded valley, not an ocean; a strong swimmer could cross its whole grey-bright width in a morning — but it is deep where it matters, and it is warm all the way down, warm as held hands, because the same springs that filled it still push up through it from below. Nothing grows here. Not weed, not grass, not the smallest algal thread. The water is the only thing in the district that was never made, and everything that lives, lives on what the stone itself gives up.

And the district has exactly one high place.

It is the top of a tower, and the top of the tower is ninety feet under the surface. That is the first thing to understand about this place, the fact that reorders everything else: the summit of the world is underwater, and has been for longer than anyone can say. The valley people — the ones who come out from the far shores in good-weather years — call the tower the House, and call its uppermost room the Bath, and both names are older than anyone can account for. To visit, you go down. You pass out of the sunlit layer, past the cool middle water, into the warm dark, and the highest point of the district rises up beneath you like a mountain being handed over in reverse.

The summit chamber is small. Eight paces by six, with a ceiling low enough that a tall swimmer's fins brush it. It could be crossed in four strides, and people have, and it never feels small to them afterward. This is the whole roof of the world, and it is the size of a room.

Every surface of it was decided by hands. There is no natural substrate anywhere in the room, no face of stone that someone did not pick or chisel or rub smooth. The walls carry guide-flutes cut in long parallel rows, to lead water where water was wanted. A bench runs two sides, undercut with a groove for sitting. Three basins sink into the floor, and the largest of these is the heart of the whole district, though you would not know it at first.

Here is what the old masons did, whether they meant to or not: they built their house directly over the spring. The warm water that comes up out of the deep rock rises now through the old cut channels, exactly as it rose before the flood, and it comes up in the principal basin and spills over its lip in a veil about as thick as a thumb. The veil is the only weather the room has. It shimmers — there is heat-shimmer underwater, at this one spot, a wobbling in the water like the look of air over a road in summer — and it is a hand's-breadth warmer than the room, and everything in the room knows exactly where it is without looking.

The spring is also the second mason. Warm water full of dissolved stone deposits its mineral as it cools, and in the centuries since the flood it has laid a smooth skin of travertine over every surface it touches — rounding the bench edges, softening the flutes, filling the chisel-work the way snow softens a furrow. The room is being re-carved by something with no hands, patiently, toward a shape that is smoother and rounder and more like the spring itself. The work is nearly done. Another few hundred years and the last crisp tool-marks will go. The room is close, now, to its final form.

Almost. The masons' marks survive in the shaded grooves — the tiny pick-dents, the mason's test-taps — and they survive for an odd reason: the animals eat everything that would blur them. A film grows over the stone, and the grazers rasp it away, and their mouths follow the chisel lines the way a tongue follows a cracked tooth. The carvings are maintained now by snails. It is the sort of arrangement this world makes without remarking on it.
