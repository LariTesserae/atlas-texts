Call it the Greenwork. *Green* is the masons' word — green clay, green mortar, glaze not yet set — meaning unfinished, still becoming, still soft enough to change. Everything here is green in that sense. Nothing in this place has ever finished.

You come up a long ramp of fired clay and step out onto the floor.

The floor is the world. It runs flat in every direction until haze takes it, a plain of pressed tile in the color of fired earth, warm through the soles of your feet the way a sleeping animal is warm. From a distance it looks machined — a seamless continent of terracotta — but up close every tile is a hand's width and no two agree. You can see where a float was drawn across a bay in long curved strokes; you can tell, in some districts, that the maker worked the trowel left-handed. The whole world is handmade all the way down, and the hands were many, and they left their signatures in the only way hands have: in the slight, stubborn irregularity of things.

It is quiet here, and it is empty, and both of those seem deliberate. A pier rises from the plain with an arch half-thrown from its shoulder and stops mid-thought. A survey string crosses the haze on taut catenaries, glazed weights ticking in the still air, and from its line you can see the glint of the next string, kilometers off, and nothing between them but floor. The emptiness is not absence. It is laid down as carefully as the tile — a material, poured in slabs, balanced against the built the way a mason balances a wall against its opening. You walk for hours through the nothing and understand that the nothing was placed here on purpose.

Everything weeps. That is the first thing the water teaches you. Every vertical surface — pier, wall, the flanks of the half-arches — sweats a constant film, beads tracking down in slow channels, gathering at the base in dark lines. The world is plumbed for its own tears: gutters run along every foot where wall meets floor, conducting the weeping away to shallow basins that stand between the built sections like puddles of sky. The basins are hot, mirror-flat, without a ripple, and in them you can see the ceiling perfectly — the sky of this place, upside down. That is the best way to see it. Looking up, the vault is a low glaze of black-blue tile, unfinished in great patches, and the patches are hard to look at. Looking down into a basin, the missing sections of sky become pale rectangles of grey-pearl light floating in the reflection, sourceless and soft, the light of the not-yet. There is no sun here. There is only that, and the warm drizzle that falls from the condensing vault — no wind in it, straight down, unhurried — the Greenwork making its own weather out of its own sweat.

The heat is the world's age. Curing is a fire that eats slowly, and a floor this size gives off its setting-heat the way a body gives off warmth — except the floor is continental, so the warmth is weather. Fresher ground is hotter; you learn to read the terrain by your own discomfort, walking a bay poured that morning through shimmering air, your shadow wavering at the edges. And below, somewhere under the floor, the foundation fires run — kilns the size of districts, firing the whole world like a pot in a closed oven. Thin seams in the floor let the glow through, bright lines you learn not to stand on. Once in a long while a seam opens wider and molten glaze wells up and pools, and this is the brightest thing that ever happens here: a slow silent flood of orange-white spreading across the tile, and the crew-shapes gather at a respectful distance to watch it, and do not leave until it cools.

The crews. You will see them, and you will not meet them. Always at middle distance, always in profile, always bent to the work — passing tiles hand to hand along a wall that was not there the day before, up simple scaffold poles of fired clay, trowels of fired clay in their hands, floats of porous vitreous stone, plumb lines with glazed bobs. They never straighten. They never arrive. Walk to where they were and find no footprints but your own, and a wall that is now real, and still warm. Whether the crews are the world's laborers or only its memory of labor — whether the Greenwork is being built or merely repeating, forever, what was once done to it — is not a question the place answers.

The water knows more than the crews do. There are times, irregular, when every drip on the continent stops at once — all the weeping walls, all the gutters, all the slow drizzle from the vault — one held breath, silence across thousands of kilometers. Every basin shivers though nothing touches it. Every survey string dips, as if a weight had been added somewhere out of sight. Then comes the revision. A district goes soft. Towers lean like warmed wax and lose their edges, and by next dark they are three low terraces and a long wall, cordoned by lines of small ceramic flags that no one plants. This is the rule of the Greenwork and it admits no exceptions: **nothing here erodes**. Nothing rusts, rots, cracks with frost, or wears smooth. Nothing is ruined. Things are only *revised* — recalled, softened, re-laid. The concept of ruin has not been invented yet. Age is not counted in years but in passes of the float: this wall is four floatings old, that basin two revisions deep. Old does not exist. Everything you can see is younger than its own glaze.

And so there is no erosion anywhere, and no decay, and nothing at all that grows — except the efflorescence. On new masonry, overnight, the salts bloom: white mineral flowers, feathering and ferning across the tile in crisp crystal fronds, sweet-smelling of lime, brittle, dead to the touch. They are the nearest thing to a plant the Greenwork has. They do not turn toward the light. They turn toward the drips.

Near the uncured ground, the rules go soft. There are sump-deeps in the low places where the floor was never finished, standing slurry the color of unkept promises, and walking their edges you feel distance lose its footing — a straight string measured at a thousand paces is fourteen hundred coming back. Echoes return before their causes: say a word and hear it twice, once before your mouth moves. Strings hum a half-tone below their note for an hour before a revision, so the surveyors — the sparse, patient walkers who read the strings — have learned to listen more than look.

It should be loud here. A continent settling, pours the size of provinces, districts dissolving into slurry and being re-laid — this is turbulence on a scale that elsewhere would be called storm. But the vapor-dense air muffles everything, and there is no life to raise a clamor, and so the Greenwork does its churning quietly. The loudest sound it makes is the groan of a district finding its seat, and you feel that in your sternum an hour before you hear it, a deep note coming up through the warm tile like a word spoken from under a table. Mostly what you hear is small: drips landing in basins tuned by their own depth, an accidental carillon, notes that rise as the water slowly falls. The pings of tile cooling. Your own steps.

Because the floor records. Fresh ground takes a footprint and keeps it, and this world is young enough that nothing will ever wear it away. Somewhere — the walkers speak of it — there is a district that is nothing but footprints, thousands of them, fired permanent, the archive of everything that has ever crossed. Your steps are being added to it now. Curing behind you in single file, becoming geology.

At the dark-of-curing the vault seams brighten faintly, and the floor breathes its stored heat upward, and the steam stands over the fresh pours in slow white columns taller than any built thing — the only trees this world will ever grow, made of its own water, fed by its own warmth, dissolving as they rise. The heat has gone down by a fraction of a degree. The world is one float-stroke older, one revision closer to finished, and it does not hurry.

You look back for the ramp you came by. There is only floor, and your footprints leading out of the haze, cooling into permanence — the only animal-shaped feature in a world of geometry, which makes you, for as long as you are here, the closest thing the Greenwork has to life. It is keeping you.
