The sea left this place in the old dryings, and it left nothing behind but its floor.

Above, the salt goes out flat to every horizon — a plain of white-gray crust, planed by wind, cracked into hexagons by a thousand centuries of heat it has no water to relieve. The sky here is a low milk-colored dome, bright without being light, a haze that gives everything a dim, even gleam and casts almost no shadow. There are no mirages. The air has no water in it, not even false water to offer. Nothing lives up here. The salt is clean of even a lichen's ghost.

The only feature on all that flatness is a breathing crack in the ground.

The people who live at the bottom of it call the crack the Chimney and the place below the Bed. Travelers find it by warmth before they see it: a seam in the salt a long stride wide, exhaling a steady column of hot, resin-smelling air. No salt rims its lips — the crust has melted and reglazed into dark glass there, and for a hundred paces around, the white plain is bare, roasted, faintly red-stained. A stair has been cut into the shaft, hand-hold by hand-hold, spiraling down through salt, then through old seabed stone gone the color of overbaked bread. The stair roars. The Bed inhales through it, twice a day, and this — not footsteps, not voices — is the sound that announces visitors: the wind of the descent, warm on the face, utterly dry.

The Bed itself is a bowl you could shout across. A bowshot wide, perhaps a little more, sunk below the floor of the dead sea like a last thumbprint pressed into it. Its walls have long since weathered into their final shape: ribbed and stable, streaked with mineral weep that never weeps, skirted by talus slopes sorted so patiently by centuries of wind that the rubble lies graded like grain — fines near the floor, slabs near the rim. Nothing up there changes anymore. The place is worn into its own state, settled, at rest with itself.

Except below. Below, everything moves.

The floor of the bowl is a dark, fused tile — the sea's last mud, baked to ceramic by the fire that still smolders underneath. It is warm everywhere. Stand on it barefoot and you can feel the seam's slow pulse. Here and there the floor vents: narrow seams that breathe upward, the people call them the Nostrils, and at the bowl's center lies the Hearth — a long fissure glowing dull red under a lens of fused sand thick enough to walk on. In the cold hours the gardeners lie along that lens on their bellies and warm themselves like lizards, and the stone under them ticks and shifts as it expands.

There is no water in the Bed. There never has been. No dew forms, no condensation runs, no rain has ever fallen through the Chimney — the milk sky above is a salt-haze, and it gives only light's cousin, light once-removed. Everything in the bowl is built, grown, and fed without a drop. Mortar is unknown here; every wall is dry-fitted stone, tacked at the joints with sand melted to glass over the vents. The buildings stand because their stones are patient and their joints are literally fused.

And the vegetation — this is the Bed's paradox, its whole trick — the vegetation is lush.

It grows only over warmth. Where the seam runs near, the gardens arrive like a tide. Torch-trees stand with their heartwood gently smoldering, bark warm to the palm, peeling off in char-flakes and regrowing. Foil-vines — bronze-leaved, mirror-thin — run across open floor at a pace you can almost watch, whole crews of pruners chasing them back from the vents each shift with hooked bronze blades, because a vent swallowed by vine is a vent that stops breathing. Chimegrass covers the warm ground in fine silica blades that tick against each other in any breath of air. And everywhere in the gardens rise the lampfleur: pale bulbs that drink heat all through the bright half of the day and spend it after dark as a soft gold glow, so the groves become the town's streetlamps without anyone lighting anything.

The lushness is concentrated, and it is the concentration that saves you. Step three paces past a garden's edge and you're back on bare fused tile, hot, resonant, empty to the far wall. The green-bronze thickets sit over the hot spots like islands; the rest of the bowl is a vast dark floor with almost nothing on it, and the emptiness is not neglect. It is the plan.

Because the Bed is a city. A small one — a town's worth of people at most — but organized like a place that has thought hard about its streets. The lanes are rings, curved concentrically around the Hearth, each one following a line of equal warmth the way other cities follow rivers. The innermost ring is gardens only; too hot to sleep there. The middle rings hold the houses, spaced far, far apart — an empty plaza between every two homes, a whole empty district between clusters — because warmth is territory here, and privacy, and safety. The outer ring, nearest the cool walls, holds the granaries of char-grain, the seed-vault, the silent houses where things are kept that must not be warmed. And off to the west stands the Ghostring: the cold foundations of a quarter the city abandoned generations ago, its stone carried east panel by panel, its glass joints gone cloudy, kept empty on purpose. The seam is slowly walking. The city walks with it, a few paces a lifetime. Ask anyone where the town will be in two hundred years and they point, precisely, at a patch of floor that is only mildly warm today.

No beasts live here. That is the first law, the covenant the gardeners recite to every child: *nothing that drinks water may live in the Bed.* Nothing that drinks water could. The air would end it in a day. So there are no dogs, no doves, no bees, no beetles in the litter, no burrowers in the warm soil. Pollination is done by wind and by hand — children go out with fiber brushes during the flowering tide and work the groves row by row. It is the quietest ecology in the world, and the people fill the gap with their own noise: call-signs across the plazas, the knock of pruning hooks, songs for the mowing.

Every house is a pipe. That is not a metaphor — each dwelling is built with tuned flues that catch the standing wind and drone, and each household cuts its flue to its own note. The town hums, a low distributed chord that changes when families change and swells enormously when the wind-tide turns: at the change of the bright half, the whole bowl inhales through the Chimney, the great stair roars like an organ's lowest pipe, every house joins in, and for a quarter hour the Bed is genuinely loud. Then it settles back to its chord. Under everything runs the seam's own voice — a deep subsonic breath, and sometimes a muffled thump from far below as a coal chamber folds. When that happens the whole bowl answers: a long, resonant hum, like a struck vessel. The people pause and wait out the note before speaking. They say the Bed rings because it is thin there, in the way a good pot is thin.

Sometimes after a deep thump comes charfall: an hour of fine black flakes drifting down from the vents, warm as fresh bread, soft as the snow the Bed has never seen. Children catch it on bark plates. Adults plow it into the garden beds, because in the Bed, soil is manufactured — char and leaf-litter and crushed chimegrass, turned by hand, year over year.

They eat from the gardens and drink the tarsap — a thick dark sap drawn cool from the torch-tree roots, which does water's work in the blood but is not water and behaves nothing like it, and no one in the Bed quite asks it to explain itself. They forge small bronze tools at ventside kilns. They blow glass. They keep open flame rationed and ceremonial — *a flame is a mouth,* the elders say, *and the Bed has enough mouths* — so light comes mostly from the place itself: the milk column from above that slants down the shaft and slowly crosses the floor like a slow clock, the red seams below, the gold groves after dark. By day every person and post casts two shadows, one pale and one red. At night, mostly only the red one remains, and the people sit out on the warm tile in the loose pools of lampfleur light, and the town's chord lowers, and nothing anywhere makes an animal sound.

It is loud, the Bed — but loud the way a kiln is loud, or a held chord: wind and drone and tick and the deep underground breath, all of it the place's own voice. It is hot, and dry as pumice down to the last grain, and dim, and mostly empty, and lush only exactly where life is possible. It is hidden so well that its whole existence is one crack in a salt plain, and it has been in balance for longer than its people keep count — a city of hands built over a fire, tending the garden that filters the fire's breath, following the warmth generation by generation across the floor of a sea that died before anyone remembers.

The garden is always arriving. The people are always receiving it. The Bed breathes, twice a day, and hums, and waits out its own long notes.
