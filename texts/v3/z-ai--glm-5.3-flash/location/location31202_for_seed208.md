# The Mill

The sound arrives first. From any direction, over any distance, it arrives first: a rolling layered roar that you take for wind until you realize wind doesn't pulse, and doesn't keep time. Then the fog opens its gray curtain for a moment and you see where you are — a country so flat it looks not finished but *finished with*, planed down past argument, the last surface of a world that has been wearing itself away for longer than anything has had a name for. There is no horizon. There is a seam: pale sky above, paler water below, and you standing in the thin wet seam where they meet.

This is the Mill. The people who live on it have no other name for it, because no other name is needed. Everything here is the same act, performed endlessly: mountains, once, somewhere far back beyond myth — real ones, high ones, the color green that no one living has seen except as a story — ground down by water into mud, mud into silt, silt into film, film into flesh. The plain is the millfloor. The food that everything eats was rock. When the people crack open a snail the size of a fist and find the shell white and dense and ringing, they are cracking open the mountain, one more generation downstream of its death.

## The ground

Walking it is like walking on a struck bell. The stone beneath the sheet-water is old past sense — dense, non-porous, polished to a dark smear by millions of years of slow sliding water, so smooth in places that your foot makes a sound like a kiss. Nothing grows out of it. Nothing has grown out of it for longer than anything can remember; the roots of the old forests are a rumor, and the soil itself is a rumor. What covers the stone instead is the *pelt*: a living skin, black-green-black, slippery as spit, a felt of microbes and fungal thread and mineral scum that drapes every wet surface and feeds, ultimately, on the rock itself. Where the pelt is thin it shows the stone's iron-red or slate-gray beneath. Where it is thick it swells into pillows and lips and weeping pads, and the water coming up through the ground passes through it and comes out tasting faintly of blood.

There are no rivers here. That takes a newcomer days to understand. The water does not gather into channels because the land has no left for it to gather — no ridges, no banks, no steps. Instead the whole country is a river: a sheet of moving water, ankle-deep at most, sliding everywhere at once toward a tilt so gentle you could not feel it in your knees. You find the direction of the down by taste. The water is sweetest at the rim of the world and brackish near the center, where it all drains into the Throat — a slow sag in the earth, nine days' walk out, where the last of the water goes down into the dark with a sound the people describe as swallowing and never as falling.

The only relief is the querns: hard residual knobs of stone, a few meters tall, a few hundred wide, scattered like beads on a string across an emptiness measured in hours. They are the roots of the roots, the last uncrushable kernel of the old range, and they stand up out of the sheet like the backs of animals. Water weeps down their flanks in permanent cold threads. Fog condenses on their crowns and drips from every lip of stone, a plinking applause that never ends. On top of the querns, the settlements.

## The loud

Understand that quiet here is a currency, and it has been extinct for a long time.

The baseline is the hiss — the sheet-water sliding over the pelt, ten trillion small cataracts the depth of a fingernail, a white roar like being inside a shell. On top of that, the drip of the fog. On top of that, the small industry of life: the rasping of ten thousand radulas on stone, a sound like distant sawing; the click and tick of shelled things going about their appetite; the leathery wings of the paper-wings overhead, who hunt by ear in the fog and speak to each other in sonar you can hear as a soft stitching.

And on top of all of that, the drums.

The drums are the loud heart of the plain — great soft-bodied singers that sit submerged in the wider pools with only their throat-sacs above the surface, translucent bags the size of grain sacks, shivering. When a drum inflates and fires, the pool jumps. But the true instrument is the plain itself: a stone plate the size of a country, wet, taut, ringing. A boom does not stop at the edge of its pool. It travels. By the time it has crossed fifty kilometers of ringing sheet it has become a pressure you feel in the sternum before you hear it, and the drums are never alone. They answer each other across the whole flat, call and call and counter-call, until at the loud hours — when the fog drops low and lies condensed in the water, clearing the air for sound — the entire Mill becomes one instrument played by ten thousand hidden hands, and speech is impossible, and small stones on the ground begin to walk, and you learn what the old ones mean when they say the world here is not a place that makes sound but a sound that has decided, for a while, to be a place.

The people wear plugs of wax and stone-wool. Children are taught to read with their spines, earbones pressed to quern-stone. And prized above all lookouts are the deaf — those whose ears the Loud ate years ago — because their attention is the only attention in the world that is not already spoken for.

It is worth knowing, then, what a quiet-hole is. Scattered through the flat are pools with black soft bottoms, dead water, unmoving, unbreathing — the film will not grow there, the shells will not live there. And these pools *eat sound*. Step near one and the hiss dies in your ears as if a door had shut on the world. The folk do not name the thing that lives in that silence. They only say: when the Drum holds its breath, walk soft, and do not stop walking until the world roars again.

## The life

Nothing here is green. Say it plainly, because it shapes everything: no grass, no reed, no leaf, no wood, no seed, no green. The base of all eating is the pelt and the film, fed on dissolved stone, and the pelt is *farmed* — though farming is the wrong word for following it. Because the film migrates. Over weeks, slowly, the living skin of a whole district slides toward the warm seeps — those rare places where heat breathes up from below and the pools sit milk-warm and faintly luminous, and the pelt grows fat enough to harvest with long shell scrapers. So the bloom moves, and the animals move with it, and the people move with the animals. Abundance on the Mill is real but it is made of distance: a swarm of grazers like a living slick of horn, then hours of nothing but hiss.

The grazers are snail-things and soft-creepers, pebble-kin, masses of them, rasping the pelt down to a gloss. The shears plow the shallows after them — armored, wide, patient, eating the grazers and the pelt both, leaving furrows that hiss for a moment and smooth away. The paper-wings take the young and the wounded from above. In the drum-pools, the drums themselves eat the water's film by the ton and pay for it in noise. Everything eats stone in one form or another; even the predators, in the end, are the mountain passing through the shape of hunger.

The people eat all of it, and their leavings have built them. Their villages sit on middens centuries deep — shell on shell, pressed and fused by age into a pale stone of their own making, so that a settlement is a second, smaller quern raised on top of the first by hands. Their buildings are low domes of dry-fitted quern-stone, roofed with drum-hide stretched over rib-bones; half of them are not houses at all but listening-huts, shaped and hollowed to gather sound out of the ground, where a listener lies with a cheek against the floor and reads the district like a page. Their paths are causeways of packed shell — the white ways, glimmering faintly even in this light, running from quern to quern across the sheet. Their tools are shell adzes, bone harpoons, notched scrapers for the pelt harvest, lamps of rendered tallow-fat with a rush of stone-wool for a wick, burning a hand's width of warm yellow in the gray — the only warm color in the world, and only when someone pays for it.

They tell time by ear, since the sky refuses to. A particular pattern of booms at the fog-rise serves for dawn; another for the fog-drop; the cold hours are known by the stone itself, which contracts in the deepest chill and splits with far-off cannon-cracks — stone-thunder, the plain shivering in its sleep. Their etiquette is acoustic: you do not hum at loud hour; you answer a tap on the causeway or you are understood to be a stranger; and there is one thing nearly everyone does once, alone, on a quern crown — tap the old stone with a knuckle, three times, and wait. The querns are dense enough to ring. The ring goes out over the hiss and the drip and the drums, and sometimes, so the folk insist, something answers: not an echo, they say, echo is where sound comes *back* — this comes *from*, from out there, from other knuckles on other crowns, a conversation of the whole flat floor of the world, too slow and too vast for any one life to hear the whole of it.

Above it all the ceiling stays pearl. No sun, no disc, no blue — only a luminous gray that dims toward one end of the day and brightens toward the other, and fog riding it like smoke under glass. Once in a great while, in the cold season, a far-off sky-split flashes — heat lightning with no heat in it — and for a single held breath the entire Mill is lit from horizon to horizon: the sheet, the shears' furrows, the white ways, the querns with their weeping flanks, the whole bottomless plain of it. The people stop and stare at that. Then the dark comes back, and the drums pick up the beat they never really dropped, and the mountain, still being eaten, grinds on a little further into mud, into film, into shell, into song.
