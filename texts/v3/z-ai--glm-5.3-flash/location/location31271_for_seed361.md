# The Smoking Mountain

From a day's flight away it is only the smoking mountain, and the pilots of the lowlands give it room. A thread of vapor rises off its summit at dawn, thin as wire, and vanishes; by midmorning the summit is just another wind-scoured crown of gray among gray crowns, cold as everything else above the snowline. The wind up there has taken everything it can and still comes, out of habit. Nothing grows on the outer slopes. Nothing stops there.

Which is why the pocket stays hidden. The hollow at the very top reads from the outside as a shallow dent, a shadow, no different from a hundred other summit dents — except at dawn, when the chimney exhales its single thread and the lowlands invent their stories about it. The fog inside never spills. The basin keeps itself to itself the way a kept secret does.

You enter, if you enter at all, by a slot on the leeward face under an overhang: a stair.

The steps are shallow as a ladder's rungs, worn hollow in their middles, and spiraled down their length with notches — a tally, thousands upon thousands of marks, someone counting something once, for a very long time, in a script nobody reads. The cold leaves you a step at a time, like a coat handed off. And the sound arrives before the light: a rolling, braided loudness coming up the slot, birdsong and water and something bell-like underneath it all, doubled — everything doubled — until you understand you are hearing the place echo itself, every call returning a half-breath late, so that the basin always sounds like two of everything.

Then the slot opens and you are in the room.

## The Room

It is perhaps three hundred paces across, walled all around by stone so old and so finished that the eye reads it first as masonry and only after as geology. Every crack has been rounded to silence. Every hollow is a closed eye. The walls are the shape wind leaves when it is done — erosion finished here longer ago than the tallies count, the slopes settled into their final angles, nothing left anywhere for the wind to take. What wears now is lichen, and the lichen is grazed to a lawn.

Open above, the whole ceiling of sky. At noon a column of sunlight falls straight down the chimney to the basin floor, and the air in it turns gold with pollen and dust and the gleam of wings.

You can count everything. That is the first strange thing about a place so loud: twelve trees, a wet wall, five finger-pools, one hearth, one stair. In all this fullness, nothing is lost in the crowd. The lushness is real but distributed the way candles are bright in a dark church — each green thing in its own circle of influence, bare warm stone negotiated between them. Twelve pillar-trees stand spaced like the posts of a hall, smooth-barked, vast, their canopies interlacing only at the center. There are no saplings. There are no dead trees. The twelve trees are the twelve trees, and the basin has agreed to keep them. Between their high branches hangs rope-moss in long dripping festoons, and the trees, in effect, garden their own sky — the fog that slides over the rim at dawn snarls in the moss and strangles into droplets, and by midmorning the whole canopy is raining gently upward into memory.

## The Water

There is no stream here, no spring worth the name. The basin's water is an economy of breath.

Two weathers a day: the wet one and the warm one. Before dawn the temperature crosses its cold line and mist forms in a flat gray ring at the basin's rim, thick as wool, and everything beaded — silk, bark, stone, wing. A wall on the north side weeps at that hour, a hand's width of dark seep that runs for an hour and stops. Five shallow pools sit in rock hollows, each no bigger than a spread hand, each refilled nightly to the same line. And along certain channels in the floor — hair-fine engraving, worn nearly smooth, older than the lichen that respects it — beads of condensation gather and roll, slowly, deliberately, uphill, until they reach the roots of the ferns and the pillar-trees. Where the engraving begins, the water forgets which way is downhill. The trees drink the sky by infrastructure. Someone engineered this who is no longer here to be thanked, and it has never once failed.

Behind the slot, a bowl of water appears at the same hour as the weeping wall, fed by nothing the eye can find.

## The Hearth

Near the center, in a bowl of stone worn glossy by ten thousand years of bodies, sits a fist of black that is not rock. It is warm. It has always been warm; the tallies around the stair suggest its warmth outlasts every countable thing. It gives off no smoke, no light, no hum — only heat, in a perfect circle, and the basin's whole ecology is arranged as rings around it.

The court it keeps is the strangest and the most ordinary thing here. Moths orbit it. Ember-mice — small, russet, warm-blooded, found nowhere else on the mountain, possibly nowhere else at all — nest in the cracks nearest it and no farther. Lizards bask in ranks. At dawn the tollers line up, the big gray bellbirds, and each drinks from the skin of condensation that gathers on the stone's top and never falls. None of this was taught. It is simply in the animals, the way their hearts are. Warmth is the capital of this place; everything is priced in it; the hearth is the mint.

Rings out from the hearth: nearest, the flowers that open only within the warmth's radius, a low bed of dark red petals; then the ferns, the seed of the moss-cutter flocks, the perches; then the cold line, where the outside weather stops like a tide against a doorstep.

## The Crowds

The fauna is overwhelming, and it is overwhelming in a particular way: not dense but total. A dozen endemic species, numerous and local, which is its own kind of multitude — and layered, floor to sky, with the migrants cycling through in waves, because the basin is a station on a sky-road nobody maintains anymore, and the traffic has outlived the road.

Gauze-wings arrive at morning, folding themselves down the chimney like letters posted through a slot — big translucent gliders that drink dew from the festoons and display in the noon light column, each pair performing its slow spiral courtship exactly where the sun falls. Moss-cutters graze the lichen lawn in rotations, moving across the floor like a slow gray tide with a memory for the schedule. Polish-beetles work the perches, grooming the feet of whatever lands, and the landing is payment. Wall-clingers hold the vertical stone like painted things, immobile for hours, then comically quick. High in the open middle, the hunters: one comes with the fog and leaves with it, and the chorus has a single syllable for her — one repeated note, then silence, then business as usual over wherever she has chosen. The basin does not grieve. It schedules.

And the tollers ring the hours, and the hours obey. Their bell-note is the metronome of the whole basin: first light, full light, the fog's arrival, the fog's release. The dawn chorus is loud enough to feel in the chest, and doubled by the walls — two of everything, always — so that three hundred paces holds the sound of a country. Who set the walls so? The perches suggest an answer and refuse it. The perches are carved stone posts, each fitted to the feet that grip it with a precision nothing worn should have. The perches predate the birds, or the birds predate the perches; the basin does not distinguish.

## The Traces

Beyond the hearth, the stair, the channels, the perches, the built is almost nothing, and that almost-nothing is the oldest thing here.

A glass arm rises at the chimney's rim — a lamp, clearly, a beacon that once guided traffic down the throat of the funnel. It has never once in living memory made light. The toller who sings dawn does it standing on the lamp, which is the oldest joke in the basin, if it is a joke. A rectangle on the north wall differs from its neighbors only in smoothness: a door, or the memory of one. The cistern bowl. The tallies going down.

And the stair is longer than the mountain. This is not a riddle. You climb back up, eventually, and the basin is unchanged and no colder for your absence, and the notch-count you kept does not reconcile, and there is nothing to do about any of that but add your own mark and let it go.

## The Day

The basin breathes. Warm air rises out of the chimney through midday and the departures ride it — gauze-wings opening like hands, riding the column up and out into the cold they were never built for, the tollers last, one bell-note goodbye that the walls double into two. Afternoon stills; the second voice of every echo grows strong as the air goes quiet; the polish-beetles work; the moss-cutters shade themselves against the pillar-trees. At dusk the insect clouds rise and the stair-mouth exhales its thin warm breath, which is the hearth, venting. Night brings the cold snap outside, and the mist line returns to the rim like a held breath released, and the finger-pools refill to their line, and the beads gather on the drinking-stones and begin their slow uphill pilgrimage to the roots.

Through the chimney, then, the stars — the basin holding them the way a well holds a coin.
