**The Bowl of Falling Sound**

You climb for two days out of the silence before you begin to hear it.

The country below the rim is pale and dead-flat, a waste of spent stone where sound goes to lie down. Nothing grows there. Your own voice dies a foot from your mouth, and the hush has a texture to it, like walking through old sand. The keepers call the flats *the Lees* — the dregs of all the noise the sky has ever dropped, drained down and down over uncountable ages until it lies in the low places, inert, flat, finished. Nothing has ever been wet in this world. There is no dew, no rain, no ice, no seep, no spring. The dim stars overhead have never once been reflected anywhere.

But sound, in this world, is the thing that falls.

You hear the bowl before you see it — a low Continuous rumble growing under your boots, coming up through bone — and then the rim itself, a broken crown of fluted stone, and pouring over it and down into the hollow beyond: the fall. Not water. A slow, thick, ceaseless precipitation of tone, ticking on the bare rock like sleet you can hear and not see, silvering the air below in faint quivering ribbons. The air changes as you step through. It goes warm — the way an animal is warm — body-warm, dry, faintly buzzing on the skin. High altitude and thin air, and yet the bowl is mild, because all that falling sound is energy, and the place runs warm with it, day and night.

The whole world, once you are inside it, is small. Half a mile across, perhaps. A hanging amphitheater walled in ringed dark stone, bounded on every side, a place you could shout across — and everything in it does. You can hear the whole of it at once, which is its strangest gift: no corner hidden, no sound lost. An hour's walk to circle. A lifetime to hear it all.

The rock here has been worn the way driftwood is worn. Nothing has ever carved this place but vibration. The cliff walls are fluted in long vertical grooves where the same tones have run the same paths for ages past counting; the overhangs are honeycombed with smooth pockets where whole chords have sat and swirled and slowly eaten their own niches. Slabs in the floor carry ripple-marks like fossil waveforms — stone that remembers a shape of air from before anything now living was born. Knock the bedrock and it answers, a low sweet ring, because the stone itself is saturated, humming with everything it has drunk. In the hollows of the floor are the echo-pools: smooth round basins where fresh sound sits after falling, turning slowly, thickening, waiting to be drunk. Grit collects in the low spots — sand worn fine enough to chime — and when wind crosses it the ground underfoot murmurs like a struck membrane.

Yet most of the bowl is bare. That must be said plainly: the lushness lives only where the channels run. Silvered ribbons of dense sound pour down the flutes and cross the floor in braided lines, and along those lines only — nowhere else — the groves stand, deep and dark and riotous, occupying perhaps a tenth of the bowl. The rest is polished ringing emptiness, balanced against the green exactly, emptiness with a voice.

The groves themselves are old-growth lush, layered and tangled, though nothing in them is soft the way wet leaves are soft. The feltbark trees lean their whole crowns into the sound-channels, straining after it; their leaves are gray-green mats of dense felt, shaped like cupped ears, trembling visibly when a front passes. Their bark is ribbed in concentric rings, speaker-cone bark, and it flexes. Cut the bark and the tree weeps a slow pale sap that hardens into amber beads — and the beads hold sound. Crack a warm one and whatever the tree was drinking when it wept plays back out of the broken faces: a moment of herd-drumming, a dusk chord, a stranger's whistle from thirty years ago. Each bead glows faintly while it plays, a warm coal-colored light, and goes dark when the sound is spent. The keepers light their homes with these. Their lanterns are little cracked histories.

Under the feltbarks grow the chime-mosses, trembling shags that harvest only the after-ring, the lingering tail of echoes, so they crowd into overhangs and potholes where sound settles last — lushness pooled in the places echoes go to die. And everywhere through the groves, the note-blooms: flowers that hold pitch the way our flowers hold scent. Each bloom carries one sustained tone, pitched to call one kind of pollinator. They open at dusk, in sequence, tuning to one another, until the whole grove is a slowly assembling chord. The moths come then — moth-bells, soft-winged, each species beating its wings at exactly the frequency of the flower it serves, matching tone to tone, so that a feeding moth is indistinguishable from its bloom until it moves.

The fauna is the wealth of the place, and nearly all of it is loud on purpose, because here loudness is safety. The drumherds walk the channels in their dozens — heavy, wide-footed, patient beasts that speak to each other through the ground, the whole bowl ringing softly under their passage like a skin over a drum. Where they tread, the groves bloom out of season; the keepers time their sowing to the herds' circuits the way lowland peoples once timed theirs to rivers. Trillers ride the falling fronts, surfing the dense bands of sound the way nothing here surfs wind, sipping the highest frequencies off the crests. Bone-whistlers work the dusk, scavengers that collect hollow bones and blow through them — a wandering, eerie fluting that is the closest thing this world has to loneliness. And in the quiet pockets, the silent places inside the deepest groves where sound has drained away entirely, the hush-stalkers hunt. They are padded in resin-silence and they walk without a whisper, and every animal in the bowl knows the old rule that keeps them alive: silence is where the hunters are. Here, prey crowds into noise. Loud is safe. Quiet is death.

There are other workers too. Slow gardener-beasts, patient as stone, spend their whole lives arranging fallen slabs into low baffles that steer the sound-fall toward their own private groves. The keepers' oldest craft is an imitation of theirs — the human walls and the beasts' walls stand side by side in the channels, and no one, looking, can say which species built which, or when.

The keepers themselves are three: an old woman, her daughter, her daughter's son, in three beehive huts of dry-laid ring-stone on a shelf above the eastern channels. No metal. Horn tools, bone awls, rope of felt-fiber, hammers tuned to the note each stone wants to break on. Each hut breathes through a chimney-horn curved to draw one chosen tone, so that inside there is cellar-quiet, dim and cool and strange — inside is the only place in the bowl where you can hear your own heart. Outside, the yards are fenced in chime-posts, hung with resonant stones and bone tubes that keep the yards loudly alive through the night, because a silent yard is a table set for hush-stalkers. Their work is the channels: mending the baffles, clearing the flutes, tapping the feltbarks for amber, walking the lines at dawn like irrigators in a country where the irrigation is made of noise. The ruins of older workings are everywhere — baffle-lines gone dry, the groves they once fed shrunk to gray stubble, a doorway with no hut behind it. The trade of this place, carried down to other bowls on other peaks, is amber: bottled sound, spent like coins, each bead a voice from somewhere far away.

They teach their children four things before anything else. That nothing here has ever been wet, and *rain* in the old songs means noise. That sound falls from the sky and pools in high hollow places, and the valleys drink nothing. That everything alive drinks sound, and stone remembers what it drinks. And that silence is the lees — the dregs, the dead — and is to be avoided, or hoarded, or feared, depending which elder is speaking.

The bowl has weather, entire seasons, a full life. The day-fall: a steady soft pattering of mid-tones, the bowl's ordinary rain. The dusk fronts: heavy rolling bands that break over the rim and set every chime-fence ringing, the blooms slamming open, the moths rising in shimmer, the herds drumming answers up and down the channels. The true storms, rare and tremendous, when the sky hammers and the bowl fills like a flooded river and the groves stand drunk and glittering with fresh sound until morning. And the nights, when the great deep tone rises — the Underhum, the bowl's own resident note, older than any channel, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, the sound that carved the whole place and keeps it alive. Once in a long while comes the Hush: the sky goes still, the fall fails, drought in a world without water. The groves fold their felt leaves. The herds stand torpid. The keepers sit in the dark cracking their oldest amber, spending their savings of sound a bead at a time, waiting for the sky to remember its voice.

Stand anywhere at dusk with your palm flat on the bedrock and you will feel it: the whole small world held on one sustained note, wearing itself and nourishing everything on it at once. Loud, warm, churning, ancient, bounded. Small enough to hear all at once.

Deep enough to spend a life inside.
