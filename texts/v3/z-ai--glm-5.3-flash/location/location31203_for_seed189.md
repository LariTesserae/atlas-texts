Under the hill there is a room, and the room is alive, and the room is new.

It is not large. Six steps wall to wall if you walk carefully, seven if you don't — the floor gives under you like the flank of a sleeping animal, plaited root-mattress, and your footprints fill in behind you the way water closes over a stone. The ceiling is a hand's reach above your head in places, lower where the hanging things descend. If you stood in the center and stretched, you might — *might* — touch nothing at all. That is the point of the center. The center is kept empty.

Everything else is green. Not hung with green — *made* of it. The chamber was stone once; you can still find the original walls in places, sharp-faceted fracture faces with the radial scars of whatever blow cracked this pocket open still printed in them, edges keen as knapped flint. But the stone is going under. A living skin has taken every surface — films and veils and thin colonial mats of tissue, laid over the rock the way skin goes over a wound — and here and there a facet shows through the green like bone through a graze, glassy and fresh, still warm. There is no old stone anywhere in this room. Nothing has had time to round.

The growth is a lining, not a filling. That is what saves the place from being a throat. The plants occupy the walls the way paper occupies a lantern: membranes, root-curtains a hand's width thick, hanging fringes that sway in a slow collective pulse because the room *breathes* — warm air climbing the crack in the ceiling in long swallows, cool air pouring down the walls to replace it, so that every curtain leans at once, together, like a held breath released. Between the colonies the membrane runs bare and taut and slightly tacky with sap-mist. You could press your palm to it and feel the room thinking.

Because it grows while you watch. That is the first thing, and the thing you will not stop noticing. Stand still for twenty breaths and any vine near you will have moved — tendrils swinging in slow arcs, fringes lengthening at the pace of a moth's wingbeat, a leaf unrolling its fist somewhere overhead with a sound like wet paper tearing. Nothing here is finished. Nothing is even *settled*. Ask the wood how old it is and its own rings will argue: four hundred years, says the grain; three days, says the dust that has not yet finished settling from the crack.

And the room is loud. Young forests grow in slow thunder; this one grows in fast thunder, continuous, with no gaps in it: pops, creaks, the tick of fiber under tension, a low fizz of division running through the walls like champagne heard through a door. Put your ear to the green skin and the sound doubles — sizzle, crackle, the whisper of a thousand small unfoldings. The acoustics are not entirely trustworthy either. Drop a pebble and you will hear it land half a second before it lands. The room is still deciding when things happen.

Light comes two ways. The crack above the east wall is narrow and sharp-lipped and widening by a grain a day — you can hear it, occasionally, a single tick like a clock that has all the time in the world — and when the sun outside crosses it, a blade of daylight falls into the chamber and the whole room flashes like the inside of a lamp: every bead of condensation lit, a haze of pollen and spore-fuzz blazing in the shaft, warmth blooming sudden as a greenhouse. Between those moments the light is the plants' own. A low gold-green glow from the sap itself, dusk-colored, the color of the inside of your eyelids. The room is never dark. It is also, in its own opinion, never adequately lit — which you would swear was true, if you watched what the plants do about it: every growing tip in the chamber leans toward a point on the ceiling where the crack has *not yet* reached. They are growing toward a light that hasn't arrived. They know the crack is coming. The garden is growing into the future, and it is not guessing.

Water here is a rumor that occasionally proves itself. The air is thick with held moisture — everything damp, everything beading, the walls sweating a fine tacky mist — but free water is scarce. Twice a day, or once every three days; the room has not settled on a schedule. Then the ceiling *decides*: all that held breath lets go at once, mist first, rising, then beads, then a brief hissing shower that strikes the leaf-drums below like applause and is gone — drunk into the floor before the last drops fall, so that the rain leaves no puddles, only a greener room and a smell of cut stems and warm loam and the faint blood-smell of freshly cracked stone.

Except in one corner. There a great leaf has folded itself into a cup and holds a handful of clear water that never evaporates and never overflows — the only still water in the room, possibly the oldest thing in it, tasting (you will not taste it, but the gardener's face after drinking suggests) of somewhere else entirely. Next to the cup, a row of small pebbles. Twelve of them. Not a structure — nothing is built here — an arrangement. A tally. It has rained twelve times. Or the gardener has decided it has.

Because there is a gardener. One. It is the only animal that lives here, a pale grey-green thing the size of a fat cat, soft-skinned, eyes like wet pebbles, and it keeps the room. It moves the room's whole circuit each cycle on wet feet, thorn in the grip of one hand — a black thorn as long as your finger, its pruning knife — and it cuts. That is where the emptiness comes from. Every tendril that reaches for the center of the chamber is cut back, every curtain trimmed off the middle air, every ambitious root redirected. The hollow you are standing in is not an accident of growth. It is maintenance. Nothing in this room is built, but everything in it is *tended*. The thorn has rooted in the gardener's fist — cuttings will root in this room, in midair, in anything — and the gardener simply rotates its grip from time to time so the tool grows straight. Tool and gardener, growing slowly into each other. There is also a reed straw, kept by the cup, and a smooth pebble used for grinding, and the pebble has gone green at the edges.

The only other natives are three moths the color of condensation that drink from the walls and are indistinguishable from beads of water until they move.

Sometimes the crack speaks with a different voice. Surface air comes down the shaft — noon-smelling, dry-grass, a whole different world's weather — and everything in the room stops at once: the fizz of growth drops to silence, the curtains quit swaying, and the gardener goes flat against the wall and flushes its skin to match the green until it is gone. Once — only once, in all the tallies — something came down: a long jointed shape that dropped through the crack and hunted the moths in a whir of wrong wings, and was gone up again before the tally pebble it dislodged had finished rolling. The room breathed again. The gardener unclenched. The growing resumed its roar.

And sometimes, at the edge of your vision, a wall goes grey. A whole facet reverting — mineral, sharp, dead — for the length of a blink, and then green floods back over it and the moment is deniable. The stone remembers being the room. The room remembers being the stone. Neither has won yet; the border between them is not a line but a negotiation, lichen-things hardening into rock at one edge of the room while rock softens into wood at the other.

Understand what this place does with things that stay. The garden does not eat. It *includes*. Sit down in the middle — the one kept-empty place, held open like a seat — and within an hour your sleeves will have taken a fine moss, your hair will have grown a finger's width, a root will have tested your bootlace and found it adequate. The room is very good at making room. It would prune itself around you. You would be tended.

So: visit briefly. Cross in six steps. Let the sun-blade catch you once, drink nothing, touch nothing, and leave by the crack, which is sharp, and which is widening, grain by grain, toward the light the whole garden is already leaning into.
