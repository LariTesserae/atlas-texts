# The Cold Nursery

You enter through a crack you will not be able to find again.

It is underground — unmistakably so — and yet it is *bright*. Not lit so much as **luminous from within**: the rock itself gives off a pale, milky glow, like the inside of an egg held up to a candle. The stone here is young. That's the first thing you understand, though you couldn't say how. This geology is not worn down by time; it is still *assembling*. Veins of mineral push slowly through the walls the way frost blooms across a window, and where they meet, they knit together with an almost audible patience. If you stare at a wall long enough — and the quiet encourages staring — you can watch a seam close. The cavern is under construction. By whom is not a question this place permits.

The space is small. A pocket-world, a room rather than a country: perhaps thirty paces across, ceiling low enough that the glow pools against it. Everything here is intimate and slightly too close, like being inside something.

Water exists only as *evidence*. Condensation beads on every surface and refuses to fall. Along one wall, a thread of moisture surfaces from the rock, travels a hand's width, and sinks back in — an intermittent river deciding whether it wants to exist yet. You learn to time yourself by it. When the thread runs, the whole chamber seems to breathe.

Vegetation is nearly absent, but not quite: sparse pale filaments cling to the dampest stone, more like nerve-endings than moss, tasting the air. Where the water-thread passes, they flush faintly rose and then dim again.

**The fauna are the thing.**

They are abundant — improbably, gloriously so for a place with almost no water and no plants — and they are all *young*. Not juveniles of known species; young the way the rock is young. Translucent grubs the size of thumbnails drift on warm updrafts with no wings yet, wings clearly pending. Soft-shelled things patrol the walls, eating the glow itself off the stone in tidy spirals, leaving darker trails that the rock slowly heals behind them. In a shallow depression where condensation gathers, a congregation of eyeless pale creatures perform slow, synchronized tilting motions — a behavior with no audience, or with an audience you are standing inside.

Nothing here is a predator. Nothing is prey either. The ecosystem is active — constantly, visibly in motion, migrations of the small and soft crossing the floor in patient processions — but nothing eats anything else. They are, as far as you can tell, *being built alongside the room*. The fauna and the geology are the same project.

The silence is total and somehow attentive. Sound behaves oddly here: when one of the drifting grubs passes close to your face, you hear not a buzz but a faint, clear tone, like a wet finger on glass — a single note, different for each creature. The room is quietly tuning itself.

And the weirdness — the reality of the place wears thin in places, like fabric. Near the center, the air has a fold in it. Look at the far wall directly and it's six meters away; look at it via the fold and it's very close, close enough to see that the rock there is *smoother, less finished* — an earlier draft of this chamber. Small scattered structures dot the floor: not ruins, but prototypes. A squat arch of fused stone leading nowhere. A ring of pebbles too regular to be natural, too imperfect to be intentional. Half a staircase, three steps, ascending to ceiling. Marks of *trying*. Whoever — whatever — is building this world is iterating, and these are the discarded drafts, scattered like sketches around a studio.

The drafts are inhabited. The small pale things nest inside them.

The dynamic is everything: rock accreting, moisture surfacing and withdrawing, fauna processing light into… something, the fold in the air slowly drifting across the chamber over hours, the whole nursery germinating. You get the strong, unshakable feeling that this room is not a place. It is an **egg**. The whole cavern, the glow, the fauna, the prototypes — an egg, cool and damp and bright, in the dark, deciding what it will hatch into.

You should leave before the thread of water runs again.

You won't be able to find the crack anyway. It may not have existed yet when you came through.
