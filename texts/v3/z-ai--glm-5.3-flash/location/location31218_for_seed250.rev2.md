There is no coast anywhere on this sea.

That is the first thing, and it takes a while to understand. The water has outgrown the world that was meant to hold it. The planet beneath is newborn — its basins unfinished, its crust still rising in ridges and blades — and the ocean arrived early, sweating up out of the fresh rock faster than the rock could learn to contain it. So the sea stands over-full, perched kilometers above the level it will someday settle to, a swelling with no shoreline to press against. Storms cross ten thousand kilometers of open water without breaking once; their swells arrive below as long, tectonic rhythms, whole weather systems translated into pressure. From underneath, a storm is a ceiling: a slow silver percussion rolling overhead, patient as herd-movement.

The water itself is cold, clean, and almost impossibly clear — and the clarity is a consequence of the world's newness. Erosion has not begun here. Young stone does not crumble; it has no history to shed. So there is no sediment, no silt, no haze. Light falls into this ocean in honest white-gold shafts, straight down through hundreds of meters of bright emptiness, and the clarity outruns the light: you can make out shapes at distances where there is technically not enough illumination to see them, silhouettes standing in a blue that is not darkness but volume. It is disorienting, at first, to see farther than you can illuminate — to watch a wall cross the limit of the sun and keep its edges anyway.

And between the shapes: nothing. This is the sea's true material. You can travel for hours — days, if days mean anything in a place with no landmarks — through pure water, unmoving column after unmoving column, and touch nothing but temperature and current. The rarest substance in this ocean is a surface. Whatever presences it has are separated by distances that weather itself needs a week to cross. The emptiness is not barren in the way of a desert, which at least has grain and grit; it is emptiness the way a held breath is empty. The sea is mostly itself.

Then the floor announces itself by sound before it announces itself by sight: a boom, deep and round, like a bell struck underwater and allowed to ring for a minute. Another. Closer. And the blue ahead acquires architecture.

The seafloor is under construction. There is no other way to say it. New stone rises from the dark in terraces — long, deliberate steps of pale rock, each edge still knife-honed, each face unweathered, glassy in places where molten mineral met cold water and froze mid-flow. Along the newest seams, faint lines of glow mark where the crust is being extruded right now: a wall shouldering upward over the span of a season, a terrace extruded in a week, stone arriving like a tide of its own. Hydrothermal chimneys stand over the vents, and they do something chimneys should not do — they pulse. Regularly. On rhythm. Not the arrhythmic stutter of ordinary geology but something closer to a clock, or to breath.

Look closer, and the regularity spreads. Mineral veins in the fresh rock branch like busbars. Currents in the water above run straight and level for hundreds of kilometers, like channels cut for a purpose — conveyor lanes, the sparse drifting life arranged along them in rows too even for chance. Vortices close too neatly. The terraces, when you measure them, arrive with tolerances. And yet nothing here is built. No structures. No artifacts. No ruins, no tools, no marks of hands. The engineering never pauses long enough to leave an object behind — or rather, the objects and the engineering are the same thing. The sea is being secreted by the rock; the rock is being printed by the sea. Each makes the other, and whoever — whatever — set this in motion built it entirely in process, not in parts. The technology of this place is the water. It performs work that current physics can describe only in fragments: directed crystallization at scale, chemistry with intent, a planetary fabricator with no machine whatsoever that you could point to.

Life is here, but barely — and only the kind that volunteers. On the freshest terraces, within days of cooling, the first growths arrive, riding the conveyor currents from somewhere uncountably distant: stiff-finned fronds and translucent veils, pigmented black-violet to wring sustenance from cold light, rooting not into soil — there is no soil, and will not be for ages, because soil is made by decay and weathering and this world has neither — but fusing directly into the new stone, root-crystal to fresh rock, an ecology that skips humus entirely. A colony takes hold along a construction seam. Then hundreds of kilometers of nothing. Then another colony, seeded in its neat rows. They grow in solitude and they fail in solitude: often the very accretion they colonize buries them, the next terrace rising through the fronds like a slow closing hand. And when they die, they do not rot. Nothing rots here. The sea reclaims them within days — mineralization, lithification — and a dead frond becomes a thin carbon film on young rock, a fossil from the moment of its death. In this world, everything dead becomes geology immediately. The only grave the sea digs is stone.

There are no animals. There has never been an animal here, and the absence is not quiet — it is structural. Motion is everywhere: currents, updrafts, falling curtains of cooled mineral water, storm-swell shuddering down from the silver ceiling. But nothing moves itself. No swimmer parts the shafts of light. Nothing grazes the fronds, nothing hunts, nothing hides. The ocean breathes and churns and builds with the total unselfconsciousness of a place where no one has ever watched it. The first eyes in this sea would be yours.

Sound is the dominant sense, and the sea is loud. Water carries a boom a thousand kilometers; the whole basin rings with the cooling of new crust, the chimneys' pulsed roar, the long infrasonic drone of a storm's fetch arriving from the surface like weather made of pressure. It fills the body. You do not hear this world so much as stand inside its voice. And beneath all of it, everywhere, runs the carrier tone — a deep, even hum that corresponds to no turbulence, no vent, no swell. It is present in the emptiness and present over the terraces, and it changes. Slowly. In the way a voice changes when it is reading.

That tone is as close as this world comes to being told something, and no one is here to translate it. There are no names here. Nothing has been named, because there was never anyone to do the naming; the ocean has no word for itself and does not need one. It has no past, either — erosion will see to a past eventually, but not yet. Nothing erodes because nothing is old. The sea does not remember; it works.

So the place lives entirely in future tense. One day the water will find its level and the shoreline will exist; the seams will cool; the chimneys will go arrhythmic and die; sediment will finally cloud the impossible clarity; the terraces will round their knife edges; erosion will begin, and the world will start accumulating a history the way old worlds do, layer on worn layer. But that day is far off. For now it is all invention: an ocean still being poured, a floor still being printed, black-violet fronds fusing themselves to stone that was liquid last season, and light — plain, ordinary, honest daylight — falling in white-gold shafts through thousands of kilometers of cold, ringing, nearly empty blue.

Even the loudness is a kind of newness. It is the sound of a world rehearsing itself, and it has not yet learned to be tired.
