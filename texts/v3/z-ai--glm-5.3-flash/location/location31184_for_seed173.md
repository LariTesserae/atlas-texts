On still afternoons, standing on the quay above, you can feel the Old Lady through your boot soles — a slow iron thud, twice a minute, rising out of the harbor floor like a second heartbeat. The town has stopped noticing it the way a house stops noticing its own stairs. Down there, under the water, at the bottom of a stone shaft behind a rusted hatch, is the Old Intake: a pump-hall the size of a chapel, drowned for longer than any ledger records, still working.

You go down by the shaft. It's a brick throat, damp, ringed with rungs worn to shallow silver bowls where three centuries of hands have been placed. Halfway down, the daylight from the hatch gives up and the lamps take over — two of them, caged, burning the color of old brass — and their brightness is a tide-gauge, because they run off a small dynamo geared to the same wheel as the pump. On spring tides the lamps burn almost cheerful. On neaps they sulk.

The shaft ends in the dry dome, which is not dry. Nothing here is dry; there is only the wet, and the less wet. The dome is a low bubble of air under a domed roof of riveted plate, walls weeping condensation in slow beads, breath fogging, a chill that gets into knuckles. A stove. A kettle. A hammock hook. A ledger shelf, and on it a line of books whose earliest volumes begin mid-story, as if the hall was already old when anyone thought to write it down. A trough under the light-well holds the keeper's garden: two rows of samphire, a straggling patch of cress, all of it sparse and much loved, watered with kettle-leavings.

A ladder goes from the dome's floor-grating down into the flooded hall, and the flooded hall is the true body of the place.

It is small — thirty paces long, twelve wide, an arched tunnel of plate and laid stone — and almost entirely empty, which is its whole character. The floor is cast slabs, dished at the ladder's foot by feet, worn so soft at every edge that nothing in the hall has a corner anymore. The plaques on the walls are worn past their own names into smooth dark ovals; the masons' marks survive only as shadows of shadows. Rust here stopped long ago; it has settled into a marbled, stable fur, a skin rather than a disease. The hall has finished becoming. Whatever it was going to erode into, it already has, and now it simply endures, and the water has learned its shape so well that the current no longer scours it — the flow goes around, politely, keeping the place by respecting it.

The features sit far apart in all that openness, like furniture in a barn. The great tide-wheel at the east end, a dark spoked circle taller than a wagon. The beam above it — the Old Lady herself — walking its slow arc, iron stroke after iron stroke, polished mirror-bright only on its bearing, furred everywhere a hand has never been. The ram-head, the valve-gear, the pipework climbing the wall in riveted veins toward the shaft. A winch for the intake grate. Silt-trays, engineered and emptied on schedule, so that even the mud here is managed mud — there is no natural floor anywhere in this place, no stone that was not quarried and laid, no surface that was not made by somebody.

And through the intake grate at the west end, twice a day, the sea comes in.

That is the hall's weather. On the flood the intake breathes wide and the water arrives with a low rushing note, faintly milky with silt and food, and the sprats come with it — a shoal of thousands pouring through the bars in a single silver rope, wheeling once in the open water (this is what the emptiness is for; the hall is a room big enough for a shoal to turn in) and then dissolving into the dark upper reaches. The mussels on the ladder open like a curtain drawn back. The shrimps in the trays tick and click, a sound like sand falling on a drum. The beam quickens a little on the stronger flow and its thud doubles off the shaft, and the whole hall becomes an instrument being played by the tide. Then the slack comes, and everything rests — the hour the keeper eats, the hour the water hangs still and the silt hangs with it — and then the ebb takes the shoal out again in the same silver rope, and the hall settles, and the pumps slow to their patient neap-tide murmur.

The animals have rules, and the rules are older than anyone now living. The fish leave before the intake surge — they always know — and the first informal law of the place is *follow the fish*. Never latch the low hatch, because the Warden uses it. The Warden is the conger eel who lives in the sealed pipe marked *№ 4*, thick as a forearm and older than the oldest ledger, and who has never once been seen to do anything threatening, only to arrive, slowly, at feeding times, with the unhurried authority of a landlord. There is a cod, too, vast and tame, who takes mussel meat from the hand on Fridays because the keepers have always fed her on Fridays and she has always seen no reason to argue with the arrangement. Crabs migrate across the open slabs each afternoon from the cold west end to the warm plume that rises off the dynamo housing, and loiter there in a scrabble of shells like folk at a fire, because even underwater, cool as this hall is, warmth is worth walking for. On lamp-lit nights in late summer, jellyfish drift in through the grate and pulse among the brass light like slow lamps themselves, and the whole shoal rises to the surface of the flooded hall to watch.

Once a year, in spring, the water goes briefly milky with somebody's spawning, and once a year, in autumn, the grate is winched up and a work-party comes down, and for a single quiet day the hall hears voices and boot-squeak on the ladder and the kettle whistling in the dome — the only loud human days it gets. The rest of the year it hears its own grammar: the beam, the rush, the drip tallying time off the arch-plates, the kettle, the click of shrimps, the ladder ringing when the keeper descends. Conversation works like this — you talk in the dome, you shout on the ladder, and in the flooded hall you stop talking and learn to read the pump. The keeper knows the hall by ear alone. When a crab gets into the valve, the beam's note changes by a hair, and the keeper climbs down before the gauge knows anything is wrong.

The water the Old Lady lifts goes up the shaft to the cistern under the market, and from there to the brewery and the baths, and to the fountain on the green that runs ten minutes at noon. The ale above is famous for it — cold, sweet, faintly mineral, a taste that is really the taste of these worn slabs and this slow iron. That is the whole modern reason the place is kept. Twelve intakes were driven beneath the harbor in the old days; eleven are sealed; one still runs, because it runs, and because its water is good, and because it has outlasted every reason to stop except its own persistence.

At slack water at night it is at its most itself. The beam parked at the top of its arc. The shoal dim in the upper dark, a suggestion of silver. The lamps at their neap dimness. The eel's pipe exhaling its small steady cloud. The samphire in the dome, wet-salted, growing the way slow things grow. Everything here is made, and everything made is worn smooth, and everything worn smooth is full of animals, and the tide comes in and goes out, and the Old Lady lifts her water, stroke by stroke, into a town that only remembers her when the fountain runs.
