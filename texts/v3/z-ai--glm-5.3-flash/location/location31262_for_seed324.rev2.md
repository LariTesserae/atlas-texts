**Valve Gallery 2, Upper Penstock — the Throat**

You reach it the way you reach any high place: slowly, and last thing before weather. A cat-track bolted to the mountain's shoulder ends at a concrete apron cantilevered over cut ground, and around the apron the rock faces are so new they still show the flutes of the drill — half-cylinders of cut granite, sharp arrises you could hook a glove on, pale as the day they were made. No lichen has found them yet. A little below the blast line a fringe of alpine turf persists, thin as a beard on a boy's chin; above it, nothing grows, because nothing has had time. Snow sits in the couloirs. The air is a little under three thousand meters thin, and it tastes of cold stone and distance.

The door is small — a steel door in a cut face, with an official stencil, *UPPER PENSTOCK VALVE GALLERY 2*, and under it, in grease pencil, someone's correction: *THE THROAT*. Above the handle, a pictogram: an ear, crossed out. It opens onto a vestibule, a little airlock of quiet, where earmuffs hang on pegs like harness for a different kind of climbing. Through the inner door you can feel the sound pressing — not hearing it yet, more like leaning on it. The printed rule says what every crew knows: talk here, not past here. Then the inner door swings, and the sound arrives the way weather arrives. Not gradually at all.

Inside is a single shallow vault, long and narrow, cut into the peak and lined so completely that you never once touch the mountain. Shotcrete walls still bearing the shadow-lines of their form boards; a membrane behind them; torqued bolt washers in ranks; an epoxy floor gritted like a landing and sloped to a central drain. Two spherical valves squat at the far end, fat steel hearts on the penstock where it comes through its bulkhead, plus a manifold, a bank of relay cabinets, one gantry crane parked high and still, a desk with a chair facing the valves. That is nearly all. The rest is floor. The emptiness is not neglect — it is clearance, engineered and signed for: room for hoses, for the crane's swing, for hands, for the day something enormous comes apart. In the Throat, the space between things is a component, machined as carefully as the things themselves.

And everything is wet. Not flooded — *weeping*. The mountain's groundwater, defeated by the membrane, gathers at weep ports and runs in copper pipes to the drain, and the concrete between the ports sweats in a fine constant exhalation, so that every surface glistens and rags laid down overnight never dry. There are two waters in this place and a doctrine about them: the mountain's water, thin and mineral and cold, and the supply water — snowmelt weeks old, pressurized, untouchable — and the two must never mix. The drain is sacred. Overhead, condensation beads on the ceiling and releases in slow arrhythmia onto a drip plate, adding its small ticks to a roar that swallows them. At the weeps, the first geology of this world has already begun: pale mineral trails a finger long, stone growing the way candlewax does. Give it a century and the walls will have beards.

The cold comes from both directions — the snowmelt in the pipes and the thinness of the air — and settles at six or seven degrees, pooled and stirred: a cold eddy against the bulkhead, a warm plume around the hydraulic power unit, the vestibule kindest of all. Light is rationed here too. Caged bulkhead lamps stand farther apart than they should, one dead, one with a stutter; amber gauge backlights do the fine work; reflections off wet steel supply half the actual seeing. The world of the Throat is mostly visible as gleam.

And the sound. It is not one sound but a braid, and you live inside it. Lowest is the floor-rumble, felt in the sternum and the molars, the mountain itself turned into a speaker cone. Above it, the fundamental — a sustained subterranean note from the manifold, a single organ pipe blown by a river. Then the hisses: each fitting that leaks has its own pitch, and the crews have named them. The kettle is the bypass line equalizing. The wasp lives at elbow three. The old man is the surge vent, wheezing on its long exhale. Speech is not possible; you mouth words and laugh silently, and your own voice comes back as bone-conducted mutter, indistinguishable from anyone else's. The slate by the door and the grease pencil are the language here; the chalk lines are the Throat's literature — *Day 214: still the loudest place I've ever stood.* Crucially, none of this noise is waste. It is the health report. A new rasp is a bearing complaining; a dropped octave is a change of load; and at partial openings the valves make a sound like gravel rushing through them — cavitation, the water beginning to eat the seats. *Gravel at 30% open, unchanged,* say the logs, week after week. In this place, erosion is a sound before it is a fact.

The Throat has tides. Twice a day the world changes key. In the deep hours the machines run as pumps, hauling the reservoir uphill, and the whole braid of sound drops, thickens, settles into labor. At dawn the valves crack and the mountain comes down through here at full voice — a boom through the floor you receive with your boots, spray lifting off every surface at once, gauge needles quivering, chalk dust jumping on the slate. The surge vent thumps like lungs. And once a year, for maintenance week, the gallery is drained and the machines stop, and the silence is enormous and wrong. The crews report insomnia. The drips keep appointments you can hear. Silence here is not peace; it is exposure — the room standing undressed.

Life exists, barely, at the edges of the drain. Orange iron-felt where the seep water runs; gray films; a khaki shimmer of diatoms where the moat of one lamp reaches. In the permanent drip line below that lamp, grass seeds washed in from the reservoir screens sprout — five, seven pale translucent threads, a hand tall, doomed and unbothered, reset to zero by every washdown and every surge, returning every time. The entire animal kingdom is a dozen springtails grazing the slime, discoverable only on knees and patience — deaf, wingless, living where the roar is merely seismic. Nothing that flies survives the sound; sound is the apex predator here. Once in a great while a bird crosses below the fogged porthole in the vestibule, a shadow over white. It never stops.

The rules of the world are few and absolute. Two waters, never mixed; the drain is sacred. Hands speak; chalk records. Move slowly, for your lungs and the machines' — at this height even the fans labor, derated by thin air, and the motors run warm like climbers. The mountain is not a foundation but a colleague: it leans, and on cold nights it taps the wall, seep water finding new ways down, a knocking you feel in the floor rather than hear.

What the Throat is, finally, is a landscape in its cradle. The rock was cut this decade; the epoxy shows boot-wear in only two gray paths, the first trails ever worn here, made by feet instead of water; the mineral beards on the weeps are millimeters. Erosion has so far happened only acoustically, in the gravel-sound of cavitation, but the pits on the valve seats are real and deepening. A nursery of geology, running fast-forward at pocket scale, inside a young mountain, at the top of the weather. And when the drained week ends and the doors open again, the sound returns like water filling a mold — and the room becomes itself: small, loud, wet, sharp-edged, barely ten years old, and still learning what it is.
