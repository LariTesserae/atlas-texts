## The Stillroom

There is no way to see this place. It has never once been seen, not by anything with eyes, and it holds nothing against the world for that. The dark here is not night-dark, not a dark that waits for morning. It is structural — the dark of being inside stone — and it fills the room the way water fills a cup.

You reach it by climbing, because the room sits high, far up in the shoulder of a young mountain, above any place a stream has learned to run. The climb is made in darkness from the first turn of the path, since the way is a seam inside the rock and the rock has no windows. You go by notched footholds pecked into the stone, by a rope-groove worn — no, not worn; the groove is as crisp as the day it was cut, and your fingers only learn its shape the way a key learns a lock. The air outside is thin and cold, and your ears stop up with the rising, and then, near the end, the stone under your palm begins very slowly to warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The mountain's own leftover heat, from the gathering of itself, soaking through rock that has never known a winter. The last turn is marked by a stone gone glassy where a thousand palms have left their oil — each greeting still in it, layered, none ever removed — and then you duck under a lip of rock and the ceiling of the room meets your raised knuckles.

It is small. Nine paces long, four across, a room, not a hall — a pocket the mountain contains without knowing it, the way a fruit contains its one seed. And it is crowded. You cannot put a hand anywhere without touching three small things: a bead, a skin, a thread. Every surface carries a thickening population of forms — none of them old, none of them finished. The stone itself is raw. Its grain is sharp, its edges unblunted, its mineral faces clean as cleaved fruit; there is no lichen, no rounding, no softened corner anywhere. It still smells faintly of its own making, like wet flint and warm iron. And from that raw stone hangs the beginning of everything: soda straws no longer than a grass blade, clustered; curtains of mineral the thickness of a shadow, still wet-edged; skins like the membranes of eggs, laid over skins; terraces at the floor no higher than a knuckle, each holding a spoonful of water. The whole room is a nursery. Nothing here has had time yet, and nothing here will ever take it.

The walls weep. Not in streams — in slow single threads, each no wider than a stem, running down faces of dark rock, gathering into beads that swell and hold and finally let go. The water is always just under the skin of things: press the floor-mud and it glosses; press a wall and a bead answers; the air itself is full to the lips with it. At the low end of the room the seeps gather into a pool two strides across, and its surface sits at the exact height of its own rim — a dry-stone lip, fitted without mortar by hands long gone — and it has always sat there. What the weeping brings, some hidden throat in the stone takes away, hair-fine, unseen. The pool has never risen a knuckle's width. It has never fallen. Somewhere in the ceiling one thread has established itself as the room's official grief, releasing a single drop at intervals too regular to be believed — regular because the interval is not a habit but a balance. One drop. The quiet that is exactly the shape of the last drop. Another.

The air is saturated. Your breath, entering it, simply vanishes; there is nothing left for it to add. Nothing dries here, including you — the warmth presses close on all sides at once, uniform wall to wall, and the sweat stands on your arms without ever lifting away. There is no draft, not the ghost of one; the air has stopped negotiating. Sound behaves as strangely as everything else: the room is mostly quiet, and what sound it makes stays close. Your voice, if you use one, dies at arm's length, taken by the dense wet air and the soft films the way cloth takes a bell's ring. Your own body grows enormous instead — swallow, pulse, the creak of a knee, all of it intimate and near. Once, someone tried to bring fire in. The flame burned like a lamp underwater, hissing, guttering to a blue bruise, and went out, and they went the rest of the way by hand. What a flame would have shown — the gleam on ten thousand beads, the amber of the straws — remains unshown, and the room is exactly itself without it.

There are no plants. Light has never reached here, so nothing leafy was ever possible. What grows instead grows without color or ambition: patches of pale felt-thin film on the weeping walls, a rust-colored blush where the water carries iron, a few white threads fine as cobweb that will never thicken into anything. The patches stay the size of patches. They have always stayed the size of patches. Live things are rarer still. Most visits you will meet no one. But sometimes, when you have knelt at the pool long enough and gone as still as the room, a wake crosses the water without a visible body, and then the body: a small salamander-thing, pewter-pale, blind, its limbs like folded thread, moving with the unhurried completeness of a creature for whom nothing has ever needed hurrying. It does not flee you. It has no experience of anything arriving fast enough to matter. It passes beneath your shadow and out the far side, and the wake it leaves travels to the rim and stops exactly there. In the wet gravel, sometimes, the empty shells of snails so transparent they read as bubbles that decided to keep.

The people who climb here — a people of pecked stone and plaited fiber, whose metal is a rumor — call it the Stillroom, and come for the first water. The water of the mountain's own making, that has never been weather, never been sea, never been anyone's rain. They drink a mouthful of it from a wooden ladle that rests in two cup-marks beside the pool — a ladle generations old, and pristine, because nothing here rots and nothing here dries. It still smells faintly of a tree. Their cup-marks are crisp-edged, sharp as the moment they were struck. The fitted stones of the rim sit as tight as the day they were chosen. Wear is the only clock the hand-tool world has, and this room refuses to wind it.

That is the law of the place, and everything strange about it is only this law speaking: nothing proceeds. The stone does not erode, so the embryonic forms stay embryonic — a first draft kept forever at first-draft freshness, geology that is not history but only a beginning, preserved. Water levels itself; mud resumes its evenness behind your feet; oil given to the palm-stone is kept, all of it, layered like the rings of some slow tree. Outside and above, the mountain is busy — clouds break on shoulders still rising, frost pries at high faces, streams saw at valleys that did not exist a hundred years ago. The mountain does its changing out there. In here it has agreed to stop.

The room is one hour old. It will be one hour old forever — not the same hour, if that distinction comforts you, but any hour of it exchangeable with any other: the same drop, the same bead, the same body-warm dark. There is no earlier in it to be older, and no later with the power to arrive.

You will leave the way you came, hand over hand down the notches, into cold and then into weather. Behind you, in the dark you were never able to see, the room goes on being born at the exact pace of never.
