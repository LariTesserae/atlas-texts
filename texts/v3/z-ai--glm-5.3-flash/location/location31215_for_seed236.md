**The Felted Crown**

You meet it from below, because everything here is below except it. The floor of the world runs out white in every direction — a salt plain so flat that the horizon is not a line but a suggestion, a bright smear where ground gives up and sky begins. Cross it for days and the eye starts inventing mountains out of heat-shimmer. This one is not invented. It stands alone, a single white tooth pushed up through the plain, and from here you can see that its top is not white. The summit wears a dark nap, a low dense pile of color seen edge-on, like velvet photographed from the side. It looks almost soft from a hundred miles away. It is soft.

The plain explains the dryness before you ever arrive. No river feeds this world. No lake shines anywhere on the curve of it. When the plain storms — and it storms, great slow walls of dust that walk across the floor on legs of light — they pass in perfect silence at any distance, because there is nothing in the air for sound to ride. You watch a whole weather system cross the horizon and the loudest thing about it is your own heartbeat.

The mountain is young. That is the second thing the stone tells you, and it tells you rudely, with edges. The escarpment is a wall of fresh fractures, conchoidal faces like broken glass grown calm, glittering cream and gold where the sun strikes the crystal. Nothing has rounded it. Nothing ever will — there is no rain here to round anything, no ice, no trickle. The only weathering is salt-creep, the slow molecular slumping of warm mineral under its own weight, and that takes centuries to soften a corner. The mountain is not eroded. It is merely new. Warm, too — the rock holds the day's heat and a little heat of its own from below, and you feel it through your palms as you climb, like the flank of an animal that has been asleep in the sun.

Then the rim, and the town.

It does not look built at first. It looks grown — as if the mountain, reaching its summit, decided to keep going in a different material. Roofs rise in low tiers, every one of them upholstered in the same dark nap as the crown itself, so the settlement reads as a second, gentler summit stitched onto the first. You could walk the entire town across its rooftops and never once touch the ground. The gaps between roofs are the streets, and they are narrow, and the canopies reach across them until they touch, so that from the street the sky comes down to ribbons. Everything here is near everything else. Life on this summit does not spread; it layers.

Understand, first, the central fact of this place: the lushness is dry. Every soft thing is dry to the root. Press your hand into the felt that covers the ground — the pile-moss, indigo-black, thick as a coatsleeve — and it springs back warm and gives off a smell of wool and wax and warm stone and nothing else. No damp. There has never been damp. Cut a stem — break one, rather; the plants are more like coral than grass — and it does not bleed water. A slow glassy brine beads at the wound, thick as cold honey, and within the minute the plant has sealed itself with its own wax and gone on. The sap of this world is a molten salt, liquid only because the plateau is warm; the warmth is not a comfort here, it is the solvent. These things are alive the way a sunlit stone is warm — chemically, patiently, at the temperature of a long afternoon.

They eat light and dust. That is the whole economy. The leaves are waxed funnels and fine sieves, and the summit harvests for them: dust-falls from the plain's storms settle here, and the dome itself exhales a whisker-fine fur of new crystal that combs particles from the thin air. Where dust gathers thickest — in hollows, in joints, on flat rooftops — life is thickest, which is why the town's roofs are the lushest tier of the whole plateau. The roots, meanwhile, mine the dome itself, drinking slow brine up through the salt like wicks.
