# The Tangleholm

Come down through the green water — and it is all water here, no memory of air anywhere in the world, only a ceiling of lighter green far above where the light comes down like weather — and you will find the city the way you find anything in Tangleholm: gradually, then all at once, a darker knot of living mass resolving out of the murk into walls.

The whole settlement is grown, not built, though its tenders speak of *building* it. They are the rootwrights, and they have tools of shell and sharpened fishbone, of polished stone lashed with woven kelp-cord — and with these they do something stranger than carving: they prune, they graft, they persuade. A city of one species of giant ribweed, cultivated for longer than any of them can remember, into streets and spirals and chambers. The architecture is coral-and-coil, knobby, buttressed like old trees, everything softened by its own growth. No joint is a right angle. Every doorway is a healed wound in the plant, and the plant is always, slowly, trying to close its wounds — so each passage is kept open by patient, generations-long tending. Maintenance here is a religion of shears.

The city sits high — high for a water-world, which means it has grown itself upward off the seafloor on a single colossal root-mass, a mountain of vegetable matter rising from the plain below. There is a rim, a boundary: at the edges the ribweed thins and the city ends, and past that is only open water and distance, and the sound of the deep.

It is not a large place. You could swim the whole of Tangleholm in an afternoon, if the water weren't so loud that you'd stop every hundred body-lengths to listen. The sound is everywhere: the endless rush of the *gray tide* that flows around the root-mountain — a current, slow but ceaseless, that makes the entire city hum and groan like a ship in a storm. The kelp walls creak. Hollow chambers bellow when the tide swells and sighs. The rootwrights live inside this sound the way ground-dwellers live inside weather; they've given names to its voices, and children sleep through a noise that would frighten anyone arriving from the still, dark plain below.

They are cool-blooded, patient people, the rootwrights — the water here runs cold, fed from some deep source, and everything moves at the pace of growth. Nothing happens quickly in Tangleholm. Storms take days to arrive and days to leave. A quarrel might last a season. A wall takes forty years. The city's law is similarly slow: grievances are written into a living scroll-plant whose ink is a culture of stained algae, and by the time the dispute is read aloud, often both parties have forgotten why it mattered. This is considered a feature.

Fauna is thick here — everything shelters in a city of stems. Clouds of small silver fish, each the length of a finger, hold position in the current gaps like leaves trembling in wind. Something the size of a dog but built like a soft hammer crawls the streets on fourteen stubby legs, grazing the film of growth off the walls — the rootwrights keep them, feed them, name them. Larger shapes drift past the rim in ones and twos: long ribbon-things, ribbon-things the length of ten people, moving with an unconcerned dignity, never entering, never quite leaving. And everywhere the tick-and-tick of snapping shrimp in the walls, a sound like slow rain on a distant roof, going on behind all other sounds forever.

The water itself is the strange part, the thing no visitor could explain. It is thicker than it should be — not syrup, not anything so crude, but *lingering*. Stir it and the swirl stays. Swim through it and your wake holds its shape for a full minute behind you, a ghost of motion, before folding back into the whole. The rootwrights read these wakes the way we read footprints. A child's scattered trail through a garden says where they went, how fast, how long ago. Crime here is nearly impossible; the water remembers. And it means the whole city carries a faint, moving overlay of every recent passage — a slow choreography of fading wakes, overlapped and interleaved, so that walking home through the evening streets you drift through the ghost-ripples of everyone who came this way before you, and someone behind you, half a bell later, will drift through yours.

Above the city, the ceiling. The green haze thins upward into a pale distance, and no one in living memory has gone past it and come back to say what's there. The rootwrights have a word for it that translates roughly as *the shallows of the world* — a place, they believe, where the water itself grows thin and you would fall upward into whatever is on the other side of everything.

They are not tempted to find out. There is pruning to do. There is always pruning to do.
