# The Toll

You come up through the fog sea and the first thing you notice is what's missing: green. You have crossed an hour of cloud, and the cloud has beads of water on your sleeves, and when the plateau finally shoulder-up through the white there is nothing above it that photosynthesizes. No trees on the ridgelines. No grass in the amphitheaters. Terraces of stone rise in long finished curves — black basalt, rust-red tuff, slopes the color of dried blood and sulfur — and every wet surface of them is furred, not with moss, but with something else. The second thing you notice arrives before any detail does: the sound. A seething, ticking, tolling loudness, like the whole mountain is simmering.

This is the Toll — a highland the size of a small country, warm as a lowland, sitting two miles up in air that should be cold and isn't, because the mountain itself is warm-hearted. The springs come up hot. That is the first rule of the place, and almost everything else follows from it.

## The water

The Toll is a sponge. Beneath the whole plateau, water sits everywhere and near — a hand's depth down, any hand's depth, anywhere, the ground darkens and weeps. The cliffs do not stream so much as *exhale*: warm mineral water beads from a thousand seams, runs in glassy channels the water has spent millennia polishing, and where a spring meets the cool air at the rim, the vapor condenses back onto the stone and the stone weeps again. The cliffs sweat. The walls of the city sweat. Puddles hold between the cobbles permanently, rimmed with crust.

And the mountain has a tide. The water table breathes on a slow two-year cycle — a year rising, a year falling — and everything on the Toll, animal and civic alike, is phased to that breath. Reservoirs fill on the Rise and are rationed on the Fall. The grazers' circuits shift with it, up-slope and down. Even the sound changes: on the high tide, the plateau rings wetter, louder, more urgent; on the low, it murmurs.

The city has made an industry of catching what rises. On the rim, where the fog sea combs over the edge daily, whole fields of cloud-rigs stand — masts and mesh arrays, acres of them, harvesting the fog into cisterns with a sound like wind combing silk. Rooftops everywhere are condensers. Streets are gutters. Water runs downhill through the city in stone channels and turns wheels at every drop, and the wheels are the industry: ram-pumps, hammers, hoists, lathes, the deep thump-thump of mill-works you can feel through your boot soles in any quarter of the town called Stairfall.

## The shag

With no plants, the question of what everything eats has only one answer on the Toll, and it grows on the rocks.

They call it the shag. It is not a plant, not a lichen, not a moss — it is a felt, a skin, a pelt of microbes and fungus-thread and mineral precipitate that the warm springs feed directly: dissolved gases and metals rise with the water, and the shag fixes them into living mat. It comes in colors no plant bothers with — manganese black, iron rust, sulfur yellow, a violet film that appears only on the undersides of overhangs. It grows slowly, patient as everything here, maybe a fingernail's width in a season, and it covers every damp surface on the mountain: cliffs, channels, boulders, walls, roofs, the backs of animals. The Toll is wet fur from the air down to the water table. There is no soil anywhere; nothing rots into earth. The ground is stone, and the stone wears a coat.

The shag is the field. Everything else is harvest.

## The fauna

And the harvest is *crowded*. This is the thing that undoes visitors: they came expecting a barren mineral upland, and instead they cannot find a bare surface or an empty pocket of air. The animal life of the Toll is overwhelming — layered, near-touching, thick as the water it drinks.

On the ground and the low slopes: the cliff-wethers, big slow grazers the size of oxen, moving in patient herds along their inherited circuits, rasping the shag off the stone with tongues like wet sandstone. A wether can polish a cliff face to the gloss of river rock over a lifetime, and the herds have been walking the same loops for so long that their paths are worn into the mountain as smooth roads, thirty feet wide, without anyone cutting them. The city's streets break for wether-roads. Slowness has right-of-way here; nothing hurries a grazer, and everything knows it.

In the terraces and spring-pools: the sieve-mouths, black tadpole-things in congregations so dense the water looks solid, straining shag-spores and mineral flour from the current. Birds pick them; long-legged stillwalkers stand in the shallows for hours on two legs like folded umbrellas, then strike once, without visible motion, and have their meal. Fish-freighted channels churn silver when the wethers come to drink.

On the cliffs: galleries of burrow-nesters, whole faces of stone riddled with nests like swiss cheese, the entrance holes breathing warm air — the mountain breathing through ten thousand small mouths. Above them, the rim-birds, the bellcranes, huge and deliberate, crossing the sky in slow staged altitudes. The air over the Toll is trafficked in bands: midges and shag-moths low, the cranes and their shadows high, gliders riding the thermal seam at the rim all day. At dusk the moth-roads open — rivers of spore-carriers flowing from crust-bloom to crust-bloom — and the city, which knows its business, dims its lamps on spore nights so the roads stay straight.

And over everything, always, the graveflies — the cleanup. Nothing on the Toll dies and stays. A carcass is found within the hour and stripped within the day, and this is why a place of such crushing density never stinks of death: the ground is kept clean by a thousand small industrious employees, and it is always, eerily, clean. Visitors find this the most unsettling fact of the plateau — more than the fog, more than the tolling — that in all that teeming, you will never once see a dead thing rest.

## Stairfall

The city follows the springs. Stairfall is built in terraces up a warm amphitheater, each level straddling a spring-line, each roof angled to condense, each wall of porous stone that drinks and sweats and grows shag on its shaded side. Lower quarters are human. Upper galleries are shared — the potter-colonies, mason-wasps the size of a thumb, build their paper-and-mud towers into the eaves of the granaries and the bell-towers, and the city insures them, repairs around them, considers them architecture. A building on the Toll is never finished; it accretes. Crust grows on it, nests weld to it, mineral-rich water cements its mortar year by year. The old quarters are a single fused lump of wall and nest and stone-pelt, and they are the proudest.

The industry is hydraulic and honest about it. Wheel-pits thunder in the stair-wells. Counterweights of water run the funiculars. Warm tailwater from the mills is piped out to the shag-ranches — bare stone fields deliberately kept wet and mineral-rich — where the crust blooms twice as fast and scraping crews take their rotation, like harvesters of a crop that happens to be alive and immobile. Salt pans at the terrace edges crop what the water leaves behind. There are furnaces — you need fire for glass and metal — but the deep engine of the place is falling water, and everyone knows the mountain is the employer.

You can eat here, is the thing. The wethers are meat; the sieve-mouths are roe and protein; there is a spring-yeast the vats grow. The city eats the same chain everything else does. It is one guild among many, and it behaves like one.

## The covenant

Which brings you to the Sunken Quarter, at the western edge of Stairfall: a district that, three centuries ago, drilled. They wanted the water directly, at depth, under their own power. The floor of the plateau gave. What is there now is a warm lake in a square of drowned rooftops, and in it live white blind things — sieve-mouths grown long and pale and wrong — and the city has left it exactly as it is, as a lesson you can drink from if you're determined.

The covenant is carved in stone at every springhead, and it is short:

*Take the water as it rises. Never pierce the floor.*

And: *Leave the Keepers their ground.*

The Keepers are the strangest fact on the Toll, and the locals will not over-explain them. Enormous animals — slow, gray-plated, taciturn — that live at the major springheads and seem to do nothing at all except be present. They do not graze. They do not defend, exactly. But where a Keeper stands, the spring runs clean and the shag grows true, and where Keepers have been driven off — it has been tried, once or twice, in greedier centuries — the springs silt and sulk. So they are left alone, and fed, and at night, when they call to one another across the miles, the deep bell of it goes through the bedrock and up through your feet, and that is the sound the plateau is named for.

## What it sounds like, what it is

Stand on the rim at dusk. Below you the fog sea is rising back up the walls of the world, and the Rim Nets hiss and comb it. Bellcranes cross overhead in stately relays, calling in bent-brass tones. Somewhere below, a wether herd moves through the terraces at its permanent walk, and the city gives it the whole street, wheels idling, people leaning in doorways. Drips count everywhere, ten thousand clocks out of sync. The mill-pits thump. A moth-road opens, a river of pale wings, and a shag-bloom ahead of it glows faintly green in the last light.

And under all of it, the toll: Keeper to Keeper, through two miles of warm wet stone, a note you hear with your ribs.

Nothing here is fast. The mountain has finished being carved — the erosion is done, the domes and channels are in their final form, and all that remains is polishing, and grazing, and the long tide going up and coming down. The city doesn't conquer the plateau; it keeps house on it. The animals don't fear the city; they route around it, and through it, and nest in it. The whole vast warm crowded mechanism is in balance — the wet stone growing its skin, the skin feeding the mountain of meat, the meat keeping the paths worn, the water rising, always rising, patient as anything.

It is the loudest quiet place you will ever stand, and the slowest teeming, and the greenest landscape in the world with no green in it at all.
