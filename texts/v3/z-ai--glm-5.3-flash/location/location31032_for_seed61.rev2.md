# The Finished Town

You reach it by following water downward. Somewhere under the swell of an unremarkable hill, a stream goes into the limestone and does not come out, and if you are stubborn enough to go in after it — on your belly, through a squeeze that fits a person the way a keyhole fits a key — you will feel the place before you see it. There is breath on your face in the crawl. Warm-wet, steady, exhaled. And there is a note. It enters your sternum before your eyes have anything to do with. You are being swallowed down the throat of an instrument.

Then the crawl opens, and you are inside a town the size of a chapel.

The chamber runs perhaps forty paces long and half that across, domed to the height of a tall tree, and every meter of it is built. Not occupied — *built*, densely, wall to wall, the way a ship is built: terraced dwellings rising in three tiers up the walls, stair-lanes no wider than a coffin, arched bridges of single slabs crossing the channels that thread the floor, a little paved plaza, a cistern tier, galleries of niches. A whole settlement's worth of infrastructure compressed into a pocket of rock, every surface worked by hand, every handmark worn smooth. You could shout across it. You cannot, because there is nothing to shout over.

Water is the material the town is made of, in the way other towns are made of timber. The walls weep. Not in streaks but in a fine universal beading, the stone sweating at a hundred points, the ceiling raining upward-downward in soft irregular-seeming drops that are not irregular at all. Each drip falls into a carved stone cup. Each cup is positioned to catch its own drop and no other. The air is thick as wool with moisture; your clothes take on weight within minutes; the chill gets into your wrists and stays. The pool at the center — round, stepped, walled like a civic monument — sits at a line marked by a polished band on its rim, and the band has not moved. An overflow flue drains the excess with a sound like held breath. The water table passes through this chamber and the town has pinned it to exactly this height, the way a dressmaker pins a hem.

Here is the thing the builders understood, and spent generations earning: water only carves when it is hungry or full. Make it exactly saturated — blend the cold soft seep with the mineral vein until the mix neither dissolves the stone nor feeds it — and the water becomes inert. Finished. It flows without cutting, beads without staining, drips without deepening its cups. The erosion of this place is complete: everything that water could wear is worn to the exact smoothness water wants, and everything water cannot reach still bears the tool marks of the people who made it, crisp as the day they were cut. The boundary between the two — polished and unpolished — is a shoreline that never moves. You can walk the chamber and trace, on every wall, the precise territory of the wet.

The sound is the town's other half, and the town's whole argument.

The crawl you entered by is a flue. Air moves through it, driven by pressure differences the builders engineered with ground-stone valves, and it enters the chamber through the great low pipe — a drilled shaft in the dome that speaks one note, deep, like the after-ring of a struck bell that was never allowed to fade. Around the chamber, dozens of smaller stone pipes stand where water spills into them; each one speaks an overtone, tuned by fractions of a shell's thickness. Some roofs are slabs of stone cut thin and laid on resilient packing, and the ceiling-rain lands on them in rhythm — a fixed rhythm, counted by the drone, every drip landing in phase with every other drip. The sum of it is a chord. Loud — loud the way a waterfall is loud, felt in the teeth and the ribs — and absolutely without variation. There is no melody. There is no development. There is the chord, and under the chord, if you press your ear to a wall, the sound of the water doing the one thing it will do forever: nothing.

Speak, and your voice is flattened into it. The chamber swallows words whole and returns them as overtones.

The light is dim and comes from below. Thin skins of pale glow live in the wettest lines — in the channels, at the pool's rim, in the weeping seams — a film of something at the very threshold of life: not moss, not mold, not anything with a leaf or a stem, just a cold luminous slick, the last and least of growth. It gives a moonlight-underwater illumination; you see shapes, edges, the shine on wet stone; you do not see color. The lamp niches carved beside every doorway are empty, and — this is worth a long look — there is no soot anywhere. No smoke shadow above a niche, no char, no ash. This was a fireless town. Everything in it was cut with sand and patience: bow-drills, shell saws, abrading cord, years. The only metal is a handful of hammered copper pins in the great valves, green-stained, and the green is stable — the air is saturated to precisely the point where verdigris holds its color and goes no further. Even the corrosion finished.

The buildings tell you what the town was *for*, and the answer is: the water. The plaza is a market of spouts and measuring-grooves, stone gauges graduated in shell-widths, little weirs where a volume could be counted and certified. Whatever currency these people used, it was measured water, banked in cisterns, audited by drip. Storehouses hold ranked jars sealed with clay — inside them, sand, ochre, salt, tools in oiled rows: the inventory of a craft, not a harvest. A tier of benches faces the pool like a council facing an altar, and of course it is an altar; of course it is also a gauge. The doorways have no doors. Nothing here needed shutting out.

And no bones. No graves, no ashes, no bodies. The people finished and walked out, and it seems the finishing and the leaving were the same act. The last thing they built was the exit-squeeze, low and humbling, breathing against your face, so that anyone who came after would arrive the way you arrived: through the throat, into the note.

The ecology of the place is four sentences long. The glow-film grows on the wet stone — its growth is the only growing left, and it is exactly matched by grazing, so the film neither spreads nor dies, a field held at one green-lit moment forever. A single pale snail does the grazing now, its rasp lost under the chord. In the pool, shrimp drift, white and translucent, and the count always comes back the same: seven. One blind fish orbits them. Nothing has eaten the fish; perhaps nothing will.

A visitor's watch says what the town will not: the drips keep time, the film pulses at one period, the fish keeps its orbit, and the temperature of the air does not drift by a breath's worth between dawn and dawn, if such words even apply. This is a place with rhythm and no history. Its strangeness is not that anything impossible happens — it is that nothing happens at all, perfectly, on purpose, at volume.

When you leave, crawling back up the throat against the exhale, the chord follows you into the squeeze and holds until the last bend, and then you are under the hill in the ordinary dark, and the note is gone — from *you*. Not from the town.

It is not ruins. Nothing there has fallen; nothing has risen. The water is exactly as wet as it will ever be. The chord is not music and not an alarm and not a memorial — it is the sound of the balance holding, proof offered continuously, a certificate the town has been signing in one sustained tone since the last hand left the last valve.

The town is over. The over lasts.
