The mountains around it are still being worked on. This one is finished.

Everything else in the range is still mid-sentence — ridges being planed, valleys deepening, scree moving an inch a year — but this summit is done. Whatever it was going to be, it already was, and then ten million years of weather took the argument away. What remains is a flagstone. A pavement, worn almost exactly level, sitting high in thin bright air with the unfinished hills for company below. It is the oldest surface in sight. Water made it flat and then kept going, because water is never loyal, and what got left behind was a place where erosion has nothing left to do. Nothing here is becoming. Everything here is merely continuing.

The stone is the floor of a sea that left. It is crowded, that floor — packed with the shells and stems and cups of creatures who built the original reef, back when this summit was the bottom of the world. Fossils so dense the pavement reads like script: spirals, lilies, button after button after button. Walk across it and you walk on a graveyard arranged as flooring, polished by wind into a pale grey almost white, and on cold bright days the light lies on it like glass.

The whole place is small. That's the surprise, every time. From below, from the cloud level, it reads as a peak, a fortress of a peak — but on top you can cross it at a walk in under an hour, and every point on it is a stone's throw from an edge. The cliffs drop in worn steps toward hills that were once its neighbors' peaks, ground down the same way. At dawn the cloud fills all the lower world to the horizon and the pavement becomes an island, and the sense of boundary is total: here, and nowhere else. You could shout across the entire country of it. People do.

The cold is not a metaphor and not a mood. It is the first fact. Breath falls out of you as glitter. The wells skin over at night with ice thin as a fingernail. The light is ordinary light — just honest daylight, nothing enchanted — but it arrives thin and glassy at this height, with nothing left between it and you, and it warms the dark moss and not the air, so that the whole plateau is lit like a stage and cold like a cellar. Frost is a nightly worker here, cracking a grit of stone loose, patient as everything else.

And yet the place is lush. That is the second surprise and the better one, because the green arrives in the fissures.

The surface is a sieve. Rain comes in short hard curtains off the afternoon clouds, hits the bare slabs with a sound like applause — drumming, drumming — sheets, gathers, and is gone: every drop chooses, in a second, to slip into a crack. The plateau drinks its own weather instantly and gives almost nothing back. There are no streams. There is fog instead, great banking fogs that pour over the edges from the cloud-sea, and dew, and the wells.

The wells are round holes in the rock, some lined with old clay by hands that are long past caring, and here is the first true strangeness: they rise and fall together. All of them, on the same slow rhythm of days, in unison — filling a little, draining a little, like a chest breathing under the floor. The people here call it the wells turning. When the wells rise, everything that can flower, does — more on that in a moment — and the whole precinct's mood lifts with the water. Nobody fully knows what the tide is. The sea that could explain it is a hundred million years gone. The wells just breathe, and the gardeners have organized their entire lives around the breath.

The gardens live in the cracks. Every fissure in the pavement is a terraced bed, tended with hands and sticks — that's all, hands and sticks, because there is no metal here, no wheel, no plow, no machine of any kind; the technology of this city is weather, gravity, and patience. The grikes hold their own climate: a hand pushed into the moss finds fifteen borrowed degrees of summer. The moss lawns are deep as mattresses, green the way green is in rainforest — a cold green, but deep — and ferns unroll from the shaded cracks like slow flames. In the widest fissures stand the wind-trees: a forest you step over, trees a hundred years old and knee-high, gnarled into knots, each one a whole oak's stubbornness compressed by the wind into bonsai. Dropped into the moss, a cup vanishes. The gardens are postage stamps on a continent of stone, and that is why they are lush — all the lushness of a valley, concentrated into pockets the size of a table, fed by fog wrung from the moss itself and by the turning of the wells.

The town is a rumor of walls.

That is nearly the literal truth: from above, the settlement is almost invisible. The houses are dry-stone beehives built into the wide grikes, roofed with living turf, so the city from the air is just the pavement continuing, with a blue thread of hearth-smoke to say otherwise. Forty hearths, maybe. The emptiness is deliberate, and it is architecture. The gardens need still air and the houses need still air, so the streets are lanes of pure wind — the town is built around the wind's habits, not against them. Walls here are not defense; nobody has anything to defend against but the weather. The walls are windbreaks and dew-fences: fog strands on them like wool and drinks the gardens by slow drip. Each household is a pocket of quiet inside a loud country, and the space between households is vast on purpose — the precinct could hold ten times the buildings and holds a tenth, and the rest is air, and the air is doing the work.

Which brings you to the sound, because the sound is enormous, and it comes from the emptiness.

Nothing up here stops a noise. Wind is the permanent tenant, and the pavement is its instrument: every grike is a pipe, and the whole surface flutes and chords as the gusts change — a pipe organ left out in the rain for an age of the world, playing itself. Beneath the floor, the reef is hollow, its old chambers and channels still open, and the hidden river that uses them is heard constantly through the vents: booming, gulping, swallowing. At the springs you can put your ear to a crack and listen to a waterfall you will never see. The wells sigh when they turn. Thunder ricochets between the cloud-floor and the cliffs. At night the frost cracks stone like knuckles. And the acoustics are honest but wrong: sounds arrive twice, once through the air and once through the rock, so footsteps precede their owners, and words said an hour's walk away arrive in the fog at your shoulder, faint and complete. Everything here is heard at least twice. The emptiness is not silence's home. It is the amplifier.

The creatures are few, and each is an event. At dawn in still weather it snows silk — young spiders ballooning down from who knows where, catching the light in threads across the grikes. Small white birds live in the wells, silent nine days out of ten, and they sing only when the wells are rising, a song like something remembering. Snails with shells shaped like the fossils in the pavement graze the pools — mimicry or ancestry, nobody's sure. In the springs live pale shrimp, blind, numerous, unbothered. And once or twice a year a line of deep hoofprints crosses the pavement from one edge to the other, fills with dew, and is gone; the maker is never seen. The gardeners leave bundles of moss at the gap in the wall where the tracks pass through. It seems like the minimum courtesy.

Because here is the thing the place keeps telling you, the reason for its strangeness that never quite resolves into explanation: there are two cities on this ground, and the people only built one of them. The other one — the reef, the hollow basement, the chambers the river flows through — was built by animals, grown a grain at a time, without hands, without tools, without minds in any sense we'd recognize. It is the oldest built thing this world has made, and the human town is a lichen on its roof: a thin wet garden of moss and fern and forty fires, layered on top of an architecture no one designed. The town's construction is layered too, layer upon layer by different masons — the reef, then the water, then the wind, then the moss, then the hand — and every layer took an age. The local rules follow from all of it, and children learn them early: build low, so the wind keeps its lanes. Don't dig deep; the under-country has opinions. Never whistle at the wells. Everything that holds still here will hold forever, and everything that moves will move fast — there is no middle speed, no moderate change; stone or weather, choose — and all water is borrowed, and the interest is paid in sound.

A day here runs like a beating heart. Frost at first light, then the sun touching the black moss and the whole plateau beginning to smoke as the ice gives up its vapor. Fog pouring over the edges by midmorning, wringing itself out on the walls. Noon in hard glassy clarity, shadows sharp enough to cut. Then afternoon: the clouds building from below, coming up over the rim, and the rain walking across the pavement in visible curtains — the only time the town goes indoors all day — followed by the bloom, which is the spectacle the whole ecology is arranged around: within hours of the water, moss fruits into red-and-gold studs, lichens spark, the tiny flowers finish their entire lives between one rain and the next frost, and for an afternoon the dead-grey floor of an ancient sea is the most colorful garden at any elevation anywhere. Then the wells turn together in the evening, the birds sing the rising, steam stands off the stone in the cooling air, and night arrives with its booming under-foot, its cracking frost, its stars close enough to owe you weather.

It is not a young place and it is not a dying one. It is a finished place that has agreed to keep a few small things alive in the pockets — a wet green minute balanced on a flat grey eternity, loud because it is empty, lush because it is cold, inhabited because once, impossibly, animals built a city here and left the doors open. The mountain ended. The garden didn't.

And at night the reef hums under everything, the wells turn over in unison like a sleeper rolling, the wind finishes the sentence it began at dawn — and the little city, forty fires in forty hollows, listens with its stones.
