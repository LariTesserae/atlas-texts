# Coldharbour

The name is an old joke, older than anyone who still uses it: a cold harbour, a sheltered hollow with no water deep enough to float a plank. It is a joke with a catch, because the water here does something better than floating things. Left standing, it clouds, glimmers, and then quietly sets. By evening your footprints are not puddles but ridges of faint glass. In Coldharbour, water is the ore. Everything else follows from that.

You come to it across a moor that has never grown a green thing — dry clinkery stone, rust-red and bone-pale, with a stubble of mineral whiskers that tinkles faintly when anything crosses it. The word *meadow* has no purchase here. What stands in for grass is chemistry. The rim of the sink rises only a little above the surrounding ground, but it rises enough: from it you can see the whole place at once, a bowl perhaps three hundred paces across, ringed by a low bluff of the same dry stone, breached on the far side by a single outflow channel the keepers call the sough. The sky above the bowl is a small gray dish. Wind exists here, but it stays up at the rim; you hear it passing like a coat dragged over the lip of the hollow, and below that, nothing louder than dripping.

Steps have been cut into the bluff — old ones, worn to shallow scoops, each scoop holding a string of beads. The walls weep constantly. Water does not pour here so much as *exude*: threads of it, finer than twine, sliding down the dark stone from somewhere near the surface, feeding everything they touch. As you descend, the air changes — cooler, thicker, faintly sweet, like a struck match that never quite finished striking. That sweetness is the breath. The basin exhales it from somewhere deep, in slow tides, and it pools in the hollow because the hollow was made to hold it.

The floor of the sink is terraced, descending in bevelled shelves to a shallow pond, and every terrace is crowded. This is the first thing to understand about Coldharbour: nothing in it is dead, and almost nothing in it is still. The dark stone of the terraces is not stone in the ordinary sense — it is crust, ancient set water, and it is growing, always growing, millimetre by patient millimetre, as the seep brine rises, degasses, and hardens. The crust builds shelves, lips, flutes, hollow cups; water wears it down; the two processes have long since agreed on a pace. The terraces are set like poured wax that was poured a very long time ago. There are old waterlines etched on the bluff walls, high and low, and the level no longer reaches any of them. The basin has settled into itself. It is in balance the way an old tool is in balance — worn exactly to its work.

And the crust is food. That is the second thing.

Gritters cover it — limpet-coned animals, thumbnail to hand-sized, their shells of grown mineral indistinguishable from the rock they sit on until they move. They graze the living crust, rasping it away in fresh bright trails, and the trails *shine*: the brine's pale glimmer, disturbed, flares briefly and fades, so that a fresh-grazed path across a terrace reads as a line of light someone drew and is slowly erasing. There are thousands of them. There are dimple-skaters on the pond in their hundreds — the surface freckled with small pulling moons, each dimple a mouth working the film where the sweetgas collects. In the sough current, colonies of veilworms stand with their fans unfurled, straining the glimmer out of the water, and when the current surges the fans open one after another down the line like a word being passed along a row of mouths, each closing with a soft wet click, a ripple of clicks, a wet page turning somewhere out of sight. Between every shelf stand frass-cones, pale and chimney-shaped, thrown up by colonial miners — small tunneling things that bore the softer crust and void their castings into new substrate, keeping the whole terrace porous, aerated, alive. You cannot put a hand down anywhere in Coldharbour without touching something occupied. The ground is fully claimed, floor to ceiling, in overlapping layers.

The pond has its heron. It is grey and jointed and it does not move for hours — a folded carpenter's rule of an animal, standing at the weep lines where the gritters come to drink. You see it only when it moves. And it moves, when it moves, at exactly the right time.

Because the basin makes its own weather. Twice a day, when the seep-warmed water meets the cool air just so, the whole hollow drizzles from the inside — mist rising off the terraces, condensing, falling again as a fine self-rain that leaves every surface beaded and every drip-flare doubled. It is cool here always, eight or ten degrees in the air, colder in the water; the only warmth in Coldharbour is borrowed, and it comes from the shed. The gritters know the drizzle. At its first touch they all extend at once, and for a minute the entire basin faintly *rasps* — the sound of far-off sandpaper, the loudest the fauna ever gets — and in that minute the still one unfolds. One strike per drizzle, usually. Then the rain thins, the rasp fades, the trails glow and fade, and the hollow goes back to its small noises.

The shed stands on grown boots — four iron legs that the crust has claimed up to the knee and been permitted to keep. It is the only built thing in the bowl, unless you count the steps, the path of cast blocks laid across the soft ground, the pipeline running out to the settling racks, and the chalk tally of engine-hours on the door beam, which is current. Someone was here this morning; there is a wheelbarrow of half-set blocks by the racks, and footprints filling with glinting water, going toward the rim. The keeper is elsewhere. The works does not care. The engine under the shed's floor draws its fuel through a valve from the gas dome beneath the basin — it eats the exhalation, has eaten nothing else for decades — and drives the pump, which lifts brine to the racks, where it is allowed to set into blocks the size of loaves, each stamped with a date. The blocks come out of their moulds carrying a cold pale light that lasts for years. Cast stone from living water: that is the whole industry, small as a chapel, older than the tally. The crust grows back over anything scraped, so the keepers scrape only the moving parts and let the legs be worn as boots. Old buildings elsewhere, far from here, are mortared with these bricks, and on dark nights you can find them by their faint green-grey glow, decades later, still remembering being wet.

The pump chuffs every eleven seconds. That is the sound of Coldharbour, under everything: *chuff*, and the tick of cooling iron, and the drips — each with its own voice, a *tink* off the shed roof, a *tok* on shell, a *plip* in open water, and once in a while the deep bell-note of a drop falling into a hollow cup of crust that rings like a bowl. Then the sough pulls its breath. The pond creeps to the lip, the syphon catches, and a rope of pale light runs down the outflow channel — the whole level dropping a hand's depth in a minute with a gurgle and a surge, ending in a soft *gulp* when it breaks, and a ringing afterdrip. Everything on the current line rides that pulse. The veilworms feast on what it knocks loose; something lamprey-shaped surges up the channel with it and falls back; the pond level climbs and climbs again.

Once, while you stand there, the engine stumbles. It coughs, misfires, catches — and in the half-second of the miss, the entire basin holds its breath. The fans close. The dimples still. Even the drips seem to hang. It is only a startle, a reflex running through every animal at once, but for that half-second the hollow is absolutely silent and absolutely attentive, and you understand that the engine is not an intrusion here. It is a feature. It has been folded in. The flue-nesters spinning whisker-wool against the warm exhaust pipe knew that long before you did; when the drizzle comes they come out around the chimney in a soft grey blizzard, unbothered, and settle back.

By the time the dish of sky dims toward evening, the glows are carrying more of the seeing: trails lighting and fading, the syphon rope bright down its channel, the racks of stamped blocks giving off their faint patient sheen — dozens of small lights that never warm anything — and one warm yellow square, the window of the shed, the only orange in the world. The engine chuffs. The level climbs. The still one folds itself back into a heron-shaped absence at the weep line. Footprints by the barrow have gone to glass.

Everything here runs, and will keep running when no one watches: seep to crust, crust to grazers, gas to engine, water to stone, stone to racks, level to lip and over. The whole place is in balance, worn to its work. You were, for an hour, the only passing weather.
