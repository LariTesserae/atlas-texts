# The Hill

The country is made. All of it. Walk in any direction from the gates and you walk on laid ground: terraces of packed paste, retaining walls curred dark with age, causeways stitched across the lowlands on piers of soil-and-saliva. There is stone somewhere under all of it — there must be — but it is a rumor. Deeper than the deepest well, deeper than the oldest floor, the natural ground lies buried beneath centuries of the nation's work, and no one has ever opened a trench far enough to meet it. When the builders dig, they dig the hill's own past. The substrate is archive.

The land itself is a high country — a cold, dim plateau where the sky stays low and grey and the sun is a paler patch behind haze, never a disc, never a heat you can feel on your face. Into this chill the hill rises like an argument: a mass of spires and chimneys the size of a valley, its summit a day's climb above the terraces, its bulk so large and so warm that it makes its own weather. From far off you know it first by the plume — a soft standing mist above the crown, the hill's breath condensing in the thin cold air. It looks like the hill is smoking. It is only exhaling.

Rain comes rarely and arrives like a decision. Between rains the country is dry in the way of high places — your lips crack, the hay terraces rattle — but the hill keeps its damp the way a ship keeps cargo. Cold nights chill the outer crust; warm air from the deep halls meets the cool walls and beads into dew on the inside; channels no wider than a finger collect it, drop by patient drop, and feed it down to the spongy reservoir galleries and the cisterns. There are whole districts built around water: cool stone-less vaults where black frogs sing in the overflow pools, where moths drink the seep lines, where the wall itself is soft with moisture and the gardeners come at the damp hours to press their mouths against it. Pockets of wet in a larger dryness. The hill drinks a storm for a season.

The outer crust has finished becoming what it is. Rain, over generations, has fluted the old spires into long vertical grooves, worn ledges into shelves, blackened everything with lichen — and every storm's new damage is answered, slowly, by repair crews who work for weeks patching a single breach. So the hill stands in balance: worn and mended at the same rate, its shape settled, its substance perpetually renewed. Nothing on it is young except its work. Swallows nest in the flutes of the old towers. Bats fold into the vent mouths at the dimming. The surface teems the way a cliff teems — lightly, at its edges — because the true weight of life is inside.

You hear the inside before you see it. The approach roads carry the hum up through your feet.

At the gates the sound becomes real: a roar with texture. Close up, the doors are worn smooth as river stones, thresholds polished by a billion passages, and the scent-toll hangs at the entrance like a wet cloth — the nation's smell, which admits and refuses. Soldiers with sealed, helmet heads stand in the traffic; they do not slow it. Inside, the corridors take a body's width and the bodies come shoulder to shoulder, a river of pale blind traffic flowing both ways, so dense that passing is a cooperation and crowding is simply weather. The dark is total. Eyes give up within three steps. From there the hill is navigated entirely by ear and air: current-gradient, scent-rope, and above all the drums.

The deafness is the hill's nervous system. Every wall is a drumskin; every footfall lands somewhere. The baseline is never silent — a million jaws grinding pulp in the granaries, the dry rustle of guests in the thatch, the high thin saw of beetles — and over it runs the language: alarm drums that ripple out through the mass faster than anything with legs could travel, so that a breach on the north face is known, and being repaired, before the sound of it has finished echoing. When the whole nation drums at once — and it does, on the bad nights — the hill itself resonates, a bass note you feel in your teeth, loud enough to be the only thing in the world. The guests have adapted by going quiet in compensation. The moths hear through their wings.

And below the granaries, in the warm wet dark, the gardens. This is the green of the place, though green has no meaning here. Halls the size of fields, stacked terrace on terrace into the depths, all of it planted: comb after comb of chewed wood paste, spongy and grey-white, fruiting over with the garden fungus in buttons and brackets and fine velvet lawns — white, gold, bruise-mauve where the oldest beds blush. Gardeners move through the rows ceaselessly, weeding, turning, inoculating, cropping. The air is jungle-air, thick and sweet-rotten and humid enough to fog. Nothing here has ever needed the sun; the gardens are lit by nothing, or by the faint cold glow of the rot itself in a few deep chambers, just enough to see your own hands by. The harvest never ends and the planting never ends, and the abundance is not a season but a condition. Acres of food, growing in the dark, in the middle of a dry cold country, at altitude, in the warm.

Warmth is the hill's oldest engineering. The heat comes up from below — the packed billions of bodies, the compost of the deep beds — and it stratifies honestly: granaries dry and cool, gardens temperate, and at the very center the nursery halls, warm as blood, where the air is soft and the eggs lie in candlelit... no — in no light at all, in attended rows, tended by nurses who groom each one. There is a queen's chamber at the warmest point of all, a long dim hall, and the pulse at the center of it is slow, and vast, and animal, and no one makes anything of it. It is simply the hearth the whole structure is insulated around.

The hill breathes on the day's turning. When the cold comes down outside, the warm air inside rises and draws fresh air through the low galleries; when the outside warms, the draft reverses and the chimneys take it back. Put your hand at a vent mouth and feel the tide change. This is the great motion of the place — not one event but a slope, a gentle endless gradient, heat and damp always sliding from where they are plenty to where they are few. The builders ride it the way sailors ride a current.

Everything else here is slow. A generation adds a storey. A gallery abandoned in the low quarters will be re-colonized and migrated over years, its walls re-chewed, its tunnels widened to the new traffic. After a bad storm the repair of one flank can outlast the crew that began it. And because the hill is always eating its own past — old floors mined for paste, failed chambers rendered back into raw material — its age is not preserved but recycled. The hill is older than any of its materials.

Once a year, when the rain finally comes, the hill does its one loud mad thing: the flight. Chimneys disgorge winged ones by the hundred thousand, and for a night the deafening reaches the open sky — a hurricane of wings, the frogs roaring from the cisterns, swallows and bats and everything that eats flying meat arriving in answer. By dawn the sky is littered with pairs of discarded wings and the terraces are silent, and somewhere beyond the horizon a hundred small hills have been founded. The old hills on the far skyline — fluted, dark, some of them quiet now — began exactly this way.

That is the whole of the law here, and it is not a strange law: nothing is owned; every corridor has two exits; the drum is obeyed; waste is building material; everything is eventually eaten, including the hill. The place holds together not by staying the same but by never stopping. It is not finished. It was not finished a thousand years ago. It is only a nest, built by bodies, out of the country it stands in — and the country is made, all the way down.
