# The Weeping Table

You come up over the last shoulder of rock and the land simply opens — flat, wet, and warm, a table of country sitting on the sky. Behind you the climbing path ends at a crisp lip you could step off. Ahead, the whole place lies in view at once: every pool, every stand of green, every small dark structure, and all of it bracketed by edges. There is no interior here, no hidden folds. You can see the rim in every direction, and the rim is falling away.

Down in the old country, far below, they call it the Weeping Table. It is maybe two hours' walk across, a marsh carried up to altitude on a raft of new stone — and it is new; nothing about it has been softened yet. The edges are knife-crisp. The outer walls drop sheer for a long time, pale rock unweathered, unrounded, streaked with dark wet lines the water has drawn down it like ink on a fresh page. There is no soil to speak of. There has not been time.

The first thing your body learns, before your eyes finish their inventory, is the heat. It comes up through your boots. The entire surface is warm — a low, even warmth, like standing on a sleeping animal — and it is warmer along lines. The seams. They are unmistakable once you see them: straight channels in the stone where the rock has been fused to a dark glaze, running flat across the Table, meeting at angles too exact for any river. The ground is warm everywhere. Along the seams it is hot — hot enough that near the junctions you keep your palm off the stone. The heat has a geometry. It follows buried straight lines that converge and branch, and nothing about the pattern is natural, and nothing about it is hidden, either. It is simply there, underfoot, like the veins of a leaf pressed up through skin.

And the green has found it.

This is the strange arithmetic of the place: it is sparse, and it is lush. Both at once. Between the seams lies mile after mile — well, hundreds of yards after hundreds of yards — of pale young stone, thinly crusted with the first moss, punctuated by gravel fans and fresh scarps, holding almost nothing. Empty, quiet, mineral. Then you reach a seam, and the seam is a garden. Moss deep enough to swallow your ankle. Ferns unfurling in the constant warm steam that rises off the glazed channels in perfectly straight columns. Horsetails in dense dark whorls. And at the junctions, where two hot lines cross and the ground is richest, small groves of alder-like trees — lowland trees, marsh trees, trees that have no business at this elevation — growing extravagantly, every leaf beaded, dripping constantly, the drips falling in long unhurried strings. The green runs along the seams like writing along ruled lines. From the rim, on a clear morning, the Table looks planted. Not by farmers. By whatever put the warmth there.

Because the warmth is not natural either, and neither is the height.

The old country below is low and soft and green-brown, and it lies a very long way down — a fall that would take a stone four seconds to finish, falling through drifting layers of mist that the Table sheds constantly. The stone did not fold upward. There are no tortured strata, no tilted beds. The Table is flat, intact, and high — a slab of valley floor lifted whole into the sky, recently, quickly, in a way rock does not do. And it kept what it carried. The summit fen is not a mountaintop marsh. It is a lowland marsh that now lives at altitude, complete — cattails, sedges, the sour green smell of peat water — a piece of the former valley floor, transplanted, still alive. The first inches of true soil are forming here, the first soil this stone has ever made, dark and soft at the fen's margins, and the plants growing in it are refugees that survived the ride.

Someone helped them survive. That is the part you cannot stop circling.

Water behaves wrongly here, and always in the same direction: upward. The daily rain — and there is daily rain; the Table makes its own — falls at noon, warm and perfectly straight, without a breath of wind to slant it, out of the mist ceiling the place builds over itself by mid-morning. The rain recharges the pools, the pools feed the seeps, the seeps feed the weeping rim, and the rim pours the water off the edge in a thousand thread-falls that merge into sheets. Everything drains outward and down. The Table is a roof, and a roof spends its water. And yet the arithmetic does not close. More leaves than arrives. Year over year, it should be drying.

Then, on your third morning, you see the rill.

A thin line of water running inside one of the glazed seams — running uphill. Climbing a grade that any eye can judge, steady and unbothered, for a hundred paces, until it slips beneath a low dark dome and is gone. You crouch beside it for a long time. You put a twig in it and watch the twig travel up the slope. Something under the Table is lifting the water back — cycling it, spending effort, hour by hour, to keep a valley's marsh alive on a roof in the sky. The engines that raised this place, whatever they were, are not all dead. Some of them are still working. The heat, you realize, is not leftover. It is expenditure.

The structures themselves are scattered so thinly you might walk the whole Table and find only a handful: low domes of dark vitreous material no taller than a wagon, ring-fittings bolted into living rock, a few thin spines whose purpose is not guessable, one seam left open with rungs descending into a warm dark that breathes. The breathing is real. Put your hand over the open seam and the air rises out of it in a slow rhythm — and if you stay still long enough, you feel the same rhythm in the ground everywhere, the whole Table inhaling and exhaling on a single patient cycle, a tide with no moon. Near the domes, water will not settle. Rain lands and beads and runs off, pools refuse to form, the ground stays dry in a neat circle, as if the structures had opinions about wetness. Some of the domes are cold and have gone inert, their glaze crazed, moss creeping over them — those have been dead a long time. Others are warm, and near those the mist makes a slow standing spiral above the vent, a helix of steam that never tears apart, because there is no wind. There is never any wind. The air here is like the air of a room: it goes up, or it stays still.

The quiet is the other thing you do not get over.

Everything is moving — steam rising, water falling, rain dropping, seams breathing, the slow grazing of the browsers — and almost none of it makes a sound. The warm layered air carries sound upward and away; your footsteps seem loud in your own skull; you hear your pulse, your swallow, the creak of your pack. The rim falls, thousands of them, pour in silence off the edges — you watch the water go over and hear only a faint hiss, and only in the stillest part of evening. Occasionally the air layers conspire and a sound arrives out of nowhere with impossible clarity: one thread-fall, suddenly, in your ear, as if you stood beside it, for the length of a breath — then gone, and the hush returns. The noon rain makes almost no noise at all, just the fat drops. At dusk the stone ticks — hairline cracks opening in the day's heat, closing in the night — a small dry click, click, the only regular voice the Table has. And once each night, something deep below sounds: not heard so much as felt, a slow swell through the rock, up through your boots and sternum, like a held note or a great pump turning over. The Table clears its throat. Then it goes back to sleep.

The animals are few enough that you could count them, if you ever saw them all.

You will not. You will see the tracks of the seam-browsers first — round, soft prints pressed into the moss along the warm lines — and only later, at a distance, the browsers themselves: low, grey-green, salamander-slow things that graze the seam-gardens, keeping the green lines cropped and neat, tending the planted look without meaning to. There are perhaps a dozen on the whole Table. The pools hold swimming shapes that are mostly suggestion, pale and unhurried, visible only as a bending of light. At noon, two or three thin-winged skimmers hang at the mist ceiling, drinking from the air itself. And at dawn, if you are lucky, you see the rim-wader: a long-necked pale bird that walks along the outer edge behind the curtain of a falls, inside the falling water, fishing the sheet. You see it once, far off, its neck making a slow S against the drop. It does not know it is rare. Everything here is rare. That is what young land is — a place where every living thing is still an arrival, still a first colonist, still outnumbered by bare stone.

The strangeness has small signatures, and they accumulate.

In the still fen pools, your reflection arrives a half-breath after you do. You learn, quickly, not to test it twice. The steam over the hottest junctions holds its spiral too long, too perfectly, geometry surviving in a medium that should shred it. The compasses of the old country, if the stories are true, wander on the Table — though you have none, and the Table has never once confused your sense of down, which is a mercy, given the edges.
