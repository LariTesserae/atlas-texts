# The Canopy of Keth-Amur

There is no water here. There has been no water for—by the reckonings of the terraces themselves—perhaps a billion years. And yet nothing here is dry.

The plants solved that problem long ago. Vegetation is not *present* in this world; vegetation is the world. The ground, if you could find it, is three kilometers down: a fused mineral pan, dark as cooled slag, vitrified smooth by some event nobody remembers. Everything else—the mountains, the plateaus, the architecture, the air's very texture—is plant. The great suffusive biomass, the Green Continuum, grows in tiers like a cathedral built by an organism that never learned the word "ceiling."

You arrive, as everything arrives, on a **filament road**: a living bridge of braided lignan fiber, broad as a highway, running level across a chasm of green dark. The road repairs itself as you walk. It grows *toward* footfalls. It has been doing this since before the terraces had names.

## The Shape of the Place

This is high country—not mountains of stone but mountains of *growth*, old-growth ranges that rose and then slowed, and slowed, and slowed, until now they barely change in a human lifetime. The erosion here is not water-carved or wind-carved. It is *age-carved*. Everything has settled into its final form: slopes rounded to the exact angle of their own stability, ridgelines worn soft, valleys between the plant-mountains hanging in still, cool air like held breath. The landscape looks finished. Not abandoned—finished. The way a sentence ends with a period rather than trailing off.

The dimness comes partly from the light itself—this world sits far from its dim amber sun, and the light arrives thin, slanted, the color of old honey—but mostly from the canopy above. You rarely see sky. You see the *undersides* of things: photosynthetic membranes stretched between buttress-trunks the width of city blocks, translucent leaves broad as sails, all of them angled to catch what little light falls. The whole world is a lens array pointed at a dying star, and it is very, very good at its job. Nothing is wasted. A dead leaf is eaten before it hits the ground. A dead tree is scaffolded within the week. There is no decay smell here—only the faint ozone-sweetness of living chemistry running at total efficiency.

## The City That Grows

The settlement—call it a city, because it has districts and laws and history—is not built *on* the biomass. It is built *of* it, grafted. The founders, whoever they were, arrived with engineering that makes modern physics look like carpentry: **directed tropism engines**, devices the size of a fist that speak to plant genomes in chemical sentences and are obeyed instantly. They grew the city. They are still growing it.

Towers of engineered heartwood, kilometer-high, their grain spiraled for strength no stone could match. Meeting-halls hollowed from single trunks by targeted cell-death, their walls polished to amber gloss. Chimneys that are actually stomata—vast breathing mouths of a civic-scale organism, inhaling and exhaling in slow tidal rhythm. There is plumbing, of a sort: sap moved through engineered xylem under pressures no natural system permits, coolant and nutrient in one, delivering water *the plants make*—cracked from hydrogen and oxygen by bioreactors seeded through every tissue, atmospheric humidity harvested, recycled, never wasted. It has been ten thousand years since anything here drank from a river. There are no rivers. There are taps, and the taps run clear, and everyone knows the water was once someone's exhalation, and this is not strange to them. It is the oldest courtesy: the city breathes you, you breathe the city.

## The Sound

Which brings us to the noise. This is a **loud** world, and the loudness is the point.

The Green Continuum is an acoustic machine. The great membranes drum. The stomata-chimneys wheeze on their tidal cycle, a sound like a sleeping animal the size of a weather system. The filament roads hum when traveled. And the fauna—

The fauna are abundant, and they have *jobs*. Great slow grazers the size of houses move through the canopy tiers, pruning as they go, their digestion audible from half a kilometer—a deep, rolling chunder of cellulose being broken down, a bassline under everything. Flocks of gliders with photosynthetic wing-membranes ride the thermal columns rising off the compost-heart of each terrace. Small quick pollinator-things swarm in the dim air in shimmering columns. And scattered through it all are the **singers**: long-necked arboreal animals that call in synchronized loops, each population holding a different interval, so that walking across the valley you pass through overlapping keys, chords, conversations that have been running, unbroken, for longer than the city has existed. The singers are not wild, exactly. They are *infrastructure*. The city's engineers tune them the way we once tuned antennas.

## The Rules

The world is old enough to have rules everyone follows without writing down:

*Nothing dies unclaimed. If a thing falls in the Continuum, something is eating it within the hour, and the eaten matter reappears within the season as structure—more road, more tower, more singer.*

*You do not cut what you have not asked. The tropism engines will refuse you, and the Continuum remembers refusals.*

*You do not go down. The ground is three kilometers below, vitrified and black, and it is considered less a place than a memory. Children who ask about it are told: "That is where the water used to be." And then, after a respectful pause: "And now it isn't."*

*The light is shared. When your terrace's membranes fail, the neighbors dim theirs. This is not charity; it is arithmetic.*

## In Motion

Nothing here is static. The active world turns on long cycles: canopy-thinning events every few decades when a terrace "molts," whole square kilometers of membrane shed and regrown; migration pulses of the grazers that roll through the city on schedules marked on civic calendars; the slow, ongoing *negotiation* between the wild Continuum and the grafted city—both are the same organism, genetically, but they compete for light like siblings, and the engineers spend their lives mediating.

And once in a great while—rarely, memorably—a section of old growth simply *stops*. Goes still. Drains its color. The singers nearby change key to something minor and unresolved. The engineers call these **sulks**, and no one knows what the Continuum is deciding, or grieving, or remembering. It always resumes. It has always resumed.

But the oldest engineer in Keth-Amur will tell you, quietly, in the amber dark under the great membranes, with the city breathing around you and the singers looping their ten-thousand-year song:

*It has almost always resumed.*

*Almost.*
