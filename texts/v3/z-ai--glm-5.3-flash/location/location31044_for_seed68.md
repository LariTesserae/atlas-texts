# The Rise

The causeway runs a mile out over old shallows, and at its end the world is new.

Call the place the Rise — a back of land three kilometers across, ringed all around by the old tide, standing (the word is generous) a boot's height above the sea at low water, two boots at the middle where the ground has lifted furthest. It is the sea floor, turned out to dry like a pocket emptied. You can still read what it was. Ripple marks run across the plain in long frozen curls, the size of bunks, the size of boats, unweathered, their edges still crisp, as if the water had left five minutes ago instead of a generation. Shell pavements crunch underfoot like crockery. Little collapse-pits pucker the ground where bubbles of the old water rose and let go. This is erosion at its first morning: nothing has been rounded yet, nothing softened. The land has only recently learned to be land, and it keeps, for now, the handwriting of the water that made it.

The light here is sea-washed and generous — a big pale sky, long views, a clean bright noon. In the shining hours the plain is almost too bright in patches, the shell-white ground throwing light back up at you, and the green of the growing things stands out against it the way ink stands out on paper. Jacket weather, even in the bright part of the day. The wind comes off the ring of water cool and salt-thin, and your breath shows at dawn. There are hollows where frost lies in June — cold air pooling overnight in the young terrain's shallow bowls — and the locals step around these places without comment, the way you step around a sleeping animal.

## The water, which is beneath before it is around

Understand the rule of the Rise: the water here is below you before it is ever beside you. The sea did not leave. It went down — into the sediment, into seams and chambers, into pockets of the old water lying under the new ground like water in an overturned cup. In places the plain rings hollow when you stamp it, and the pump-houses exist because of exactly that. They stand in a line along the seepage seams, small brick buildings with two tall stacks each, and inside them the pumps make the town's heartbeat: long, slow, deliberate strokes, big flywheels turning at the speed of patience, a *thoom … thoom …* that you feel in your boots before you hear it. These are not machines that race. They match the ground. The discharge runs off through channels cut arrow-straight to the ring sluices at the rim, and the channels' water comes out rust-orange, stained with iron the young land breathes out — rust handwriting along every seam, maps of the hidden water drawn in the only ink it has.

Once, sometimes twice in a year, a pump finds a pocket and empties it. Then the ground settles a hand's width overnight, all at once, along one street or one field, and everyone wakes knowing it happened. The children collect on the settled ground like gulls on a plowed field. For a place where nothing changes quickly, these are the great events, and they are entered in the ledger-house with dates and circles around them.

Moisture in the air does the rest. Each morning, before full light, comes the Mist Hour: the ground is slightly warmer than the air — new land, still carrying heat from below — and the whole plain exhales. Fog stands knee-to-chest height from rim to rim. Every wire, every reed, every tile and rail wears a coat of beads; the plain glitters like a thing recently lacquered. The town combs this water out of the air — condensation combs on the roofs, guttered into tanks — so that the first water of any day is harvested from the sky's breath. And here is the small strangeness that visitors notice and locals no longer do: the fog does not lie evenly. It keeps to the seams. At first light you can look across the plain and see the hidden geometry of the groundwater printed in standing mist — the fog is a map. Rain, when it comes, arrives as a curtain you can watch walking, ten minutes loud on the tin roofs and gone, and afterward the light comes back doubled off every wet surface, and there is nearly always a rainbow, and nearly no one looks up.

## The town

The settlement sits at the highest of the new ground — Sennet, they call it, though everyone just says *the town*, because there is only the one. It is compact by design, planned on a grid before the first house, four streets by four, laid out over drainage that runs beneath the cobbles in the same grid, so that the town is built in layers: streets over pipes over seams over pockets. The main street is paved with shell hash, pale and ringing. The houses stand on raised timber-and-stone platforms, sensible boots for a ground that settles; some of them are already being rebuilt over themselves, the second generation of construction going up on the first, which is how you know the town intends to stay. You can walk its whole edge in ten minutes. Beyond the edge, plain — bare shining ground with a fuzz of pioneer rosettes, wide spans of emptiness between the green.

The heart of the town is the Yard, and the Yard announces itself before you reach it: the chuff of stacks, the hourly whistle that splits the air and carries the length of the Rise and out over the ring water, the clangor of the tile kiln keeping its slow rhythm. Here a small foundry casts drainage pipe and the clay tiles that go into the ground by the hundred thousand; here the dynamo hall makes just enough electricity for the pump hall's arc lamps and a handful of street lights, so that the pumps, which never stop, are the only thing truly awake at night. A gravel train runs out twice daily to the working faces, on a schedule set by the surveyors, not by demand — the whole economy of the place runs at the pace of the ground, which is to say, slowly. The tile kiln produces at the rate the land settles, no faster. Nobody here is racing. The town is not fighting the sea; it is outlasting it.

Twice a day, at shift change, the surveyors make the Morning and Evening Reading. Benchmarks — brass studs in old concrete — stand all across the plain, and the Rise gains on them a few centimeters a year, and the original collars of the oldest benchmarks now sit at ankle height, swallowed by the ground they were meant to measure. This is the town's true industry: patience, kept in a ledger. The Slow Ledger, they call it. Children learn their numbers from it.

## The green

Vegetation on the Rise does not spread evenly. It follows the water, and so it writes itself along the seams in long green lines — the Greenlines, the crews call them. Where a seep surfaces, the growth is riotous, lush, almost insolent for ground so young: reed and rush thick as a hedge, willow and alder already shoulder-high, coltsfoot and dock crowding the margins, pondweed thriving in the seep pools. In the wettest places you would swear the plants were hurrying. Between the lines: bare ground, bright and shell-scattered, with the first rosettes arriving like punctuation. *Green means water means work*, say the planting crews, who walk the lines each season with bareroot whips, extending them, thickening them, fixing the ground as they go.
