First there is the notch. The country above is old — low ridges of rust-dark rock, seamed and settled, the kind of ground that has finished becoming whatever it was going to be — and in it, between two shoulders of stone, a gap barely wide enough to walk through. Then the drop, and the pocket opens below you like a room. Two hundred paces across, shallow-walled, floored with light. The sky over it is a bright panel, widest at noon, and the sun only reaches the floor for a few hours around the middle of the day. Everything else arrives the way sound arrives in a room: by reflection, by seepage, by patience.

The air is the first event. It is hot and wet at once, thicker than air has any right to be, heavy with warm brine and the smell of wet stone and something faintly ferrous, like a licked nail. Moisture stands on everything — your sleeves, the rocks, the pipes — not as rain but as sweat. The atmosphere here is near its limit; the pocket's air can hold almost nothing more, so it gives the excess back as beads, as films, as slow drips off the rim. Which is why, before you see anything at all, you hear the place breathing.

Because it is loud. That is the second event, and it takes a moment to parse: a deep, deliberate stroke, felt in the sternum more than heard, arriving every eight or ten seconds like a heart at rest. Woven over it, a fine constant texture — ticking pipes, the gurgle of an outfall, scattered drips from the rock walls each keeping its own interval, a hiss of steam somewhere you can't yet see. The low walls return all of it, softened, so the basin is loud with itself. Nothing shouts here. The noise is patient. It took a long walk beside the rim to understand that the loudest thing in the pocket is also the slowest thing in it.

The floor is new. That is the whole point of the place, though nothing announces it except the ground itself. Sheets of fine silt lie stacked one on another, each a few millimeters thick, and the youngest surfaces still gleam wet and mirror-flat, holding the sky-panel upside down. Because nothing here is old enough to be blunted, every feature is sharp: rills cut finger-deep as if yesterday, crisp levees standing along the outfall channel like pushed-up sleeves, mudcracks with edges clean as cut clay, and fields of salt grown in whisker-crystals that catch the light and tick faintly as they dry and split. The ground is warm. Lie down on it and it is like lying against an animal — a faint blood-heat coming up through the silt, because the crust here is young and thin, and the deep is not deep.

The water is everywhere and mostly nowhere: pools sheet-thin and utterly still, warm veins where the discharge threads across the floor and steams in the cooler hours, gray-green standing water that skins over with color — rust where iron comes up, black at the hottest threads, a pale felt of algae in the oldest pool near the wall. It is not quite water, any of it. It is heavy with dissolved mineral and suspended silt, and it behaves like something with an errand: spreading, settling, sinking. The floor drinks it. That is the local rule, learned by whoever built the works here and obeyed strictly ever since — new ground can only be laid down at the rate the water can drain away beneath it. Push faster and you make a lake, not a landscape. So the engine goes slowly, because the land will accept nothing else.

And the water comes back. Down the faces of the old rim rocks — the only old thing in the pocket — cold threads seep out of the stone and slide to the floor, each thread cutting its own tiny channel, each channel stitching a line of green. This is all the life the place has: jointed succulents crowding the seeps, a stiff rush, rust-and-black algal felts, thin as paint. A wetland with almost no green in it. The fringe advances onto the newborn sheets at the pace of a fingernail — a centimeter in a decade — and stops where the water turns wrong. Seeds ride in on the rare wind through the notch, sprout along the cold weeps, and go no farther. The pocket circulates: warm water in at the center, cold water out at the walls, and the difference between the two is the whole metabolism of the place.

The works themselves sit at the heart of it, sparse as everything else. One squat engine house of dark timber and steel, stained to its own drip-line with mineral bloom, a vent stack releasing a pale steam-ghost that rises a few meters and flattens, defeated. A painted plate, weathered to ghosts of letters: SPREADING GROUND No. 7. Pipes on low trestles cross the floor to a slender boom that slews one step each hour with a hydraulic sigh, laying the outfall along a fresh line, then waiting an hour, then stepping again. The engine burns nothing you'd recognize as fuel; it drinks the warm ground itself, low-pressure and lazy, which is why every motion it makes has the tempo of sediment. There is a hut with the door shut. There are gauge stakes, some notched, the ground already climbed past several of them. And there is one thing that stops you longer than it should: crossing an old dried sheet, a line of bootprints, crisp as if made this morning — except that silt has held them perfect for thirty years, because no rain ever falls here to blur them, and nothing else has ever walked on them. They cross the floor and stop.

What doesn't exist here is animals. No midges over the still pools, which is the part that unnerves — a wetland owed insects the way a bell owes a clapper. No birds. No tracks but the old ones. The wider country presumably teems, but nothing has ever found a reason to cross the notch, and so the absence has a shape of its own, a silence shaped like the space where wingbeats would be. Only four things move in the pocket: water, steam, the boom's slow arm, and light.

Slowly, that is all this place does. The floor rises millimeters a year — the stakes say so — and each year lays down two thin sheets, pale and dark, a ground that is quietly writing itself into a book. The trestles have been raised twice in their lifetime; the engine stands on legs; everything here is built in anticipation of being buried by the very thing it is making. Decades out, the basin fills, the outfall climbs, and one day the pump will be lifting its slurry onto a plain of its own manufacture, until someone — or no one — judges it has reached the height of the rim and lets it stop.

At night the floor gives back its day. The new crust cools and contracts with small sharp ticks, like a kiln going down, and the steam plume shows silver in the narrow panel of sky, and the stroke goes on, every eight seconds, patient as the thing it is building. It is strange, standing there, to realize you are watching land be born at a rate you can almost hear — which is exactly the rate you can hear. The newest ground in the world, still warm, still weeping, still remembering how to be water.
