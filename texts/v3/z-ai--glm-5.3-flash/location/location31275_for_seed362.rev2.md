First, the light.

It comes from no direction you can name. There is no sun to point at, no window to blame — the light is simply *furnished*, the way a room is furnished, in quantity. It lies across pale floors — ash, birch, something paler than either — and the floors drink it and give it back faintly, so that everything glows a little, even the shadows, which are sharp but not dark. The air is cool. Winter-sun cool, though there is no winter and no sun. And the smell — that is the first thing to understand. Cedar. Wax. Linseed. A sweet dry resinous dust-smell everywhere, and nowhere, in any of it, the smell of damp. Nothing here has been wet for a very long time.

You are standing on wood. Take that slowly, because it is going to keep being true.

Walk, and your footsteps make almost no sound. Speak, and your voice falls dead at your feet, as if the air here refuses the job of carrying anything. But press your palm flat to the floor — or lie down and put your ear to it, this is what people do here — and the wood is *loud*. Somewhere below, a gear train turns, and you feel it as a slow tidal pressure in your sternum rather than hear it. Somewhere off to the left, a tappet clacks, and the clack arrives through the floor through your boot soles a half-breath before or after the machine you can see across the hall makes it. Everything you hear is slightly elsewhere. This world keeps its silence in the air and puts its voice in the timber. Distance is a thing you hear with your bones.

Look up, when you dare. There is no sky. There are floors. Deck after deck recedes into a bright haze of perspective and drifting dust — joist-lines converging toward some vanishing point that never quite arrives — and below you, more floors, and below those, more, down through strata you will never visit, down to the Kingpost, the single colossal beam at the world's center, into which everything else is ultimately jointed. There is almost no *up* in this place. There is only *across*. Horizons are measured in days of walking. You can stand at the edge of one of the great halls and watch a dust-devil cross it — an actual whirlwind, a slow brown spiral of sawdust wandering between the machine-legs on thermal draft, the closest thing this world has to weather — and it will take the devil an hour to reach you, and it will arrive with a sound like a held breath.

The whole world is one machine. That is not a metaphor; it is the local industry. Great gears of laminated oak mesh below the floors, cut with teeth the size of houses, run in wax and silence — wood on waxed wood barely whispers, so the Train's turning registers as vibration, as the shiver in a cup of oil left on a table. Fiber belts run for kilometers. Pneumatic lines carry compressed air in bamboo-bore pipe. In the Oil Gardens, presses the size of barns squeeze seed-cake for lubricant, and the presses themselves are seedcake-colored, and everything — floors, walls, roof-decks, the bones of the machines, the machines, the tools, the brooms, the doors — everything is wood, fiber, wax, oil, resin. Nothing is stone. Nothing is metal, except a rumor of iron somewhere near the center that no one has ever needed to confirm. There is no natural substrate anywhere. The ground is built. The *sky* is built. And it is all, in the strict chemical sense, plant: a continental mass of desiccated, seasoned, worked vegetable tissue. Wood is just plant that gave up its water and kept its shape. This world is a forest that decided to become furniture and then decided to become everything.

It sheds, of course. Sawdust falls here the way snow falls elsewhere — fine, bright, unhurried — and it falls in cycles, worst at lampfall, when the whole structure changes temperature and breathes. You will learn the tick. At the change of the light, every beam in earshot adjusts itself, a dry conversational ticking that moves off through the halls like slow applause. Sweeper-machines work the great floors in patient parallels, herding the dust into hoppers, and the dust comes back as board, and the board comes back as floor, and nobody finds this remarkable.

There are animals. Few. That is the point of animals, here — to be few. Pale millers come out of the joinery at dusk, big soft silent moths that lay their eggs in seams, and their season is watched and counted and smoked back down with resin-fumes until it passes. Their larvae — the fret — are not destroyed but *employed*: hatched in troughs, guided along waxed lines through hanging sheets of timber, they eat their way through in patterns, corridors of perforation, lace-screens, grillwork no chisel could match, and then the work is kilned and the workers are gone. Where other worlds keep beasts, this one keeps *postures*: automata of clockwork timber, ladder-things that climb to trim the upper lights, broom-things parked mid-aisle at rest like grazing cattle. And keepers — you will not see the keepers. You will see their work. Oil cups filled to the line. Wax laid fresh on the rail of a stair no one uses. A tarpaulin folded with military tenderness over a lathe. Once, far down a hall, a figure crossing a perspective-line, walking with the unhurried gait of a thing made of timber, and then a turn, and gone.

Now the strange part. Take a core sample anywhere — the listening-stations do it, meters of pale spiral pulled up out of the floors — and read the rings.

The rings are wrong.

Some rings are wood. Some rings are *corridors* — thin strata of floor and wall, a compressed hallway running through the heart of a beam like a vein. Some rings are amber, and in the best one — it is kept in a case, and people queue — there is a miller, whole, wings open, mid-flight four thousand years before it finished being flight. Some rings contain machinery that predates the machinery above them and is more advanced than the machinery below. Count the rings and the history contradicts itself; a fortieth year occurs inside a ninth. And the answer, when the keepers finally say it plainly, is simple and it is not simple at all: the Joinery is built from previous Joineries. Salvaged, milled down, relaid. The world tears itself out and builds itself again from the same timber, has done for longer than the rings can agree on, so that every beam is a palimpsest and history in this place is a *material*, harvested and re-milled, and the grain you are standing on has been a road, a cathedral, a holding cell, a garden, several times, in several worlds.

The grain itself misbehaves. Follow a plank's grain with your eye and it enters the beam as oak and exits as something dense and black and ringless. Knots occur in geological formations. Some floors' grain spirals, and if you walk with it — people do, to feel it — you arrive somewhere slightly rotated from where geometry promised. At certain distances, grain reads as roads and roads read as grain; you will stare down a crack in the floor and not know until you've thrown a dust-seed into it whether you've found a meter's flaw or the mouth of a valley.

And in one district — the Cambium — the distinction this whole world is balanced on simply gives way. The Cambium is the living margin, the one place where growth is permitted, and it is growing. Not softly. *Architecturally.* Corridors bud there overnight: walls extrude as clean beautiful lumber, joints form along lines of figured grain, balusters leaf out in pale hard bunches that are planed off by morning if they don't conform. The scions tend it — they say the blueprints are kept in the heartwood and the heartwood consults them — but no one can any longer say whether the world was built and is now growing into its own intention, or is growing and has learned to build. Made things become grown here. Grown things were always made. It is the one question the rings won't settle, and it is not considered a problem; it is considered the *weather*.

One more thing, because you will ask. The word *rain* survives. It is in the children's rhymes. It refers to nothing. There is a sealed vial in a niche near the Kingpost — you may look, you may not touch, you may not even breathe near it — containing the last known sample of the old medium, and school-groups file past it with the particular hush they save for dangerous things. *Rot* is the sin-word here, the old obscenity. The Joinery is kept dry the way other worlds are kept warm.

And once, in the deep listening-wells, the pattern — slow, vast, patient, still going down — that the stations have tracked for as long as there have been stations. The Borer, they call it. A worm, say some. The world's growth, say others. The next Joinery, chewing its way toward being needed. It is heard, never seen. It may be the only true animal left. It may be the floor itself, deciding.

Go to the edge of a hall before lampfall. You will feel the next cycle coming through the soles of your feet before the light changes — a gathering, a breath, the whole continental machine leaning toward its tick. Dust rising into the furnished brightness. Everything seasoned, everything set, nothing decaying and nothing racing, a world comfortably in its own state.

And somewhere under the horizon of floors, someone knocks twice on a trunk-line — a hello, a report, a *still here* — and the wood carries it toward you across a hundred kilometers of grain, arriving at last, faint and precise, through your boots.

Knock twice back. The timber takes the message the way this world takes everything: slowly, dryly, and without losing it.
