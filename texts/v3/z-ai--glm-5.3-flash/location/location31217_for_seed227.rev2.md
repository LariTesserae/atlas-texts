The fog lies on this country the way snow lies on others. It is a low place — a plain that barely clears the water table, drained so slowly that the grass drinks from both ends, roots and leaves. What grows here grows thick: a green so satur­ated it reads almost black in the fog-light, bent under weight of beads, dripping internally all day without a single drop of rain. The land hums quietly with moisture it never quite spends.

And set down in the middle of it, missed by every eye that passes, is a shoulder of old pale rock no taller than a cart. Its flank carries a seam, knee-high, black, exhaling faintly. You would have to be told about the seam. You would have to kneel in wet grass and put your face to it and crawl.

Inside, the rock opens into a single room. Not a cave system, not a passage-network — one chamber, intimate, big enough for four people standing, small enough that a whisper touches every wall. You can cross it in three steps. Nearly all of it, when you first arrive, is nothing at all: cold air holding still, darkness, absence. The floor is dry underfoot in the way of things the water finished with long ago — smooth, rounded, scrubbed by flows that no longer exist. There is nothing here to bump into. Reach out and you touch nothing; take five steps in any direction and you have crossed the whole world of the place.

The only light comes from a flaw in the roof. A crack, a finger's width, admits a thread of gray — fog-light, directionless, more a memory of light than light itself. Stand under it and you can see your own hands. Stand anywhere else and you cannot. Your eyes take long minutes to make their adjustment, and when they do, the room reveals itself in one stroke:

Green.

One wall of the chamber is lush beyond reason. Where the crack's thread falls, and along a seam that runs down from it, vegetation has crowded into the available world with total commitment. Moss in pelts so dense it hangs like fur, deflecting from the stone in ropes and curtains. Thin ferns with wrinkled, patient fronds. Liverwort spread glossy as wet paint. Every strand beaded with condensation, each bead holding its grain of the gray light, so the whole wall glitters faintly, like something breathing skin. The green is darker than outdoor green — light-hungry pigment, nearly black where it bunches densest — and it lives on almost nothing: a thread of fog-light, and fog itself, which the hollow drinks.

Then, within a meter of the seam's reach, the lushness stops. Hard boundary. Bare stone, dry, empty, going backward into the dark. The wall looks like the edge of an idea.

Everything about the water here is arrival without movement. Condensation beads on every cold surface and runs nowhere. A seam weeps at long intervals — a drop gathering at its lip for a while you could count, then letting go. In a shallow saucer of the floor sits a pool thin as spilled tea: this is the water table itself, showing its face through a pore in the rock, absolutely still, holding the crack's light like a coin at the bottom of a pocket. Nothing flows. Everything waits. The place is damp the way a cellar is damp, the way the underside of a cold jug is damp.

And every sound the water makes is enormous.

This is the hollow's secret and its nature: it is nearly empty of everything except sound. The chamber, worn into its settled form by ages of water that have since found better things to do, has arrived — the way an instrument arrives at its tuning — at a shape that answers. When a drip falls, the room reports it to every wall at once. Each landing spot has its own voice: the pool a deep wooden *dong*, the moss mats a fat *tup*, bare stone a hard dry *tick*. You could map the room blind. You could learn its geography by ear faster than by hand.

And the hollow breathes. It is the great event here, the weather of the inside. Perhaps thirty times a day, as pressure leans on the plain outside, the seam presages it: your ears fill slightly, a hush begins far off in the rock like wind approaching through a forest — except there is no forest, there is no wind, there is only this. Then the inhale comes. Fog bends inward at the roof-crack. Air arrives cold, tasting of the grass and the green world above, and the sound builds from a sigh to a rush to a roar you feel in the ribs before you properly hear it — deafening, absurdly, in a room you could span with your arms, a flood of sound pouring through a space containing almost nothing else. The crack flutes one thin held note through it all, wire-bright, the hollow playing itself. The breath lasts minutes. Then it fades, and the silence that follows is not silence at all: it is the seep whispering through the moss, amplified by a drumhead of thin stone behind the wall; it is your own pulse, suddenly externalized, audible in the rock as if your heart had been asked to wait its turn.

Between breaths, the drips keep the clock.

Hands have been here. Low in the entrance throat, where knees and palms fit the stone, the rock is polished — worn glassy by passage, many passages, over a long time. On the soft pale wall beside the crack stand rows of small cupules, pecked out with another stone, their edges now so weathered they've softened into suggestion. Higher up, where an old coating of mud once covered the flowstone, fingers fluted swarms of lines — meanders, feathers, the pure doodling of hands in the dark — and the centuries have let the surface settle back until the marks sit nearly flush, visible only when you stand in the one place where the crack's light lies low across the wall and grazes sideways. There are char smears where pitch-flares leaned. There is a flat slab set upright as a seat or a table, and near it, sealed into a curtain of flowstone, the hole where a stick once lay: the wood long rotted away, the stone remembering its shape around the absence. Nothing that lasts was ever left here. Whoever came, came with hands and fire and patience, and it seems they came to listen — the marks cluster thickest exactly where the acoustics are best, where the breath roars loudest, where a held note comes back to you doubled and strange.

The residents are few and keep appointments rather than populations. A single bat some seasons, hanging in the far dark third of the chamber, announced not by sight but by the tick of claws adjusting, the dry leathery shuffle of wings folded tighter; its droppings fall beneath it and feed the moss, one small black tribute to the green wall's appetite. Spiders string their webs across the breath-path and catch what the breath carries in — fog-gnat, midge, the occasional lost moth that fetches up blinking at the crack. Springtails herd invisibly in the moss. Once in a great while the pool acquires a visitor — a frog, a bird coming down to the only open water in a meter of country — and its splash is a catastrophe the room reports for a full second afterward.

Time moves here, but on the hollow's own terms. The moss advances perhaps the width of your hand in a decade; stand in this room every year of your life and you would barely see it. The flutings fade another shade per lifetime. The rim of the pool accretes a skin of calcite, thickening coin-slow, century by century. The great carving is finished — the water that made this place found less and less to do, and now merely visits — so the hollow holds its shape the way an old person holds a posture: settled, in balance, arrived. Nothing here is eroding anymore. Everything here is simply *keeping*.

And that is the last thing to understand about it: this is the emptiest full place you will ever stand in. Nearly no matter per volume — walls you can't reach, a floor you needn't watch, a whole intimate world made almost entirely of cold air and dark. What fills it instead is sound, which weighs nothing and takes no space and is, in this room, the most abundant substance in existence. When the breath comes through, the hollow is fuller than any crowded street, fuller than a forest at dawn, and you could pass your hand through all of it and touch nothing.

Crawl out, eventually, into the fog and the green dripping plain. Behind you the seam exhales faintly against your neck. Somewhere inside, on schedule, a drop lets go of its lip and falls, and the room announces it — *dong* — to walls that have been listening this way since long before anyone thought to kneel, and will go on listening, one fog-meal at a time, long after the last hand has forgotten why it came.
