# The Draft

They call the plateau the Draft, and they do not mean the wind. They mean it the way a carpenter means it: this is the working copy. The world here is not finished with itself, and everyone who lives on it knows so the way you know a floorboard is loose — constantly, and with affection, and with a certain wariness.

You come up to it from below, though almost no one comes from below anymore. The rimwall takes two days of switchbacks, and somewhere in the second day the air goes thin and faintly sweet, like the inside of a bell. Then you enter the Roof. The Roof is the plateau's own breath — a permanent lid of condensate hanging a few hundred feet over the whole country, gray as pewter, holding what little moisture this height allows. Walking through it is like walking through a held breath. You break out above it into a gloaming that never quite commits to being day. There is a sun; it is a pale coin behind the lid, a rumor of noon. Shadows here are soft as breath on glass. The only hard shadows on the entire plateau are cast by the finishing engines, whose forges burn small and fierce, and people collect those shadows — pin them in boxes, trade them like relics — because a shadow with edges is a rarity this high up.

The light stays dim all day and dimmer all night, but the plateau glows faintly the way milk glows. The stone itself is the reason. The locals call it tenderstone, and it is — geologically speaking — a rumor. Pale as unglazed porcelain, faintly warm where the fog lies on it, it dents under a boot heel if you stand still too long. The ridgelines are unsure of their own height. Spires lean like milk teeth yet to settle. Press your ear to a south-facing slope at dusk and you can feel it thinking about being a cliff. Erosion, in the sense the lowlands know it — the patient rubbing-down of what already exists — barely happens here. The opposite happens. The land is arriving. Ridges rise a hand's width per lifetime. A valley deepens at the speed of a grandmother telling a story. Nothing here is finished, and nothing here is rushed.

Assay, the city, is strung across the middle distance like beads on a slack cord. It is a real city — it has a hall, a bell, a census, opinions — but it has never once considered crowding. Every structure stands in its own field of emptiness, and the emptiness is not waste; it is legal, and it is metaphysical. Buildings up here need room to settle into themselves, the way new cheese needs room, the way new ideas do. There are houses in Assay that are only wall-shapes, half-drawn, uncommitted — and people live in them anyway. You can tell an occupied proposal by the smoke. Smoke that rises politely, that takes its assigned shape, proves the hearth is true, and a true hearth will finish the house around it eventually, at the plateau's pace, which is to say over decades. Between the districts run causeways and low rails laid dead straight, and the rails are not merely transport. They are straightedges. Distance on the Draft is not real until it has been surveyed twice, and a road is how you make a lie of ground behave. Walk off-road in the tender flats and a two-hour errand can eat a season; walk the rail line and the miles stay honest. The infrastructure is the physics.

The engines are enormous because the work is slow. The great stamps — the finishing works — are halls a mile long, housing flywheels that turn once a generation and pistons that take a full day for a single stroke. Once a day, at dusk, the main stamp falls. You hear it first in your teeth. It is a single deep note that rolls out across the plateau and takes minutes to die, and each fall of it firms a ridge by a finger's width or subtracts a hollow, or sets a shoreline one hairsbreadth closer to deciding to be a shoreline. The city's whole calendar is geared to the main flywheel's sixty-two-year rotation; when it completes its circuit there is a festival called the Return, and old people weep at it for reasons they can't fully explain, and neither can anyone else, which is precisely why they weep.

Water up here is mostly rumor too. It almost never rains — twice a year comes the Wet, an event the entire city treats as a feast, everyone standing out under eaves in their best clothes while the Roof, briefly, fails to hold — but dampness is everywhere, in pockets, in ambush. Everything that can hold condensation does: rails beaded at dawn, hoarfrost on the tender slopes that sublimes away by mid-morning like a debt repaid, seep-springs where the unfinished stone simply weeps, clean and cold, from seams that haven't decided what they are yet. Around these the plateau does its one lush thing. Moss banks grow deep enough to sleep in. Fern seams stitch the wet gullies in a green so saturated it looks printed. There are dwarf forests — knee-high alder, a century old, that will be shin-high for your grandchildren. The green comes in pockets and is ferocious in them; everywhere else is pale stone, and the contrast is the plateau's whole visual argument.

And the fog fields: acres of black mesh strung on poles, harvesting the Roof. They drip. All day, all night, a thousand drips at a thousand different tempos, and the terraces below them are the greenest and loudest place on the Draft, loud with water and with the small commerce of things that love water.

The fauna has learned the truth of the place, which is that the gaps are where you live. Marl-hogs graze the tender slopes in herds of hundreds, eating proto-stone, and their digestion finishes what the geology hasn't — their castings are the first true sand this world has made. Fringe-feeders hang from the causeways like dull lanterns, filtering mist, humming in their sleep. Amendments — small, quick, morally neutral thieves of geometry — come out at night to move survey stones and rearrange cairns, because they like the world redrawn, and every morning the survey crews walk their lines again, sighing, putting it all back. And there are the long ones. Contour-walkers. Bodies like weather seen from above, rare individually, everywhere in their effect: a contour-walker's footprint becomes a valley that deepens for five hundred years, and the finishing works schedule around them with the patience of people who know they are junior partners in this project.

Twice a day, at shift change, the emptiness fills. The hog herds pour down to the seeps; the feeder-flocks lift off the mesh in gray spirals; the amendments run the ridgelines like sparks along a wire; and the sound of it all is the plateau's real weather — hoof-clatter, hum, chitter, drip — rising off the flats and then settling, all at once, into the drone.

Because the Draft is loud, in its slow way. The works' drone never stops; you feel it in your molars before you identify it. The wind organizes itself in the unfinished canyons — the Organ Fissures — and plays them, and because the fissures deepen by centimeters a decade, the old songs come out in new keys every generation, and the plateau's music has to be retuned like an orchard is pruned. The bell in the Ledger Hall has a decay so long it is measured in lamp-oil: they relight the wick before it finishes. And under everything, the groan — the plateau rising, a groan with no starting point and no urgency, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat over a thousand years.

Here are the rules, as the wardens teach them:

Name nothing hastily. Naming is finishing, up here — a named thing hardens, commits, stops considering alternatives — and the Ledger Hall holds the names the way other cities hold gold, spending them slowly, on purpose, because they do not want a world that was rushed.

Count your children coming down from the fog fields. All of them, twice.

Do not leave anything you love overnight in the tender zones. A tool left on a south slope will be partially become the idea of the tool by morning.

And if you come to an errata — one of the soft spots, the places where a finishing failed or a rewrite is pending, where the air echoes before you speak and a hillside exists in two opinions — walk through at a steady pace, look at nothing twice, and do not accept any food. The herds that graze the errata exist plurally; you will see a herd of nine animals standing in eleven positions, and it will resolve as you watch, and the resolution is beautiful, and you should not stay for it.

Some of the stamp-wardens have gone unfinished themselves, in the old way — a hand gone translucent at the edges, a face that photographs differently each year. They hold cured stone in the affected places and are stamped lightly, ceremonially, once a Return, and they keep working. They are the plateau's most trusted citizens. It takes one to know one.

At dusk the main stamp falls, and the fog-drinkers lift off the mesh, and the hard forge-shadows swing their long sure arcs that no other shadow on the plateau is allowed to have. Somewhere on the rim a watch family is minding a single crack — the same crack their family has minded for four generations, one finger's width of progress so far, with pride. And if you stand very still in the emptiness between districts, you can feel what everyone here eventually admits they feel: the ground under you becoming true. A finger's width at a time. A valley being subtracted somewhere to the north. A ridgeline making up its mind.

It is a world's morning, and the work is on schedule, and everything alive has crowded in early to watch.
