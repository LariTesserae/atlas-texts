# Wick, in the Bowl

Come down the Stair — the long cut staircase in the rim, four hundred steps worn to shallow glassy dishes by feet and water — and the first thing you lose is the horizon. The wool takes it from you partway down. By the last steps the sky is gone entirely, replaced by a lid of fog that glows a dim, directionless grey-amber, as if lit from nowhere in particular. Some days a brighter patch drifts up there, a blister of light that never quite resolves into a sun. Nobody in Wick has seen a star. Nobody expects to.

The town is small. That is the second thing you learn, and it never stops being true no matter how long you stay: end to end, across the whole floor of the Bowl, is a ten-minute walk. Four hundred people, ninety-odd structures, one great engine. But it is a *city* in the way it organizes itself — gridded, plumbed, zoned by function, raked like a garden so the fog moves through it in intended channels. The buildings don't crowd. They stand apart on stilts and rubble mounds above the seeping ground, connected by pipe-bridges and covered walks, each one placed where the wool condenses on it best. The town is sparse the way a machine's parts are sparse: everything spaced to work.

The heat is the third thing, and it is everywhere. The air comes into your lungs warm and thick, like the inside of a mouth. Your breath never fogs here — the air is far past that. Your sweat doesn't cool you; the air is too wet to drink it, so it just rides on your skin. Nothing in Wick is cold. Metal sits at blood temperature, so every handrail, every ladder rung, every pipe feels like touching a hand. The only cool places in the world are the deep cisterns, and people visit them the way the devout visit shrines: descend the cistern stair, stand a minute in the cold-smelling dark, feel the water's breath on your ankles, climb back up into the warm.

**The ground weeps.** That is not a figure of speech; it is the town's condition. The Bowl is the lowest land for a hundred miles in every direction, and every drop in the world is trying to get here. The stones seep. The clay trembles underfoot in wet season. Gutter-stones drip in their sleep. The water table sits so near the surface that a hole dug knee-deep by noon is a well by supper. And beneath it all runs the Old Sough — square-cut drainage channels, older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother, their walls worn to a slickness that predates the town and will outlast it. No one knows who cut them. They still run. The town's foundations rest on them the way a boat rests on a keel, and it is because of them that the land is at rest: the drainage was solved long ago, by someone, and everything since has merely settled into that answer. Wick did not shape this place. It moved into a shape that was already finished.

**The industry is a wringing.** The rocks weep bitter — mineral tears, briny and white-crusted. The fog is sweet, or nearly. The town's whole labor is the separation of the two: condenser towers of black latticed iron standing apart across the floor, each one raining quietly *inside itself*, internally, all day, the fog surrendering its water to metal slightly cooler than it is. Sluices under each tower feed flumes; flumes feed cisterns; cisterns feed the sweetwater vats, which go up the Stair in glass. On the rooftops, shallow salt pans drink the bitterwater and give back pale salt. The Lung — the great beam engine at the town's center, the only thing in Wick that hurries — drives the pumps, the dynamos, the fans. Its beat is the town's heartbeat. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it. Arc lamps hiss on their posts all through the dim hours, amber halos floating in the wool, and below them the weeping stones wear a low green shine: stonebloom, a fungus that glows faintly on every permanently wet surface, so that at "night" the town is lit from below as well as above — amber rings over green ember, everything else dark and dripping.

**And the plants listen.** This is the strange grace of Wick, and the thing that took the longest for the first settlers to understand. There is so little light here that light stopped being the thing life organized itself around. The great catch-trees — the Listeners, the town calls them — grow enormous parabolic leaves, dish-shaped, glossy dark green, each one wide as a door, and each one *turned toward sound*. Toward the Lung, mostly; the Leafwardens reposition the young ones by hand until they settle. A Listener drinks from its own dish: condensation runs down the leaf's curve to a focal point where a specialized dripper-growth swells, and the harvest rounds collect from spigot to spigot, bucket by bucket, the way other towns collect eggs. The leaves are luxuriant — lush past reason — but the trees stand far apart, one at each appointed station, like streetlamps of a vegetal civic grid. Children keep them as pets. There being nothing else to keep — no birds, no mice, no fish in the flumes, not so much as a midge in the lamplight — a child will whisper to a seedling for years until the young leaf learns to face *that voice* instead of the Lung. The leaf is the pet. The leaf listens.

When saturation peaks — after wool-tide, when the fog comes down thick and the whole Bowl seems to hold its breath — the Listeners fold. All at once, like umbrellas closing, shedding tons of gathered water in a single glassy sheet. The town calls it the shrug. Streets clear on schedule; the flumes surge; the wheels spin fast for an hour; and the arc lamps measurably brighten, running on the plant's gift. The town's literal light is coupled to the behavior of its trees. Watchers on the tower catwalks call the fold the way other places call weather.

**The Bowl keeps what it hears.** This is the deepest strange rule, and everything in Wick accepts it the way farmers accept rain. Sound here does not fully die. It gets quiet, and it gets slow, and then it comes back — the give-back — out of the fog, from no direction, softened. The shift bell rings twice: once now, and once later, whenever the Bowl decides to return it. Pipes knocked in code repeat themselves down the hours like a patient conversation. Old-timers claim the give-back sometimes arrives *early* — that you'll hear the bell a half-breath before the striker hits it — and set their clocks accordingly. Once a year, for maintenance, they shut the Lung. The Quiet. Everyone dreads it. With no machinery to mask the kept sound, the Bowl plays back what it has stored: rhythms, bells, the ghost of a hammer on a hull, something that might be laughing, might be weeping, might just be water finding its level. Four hundred people stand in the streets and listen to their town remembering itself, and then they restart the engine, gratefully, and the loud warm present floods back in.

**Nothing has eyes here.** No gulls though the wet lies everywhere. No rats in the granaries, no mosquitoes over the sumps, no moths in the lamp-halos — the halos hang empty, perfect rings with nothing living in them. Rain barrels breed nothing. The absence is total, and the town has metabolized it the way it metabolizes everything: the sumps are clean, the food stores never spoil from vermin, and no one owns a lock. But you will see people pause at lamplight, at dusk, with a look like a word they've forgotten — reaching for a birdsong that was never coming, in a place so loud with everything else.

Night in the Bowl is a deepening of the grey. The wool settles to its lowest tide. The sift begins — a fine drizzle falling *out of the fog itself*, hissing softly on every leaf and roof at once. The Lung keeps its time. The bells ring, and ring again later, gentler. And if you stand still at a street's edge and speak — just speak, to no one — somewhere down the row a dish-leaf will turn toward you, slow as a door, and hold.

And the Bowl will note it. And give it back, much later, softer, in the dark — like something down there, learning to say it.
