Come down into it and the water thickens around you like weather. Not cold — the cold lives higher, nearer the lost surface — but thick: a pale grey-milk of ground stone that the young mountains below are forever shedding. Light here has no direction. It enters everywhere and finishes nowhere, so there are no shadows to speak of, only gradations of pale, and distance does not darken the way distance should. A thing far off does not loom out of blackness. It resolves, slowly, the way a face resolves out of fog, and the folk of this place say that nothing ever *arrives* in the milk — everything is remembered into view.

Below you, the drowned highland takes shape: the Newfells, a dozen summits and the long saddle-country between them, standing on skirts that fall away into a dark no lamp has ever crossed. You are high up here, though nothing tells you so. The crests rise almost to touch the underside of the world; one day — the folk reckon it in lifetimes — a summit will break through, and the milk will learn what wind is. But that is far off. What the rock knows now is only its own newness. Edges are crisp as split flint. Fractures run clean. Boulders sit exactly where they fell, and not one of them has worn round; there are no river-valleys here because no river has ever run. The sea has not yet learned its oldest trade. It carries the mountains' flour and lays it down in velvet drifts, and bites nothing.

The first rule a visitor must unlearn is the direction of warmth. Cold is the sky of this world; warmth is its ground. The newborn rock has not spent its heat, and along the fault-lines the water shimmers where seeps rise — the warmveins — and every living thing arranges itself around them the way towns elsewhere arrange themselves around rivers. Follow a warmvein downslope and the world greens. First a furring of pale moss on the clean stone, then turf, then the great ribbon-forests: kelp anchored by holdfasts older than most family lines, blades rising fifty, eighty lengths, canopy laid over canopy until, mid-valley, swimming becomes less like swimming than like moving through weather made of leaf. Light filters down through it in coins. Beneath the canopy are the soft gardens — flesh-colored coral-plants, sponge-trees like gray fists, meadows of sea-grass in the saddles — and beneath those, the warm stone itself, veined and gently breathing.

The lushness is dense enough to be a material. In the Valewarm, the greenest of the saddle-valleys, you push growth aside the way you'd push through hanging cloth; midwater is layered with drift and pollen and the pale strings of spawning weed, so that volume itself feels occupied, silted, upholstered. Nothing here is empty. Even the distances are full of something only slightly fainter than the near things.

The folk build where the warmveins build. Their towns are strings of stacked terraces clinging to the warm flanks of the saddles, houses dry-set without mortar, the seams painted instead with a cultivated stony crust that grows and welds and slowly repairs itself — masonry by patience, a technology of tending rather than making. Houses stand wall-near-wall in the old lanes, so close that rain, if there were rain, could not find the ground. Warm seeps are channeled through the towns in open stone sluices; the poorest houses sit at the cool edges of the flow and the granaries at the heart of it, and everyone knows the temperature of their address. Causeway-roads run along the contours from town to town, lined with lamp-strings: pots of glow-moss on kelp cords, which fruit only near warmth, so that the roads glow faintest exactly where they are safest, and the whole range at night — at what passes for night — reads as a constellation laid on its side, marking the veins of a sleeping animal.

They are a soft-voiced people, and this is not temperament but physics. Sound carries appallingly well in the milk: a dropped mallet can be heard three valleys off, arriving with no direction in it, like a thought that isn't yours. So distance-speech is done in signs and lamp-flash, and the spoken word is kept for the breadth of an ear. Children learn hand-talk before voice. Tools are shell adzes, stone mauls, bone needles, cord of braided ribbon-bark; there is no smelting and there never will be, because any heat a person makes here belongs to everyone within a mile, and no one wants to be the one who spent the town's warmth on a trinket. Their forging is cultivation: of crust on stone, of moss in pots, of the forests themselves. A holdfast in a family garden may be two hundred years old, inherited like a name, harvested by pruning and never by pulling.

The quiet of the Newfells is not emptiness. It is discipline laid over a place that carries sound too well. Listen closely and the inventory is small and dear: the tick and shift of stone that has not finished cooling; the hiss of a warmvein swelling; rope easing against a terrace-wall; the silk sound of blades parting around a swimmer. A whole town at its loudest is a kitchen at its gentlest. And once in a generation the mountain itself speaks — the Long Groan, some fault settling, some ridge easing into its own weight — and every settlement on the range hears it in the same instant, unbroken across the milk, the only voice they all share. Work stops. Neighbors touch shoulders. The young are told: the mountain moved in its sleep, and it moved for all of us alike.

Animals are rare here; the lushness is nearly all botanical, and what fauna exists is counted like treasure. The folk keep census on shell tallies. The grazers — the aunts — are vast soft movers that mow the ribbon-canopies, slow as weather, each one known by name and by the particular drape of her mantle: Aunt Pell, Aunt Marrow, Aunt Who-Waits. When an aunt passes a town, work pauses for the day and a garden is left unmown for her; her route is charted afterward and argued over. Small pale shoaling fish live in the holdfast roots — peerfish — and a school surfacing into view is a festival of pure watching, remembered for years. And once or twice in a lifetime, from the black country below the range's skirts, where no warmth reaches and no lamp goes, a bass pulse rises: felt in the chest more than heard, slower than any heartbeat. The folk do not name what makes it. They mark only the date, on the same stones where they tally the aunts.

In one cold-edged valley stands the drowned wood: trees, black and upright, bark whole, unrotted in the still cool water — a forest that was standing when the flood came, so recently that the sea has not yet managed to push it over. The folk do not anchor gardens there. Lamps are sometimes left among the trunks, small pots of borrowed starlight in a place that keeps its own counsel. Whether their people came down with the water or were always of it, their grandmothers disagree; the trees are the only witnesses, and they say nothing.

On the highest flats, near the summit called the Window, new stone arrives. There is no violence in it — the range simply grows — sheets of smooth grey welling up between old fractures, blood-warm, featureless, like the world remembering how to make ground and not yet having decided what shape it wants. The folk call these grounds the Nursery and harvest them as fields: young stone is soft enough to dress with shell tools while it cools and hardens into something that will outlast anything old. The tick of cooling sheets is the Nursery's only voice. Quarry here is agriculture; lintels, lamp-posts, and the tall census-stones are crops of geology. Erosion will come eventually — the milk will sharpen its teeth someday — but the folk have arranged their lives around the fact that the world is still being made, and have made themselves part of the making.

The Window itself stands a fathom under. There the milk thins to bright water, and the surface becomes legible from below: a trembling silver ceiling, ripple-ringed, the only sky the Newfells have. Pilgrims rise to press their palms against the almost-air. Each lifetime the Window shoals a little more; the mountain is rising, patient as a swell. No one alive will see a crest break through, and no one minds. The whole culture is built for durations longer than a life — walls that grow, gardens migrating year by year up the warming slopes toward the thinning light, towns that will one terrace at a time be surrendered as the newborn rock spends its heat and the lush band narrows over the slow ages. They do not grieve this. Slowness here is not loss; it is the medium everything is painted in.

Somewhere below, a warmvein swells and slackens — the mountain's sleeping breath, the closest thing the Newfells keep to a week. Somewhere above, the Window brightens by less than a measure. And the milk holds all of it, pale and layered and hushed, the way a held breath holds a promise.
