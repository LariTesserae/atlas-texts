You hear the place before you see it, and what you hear does not behave like sound. It is a beat, deep and patient, arriving from under the ground every seven seconds — never a seventh early, never a seventh late. Above it runs a mid-register drone, the pitch of a dynamo heard through a wall, and threaded through both a thin fixed whistle, high and unvarying. The sounds are loud. But because nothing in them ever varies, they stop registering as events. Within an hour you no longer hear the plain. You simply stand inside its noise the way you stand inside its warm air.

The air is warm the way laundered linen is warm, blood-warm, with no edge to it. The light is ordinary — a hazy afternoon sun in a pale sky, nothing dramatized, shadows soft and correctly angled. This ordinariness matters. The strangeness of the basin is not in its sky.

You stand on the floor of a low country, a shallow depression so flat that the horizon reads almost as a rule drawn across the eye. The ground is sorted stone: gravel plains, then a band of rounded cobble, then pans of fine pale dust that will not lift no matter what the wind does — and the wind here blows constantly, from one quarter, at one speed, and moves nothing. Every surface has reached its final shape. The gullies finished deepening long ago. The stones are worn to exactly the angles the wind wanted. This is a landscape that is not eroding; it has *finished* being eroded, and now simply holds the result.

The presences are scattered. You can walk an hour and meet nothing, then crest a gravel swell and find one.

At first you take it for a boulder — a whale-back mass of grey stone half-sunk in the plain, rounded by ages of that patient wind. Then you notice the dew. The upper collar of the mass is cold — the only cold thing in the world — and it sweats. A fur of moisture stands on the metal-dark surface, and the drops gather and fall at fixed intervals; you could count to them. Below, the base of the mass is warm, almost hot, breathing heat into the ground. Fins rise from its shoulders in ranks like organ pipes. A disc lies alongside, worn smooth as a pond-stone, and only its perfect roundness betrays that it was ever a flywheel. Where seams run through the body, erosion has worn the rivets flush: you find a line of regular dimples in stone, half casting, half crystal-growth, and you cannot decide if you are looking at machinery that geology adopted or rock that machinery informed. It does not matter, and the plain does not care that it does not matter.

The working is mostly underneath. The visible hulks are collars and vents; the mechanism they serve extends below the whole basin, a single regional machine whose parts surface here and there like knuckles through a sheet. It runs at industrial cadence. Nothing on it moves quickly. Something like a governor arm swings and returns each cycle to precisely the same angle, and that is the closest you come to seeing work being done.

Water here is a surface event. There are no streams, no rivers, no lake anywhere; there is nowhere for water to be going. The engines sweat their quota each dawn, and the moisture runs off into shallow pans and pockets — brackish, warm, exactly the same depth every day. Their surfaces hold standing waves, tuned to the deep beat, with nodes and antinodes in fixed positions: the water laps at the same points of the same rims forever, and pebbles at the waterline are polished at only those points. Once a day, always at the same minute, a brief shower crosses one sector of the basin — a few minutes of warm rain on the same stones, evaporating by the same afternoon hour. The rain here has no memory. Nothing drains and nothing gathers; no water carries silt, so no water tells any story.

And the sound has geography. Because the engines' phases never drift, some hollows are places where the beats stack — a permanent drumming you feel in the sternum, where the puddles shiver and fine gravel creeps a finger's width and creeps back. Other cells are places where the phases cancel: near-silence, oppressive, where for the first time you hear your own pulse and the tiny tick of your joints. A map of this basin would be drawn in loudness the way other maps are drawn in elevation, with contours of thunder and quiet, and anyone who lived here would name places the way we name winds.

The nearest thing to life is film. On the damper stones grows a black felt, fine as velvet, neither plant nor animal — a microbial crust that takes its living from the warm mineral and the nightly dew. On dry ground it gives way to a white powdery efflorescence, and at the rims of certain pans, needles of crystal bloom each night and dissolve again by the same afternoon hour, so that they never get longer. There are no stems, no leaves, nothing green in the entire basin. The closest thing to a forest is an engine's fin-bank furred grey with crust, and it is unclear whether the crust is growing on the machine or the machine is cultivating it.

The animals, such as they are, keep the schedule too. Travelers' accounts agree on one: a low, broad-footed thing with hide like cooled slag, about the size of a cart, that appears beside the third flange of this engine at the same minute each morning, laps the dew-fur for four minutes, and moves on. It walks a circuit of a dozen engines, arriving at each on time, every day. It is not rare — it is punctual. In any single place it exists for four minutes out of twenty-four hours, and so, if you have not come at the right minute, it may as well not exist. Other scheduled things live in the seams: small thermotrophic forms that bask along the warm joints in the same arrangement daily, like filings in a fixed magnetic field. Nothing here wanders. Wandering would be a change.

Because that is the law of the basin, the one the engines enforce: nothing accumulates. Every process runs, and every process cancels itself by day's end. The morning adds dew; the afternoon subtracts it. The shower lays its water down; the heat takes it up. Wear, if there ever was wear, has long since been balanced by whatever the machines do down in the dark, and every surface now sits at its resting form. Time passes — the sun crosses the sky, the light is honest and normal — but the plain does not add it up. Each evening subtracts what the morning spent. The engines' product is not power, not ore, not anything you can carry away. Their industry is the present tense. Whether they were built or grown is undecidable, and perhaps the distinction never applied; perhaps the builders' only surviving artifact is the schedule itself.

Walk a day and you understand the scale of it: a day between engines, and beyond that, at the horizon, a line of them like fallen books on a shelf — and every one doubled in a permanent mirage that floats it a hand's width above its own reflection. The sky holds steady. The basin holds steadier.

Your own footsteps are the only unscheduled sound in the world. In the loud hollows you cannot hear them at all; in the quiet cells they ring absurdly, a visitor's noise, off the beat. Your boots leave prints in the dust, and the dust — which will not lift, which moves for nothing — holds them exactly until the scheduled shower crosses your sector at its fixed minute and smooths them flat. You last one day here. That is the local allowance for a stranger: a single cycle of notice, then the world resets.

When you finally walk out, the beat does not change. It does not see you off. Seven seconds, and another seven, and the plain closes over the place where you stood the way it takes the rain — completely, and without keeping any of it.
