Coming down off the pass, the first thing that changes is your hearing. Not because of any sound — because of its absence. The bowl below you is a full day's vision across, ridges beyond ridges, and not one of them makes a noise. Your boots on the last scree of the trail are the loudest thing for miles, and then the scree runs out and you step onto the mat, and even that is gone.

The mat is the floor of this world and it is not a floor. Kneel and part it with both hands and you find no soil, no stone, no mineral anything — just deeper weave, roots braided through roots through rhizomes through felt, going down past the reach of your arms. The ground here is a rumor. Geologists would have to break in to see the bedrock, and the valley would heal over the incision before they finished. Leave a bootprint and return in a month: green. Leave a tent. Don't leave a tent. The place abhors a surface. Expose the dark earth anywhere and the mat moves toward it from every side with the unhurried thoroughness of a work crew, and within a season the wound is skin.

Everything is plant. Not a forest of plants — a forest *of* plant, as if the word had changed from plural to substance. Wood does the work of beams, root does the work of cable, moss does the work of insulation and upholstery and paint. The cliffs are draped, the boulders — if there are boulders — are upholstered, the air at chest height is a loose felt of hanging filaments that touch your face as you pass. Walking is a matter of being handed from organism to organism. The density is not choking; it is layered, near-touching, upholstered, like moving through the inside of a thick coat.

And the forms are old. That's what unsettles before anything else does. The ridgelines are rounded and finished, worn into the patient profiles of land that stopped arguing with water an eon ago. The terraces stepping down the bowl are smooth and settled, braided channels worn into the slope like old field boundaries. Every shape says *this has always been so.* And every molecule of it is weeks old. The surface renews itself constantly and the forms never move. The shape of this valley is ancient; the substance is younger than your boots. It has the property of a river — permanent form, perpetual replacement — except that the whole valley is the river, a standing wave of tissue that climbs its own terraces a millimeter at a time, generation dying below, generation extending above, the wave traveling and the riverbank
