**The Crown Garden of the First Pylon**

The sea is young, and you can tell because nothing has taken hold of it yet. No weed in the seams. No silt in the corners where welds pool their shadows. Nothing fouls the hulls of the world here, because nothing that fouls has been invented yet. The bronze stays bright. The glass stays clear. Everything looks like it was finished yesterday, because in every sense that matters, it was.

And the sea is empty. Warm, still, sunlit near the top and going blue-black below, with no weather on it — the atmosphere of this world is still settling, and wind hasn't started. The surface barely ripples. From underneath, it is a silver ceiling, faintly wavering, stretched over everything.

In all of it there is one vertical line.

The pylon rises out of a depth the light doesn't reach. You can swim down along its skirt and lose the daylight and still not find its footing; the bottom is a rumor. The shaft climbs for what feels like a mountain's height — because it is one. It is the only mountain this world has so far, a manufactured one, cast in great curved panels of pale stone with bronze fittings at the joins, and its summit — this is the strangeness, the thing you don't believe until you rise past it — its summit stands entirely underwater, only twenty meters or so beneath the silver ceiling, where the young sea is warmest and the sunlight still arrives nearly whole.

There, the shaft flares. It opens into a terraced crown, a platform the size of a small grove, ringed by a low weir and a rail, and the whole of it is planted — and the whole of it, plants included, is made.

Swim the rim first, before you go in. Put one hand over the weir wall and one below it. On the inside: water that is warm as a summer lake, faintly golden-green, faintly thick with nutrients, hazy with drifting pollen. On the outside: the open sea, cooler, bluer, cleaner-tasting, younger. The two waters lean against each other along that wall and refuse to mix. There is a visible line there — a shivering boundary, holding, like oil and water that have agreed to a truce. The garden keeps its own water. The sea keeps its. Nobody has explained why this works. It simply does, the way things work here, in a world whose rules are still being written.

Then go in.

The garden is packed. That is its first fact and its governing law. There is no ground, only trays — engineered beds of dark granular medium in cast-stone frames, laid edge to edge with no gap wider than a hand. There is no open water except a central channel and the seams of the walkways, glazed tile running between the beds like grout between leaves. Broad-ribboned kelp-florets rise from the trays in ranked rows. Grass meadows comb in the almost-current. Vines are trained along the conduit runs — the copper nutrient pipes and the rubber-sheathed power lines — so thoroughly that in places you cannot say where the machine ends and the plant begins, and it wouldn't matter if you could, because the machines were built to be climbed. Flowering stalks press against flowering stalks. Leaves overlap leaves in layers two and three deep. Every niche is filled. Every surface that could hold something green holds something green, and above it all the silver ceiling mirrors the whole arrangement back upside down, so from the channel, looking up, the garden pours downward out of the sky and is twice as full as it is.

It is warm in here. The sun does part of it — the top layer of a young sea holds its heat like a held breath — and the pylon does the rest. The shaft below the terrace is the engine that runs this place: pumps, exchangers, batteries, all sealed in the column, breathing for the garden. Warm plumes rise off the vent grates in the platform's skirt, visible as a slow lensing, a shimmer like heat over summer road. The water on your skin is bathwater. The whole crown sits in the ocean's summer, permanently.
