There is a noon underneath the world.

The ceiling of this place is stone that has not finished becoming stone — a seam of half-made rock stretched like a membrane across a valley's length, and on its far side, a couple hundred feet up, the ordinary sun. Light comes through the way light comes through a hand held up against the sky: warm, pearly, sourceless, arriving everywhere at once with no direction to point to. It is bright here, evenly bright, and because the whole roof glows rather than any one place in it, shadows are small and loyal things, pooling directly beneath their objects and pointing nowhere. Far off, where the roof curves down toward its seams, the light deepens to a rose like the inside of a shell. It is the same noon it has always been.

The stone sifts the weather out of the sunlight. Whatever is happening up there — rain, season, night — none of it passes through; the rock takes all of it and gives back only this one settled warmth. So there are no gradients, and without gradients there is no wind. The air has not moved since the air was made. Warmth lies on the skin like a laid palm, patient, without press or flicker, and the air itself is thick and green-tasting, a held breath you are borrowing.

Follow that glowing roof down to where it meets the floor, and you can read how young the stone is. Nothing here has weathered, because nothing has been given the time — or rather, time has never been admitted. The walls are raw: strata that begin and trail off before becoming strata, ribs of rock smooth as cooled wax, pale drifts of mineral wool packed into the corners of the world. There is no rubble anywhere, no scree, no sand of broken-down anything. Dig in the floor and the soil gives out after a forearm's depth — one black handful of first-soil, crumbly and sweet — and then the parent rock, warm as a kiln the morning after. On top of it all lies moss, deep as a mattress, exactly as deep as the day it arrived.

And it is all garden. Vegetation does not decorate this place; it constitutes it. Even the ground is a plant — the soil itself is root-woven down to the stone. Over the moss lie herbs, then shrubs, then the understorey, then the canopy, every layer touching the next, vines stitching the whole stack together, green layered on green to the point of saturation, so that you rarely see far ahead and the openness of the valley is vertical: lie back in the moss, look up through a gap, and the roof burns white at the end of a hundred feet of leaf.

The great trees — call them vault-bearers — hold the ceiling's light on crowns of leaves broad as doors. Their timber is pale gold; cut any branch and the grain runs straight and ringless, because a ring is a year, and this wood has no years. It did not grow. It arrived grown, at the exact pitch of fullness, and holds there. Ferns stand everywhere beneath it with their fiddleheads held at three-quarters furled, forever almost. And every flowering thing in the valley is a white cup at full open, petal spread, and no petal drops. The whole valley hangs at the moment of its first flowering, and the moment does not pass. There is no litter on the floor of the world — no rot, no wilt, no black wet hollow of decay — because nothing here has ever let go of anything.

The scent pools, too, in the windless strata: crushed moss in one place; a standing wall of white-flower smell around each grove, with borders you can feel like a doorway; warm mineral near the seams. You pass from scent-room to scent-room as through chambers, and nothing mixes at the thresholds.

Water is a rumor here that the ground mostly declines to confirm. Most of the valley is merely damp — damp moss, damp air, a dampness felt as faint weight — and there are dry quarters where the moss pales to silver and crunches faintly underfoot, and the ferns drink only from the sky-sweat on their own faces. But in the low places the rumor comes true. The pools sit in the green like mirrors laid down and forgotten: a long lens-shaped one, warm as the inside of a wrist, so clear it lies about its depth; smaller ones cupped in moss, ringed with the drinking roots of everything nearby. Thin films of water glaze the undersides of leaves and never run. Every point in the world gathers a bead — leaf-tips, twig-tips, the thorn of a bramble — and the beads hang, and mostly they hang forever.

Mostly. Once in a long while — there is no interval to know — a bead lets go somewhere in the roof and falls a hundred feet into a pool, and the sound is a single round plink with a long tail of nothing after it. Rings cross the water, argue briefly, and smooth. That is the nearest thing to weather here, and the nearest thing to time.

You could walk from one seam of the world to the other and count all its animals on one hand, with fingers to spare. There is the moth: hanging from a twig by four feet, wings a spread of two hands, dusted the same pearl as the roof, beaded with condensation like a stone, and it has hung there since before the idea of waking. There is the sipper: a sliver of green with a tongue like a thread, going from white cup to white cup, never seeming to take anything away — or taking away exactly what returns. In the shallows of the lens lies something pale and wide-mouthed that may be a fish and may simply have decided to lie down; it moves a body-length in a season, and its stillness is of a piece with everything else's.

And there is the grazer. Tall as a deer, paler than milk, skin like the inside of a petal, standing at the pool's edge. Behind it, crossing the whole valley from the eastern seam, runs a line of footprints — forty-odd dimples in the moss, each one perfect, each one permanent. It has taken perhaps forty steps in the history of the world, and every one of them is still, in a sense, happening. Occasionally — once in the memory of anything — it shifts its weight, and the line grows by one print, and then that is true forever too. Its eyes are wet and unhurried. They do not widen when they find you. Widening would be a change. Once, a sound came out of it: a low wooden note, like a root struck underground. It has been heard the once.

On a rise in the middle distance stand the made things — few and far between as the animals, and made the way they had to be. Three low domes, woven from saplings bent while living, and the living stems have long since swallowed the lacing, so that no tie remains, no seam, no cut end: whoever built here understood the temperament of the place and cut nothing. They bent what was alive and let it keep its bentness. The domes stand like the shells of something that decided, gently, not to hatch. Near them, root-rungs rise toward the roof in a ladder that stops a body's length short of anything at all. And there is a doorway with no wall: two stems and a lintel, framing the pool — the only picture in the world, hung unblinkingly since it was hung. Before it, on a flat stone, sits a shallow bowl of pale resin, and the bowl is always exactly full. Drink from it and it is exactly full again by the time you look — not refilled so much as declining, permanently, to have been empty.

A line of footprints — broader than the grazer's, deliberate — comes out of the green, walks the rise, circles the domes, tests the stair, stands a long while before the doorway, and goes down to the water. After the water there is no more record. The water keeps no ledger.

This is the rule of the place, and it is less a law than a temperament: nothing proceeds. Things happen — a bead falls, a note is struck, you arrive — but nothing develops. The solid world keeps every event it is given, exactly, always: a snapped stem stays snapped, unrotted, unhealed, the one breaking, held. The water alone forgets. And so the valley holds at the first minute of itself, embryonic and eternal at once — a beginning that never became a middle. Whether it is a world kept the way a seed is kept, viable and waiting, or a finished thing displayed forever at its opening instant, the place does not say, and the roof glows with the same noon either way.

The quiet, meanwhile, is not empty. It is full of held instruments. The green drinks each small sound within a few feet — a footstep lands like a coin into deep wool — and beyond that, nothing: no wind to rub the leaves, no water traveling, no wings abroad except the moth's, asleep. Your own pulse will be the loudest animal you meet. If you crack a stem — and you should not — the report will be enormous, a shot fired in a library, and it will be the first and only breaking, and it will always be.

Go gently, if you go at all. Whatever you do here is kept. Your footprints will be as permanent as the grazer's; walk where you mean to have walked. And if you leave, leave the way the older prints left, by the water, where the record ends. Or stay a while beneath the one fist of pale stone that hangs from the roof a finger's length down — the one piece of the ceiling still admitting it was forming — its terminal bead already gathered, shining, at the tip.

It does not fall. It never falls. It is always, exactly, about to.
