Begin with the absence, because everything here is arranged around it: there has never been rain. Not a dry season, not a drought — simply no water, anywhere in the record, in any stratum, in any story the place tells about itself. The air is so dry that cold doesn't bite; it just sits against the skin, clean and mineral, like standing in a kiln the moment after the fire is drawn. When something moves fast, no dust rises and no mist. The world has never heard rain, and it has built its entire body in response.

What it drinks instead is light. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing you stop noticing: here light behaves like a fluid. It is collected, piped, rationed, banked, and wasted. The sky is a vault — a continent-wide canopy of layered canopy, mirror-leaves polished waxy and never damp, lens-fruits the size of villages — and through it runs a distribution grid, thick horn-glass arteries carrying focused brightness down from the collectors to the districts below. At junctions, prismatic buds split the flow into spectra the way a river braids. Noon is delivered by shaft. Some halls receive their light secondhand, three reflections old, slightly gold. Some receive it fresh and blue-white and almost violent. And a few — the deep archive districts — live on stored noon, drawn from glass pods stacked like wine in cool cellars, some of it centuries past harvest. Stand in old light long enough and you begin to feel its age. It is dimmer, heavier, patient. The light has vintages here. There are connoisseurs.

Underfoot: floor. There is no ground in the wild sense — no rock, no soil, no mountain anywhere on the surveyed extent. The Floor is grown planking in slabs the size of city blocks, pale gold-grey, grained like timber and worn like a staircase, springy in the way of living wood. The seams between slabs have healed into amber, because the sap of this world is not water at all: a clear oily fluid, faintly sweet, faintly of machine oil, that never freezes and cures to glass when exposed. All the windows of the world are healed wounds. The industry farms injuries for glazing — planned incisions along the great stems, tapped and channelled into moulds, and the architecture scars itself on purpose to let the light in. Nothing dries here because nothing was ever wet. Nothing rusts. Things wear, but slowly, and what wears them is light itself: surfaces in the bright districts are photon-polished, soft-edged, shining without heat.

For the whole plain is low. The horizon does what horizons do inside a built vault: it rises, gently, so that far away you can watch the ceiling come down to meet the floor, the world curving up like the inside of a lens. Through certain engineered apertures — lens-wells, aligned with a patience no one alive remembers — you can see stars. Whether they are the actual stars, or lamps placed where the actual stars would be, is not settled, and the machinery that maintains the wells has never volunteered an opinion.

Everything is made, and almost everything is alive, and these are the same statement. There is no natural substrate because there is no "natural": even the feral patches are escapes from plantings. The vegetation is the architecture is the industry. Follow a freight line under the galleries and you pass cold forges where pressure and enzymes do the work of blast furnaces — no fire, never fire, the one absolute taboo in a world that is essentially continental tinder — and lathes driven by a single stem, thick as a district, grown into a spiral over nine hundred years and still turning its line of cutting tools through wooden gears worn to a mirror shine. Gauges are flowers: pressure shown by the aperture of a petal, current by the colour of a throat, and when something fails, the alarm is not a siren. The world's sirens are smells — a bitter green note released downwind, and every walker-tool within three districts turns its head.

The air is cool everywhere, and this is not poverty but competence. The plant drinks light and the light does not linger; conversion is instant, waste is scrubbed upward, and the touch of a sun-facing wall is room-temperature. In the oldest districts, resin exhaled slowly over centuries crystallizes on the rafters as hoarfrost — white feathers of gum, cold to the tongue — so that even here, in a place with no water, there is something that looks like frost. Underfoot in the basins lie dunes of chaff, the world's shed skin, pale and dry, and when wind moves through a basin the chaff hisses like dry leaves, which is the closest thing this world has to surf.

It has weather — real weather, of its own, born of its own scale. Not rain; the sky doesn't know how. Instead: spore-fall, whole days when the high galleries vent and pale spore comes down like slow snow, deepening the silence, masking the machinery in white. Pollen fronts that cross the continent over weeks, gold glitter-haze that refracts the light-grid into trembling columns and makes long looking slightly hallucinatory. And charge-weather, when static crawls the vault's ceiling veins — sheet lightning, green-white, running for a hundred miles along a single luminous nerve, and utterly silent.
