# The Yearling

The first thing you notice, crossing up onto it, is that your footsteps have gone quiet.

You have climbed for six days through the rim cliffs — black, glassy, unweathered rock that still chips like fresh flint, edges never rounded by any rain old enough to matter — and then the slope eases and you are out on top, and every sound you make sinks into the ground and does not come back. The ground is not rock. It is not soil. It is a felt.

That is the word the people up here use, and it is the right word. The entire tableland — and it is a tableland the way a sea is a sea, big enough that its weather is its own — is covered in a living mat of plant, root-woven into root-woven, a few fingers deep in most places, a forearm deep where it has had centuries to thicken. It covers everything. There is no bare ground anywhere; you could walk a season in one direction and never see stone, though stone is under all of it. The felt has sealed every crack, spanned every hollow, grown over the rim cliffs' broken shoulders and down into their crevices. Plant-as-substrate, made literal: the ground *is* the plant, and the plant is the world's skin.

The rock beneath — basalt, black, sharp-edged, young — is riddled with tubes and blisters and open seams. The table is hollow. It was poured and blistered and cracked open in the same slow catastrophe that lifted it, and nothing has had time to fill it in. The felt grew across the holes the way skin closes over a wound, and now you walk on a membrane stretched over a continental drum.

The people call the whole place the Yearling. They mean it plainly: a beast in its first hide, still growing, not yet done being carried into the world.

## What is missing

Stand still and look, and what you notice second is the emptiness *above*.

Nothing on the Yearling is tall. The felt lies flat. The wind and the cold and the thin young soil conspire to cap anything vertical at knee height — cushion-things, moss-things, a stunted rim-scrub that crawls instead of standing. The only things that rise above a person are the bone masts the herders plant, antler and megafauna rib lashed into poles a few meters high, and against a world of flat these read like towers.
