# The Weeping Country

The first thing to understand is that nothing here has ever grown. No soil was ever made; no seed ever landed and took; no root ever found a purchase worth the trouble. The plateau sits too high, too cold, too wet in the wrong way — wet like a wrung cloth, wet from the inside out. It is a country of stone and water and light, and it is finished. That last part takes a while to understand, and once you do, you never quite stop hearing it.

From the rim, the high country opens as a forest of pale blades — karst needles ranked to the horizon, chalk-white and gray, shoulder to shoulder in aisles sometimes wide enough for two people and sometimes not for one. The blades are old in their shapes: fluted, scalloped, worn smooth at every edge, the way driftwood is smooth, the way a thing gets when it has been touched the same way for an unthinkably long time. Between them, pools lie in the gaps like panes of glass laid flat, window-clear and so heavy with dissolved mineral that they mirror without a ripple. The sky is underfoot as often as overhead. More than once you will step where there is no step, because the ground and the air are the same color and the same shine.

And every face of every blade is wet. Not splashed, not rained on — *weeping*. Water stands on the stone in droves of beads, each bead holding a small cold sun, and seams of it run down the flutes in threads fine as wire. The whole plateau is a soaked sponge held up into the sky on a floor of unyielding rock. Somewhere beneath the needles there is a reservoir the size of a mountain range: a hidden river that traveled a hundred miles in the dark, under other countries, and arrived here to find no way down. So it comes up. It comes up through everything. The stone is colder than the air, and the air, this high, is always near its dew point, so the sky wrings itself out on the rock all day and all night, and the rock, already full, gives the surplus back at its edges. At the rim of the plateau, in a ring miles around, ten thousand springs step off the edge of the world at once.

That is the sound.

The sound is the weather here. It has components if you force yourself to hear them: the hiss of sheeting water on ten million faces of stone; the tick and chime of drops letting go and landing; a boom far underfoot where the aquifer moves through siphons in the mountain's bones, so the whole plateau rings faintly, continuously, like a struck bowl that never finishes its note. And over everything, the ring of the rim-falls, surrounding you from all sides at once, so that the roar has no direction and therefore no source and therefore no edge. There is no wind up here — the stillness is total, part of the same equanimity as everything else — so the sound never varies. Not in hours. You begin to understand that a noise this constant stops being an event and becomes a substance. It is the medium the country floats in. Silence, if it ever came, would be louder. Speech is for touching forearms. Shouting barely works. You hear your own pulse, and you are startled by how loud it is, and then you understand that this is the only irregular sound for miles: you.

The brightness is of the same order — total, and cold. The light at this altitude falls through almost nothing, and the wet stone gives nearly all of it back. Every weeping wall is a wall of sequins. The mist stands in the aisles, unmoving, lit from every direction at once, so that things in it cast four faint shadows and none of them are wrong. You squint in the cold. The light warms the surface of your skin for exactly as long as you stand still, and no further; the stone takes the heat back out through your boots, and the water, if you dare cup it and drink, tastes of cold rock and a hundred miles of darkness.

Look closer at the stone and it is not stone alone. Every surface wears a skin — a varnish of living film, black and rust and a deep violet, slick under the fingers, simpler than moss and older than any moss. This is the country's whole botany: no leaf has ever turned here, no frond, no blade of grass, nothing green in the history of green. The film is what sealed the stone. In some former age the water cut this forest in a fury — the blades are that fury, preserved — and then the water dissolved everything it could take, and became full, and stopped. Now it can neither carve nor coat. It only passes through. The film finished the job of closing the wounds, and the landscape settled into its own final shape the way a sentence settles when the last word lands.

So nothing here is being made anymore. Everything here is *kept*. Most landscapes are verbs, ongoing. This one is a past participle.

Life exists, but on the plateau's strict terms, and the ledger balances to the life. Flat pale grazers the size of fingernails ride the weeping walls, licking the varnish in paths so slow you must watch an hour to see that the paths move. In a few of the misty aisles, thin clouds of motes hang in the light — motes that are animals, drinking the condensation as it forms, the only swarms in the country and even they are sparse, more suggestion than crowd. And in the pools stand the tall ones: long-legged, waxy-pale, smooth as the stone they imitate, their eyes like two drops of water. They feed by standing. They stand by standing. You might cross the whole plateau in a day and see three of them, and perhaps one will move — a slow unfolding, a single step that sends no ripple worth the name — and that motion will be the only change you witness from horizon to horizon. The plateau permits a fixed number of lives, exactly as many as the water can carry and no more, the way a ledger balances or it does not.

There is nothing to build with and nothing that builds. No path, because no feet have made one. No cairn, no cut branch — no branch. The only marks ever left on this country are the water's, and the water stopped leaving marks an age ago. Whatever rules govern the world down in the lowlands — seasons, weather, appetite, decay — are suspended up here by saturation. Cold instead of winter. Glare instead of dawn. The roar instead of silence. Each day is a copy of the day before, struck from the same stone.
