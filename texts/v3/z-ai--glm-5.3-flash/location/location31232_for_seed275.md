# The Undergrowth

There is no sky here, and no horizon either — only grain. The whole world is interior: galleries, vaults, long descending ways, all of it cut through the living wood of a root-system so vast that no one has found either its beginning or its end. The first shafts were driven upward, generations ago, and lost their nerve in the dark. Below, the wood continues past the last map. The surface, if there is one, is a religion. People call it the Green Above, and they hang their lamps on the walls that face it.

Nothing here is wet. That is the first thing the body has to learn. There is no damp in the air, no drip, no seep, no sound of water anywhere, because there is no water anywhere. The Wood — everyone just calls it the Wood — runs on other chemistry: its sap is a clear volatile oil that smells of turpentine and camphor, and everything alive here drinks that, or drinks the thick amber honeydew that the glow-lichen exudes in slow beads. *Wet* is a word from old stories, and calling something wet is still, in the markets of the town, a mild insult. Rain is a bedtime-tale kind of thing, like dragons.

The wood itself runs in every color from pale straw to deep organ-amber, and the grain lies in strata like geology, because here it *is* geology. Where you cut it, the wall weeps a thread of clear oil that hardens within hours into a glass-bright bead of amber. Rooms smell of dry sweetness, like apples left too long in a warm loft. The grain runs for kilometers without interruption, and because cutting across it is brutal work, all architecture follows it — stairs spiral the way the grain spirals, streets bend where the grain bends, and so the town looks less like something built than like something grown, which is fair, because it was both.

Light in the inhabited galleries is the light of an overcast noon, and it comes from the walls. The bright-veins are channels of phosphorescent tissue that the lamenders cultivate like hedges, trimming them along street-lines, feeding them droplets of oil from flasks. Here is the quiet genius of the settlement, and the reason light is not merely comfort but infrastructure: the Wood grows toward glow. Light a vein into a dark gallery and within a season the pale new wood begins shouldering toward it. The lamp tells the tree where to build. Every lamplighter is, without knowing it, an urban planner.

The town is called Kerf, after the slit a saw leaves, and it is a town of enormous rooms and few people. Space costs nothing here — there is always more wood — so what you pay for is warmth and glow. The poor crowd the warm streets near the distilleries, where engine-heat keeps the air almost blood-warm and the rent is cheap because the fumes are thick. The rich live in the cold far galleries, one household to a vault the size of a grain-barn, and measure wealth in lamplengths. Between the districts lie the Aisles: stretches of dark, empty galleries where nothing is built, nothing is lit, and the grain runs on into blackness with only the occasional cultivated vein burning far off, like a lamp forgotten in an empty house.

Kerf works. It works the Wood the way a coastal town works the sea. The tapfields are groves of spigots driven into living vein-walls, oil running off into amber flasks. The distillery quarters stand full of tall clay stacks condensing turpentine vapor, their presses groaning day-shift and night-shift, their heat turning the thermocouple-junctions that ring the shift-bells and carry the signal-wire down the trunk-lines — crude electricity, enough for bells and lamps and not much more, and everyone agrees that is probably for the best. In the auger-yards, teams of borers as tall as houses chew new streets on schedule. Beside them work the mouth-worms: grazer-caterpillars the size of oxen, guided by keepers with hooks and oil-cakes, who excavate galleries by eating and leave tunnels round and smooth and faintly polished by the passage of hides. Half the roads of Kerf were digested, not carved.

All fire sits in stone. There is stone here — nodules of true mineral scattered through the dry matrix between roots, pried out and quarried like treasure — and every engine in the settlement rests on a cradle of it, every hearth is ringed with it. Open flame is licensed, lit only from the single mother-lamp in the fire-wardens' hall, and its keepers are the closest thing the town has to clergy. Far out in the western galleries, deliberately unlit, lies the Hollow of Ash: a whole chamber of charcoal, a burn from before anyone living, where nothing has grown since. They keep it dark the way other places keep tombs.

The fauna is everywhere, and most of it is useful before it is dangerous. Grubs riddle every beam; borer-beetles the size of fists mine their own minor weather through the walls, and in borer-year, when they hatch in clouds, every household seals its doors with waxed hide and waits. Glowmoths swarm the lumen-gardens, pollinating the veins. In the Aisles graze the wild mouth-worms, and after the grazers come the velvet-jaws — long, silent, quick-eared hunters that flow down the dark galleries on padded feet. The quiet of the Undergrowth is partly their doing; a noisy animal here does not last. So when people must cross an Aisle, they cross in convoy, lamp in front, and one person walks behind with a clapper, making the only loud thing in the world on purpose. In a place this quiet, noise means *not prey*.
