# The City of Standing Water

There is no rain here. There has been no rain for as long as the city has existed, which is as long as anything has existed, and yet the water never stops falling.

It falls from a sky that cannot be seen — the city is too high, the clouds too low, and between them hangs a permanent grey-white luminosity, light without a source, thick enough to swim in. The air is saturated. Every surface weeps. Stone weeps. Walls weep. The terraces of the great stairways glisten endlessly, sheets of water sliding down ten thousand steps without ever eroding them, because nothing here erodes, nothing here changes, nothing here has changed since before change was invented.

This is the puzzle of the place, and you feel it before you understand it: a city built in equilibrium. The water moves; the water has always moved; the water will always move in exactly these channels, over exactly these lips of stone, pooling in exactly these basins, spilling over exactly these rims. Waterfalls of identical volume pour from identical ledges at identical rates, forever. It is a deafening place — the sound is the first thing that reaches you, arriving like a hand on your chest. Every fall of water, every hiss of steam off the hot terraces, every overflow's splash is tuned to the same colossal chord, and the chord does not vary. There is no wind. The sound has no texture of weather in it. It is architecture made of noise, load-bearing sound, and you begin to suspect the city is *held up* by its own roar.

The vegetation has taken this as an invitation. Ferns the size of ships' masts grow from every crack, their fronds heavy with condensation, each droplet caught mid-drip. Moss climbs the walls in velvet layers a foot thick. Flowering vines — blooms like wet porcelain, pale, scentless — curtain whole facades, and the green is so lush, so saturated, that it reads almost as a color the eye wasn't built for. But look closely at any plant and you'll find it isn't growing. It *is grown*. Fully grown, forever grown, caught at the peak of its own lushness like a note held past all endurance. No seedlings. No dead leaves. No rot, though everything is wet enough to rot in an hour. The ecology here is a garden frozen at the exact moment of its own abundance.

And there are no animals. Not insects, not birds, not worms in the wet soil. The silence-within-the-sound of that absence is the second thing you feel. The vines sway, but nothing moves them. The pools ripple, but something invisible — something structural — is rippling them, the way a bell is rung. You realize the water's endless identical motion and the plants' endless identical lushness are the same phenomenon: a world stopped at maximum, at climax, at fullness, and forbidden to proceed.

The builders are gone, or were never there, or *are* the place. No tools, no machines, no artifacts — only the construction itself, and what construction. Terraced cities climb the mountain's shoulders for miles, block upon block, causeways spanning drowned valleys, aqueducts that carry water from one weeping height to another in permanent circulation, serving no purpose that a living city would recognize. Stairways ascend toward the white sky and end in air. Arches stand over nothing. Every surface is worn smooth in the exact pattern of millennia of use — foot-polished treads, hand-polished railings — and no foot has ever set on them, or every foot has, once, all at once, at the moment the world was fixed.

The heat is the last thing, and the strangest. Hot as a greenhouse, hot as breath on glass, and yet the water never evaporates away. It steams, yes — mist rises off the terraces in slow white banners, softening every distance — but the total water never diminishes. Condensation returns exactly what vaporization takes. A sealed accounting. A balanced ledger. The whole city is an equation holding its breath.

Stand still long enough on a high causeway and the deafening roar begins to resolve, the way a chord resolves into its notes. Underneath the water-noise there is a hum. Underneath the hum, a tone. Underneath the tone — and this is where the weirdness of the place stops being picturesque and starts looking back at you — a rhythm. Slow. Vast. Once every few minutes, maybe. It is either the city's heartbeat or the tick of a mechanism that will one day finish counting, and when it does, everything held in equilibrium — every mid-drip droplet, every held bloom, every note of that eternal chord — will be allowed to *continue*.

Nothing in the city wants that. The vines lean away from wherever the rhythm comes from. The water, in its channels, hesitates — an almost imperceptible stutter in the flow, once per beat.

You should not stay until the next one.
