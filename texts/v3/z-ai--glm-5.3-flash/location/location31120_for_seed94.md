# The Sough

The edge arrives before you expect it. One moment the ground is rising in long, gentle swells, moss to the horizon; the next, the land simply stops, and you are standing on the lip of the world, looking down two miles of falling air at a sea of cloud.

The cliff does not fall so much as leak. The rock is old flood basalt, laid down in slow gray centuries, and between each flow the plateau keeps a hidden table of water — so the whole escarpment weeps. Not waterfalls: threads. Hundreds of thousands of threads, each one thinner than a finger, some no thicker than a spider's patience, sliding down the black stone through beards of moss that hang twenty feet and more. They merge, part, merge again. The sound of all of them together is a hiss so soft it registers as silence with texture. At the base of the wall, a hundred streams the size of rivers go down into the cloud, and the cloud closes over them without comment.

Stand there long enough and you learn the first rule of the place: **sound does not carry here.** The mist and the moss eat it. A shout travels ten meters and dies of it. A stone knocked over the edge makes no sound you will ever hear. The plateau keeps what it is given.

It has just rained. Everything beaded, everything dripping — the drip intervals different in every hollow, an irregular ticking like a vast clock that cannot make up its mind. The air tastes of cold stone and green things growing. Your breath shows faintly at dawn, not because it is freezing but because the air is high and cool and always, always on the edge of condensing. On the high knolls in the cold season there is one hour of frost before sunrise; the plateau permits itself that, and no more.

---

There is no bare ground. Not anywhere. Walk a season in any direction and you will not find a hand's width of naked earth. The word *ground* has to be relearned: here it means something that gives.

The surface is moss, and the moss has depth — two meters in the hollows, five in the old kettle-hollows where ice age ponds melted in place and never drained. It is cool underfoot and faintly warm on top where the sun lies on it. Water does not run here so much as *arrive* — every drop newly condensed or newly fallen, sitting in the sphagnum like a coin in a purse, moving inward through the mat at a walking pace, taking miles to find a crack in the basalt. The tarns between the bogs are different: black, still, cold as melted snow, windows into a downstairs. Do not linger at their rims. One of them is not empty. The only evidence is a patch where there should be ripple and there is no ripple.

The land is old. It was mountains once — you can tell from the roots of the thing, the basalt flows stacked like the pages of a shut book — and then it spent its eras being worn down to exactly what it is: a table two miles high, smooth-backed, mature, its gullies rounded, its drainage meandering, nothing raw anywhere. Nothing here is getting younger or older. Dust arrives on the mist and settles into the bog; sediment leaves in the rivers; the ledger balances to the gram. The plateau is not eroding and not building. It is *in its own state*, the way a middle-aged sea is, and it has been in it longer than most kinds of stone have names.

---

Now the machine.

By midmorning the sun has drawn a film of vapor off every wet surface on the plateau — off two thousand square miles of moss and tarn and weeping stone — and the whole table begins to breathe upward. The mist lifts off the low folds in sheets. The air, thin and bright at this height, fills with drifting spore-flocks visible as slow smoke, so that even the sky is occupied. By afternoon the plateau has lifted enough of its own water to build cumulus over its center — towers of it, stacking like a mountain range made of weather, with coastlines and shadow-countries of their own. It spends a week building a storm and an afternoon spending it. The rain comes down in slow soaks, or in one enormous warm-fronted drench that the entire surface receives at once, and then the cycle turns: night falls, the wet black rock radiates its heat away, the cloud pours back down the folds and pools below the escarpment in a sea, and the plateau inhales.

That is the engine. But the engine has moving parts, and the parts are alive.

There are animals here — fist-sized, furred, dark-eyed, unafraid — that drink at the tarns and then climb the basalt terraces to mist-height and exhale what they carried, thousands to the acre, a lift-and-release metabolism written into their bodies. Their combined labor is part of the plateau's water-raising the way the sun is. And under all of it there is the *sough*: a sound below sound, not heard but felt, a pressure in the sternum like the hum inside a seashell — if the seashell were a country. It is the sum of every thread of percolating water, every small pump at work, every micro-discharge in the moss. Lie flat on the mat at night, still your breath, and you can feel the whole place running.

If you insist on calling it industry, then it is industry with no floor, no whistle, no ruins. Intake: mist, dust, light. Processing: moss, pumps, slow percolation. Product: water, shipped daily in threads over the edge to lowlands that will never know where their rivers were assembled. Power: sun and static. And no strike will ever slow the line, because the machines are the ground, and the ground does not rust. It rots — and rotting is part of the process.

---

Which brings us to the electrics.

After rain, the ground forages. The endless friction of mist on moss, storm after storm, year after ten thousand years, leaves the plateau holding a standing charge, and it discharges not in sky-bolts but along the surface: pale filaments of cool lightning that walk the wet moss slowly, foraging for ground, slower than a hand moving under a sheet. They crackle when they pass you — the loudest sound the plateau makes, and it is the sound of a knuckle popping, if the knuckle were two miles wide.

They do work. Where the filaments feed, the air is briefly warm and the rain is briefly rich, and the moss there grows sweeter, taller, hungrier. The lushness of this place is partly fed by its own electrical industry — the plateau fertilizing itself with weather.

And there are the lines. Here is the strangest ordinary thing about the place: pale trackways worn through the moss, smooth, roadlike, running straight for miles and then bending for reasons no one can see, going nowhere and everywhere. Travelers' eyes, if travelers ever came, would call them roads. They are not roads. They are the paths of least resistance, the routes the discharge prefers, kept open by a warmth in the soil and walked by nothing. The only straight lines on the plateau are drawn by lightning, and nothing that uses them was ever made.

Small creatures work the charge. Birds roost on the highest stems after storms and groom the potential off the air, whiskers sparking, drinking light the way other animals drink at pools. They are silent about it. Everything here is silent about everything.

---

The herds come through on their circuits, and you see them ten minutes before you see them: a swell in the moss itself, a slow traveling wave of ground rolling outward from their footsteps, ringed ahead by a faint halo of startled birds. They are broad, low, wide-footed animals, pale-eyed, and they cross the quaking surface the way ships cross a sea — because that is what the surface is, an ocean given a skin. They graze in silence. They have no call. When one is taken at a tarn's rim — and one is, sometimes, by the stillness with intent that lives below — the herd does not flee. It closes its gap, the way water closes over a stone, and walks on. Death here is quiet the way everything is quiet, and cheap the way everything is cheap, because the plateau is packed edge to edge with life and can afford the toll.

Once a year, the Flush. Six days in the warm season when every cushion plant and herb on two thousand square miles opens at once — the plateau wears flowers, all of them simultaneously, a brief continent-colored blaze — and every pollinator it owns rises into the spore-smoke to work it. Then the flowers come off, and the moss takes the field back, and the gray-green table resumes its long shift.

---

At the center, you cannot see the edges. After a while you stop entirely believing in them. The horizon doubles — land to one limit, cloud to another — and the weather walking in from the interior has coastlines of its own, fronting and spending, fronting and spending, on a schedule the plateau sets and keeps. The air is bright and thin at noon, silvered and soft at every other hour. Nothing echoes. Nothing is built. Nothing was ever built, and nothing needs to be; the plateau ran its industry before builders were invented and will run it long after, its furnaces the sun, its pipes the moss, its whistle a hum below hearing.

There are no maps of it. There are only approaches, and the slow discovery, always, of the same fact: that the quiet here is not an absence. It is a mass. It sits on the land like the water does.

The name, insofar as the place consents to one, is the Sough — the old word for a soft rushing breath, and also, in another life of the language, for a mine-drain: a passage by which deep water finds its way out of the dark. Both meanings fit. It breathes. It drains. It keeps the difference between the two to itself.
