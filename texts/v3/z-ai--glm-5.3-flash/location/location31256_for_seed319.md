You hear Welling for an hour before you see it. The road climbs through fog that never quite burns off, and the sound comes down through that fog the way light does — diffuse, from everywhere, arriving already inside you. Visitors describe it as machinery. People who live there describe it as weather. It is both: a sustained roar with a chord inside it, eleven or twelve notes held forever, and under everything a slow pulse, like the plateau's own heartbeat, felt in the sternum before it is heard.

The plateau itself is a long bench of dark stone rising out of the region's lowlands, high enough that from its rim you can watch weather move across three valleys. It should be cold up there. It isn't. The plateau sits on a lens of deep water that comes up warm from its long descent through the rock, so the whole tableland breathes a muggy, mineral-smelling mildness, and the air is so heavy with moisture that it condenses on anything cooler than a hand — railing, bucket, the back of your neck. Rain, when it comes, is rarely an event. The sky simply thickens until everything drips. The locals call it the sky sweating, and they don't look up when it happens.

The geology explains the city. The plateau is a sponge: a great lens of porous stone sitting on unyielding bedrock, tilted just so, so that rain falling across an entire region seeps down and sideways and gathers here, under this one table, and is forced up through it — weeping out slowly over the whole of its surface and its face. The rim, where the water exits in volume, has been carved by its own discharge into fluted organ-pipes of stone and stepped terraces of mineral crust, each ledge dribbling, each draped in the pale stone that the water leaves behind as it goes. The erosion here is not a process you can watch. The flutes were flutes before anyone alive's grandmother's grandmother stood on them; the forms are settled, in balance, rain in and weep out, a machine the plateau has been running since long before anyone thought to put wheels in it.

The wheels came anyway. The city of Welling spreads along the rim and down the terraces, and its oldest industry is the industry of patience. The water carries dissolved stone — the plateau weeps its own substance — and in the accretion yards this is farmed: mineral water, drawn warm from the deep galleries, is run in fine threads over wooden frames for years, decades, and stone grows there like wax round a candle. Pillars, lintels, whole curved wall-sections, grown rather than cut, their surfaces stippled and waxen, ringed like tree-rings with the seasons of their growing. A pillar commissioned by a grandmother is delivered to her grandson, cured and cut. Salt and metal flowers are scraped from the pans each dry season. And through the middle of the city run the great wheel-pits — internal channels carrying heavy water down through the rock's own drop — where wheels the height of houses turn with a slow, endless torque, one revolution to the minute, grinding, driving, and in the last few generations turning dynamos as well, so that carbon filament lamps glow softly through the fog along the causeways, smudges of amber in the general pale.

The sound is made by all of it together. The Weep itself, the main discharge over the rim, is a standing roar like a sea that has forgotten its tides. The wheel-pits each contribute a sustained tone, tuned by their water and their stonework, and the great pump-engines that keep the deep galleries drained add the pulse — one vast stroke every six breaths, felt through the soles of the feet, and the exhaust, and the resonance of the rock itself, which has taken up the sound so completely that there are stretches of bare stone that hum when you press your palm flat against them. Standing water trembles into rings. Teeth ache by evening. Nobody in Welling uses bells — they can't be heard — so the city speaks in shuttered lamps and steam-whistles at set pitches, and the people speak in hand-sign, quick and eloquent, taught before speech. You can stand in the market and watch a hundred conversations happening in silence above the roar, like weeds bending in a current that doesn't touch them.

The city is spread out, because it has to be — water must have room to move, and the first law of Welling, older than any charter, is never block a seep. So the works stand apart across wide commons of wet bare stone, joined by arched causeways and pipe-bridges, every roofline curved to gather condensate and weep it through carved stone mouths into standard channels. Every building in the city is a water instrument first and a shelter second. The architecture of grown stone gives even new districts an ancient look — organic, dripped, bulging gently at the joints — and the layers run deep: lower Welling is deliberately drowned, streets become canals by design, avenues of the far past rowed through by boat, their doorways underwater and their upper windows bricked into cistern-hatches. The city grows upward as the stone grows. Nobody knows where the bottom is.

Almost nothing green wants the water — it is too mineral, too metallic, brackish with dissolved ages — so vegetation survives only in the thin places. Where sweet condensate collects in the fog-fields along the windward rim, moss grows in flat grey-green mats combed all one direction, as if ducking under the sound's weight, and sedges stand in the seep-lines, and a few pale willow-like trees lean so hard against the rock they seem to be lying down. From the far side of a valley you can see the plateau's wetness written on it in green: thin veins of plants following the water-lines across dark stone, like the ruin of a map. Animals are rarer still. Pale eyeless fish thread the discharge channels. Fog-moths drift in the warm updrafts above the evaporation yards. The one bird, the piper, nests inside the flutes of the rim and sings against the roar — a needle-call pitched to cut the chord, a stitch of silver through felt. You hear one a day at most, and feel oddly thanked for it. In the drowned streets something larger moves, once a season, seen as a bow-wave in an avenue where no boat is. No one fishes there. Not from law. From tact.

Two strangenesses the residents live around rather than explain. First, the hushes: small circles, a few paces across, scattered on the plateau and even inside the city, where the sound simply is not — perfect, dense silence, as if the roar were a lake and these holes in its ice. The chord stops at their rim like a wall. Welling marks them on maps the way other towns mark wells, keeps lamps burning in them, does not linger. Children are taught the sign for hush before they're taught the sign for food. Second, the swallow: two or three times in a century the plateau inhales — some vast seep reverses for a single breath and drinks — and the entire chord stumbles, drops, hiccups. Work stops everywhere in the city when it happens. Hands still. Lamps held up. Then the plateau breathes out and the roar resumes, and the whole population exhales with it, and no one says a word, because there is no word that fits and no one to hear it anyway.

Time here moves at the pace of the water. Fog thickens over days, not hours. The water table breathes across the year — in the high season the Weep swells, the chord rises audibly by close to a tone, the lowest drowned streets take on a step of water, and the whole city adjusts like a sleeper turning over. Distances are measured in decades. That quarter wasn't built; it was grown, and is growing still. The ring-lines in the accretion stone are read like archives — this dry year, this wet decade, the swallow of the old grandfather's time visible as a fault line in a wall. The plateau has been running longer than anything human and will run long after, patient, in balance, weeping its slow warm stone over the rim, and the sound — which seems eternal — will change too, by one tone, perhaps, across a lifetime, and the pipers will retune their needle-calls to match, and no one will be able to say when it happened.
