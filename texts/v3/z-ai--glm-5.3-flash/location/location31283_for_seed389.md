The Newcomers are younger than the town that lives on them.

There are no old stones in this range. Nothing has been rounded, no valley cut, no slope gentled into a shoulder. Erosion has not arrived yet — it is expected, the way a relative is expected, and the landscape waits for it with its edges bare. Every rock face is a fresh fracture. Every field of rubble is quake-shatter, angular as broken crockery, no grain of it having traveled farther than it was thrown. Under the plain white daylight the massif looks less like a place than like a recent event, still deciding where its outlines go.

The mountain itself is called the Rise, and the town is called Upwell, and both names mean roughly the same thing, because here the town and the stone are doing it together.

The Rise stands above where the air thins. Its summit fields are bare gray plates of porous rock — a sponge gone rigid, the frozen froth of its own birth — and this is the first rule a visitor learns: the stone eats sound. The mountain drinks noise the way sand drinks rain. Engines run in Upwell, hoists run, presses run, and you can stand three steps from a press house and hear almost nothing, only a pressure at the ear like the memory of a sound. Footfalls die at the heel. Voices carry a few meters and then simply end, swallowed. Bells were tried, long ago; they are ornamental now. The town talks with mirrors instead — heliograph towers at every tier, shutters clicking silently in code, the ordinary sun put to work as a voice. On a clear morning the whole settlement flickers with conversation made of light.

And the sun is the one ordinary thing here. It rises plain and white and does its work without comment, and the town has organized itself around that plainness the way sailors organize around tide.

The heat comes from below. The Newcomers are geologically newborn and thermally immature, still cooling from the inside, and the rock is warm the way an animal is warm — unevenly, and on its own schedule. By afternoon the bare stone is too hot to rest a hand on; the walls of the lower terraces radiate like bread ovens; the town does its heavy labor at night, by mirror-light, in shirtsleeves, while the thin air overhead goes cold and sharp. Water boils before it is properly hot. Everything lives in the seam between the two temperatures — hot stone at your back, cold sky at your face — and that seam is only a hand's thickness wide. All of Upwell is crowded into it.

The town rings the mountain's shoulders in tiers, a dense stitched architecture of platforms and gantries and spiral hoist-roads, built close enough that neighbors can pass a tool across four balconies. No gap a hand wide is left idle. Pipes braid with stair strings. Counterweights travel through people's living rooms on a published schedule, and the rooms are built around their passing. But the strangest fact of Upwell's construction is that nothing is anchored. Foundations here are opinions. Every building stands on jacks — screw-jacks, wedge-jacks, long telescoping legs with grease cups and date tags — because the mountain is still rising, a patient few millimeters a year, and everything the town owns must rise with it or be swallowed.

Which is the second rule: in Upwell, buildings grow without being built. A house fed from below gains a story every few decades as its ground floor sinks into the stone — floor becomes cellar, cellar becomes crypt, crypt becomes gallery, and gallery becomes warm darkness threaded with rock. The oldest terraces descend into the mountain through their own histories. Go down-ground on the east side and you can walk through the town's swallowed past: previous ground floors with their shop counters still standing, an old bathhouse with its tiles intact, a press house from three generations back whose engine sits entombed in mineral dark. The veinstone has found some of these rooms. It has entered the old machines through their fill-caps and oil ports, slowly, the way water finds a cellar, and once a year — the town does not agree on why, and does not agree on whether to be afraid — that buried engine ticks. Once. Not heard; the mountain doesn't permit it. Felt, through the soles, by whoever is standing near.

The veins are why the town is here at all.

The Newcomers are shot through with them: dark layered channels in the rock, carrying a slow mineral blood the workers call black milk. It is not ore and it is not oil; it is both ancestors at once. It moves at the pace of a hand-span a day along the main channels — you can watch a marked blob of it take a week to pass a stake — and where it meets the cold air it clots into a smoke-dark glass. The town's windows are cut from the mountain's scabs. The milk carries the mountain's heat in solution, and Upwell's industry is built on harvesting that: gradient engines strung between hot flange and cold sky, running on the difference, running almost silently. Press houses milk the veins through long filter-beds. Glassworks squat near the weeping faces, quenching and cutting. Nothing here is mined in the old sense.
