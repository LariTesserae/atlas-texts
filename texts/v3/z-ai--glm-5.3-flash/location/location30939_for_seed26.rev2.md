The lift opens at the bottom of a valley that has never seen a sky.

You step out onto a tram platform into air that is cold enough to bite the lungs and wet enough to feel it settling on your eyelashes, and the first thing you understand is that this is not a room. A room has walls you can imagine touching in a few strides. This has distance. The floor runs out grey and flat for nearly a kilometre before the fog takes it, and the fog takes it in layers, like geology that forgot to harden. Overhead — if it is overhead, if it is a ceiling at all — is darkness with weather in it. Eighty metres up, where the warm exhaust of the halls meets the cold deck of the structure, moisture beads and gathers and lets go, and somewhere out in the middle distance it is raining, gently, indoors, on a floor of poured concrete scored with drainage channels to catch it. The design gave up and made room for it.

They call the whole space the Vault, and the place the Underworks, and both words undersell it. Three kilometres end to end. The floor plates in the middle are wide enough that the tram, crossing them, looks like a lit corridor laid on its side and set adrift. Machines stand apart across these distances like standing stones: a cluster of aggregate silos with frost on their north faces; a yard where spare pump impellers the size of millstones are stacked like coins; a switchyard whose transformers hum a three-note chord you can hear from half a kilometre away, each phase slightly out of tune with the others, so that the old hands can tell a failing unit by ear before the instruments admit it. The emptiest material on site is space, and there is a great deal of it. It is stockpiled like anything else.

Everything here is made. That is not an observation, it is a doctrine, posted on laminated signs at every stairwell: no natural substrate. Where the excavation reached bedrock, the rock was sealed under membrane and shotcrete and never shown again — it was treated like contamination, because the water moving through this place is the product, pumped from the aquifer that presses in from all sides, treated, measured, sent up to the cities on the cold plain above. Hydrologically speaking, the rivers of the surface world are overhead. Not one stone is visible. Even the marsh is made: along the southern edge runs the Filter, a ribbon of treatment ponds a half-kilometre long, and its mud is a manufactured article with a batch number — graded slag and clay laid in trays, planted with reeds that are technically equipment. Nothing here is natural except what arrived without permission: the water, the air, the rust, the life.

And it is young. That is the strangest register of the place, under the strangeness of everything else: thirty years old, and the concrete still crisp. Formwork tie-holes sharp as drilled buttons. Date stamps readable in the set concrete of the quays. Rust only beginning, freckling the bolt heads like a child's first ink. A landscape this new should have no history, and it doesn't — but it has erosion already, fast erosion, because cold water working on young construction is impatient. The freeze-thaw has spalled a corner off the north quay the size of a truck. Weep stains are ordering themselves into long rust teardrops down every wall. And last spring, meltwater found a seam under the slag berms at the east end and cut itself a channel nine metres deep in a single season — the first creek in the history of a place that was built instead of born. There is a standing argument about whether to pipe it, line it, or let it be. The argument has run two years. The creek does not attend. It is the only thing on site that keeps no schedule, and people visit it on their breaks just to hear it — a thin, clear sound, water over slate-grey aggregate, that exists nowhere else in the Underworks. The surveyors' maps are already wrong where it flows.

The weather has a system, and people forecast it. The foundry and the pump halls exhale warm, damp air that rises until it hits the cold underside of the deck and turns to fog and then to drizzle, so that down the centre of the vault, along the aisle the crews call the Rainway, it rains most of every shift — sparse, cold, persistent rain, applauded off concrete, collected in channels, sent to the sumps. Downwind of the ventilation stacks it does something else: the warm exhaust crystallizes in the cold and falls as rime, slow and granular, all year, and has drifted into a grey snowfield against the north wall that never melts, because the heat never reaches there. They call it the Snowfield. Out at the perimeter, in the cold ring where nothing on site warms the air, ice grows on the weeping walls in furrows and fins, white on rust, and advances a centimetre each winter. The wind, when there is wind, comes always from the northeast, because the ducts say so. The seasons arrive in only one form: the temperature of the water. In spring the aquifer breathes in — the melt weeks they call high water — and the marsh lifts a finger's width, the weeps run silver, doors stick in their frames, and the deep pumps take on a third shift. You can hear the change before anyone announces it.

Because everything here is audible. The pump hall at the west end is a cathedral of iron — five machines the size of houses, running in loose rotation, their stroke arriving less as sound than as a sub-bass you feel in the sternum and the teeth. Boxes of ear protection stand at its doors like alms. Out in the vault the sound softens to a general loudness: the duct-wind that never stops, the trundle of the overhead cranes whose lights cross the dark high above like slow satellites operated by people you will never see, the crackle of welding somewhere in the yards, the twice-a-shift tram with its bell, absurdly musical. Shout in the empty middle of the Vault and your voice comes back twice — once off the near machinery, and once more, seconds later, fainter and higher, from somewhere above the fog where the drawings show no surface at all. No survey has found what returns it. The crews have stopped speculating, mostly.

The quietest place is the Filter, and people go there to rest their ears. The water comes to it cloudy and leaves it clear, through ponds in series, through reed-trays and gravel beds, and along that half-kilometre almost all the green on site is gathered: the planted reeds knocking in the duct-draft, algae films brightening the weirs, moss meadows cushioning the warm condensate pipes in unofficial colonies that scrubbing crews remove on rotation and that return after each scrubbing a little sooner, as if learning the schedule. In the storerooms, mushrooms fruit off the old timber formwork in the dark. And in the drying room, under a grow-lamp, in a paint bucket with holes drilled in it, grows a birch, thigh-high, persistent. It is called the tree. Nobody proposes expanding its category.

The fauna is thin and therefore, when it appears, it is an event. The ponds were stocked with pale, slow fish — *grazers*, officially — that eat the algal film, and every few weeks the warm lines throw a bloom of small white moths that rise into the lamp domes and spin there by the thousand, while the grazers take them at the surface with soft plops, a predator-prey loop running its tiny circuit under artificial noon. Rats came in with the feed grain years ago, and in the second winter two owls came down an airshaft after them and stayed; they hunt the tram verge at night like small officials making rounds. And two springs ago a heron came down the overflow shaft and did not find its way out, or did not wish to. It works the shallows at pond six. When it is seen, the radios carry it the way other places carry weather: *heron at six. Heron at six, heading south.* For a moment the whole wet grey valley shares one small interest.

The people are sparse — this is not a town so much as an argument with water, staffed in shifts. You hear them more than you see them: voices pitched to carry over the general loud, a bell marking shift change that reaches every corner of the Vault, the canteen's orange window at the far end with its breath of warmth visible in the cold air like a campfire's. New workers see grey everywhere and grieve a little. Old workers stop seeing grey and start reading it — every stain a date, every weep a character, the rust freckles a calendar. The rules are simple and posted: nothing natural touches the water after Stage Four; no soil on site; all substrate engineered. The exceptions accumulate anyway, at the margins, one moss cushion, one creek, one heron at a time.

From the north gantry at shift change you can watch the whole metabolism run at once: the deep pumps beating their slow iron heart, the fog turning over in layers, the Rainway applauding steadily somewhere out in the dark, the creek arguing in its channel with a sound like no other sound here, the tide breathing in and out with the year, the crane lights crossing like satellites, an owl crossing a lamp dome with its rat. Nothing is idle. Nothing was found here; everything was made, down to the rain and the mud and, lately, the weather. It is dim, and cold, and loud, and wet, and young — a valley that was built instead of discovered, already eroding at its crisp edges, already leaking into an ecosystem, already leaving its plans.

Everything here is made. That was the idea. The water has others, and the water has time.
