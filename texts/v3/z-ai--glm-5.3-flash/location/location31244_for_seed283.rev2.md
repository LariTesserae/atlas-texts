There are no shadows in the basin, because there is no light bright enough to cast them.

Light exists — a gray pressure far overhead, where the crowns of the great trees interlock into a sealed ceiling — but it arrives down here as a rumor, a barely-lighter darkness with a direction. The sky is a theory. The sun is known only as warmth banked into wood. And the warmth is everywhere. That is the second thing the basin does: it hands you the heat. Not sunlight heat, not weather — heat as a medium, the way water is a medium for a fish. It comes up from below, out of the deep rock under the floor, old and patient, and the ceiling holds it in, and the air between is thick with it, wet and close and moving only when it must.

Underfoot the ground gives slightly, a mattress of leaf-mold as deep as anything has ever fallen. Every step expresses a smell: green rot, warm wax, the sweet cinnamon of crushed stems. Press a palm flat to the soil and a hand-span down it is blood-warm, and stays warm, and is warm again tomorrow. The whole floor of the basin sits on low fire. This is the engine's first secret, and it explains the rest: warmth does the work that water usually does. A plant with warm roots needs only sips. The basin is lush the way a greenhouse is lush — heated from beneath, fog-fed, and never once by accident.

Because the water here is thin, though it is everywhere. That is the local paradox, and every living thing is built around it. It rains, rarely — perhaps twice in a year, warm rain that lasts a night and a day, and the whole ecology is staged for it like a theater between performances. In between, the water arrives as fog and stays as film. The ceiling crowns comb it out of the air; every leaf-tip is drawn to a point to shed it; and so the under-story has its own private rain, a fine sizzle-tick of drips passing leaf to leaf, falling most of the hours of every day — rain that comes from below the sky. That leaf-rain is the sound-floor of the place. You hear it before anything else, and you never stop hearing it, and it is nearly all you hear.

For all that dripping, the basin is dry in the way that matters. There are no rivers. The streambeds are dry roads — long, cobbled alleys running from the rim down to the middle dark, carved in some rainier age and now kept like furniture, their stones polished round as things handled. They are the cleanest walking in the world. Animals travel them in single files you feel through your soles before you smell. Water lives in pockets instead: a cistern grove of swollen pale-trunked trees that hold seasons of it, cool to the touch along their flanks, scarred with old chew-taps callused over like sealed welds; hollow stems that have become wells; a single spring-line where the aquifer pushes up through a boil of warm sand and runs bright for a few hundred meters before the litter drinks it back. In the cool hours, steam stands along that stream in curtains. It is the only open water, and it is never unattended.

The vegetation is packed the way a wall is packed with brick. All the green commerce happens upstairs, in the bright stratum the floor never sees; down here the forest lives on exports — falling flowers, falling fruit, falling leaf — and the exports never stop, because the crown above is a single continuous factory-roof of foliage with no gaps in it anywhere. Below, the trunks rise buttressed and dark, cool on one face and warm on the other; the middle registers are stacked solid with ferns and lianas and young trees waiting generations for a hole in the ceiling; moss drinks the fog directly out of the air and drips it downward; lichen closes over every last surface. Where a trunk is wounded it seals itself with resin, and the sweet incense-smell of that sealing is the closest thing the forest has to a raised voice.

In the low hollows the litter collects knee-deep, and there the place keeps its vats. Decomposition is exothermic — the vats are warm from their own rot, faintly steaming at the edges in the cool hours, smelling of bread and beer. The fungus estate works them without pause: white threads binding everything, bracket shelves in ranks, wood becoming soil in slow assembly lines. Animals come to eat the yeast-rich mush, and one kind of browser sleeps in the warmth of a vat the way a cat sleeps on a boiler.

Now the moving parts. The first law of the basin is quiet — not empty quiet, not the quiet of absence, but maintained quiet, mutual, like a library everyone signed. Because in this packed dark, sound would carry too far and tell too much, so the animals have mostly stopped making it. They speak through the floor instead. A heavy bull a mile away arrives as a sentence in your soles. Herds pass as tremor and smell and a warm displacement of air; you never see them, you feel them the way you feel a train in a station wall. The basin's conversations happen underground, in the peat, at a pace and a distance the ears are useless for.

The abundance, though — the abundance is everywhere and touches you constantly. Stand still for an hour and you will be touched by a hundred small lives: moths settling on your sleeves like flakes of ash, a beetle crossing your boot with the confidence of a game piece, something drinking the condensation off your knee. The air between knee and head has grain. Swarms pass like slow weather; when they cross the ceiling, the gray dims and brightens. The litter is granular with bodies at every scale, worms of real length, beetle-oxen turning the vats' contents, colony ants running galleries that are the wiring of the whole place. Termite mounds stand up through the under-story like organ pipes, and they are exactly what they look like: ventilation stacks, drawing air through the soil. Put your ear to one and you hear the only steady sound in the basin — a hum, the sound of the plant running.

At the spring-line, a truce holds, and it is not a mystical truce but an engineered one: the mud is too soft to lunge from, the crowd too close, the water too small. Hundreds of bodies rank themselves in the steam, shoulder to shoulder, the predators drinking a deference-length downwind, everyone in roughly the same order as the night before and the night before that. Some of the hunters here have eyes not worth the name; they read the water's fields directly, every muscle of every body a faint current, and they can follow a heartbeat through a foot of opaque warm mud. That is the other sense in which the basin is electrified — threaded, everywhere, with the small fields of packed life.

If you needed one word for the place you would choose *works*. It is less a metaphor than the nearest available word. The vats are its furnace. The seep-fields are its boiler, breathing vapor day and night across warm mud stained rust-orange where older fields burned out — the only red in this green-black world, oxide in long slow lines, like the works' history written in stopped clocks. The emergent trees and the termite chimneys are its stacks. The cool stones and the undersides of great leaves are its condensers, where the fog gives itself up a drop at a time. The dry roads are its conveyors, always carrying something somewhere. The roots are its pumps; soil is its product; green is its next batch. Water here is kept on books. Every drop is counted, stored, cisterned, and eventually spent.

And nothing was ever built. That is the deepest strangeness of it, stranger than the steam or the flames: three small blue pilot-fires burning at gas vents for as long as anyone could have remembered, moths circling them to the char of a spent-wing apron at their feet — and no artifact anywhere. No cut stem, no stone set on stone, no made thing. Only silk, mud, jaw, and time. The works has no maker. Nobody clocked in, and nobody will clock out.

The laws the basin keeps are few and old. Nothing dries. Every body is a cistern, and to lose your water is the only emergency there is. Silence is mutual, and the ground does the talking. At the water, nobody hunts. Change is generational: a fallen crown takes a lifetime of trees to close, the spring-line wanders a body-length in living memory, the shallow rim — a wall only the way a bowl has a wall — has not moved in an age. Every gradient here is weak. The world tilts by degrees.

The basin breathes about once a day, a slow tidal exchange, warm air rising through the stacks with the fog drawn through the trunks behind it, the whole thing turning over with the sound of a single steady drip doing its arithmetic.

The true rain is coming. It always is, and it is never in a hurry, and nothing down here hurries to meet it — because the works runs on drip and patience, and the sky owes it a rain, and the basin has never once been in arrears.
