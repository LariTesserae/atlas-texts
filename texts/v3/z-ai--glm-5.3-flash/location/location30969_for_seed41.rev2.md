You come into it on your elbows, through a gap under a root buttress on the shaded flank of a hill, and the first thing that meets you is warmth — not the dry warmth of sun-baked stone but a greenhouse warmth, wet and green-smelling, like breathing inside a loaf of bread that has just been baked and planted. The air has weight. Within a minute your skin begins to fog. Within five you have stopped noticing.

The room is small. Forty paces long, perhaps, and half that across, with a ceiling that rises from shoulder-height at the walls to the height of three men at one corner and nowhere else. You could shout in it and the shout would die within ten paces; sound does not survive here. It is the quietest hot place there is. What noise exists is small and timed: a drop of water choosing to leave the ceiling and falling a long way to strike a leaf with a sound like a tapped fingernail; another, later, striking stone; a frog somewhere in the wall giving a single low note, and another frog, in another alcove, answering it after a pause so long that the exchange feels less like conversation than like two clocks agreeing.

The rock itself is old in a settled way. Nothing here is still being carved. The walls wear scallops and flutes cut in some wetter age when the water table stood higher, now polished by millennia of condensation into a skin like the inside of a shell. Mineral veins run through in rust and jade. The chamber has finished becoming what it is; the work now is maintenance, not construction, and everything alive inside has arranged itself around that fact.

Above, near the tall corner, the ceiling opens into a keyhole-shaped crack — a seam too narrow for you, wide enough for birds and rain. Through it comes everything the room receives from the sky: a hush of wind through leaves you can hear but not see, a filtered suggestion of daylight, and, when it rains on the hill, a brief patter-shower that is the loudest event this room ever knows.

The light is worth waiting for. Midmorning, the crack admits a single ribbon of pale green-gold sun, hand's-width wide, filtered twice — once by the crown of the great tree that stands over the crack, once by the seam itself — and this ribbon walks. Over some four hours it crosses the floor from the warm wall toward the basin, a slow grazing animal made of light, and the room's life arranges around it. Ferns that stay furled all day in the gloom unfurl as the blade reaches them. Moths that have hung motionless in the dark since dawn drop into the beam and graze it as it moves, herding along its edges, feeding not on the light exactly but on what the light wakes: midges, warmed nectar, the softening tips of fungi. Where the beam passes, the floor is briefly the richest ground in the chamber, and every creature holds a lease on its minute of it. When the ribbon narrows to a thread, then a coin, then goes out, the moths simply rise, drift back to their roosts, and hang there waiting for tomorrow.

Because the room is not entirely dark. Along the eastern wall, where the rock is faintly warm to the touch — a deep slow seam of heat in the hill's shoulder, warm as a stone left out all afternoon — pale threads of fungus grow in luminous rivulets, marking the warmth the way lichen marks north. They glow brightest where the steam collects, and at night, with the beam gone, they are enough light to walk by: a knee-high fog of warmth with faint blue-green coastlines in it.

The water makes its own weather here. The warm air rises along the hot wall, cools at the ceiling, and condenses — every surface sweats. Beads form and gather and run in threads down the roots and fern-fronds, and the whole room drinks the sky twice: once as rain through the keyhole, once as breath returned as condensation. Down the cool wall the trickle-lines have worn a groove in the stone, and the groove leads — deliberately, it seems — to a shallow basin hollowed from a low shelf of rock, polished smooth as a thumbnail, always holding a lens of clear water. The groove might be waterwork. It might be handwork. There is no one to say. Near the basin stands a stack of three flat stones, and in a great root-buttress there is a hollow worn to a shape that would fit a seated person suspiciously well, and a row of shallow notches climbs the wall toward the keyhole like unused steps. Someone was here once, and used nothing heavier than patience, and left, and the room has absorbed the remains without ceremony: frogs sit in the seated hollow, birds drink from the basin, a spider has strung its web across the stacked stones.

The growth is layered to the point of touching. From the crack descends a braid of tree roots — the umbilical — splitting at its ends into feathery drinking-hairs that comb the mist, beaded with condensation. Below them, a mid-story of ferns and dark shrub-fungi; below that, lawns of soft fungus flooring the warm loam; bracket fungi shelved along the root-buttresses; moss on every wall, changing color as it swells and dulls with the moisture cycle, so the room's walls turn moodier and brighter in slow alternation through the day. Nothing in this chamber is more than a hand's breadth from something alive.

And the fauna is everywhere, and nearly silent. Palm-sized beetles in loam-colored armor work the fungal lawns in slow processions, clicking softly when they collide. Snails with glassy shells graze the condensation off the walls, leaving glistening tracks that fade behind them. Midges rise in columns that part around your breath. Small brown birds — swallow-like, mud-nesters — slip in through the keyhole and build bracket nests on the high walls, and are oddly silent indoors, humming only with wingbeats. The predators here are patient ones: a fishing spider that hunts the trickle-lines; a mantis-nymph shape swaying on a fungal stalk; a slow, beautiful snake coiled in the warm notch above the hot wall that takes a beetle every few days and seems embarrassed by nothing. In a shallow depression in the loam, the floor serves as a shared crèche — pale eggs of five different creatures packed together in the warmth like goods at a market, incubated by the hill itself, each mother returning to turn her own.

The loam is the room's heart as much as the warm wall is: black, sweet, composting everything twice — once by an animal, once by the soil — and generating its own gentle heat, so the floor is never cold anywhere.

When rain comes above, the room receives it as an event. Water funnels down the keyhole seam in ropes, and for ten minutes the chamber patters — not a downpour, a patter — and everything responds at once: snails pour out, birds bathe in the basin, the frog clocks fall silent, mist rises off the warm wall in visible ribbons. Then it stops, and the room spends the rest of the day sweating it back out. In the season when the great tree drops its leaves, a drift of amber comes down the crack for a few days — a slow snowfall of food — and the whole food web surges, beetles hauling leaf-fragments like furniture, fungus blooming along every route.

Then night, and the other shift: dark-flying moths, glow-threads brightening, the frog notes spacing themselves further apart, the convection loop slowing until the air barely moves. If you sit still through all of it — and the room quietly insists that you do, since every sudden motion triggers a wave of silence that takes an hour to heal — you begin to feel the place as it is: a single warm animal, small and old and balanced, breathing once a day, eating itself and re-growing, keeping a needle of sun the way a miser keeps a coin. Nothing here is colonizing, and nothing is dying out. The chamber reached its own state long before you crawled in, and it will hold that state — its damp, its hush, its moving ribbon of light — long after your warmth stops registering as unusual.
