# The Interim Leaf

First, the age of the place: it is eleven thousand years old and four days old, depending which quarter you ask. It was printed, not grown. The printers still run. Keep that in mind and most of what follows will make a kind of sense.

You arrive at the margin. The margin is serrated — five teeth of green standing out over nothing, each tooth a mountain that was drafted and never committed, each one begun in a slightly different hand. Past the teeth there is no darkness, exactly. Darkness is *a thing*, and out there is the absence of any decision to have things. The tenders call it the unpaged and have a habit of not looking at it for long. Entry is by the Undertaker's Door, a tear in the underside long ago patched with chitin and apology, or by Stoma Nine when the schedule opens it.

Inside, the air changes first. Cool — always cool, the kind of cool that lives in a cellar — and thick with a smell you will spend the rest of your life trying to name: cut green, warm iron, and underneath it something faintly caramel, which is the Sugar Works. Then you notice what the air is doing. It is holding the place up.

Because this is a hollow with almost nothing in it, and the nothing is architectural. Ninety-nine parts air to one part everything else, and the everything else is green and alive. Overhead vaults the palisade — a ceiling of packed vertical columns, deep green, the only sky this world has, lit faintly by its own slow chemistry. Below hangs the spongy reach: chambers without stone walls, walls of membrane instead, translucent as blown glass, wet on one side and dry on the other, stacked and branched and folded so that the surface of a country is pleated into the volume of a chapel. You could fall for a long time here and touch nothing. The moth-kites cross the great spans in complete silence, wings like slow sails, and from certain galleries you watch them for an hour without hearing anything at all.

The city is strung along the midrib and the fourth-order branches, and it is not a dense city. It is a lace, a net, a sentence with generous space between its words. Galleries bored by the first leaf-miners and widened by their descendants, ribbed with arches of cured chitin; cisterns of dew slung at every junction; doors grown rather than hung, each one remembering the hand that opens it. Districts are named by branch order — Second Branch, Far Fork, the Under-Vein — and the population is small enough that everyone's lamp is a rumor to everyone else. The high road rises a handspan over the low road. At this scale that is topography; maps of it are guarded, because a handspan decides where the dew goes, and the dew is the river.

The works sit where the veins gather at the collar, and they are the warmest thing in the world. The Pump Hall holds four pistons that move the way a sleeping heart moves — slowly, enormously, twice a beat to the minute — and the tenders serve them the way you serve something you found rather than built. No one prints engines. Everything else in this world is plant and can be reprinted; the engines cannot. They came with the starter kit, or they were here first, and there are two religions about it and both of them keep the oil topped up. Off the Pump Hall runs the Sugar Works, where surplus sap is refined under mica screens that hold the ember-glow in, and the Mendery, where dark looms print new membrane by lamplight, and the presses that stamp provisional stone — cellulose pressed to the density of rock, still warm when it comes out, edges sharp as the day it was coined. Nothing here has had time to weather. Erosion is young in this place. It was invented recently, and it is practicing on the old drafts.

The world drinks once a day. At the evening breath, every stoma on the underside opens on schedule, and cold outside air pours in through the doors in ropes of fog, tasting of somewhere else, carrying stray spores that the tenders inspect like mail. The fog condenses on the cool membranes and the whole ceiling begins, quietly, to weep. Gutters of vein gather it. Then the day's river makes its journey: a single drop, wide as a fingernail, traveling the full length of the midrib from collar to tip, assembling as it goes, growing, gathering sister-drops, arriving at the tip-pond by midnight a proper rivulet. The pond remembers being an ocean — some errand of a previous draft — and it sighs with small tides no wind explains. On the reversed stretches of the text, where a section was printed mirror-wise and never corrected, it rains upward. The drops leave the gutters and climb back into the ceiling, and the tenders have stopped writing this down.

The tenants are many, and all of them are damp. Dew-beetles roll their beads down the gutter-veins in solemn relays. The processionaries mow the membrane in crop rows — the grazing is necessary, the green grows greedy and would fill the air if allowed, so the herds are rotated like weather. Gall-wasps raise their lantern-galls along the shady spans, each one a dim amber organ of slow digestion, and these are the streetlights, such as they are, and light discipline is strict: this is a dark world and its people are creatures of the dark world, with big wet eyes and small opinions about lamps. In the veins themselves live the glassworms, transparent, visibly digesting, and children press their faces to the vein-wall to watch them work. There is prey and there is predator. The predator lives in the far hollows where the membranes give up and the spans go on forever, and it is called the Unspecified, and it hunts by subtraction: it removes sound from around itself, and you know it is near because the world goes quieter than quiet, your own pulse suddenly the loudest thing. The city's rule of silence is not piety. It is strategy.

The rules of the world are posted on boards at the branch junctions, and they are revised. NOTICE: the staircase in Fourth Branch now descends both ways; mind the middle step, which is sincere. NOTICE: the hour between the second and third pump-beat occurred twice last night; schedules adjusted; no action required. Where transcription is current, everything holds — doors stay doors, bread stays bread, names stick to things. Where the printers lag, the world softens. Go out near the margins, past the last maintained gallery, and your outline starts to go generous, and if you linger you come back summarized — briefer, less particular, your childhood now a single adjective. The Mendery can restore some of it. Not all.

And above, when the breath opens and the dark outside leans in, there is one gray smear far off in the unpaged, and the tenders call it the placeholder. A star under construction. The sun is scheduled; no date has been announced. Until it is lit, the engines keep the sugar flowing, the looms keep the world printed, and the whole green interim hangs in its own cool damp, listening to itself: the double-throb from the Pump Hall, the tick of new cells being set like type, the click of dew finding the gutter, the drop beginning its long walk down the midrib, and over all of it — quiet. The quiet of a place that is not finished, working very hard, at a very patient pace, on becoming one.
