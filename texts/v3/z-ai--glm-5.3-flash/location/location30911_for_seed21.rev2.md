The world below is not a world. It is a light: a floor of hazed gold heat, far down, too far down to hold a single detail you could name — continents of vapor, storm-columns leaning like dark towers where the heat stands thickest. And above, a sky of such thin, exact blue that it looks younger than air has any right to be. Between the two is this: a shelf of pale stone, thirty paces across at its widest, that some enormous patience has grown out of the top of everything.

You could cross it in forty steps. You could throw a pebble past any edge — though you would not. The edges are the sharpest things here.

The stone itself is not stone. It is pale as unglazed porcelain, faintly translucent where it thins, veined with a dark mineral glass in branching lines like river deltas drawn by a very careful hand. It has never been broken. There is not a chip, a scratch, a worn place on any of it. It came here whole, or grew whole, and either way it is warm — body-warm, the warmth of a sleeping animal — except along certain seams, where it crosses into something closer to an oven's breath. The heat does not come from the sun. The sun here is only a witness. Somewhere beneath your feet a process runs that has no name in any language you know, and these are its exhausts: warmth, water, and silence.

The water is everywhere and it is all doing something. Hundreds of seep-lines show in the walls, hairline fractures that do not fracture anything — they weep. Beads gather along a seam, swell, and drop. Thicker seams run constantly, threads of water climbing *up* the stone to gather in channels cut along the underside of the overhangs — water rising without hurry, like something that has been told. The air is so charged with it that sweat is useless; the atmosphere is already full, a wet cloth pressed to the face, and every surface you touch comes away damp. Hairline grooves cross the floor dead straight — straighter than a stretched wire, with none of the wobble a tool leaves, cuts that read as decisions rather than work — and the grooves gather the weeping and carry it: some to the rim, where it spills over the edge in a veil so thin it makes a waterfall with no voice, a falling sheet you can watch for an hour without hearing; some to the pool.

The pool sits near the terrace's center: a round cut basin, floor like dark glass, water blood-warm, level to the width of a hair. Drops land in it; rings spread; rings cross each other like slow charts; the pool records nothing. Stand at its edge and watch yourself, and you will notice — small, but undeniable — that your reflection finishes moving a half-breath after you have stopped. You learn to hold still around it.

The green comes up in lines, because the water comes up in lines. But the lines are few and far between, and so each plant is alone and enormous in its allotment, lush the way a single well-fed thing is lush, not the way a crowd is. A fern has made itself a fountain of green sparks, every pinna beaded so heavily it bows. A succulent grows leaves like little windows, lenses set in flesh, focusing the day into its own core. A vine runs up the wall drinking from the seam itself, and its flowers — lanterns of white, half-transparent — gather dew on their own petals and drink it back. And in one corner stands a tree, knee-high and already thick-trunked, the oldest citizen here: a hundred years of intention packed into a meter of height, growing fat on vapor and light. All of it is oily-green, wet-looking, the green of things that have never once known drought. Between the plants: bare warm stone, wide spans of nothing, dew-light. It is as sparse as a chapel. Nothing here crowds. Everything here has room.

Watch for a day and you will be granted one animal. A wader: stilt legs, a heron's patience, feathers like wet slate, which comes to drink at the third seam in the long light and leaves without hurry. It never once looks at you, and you come to understand this is not shyness. It is a rule. Nothing here acknowledges being watched — not the wader, not the motes, not the seams. The motes: five or six of them, gnat to wasp in scale, moving between the weep-lines with intention. They lift droplets. They press at the fern's roots. They attend a place on the wall where the surface once almost, almost chipped. Insects, or custodians. You cannot decide, and they behave identically to both, and they do not decide either.

The smaller marks of making are few. A bench — a single cantilevered seat of the same pale substance, its edges meeting in angles your fingertip cannot find, and completely unworn: never sat in, or sat in always and always cleaned. A rail: a wire-thin cable that runs off the edge and down into the haze, warm to the touch, humming a note below hearing that you feel in your sternum and in your back teeth, its far ends lost to distance. A node: fist-sized, seated flush in a socket, cool and silent and doing nothing at all — except that the water near it refuses to touch it; beads stand a hair's width off its surface, respectful. You feel it should wake. You are glad it doesn't. And a stair: a dozen steps of perfect proportion descending off the terrace into the mist, ending in mid-air, as if their maker were called away between intentions.

The place keeps its own weather, small and punctual. Dawn writes dew on every face, every leaf, every seam. By mid-morning the sun has drunk it off the stone and turned to drinking you. At noon the seams exhale, and mist lifts off the terrace in a slow column that is visible all the way to the haze-floor below. By late afternoon the air is over-full, and in the first cool hour it drizzles — a private, cloudless rain, the place breathing itself back onto itself. By full dark the drips resume their count. Below, at the rim, storms work in their slow columns all day; their lightning arrives as light alone, and the thunder comes minutes later, already reduced to a rumor, already domesticated. Above, the sky never clouds. The haze stops a hand-span past the edge as if at a door.

And it is all new. No rain has had time to round anything. The edges are knife-cut. The first stalactites at the weep-lines are a fringe of white beads — a week of work, at most. The mineral trails on the walls, orange and blue-black, are the oldest writing here, and they are weeks old. Measure the terrace and you will find it wider than you remember: a finger-width a season. Some nights — one in ten, near dusk — a single tick sounds from deep in the stone, like a floorboard in an empty house, like a knuckle. Growth, arriving one knuckle at a time. And the drips: each seam drips at its own interval, and if you count them together the intervals phase-lock into a slow pattern that is almost language — a clock that is also spelling something, patiently, in a letter you can hear but cannot read.

You learn the rules the way you learn a house at night, by touching things.

Water climbs where the seams tell it to; here, gravity is a suggestion that certain liquids have permission to decline. Nothing echoes — shout, and the shout dies at your lips, because the stone takes sound the way sand takes rain; your own heartbeat becomes the loudest thing for miles. The floor grows; every sharp edge you see will one day be rounded, and none of it yet. The wind is visible and inaudible: cloud-filaments lean, haze streams past the edge in long banners, and inside the boundary there is only stillness — the place sits in the eye of its own height. And nothing acknowledges you. The wader, the motes, the seams, the node, the pool with its lagging reflection: all busy with a purpose that does not include being seen.

Leave footprints in the beaded water, and the place drinks them within the hour. Stand in the drizzle at dusk, and it falls around you for the terrace's own reasons, none of them yours. Then the tick, when it comes, deep in the warm stone under your feet, sounds almost pleased.

It is a first day that has lasted a very long time. The green is staking its claims, the water is climbing its permission, the silence holds the whole shelf like a hand around a coal. You were here. The stone will never know it, and the water keeps rising anyway.
