**Fellstrand**

You come to it across the flats, because there is no other way now. The old approach was by water; the water is four hundred paces farther out each lifetime, and the slipway stands high and dry above a mile of ground it once served. So the visitor walks. The ground underfoot is young in a way you can feel through your soles: every pebble is sharp-edged, every stone freshly broken, nothing rounded anywhere on this shore. There has not been time. The world here has not finished being made.

The flats are called the Renounced — ground the sea has let go. It does not feel renounced. It feels abandoned mid-sentence. Water sits in it everywhere: in seep-lines that wind through the mud like veins, in cold pools ringed with white crusts of dried brine, in shallow creeks that change their course every drizzle season so that no map of them outlives a year. Put a hand flat on the ground away from any visible water and you will feel it — a slow darkening and paling of the seep-threads, twice a day, the tide still pulling at the land's wet interior long after the sea itself has retreated out of sight. The people call this the ground breathing. You can hear it too, at the quietest hours: a long subterranean sigh, like a house settling, coming from everywhere at once.

There is almost nothing growing. A green film of algae glosses the wettest mud. Moss crusts the old stones. Sedges crowd the seep-lines in thin tufts, and at the old wrack-line a low succulent spreads in patches, fattened on salt. In high summer one small white flower opens out there, and the children go out to pick it, because there is so little else that blooms. The only trees on this shore are dead ones: a drowned forest, black stumps standing in the lower peat, older than anyone's story. When the sea has let go of a stump for a full season, the town may cut it — bog-oak, dense as horn
