Begin on the rim, where the world runs dry.

The plain above the Hollow is a cold steppe that goes to the horizon without an event — wind-bent grass, larks, hares, old stones rounded by ten thousand winters. You could walk it all day and the only change would be the wind's mood. Then the ground simply opens, and there below you, laid out like something spilled from the world above, is the sink.

It is nine kilometers long and perhaps four across, a great collapse in the land, floored more than a hundred meters down. And it is young. The scarps that ring it are still raw: unweathered cliff faces the color of wet slate and rust, sharp-edged, unsoftened, talus slopes of fallen blocks still in the positions where they landed when the ground let go. Nothing up here has had time to round itself. The grass grows right up to the breakline and stops, as if wary.

And then, everything changes at the edge.

**The water**

A river used to cross this plain. When the ground fell, the river fell with it — and now it enters the sink through a broken canyon on the western wall, plunging down in cold white steps, and spreads across the floor as a slow braided marsh. This is why everything below is wet. The water table here is not *near* the surface; it *is* the surface. The floor of the Hollow is a sponge the size of a town: give with every step, water standing between every hummock, ponds stitched everywhere like scales, the whole ground a thick dark turf that has never once, in three hundred years, dried.

Along the ring of scarps, the new rock weeps. Hundreds of springs stipple the walls where the broken strata bleed groundwater, each spring a bright bead trickling down the fresh stone, and each spring drags a thread of green with it. The cliffs are being colonized from above — curtains of moss and hanging sedge descending the raw rock year by year, so the walls are part geology, part garden, greening downward like something being knitted.

The braided river gathers itself toward the center of the sink, where it pools into the Hollow's one deep lake — cold, clear, and young. At its margins stands the drowned forest: the first wood, the trees that were standing here when the ground went down, now dead three centuries and standing still, because the water is so cold and so clean that nothing rots. Their bark is intact. Underwater, freshwater sponges have taken them over in pale green velvet, so the snags wear fur. Rafts are moored among them. Herons nest in their crowns.

At the far end, the river is already leaving. It has cut itself a young gorge through the eastern rim and is going down to whatever low country lies beyond. The sink is a temporary country. Everyone in it knows the water is departing, slowly, and the whole culture is, in one way or another, an argument with that fact.

**The green**

Cold country, and yet lush — because nothing here is ever limited by water, only by light and patience. The floor is a fen-forest: willows first, dwarfed and dense, then taller wet-wood trees leaning into each other, every branch furred with moss to the diameter of your wrist. Beneath the canopy, fern and liverwort. Beneath that, standing water. Between those layers, everything that can grow sideways grows sideways: the mats. Great floating sheets of peat and living root, a meter thick, drifting on their own buoyancy, trembling like drumheads wrapped in wool when you walk them. Whole districts of the city stand on mats.

The green descends the walls, fills the sumps, climbs the buildings, rides on the backs of the beetles. Moss is not a detail here. It is a building material, a crop, a fuel-less fuel, a soil, a mattress, and a memory — the peat of the north bogs is layered with the dead, preserved by the cold spring water, folded into the mat with a stone on top. The moss keeps them the way it keeps everything: slowly, and well.

**The city**

There has never been a cutting edge in the Hollow. No blade, no fire — the people here are pre-tool, and they build the way a gardener builds, which is to say: they persuade.

The city is grown. Houses are willows — four saplings bent overhead and knotted into a hoop, then woven and filled with peat-moss until the walls are a meter thick of living green, warm-feeling, silently shedding rain. Roofs are sedge on root lattices, plucked back by hand each year like a hedge. Floors are laid brush that rooted, laced over the water so the house sits on the sponge without sinking. Between the houses run causeways of rooted brush and packed peat; where a channel blocks the way, there are root-bridges — the aerial roots of trees on opposite banks trained toward each other across the water, grafted at mid-span, Load-bearing after a generation. A finished bridge is an heirloom. Nobody owns wood in the Hollow. You own *care* — a bridge belongs to the family that has fed it, and the city is fed, not built: dressed moss, fish remains, silt carried by the basketful and pressed into the weave. The buildings eat. The infrastructure eats.

The districts stack by elevation and buoyancy. The Terraces on the south-facing wall, where the talus has been planted into stepped fields and the best light falls.
