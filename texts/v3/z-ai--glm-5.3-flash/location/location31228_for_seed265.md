# The Dripfields

Night here is not an event; it is a condition. There has never been light in living memory — the locals (if you can call anything that lives here a local) speak of *the dark* the way other places speak of the sky. And yet the dark is not empty. It is warm, wet, and crowded.

## Terrain

The land is low — barely above the water table, which is to say, barely above the water. The ground is a sponge of black peat and fresh-cut mud, only recently risen from somewhere below. You can feel the newness of it underfoot: the terrain has the raw, unfinished quality of something still being extruded. Ridges of clay stand in sharp, unweathered fins where the land heaved itself upward a season ago. Nothing has been smoothed yet. Edges are crisp. Mudcracks are fresh. The whole valley floor reads like a page still warm from the press.

Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. It weeps from the clay banks in slow beads, gathers in shallow sheets that mirror nothing (there is nothing to mirror), and stands in the hollows at body temperature — blood-warm, faintly mineral, never cold. The air itself is saturated; moisture condenses on your skin, on the machinery, on the edges of every surface, so that the entire region drips at a slow, even tempo. That is the ambient sound: *tik... tik... tik...* of condensate falling, spaced too irregularly to be rhythm, too regular to be silence.

## The Works

Someone built here. Scattered across the marsh at wide intervals stand structures — not ruins exactly, more like things abandoned mid-thought. Pump housings the size of barns, half-sunk and still ticking. A headframe rising from a flooded shaft. Iron pipework that runs along the ground for a kilometer and then simply stops, open-ended, weeping warm water from its cut throat.

And some of it still runs. Engines down here idle in the deep dark on some fuel nobody tends anymore, breathing slow, working pumps that lift warm water a meter and spill it back. Nobody knows for whom. The machinery doesn't seem to serve the place — the place seems to tolerate the machinery, the way a body tolerates a splinter. In the darkness you hear them before you see them: a low industrial purr somewhere out in the flats, rhythmic and patient, harmonizing accidentally with the drip.

## Life

The fauna is the overwhelming fact of the region. Because nothing here has eyes — why would it, in permanent dark? — everything else got louder, stranger, and more abundant. The flats seethe. Amphibian-things the size of dogs cruise the shallow sheets, hunting by pressure-sense, trailing ripples you feel through your boots before you hear them. Insects by the trillion rise from the warm water in slow columns, and bats-or-bat-things wheel through the condensation fog above them in soft, fur-silent spirals. Worm-masses churn the fresh mud, and you can trace their progress by the collapse of clay fins hours later. Everything is touching distance from everything else. The density of life is claustrophobic for a place so open — you could walk a straight line for a day and never be more than arm's reach from something warm and moving.

None of it fears you. Nothing here has evolved fear. Nothing here has needed it.

## The Wrongness

Here is what to understand about the Dripfields: it is *slow*, and that slowness is the strangest thing about it. Change happens, but at the pace of geology wearing a worker's uniform. The engines pulse over hours. The clay fins erode over weeks. The creatures migrate in patterns measurable in seasons. And the land itself is still rising — you can hear it, some nights, a subsonic groan beneath everything, the sound of a valley being born over centuries. Stand still long enough and you *become* slow too; your pulse drops to match the drip, your thoughts space out like the rain.

But look closely at any one thing and reality frays. The condensation falls upward sometimes, in one narrow acre where the drops rise from the peat to the sky. The engines' rhythm, listened to long enough, resolves into something like language — or like the memory of language. Distances disagree. Walk a measured kilometer out and a measured kilometer back and you arrive somewhere slightly *else*: same pump, same drip, but the mudcracks have rearranged, and the bat-things wheel the other way.

There is a theory, scratched in charcoal on the side of one pump housing by a hand that presumably belonged to someone: **THE LAND IS WET BECAUSE IT IS NEW. IT IS DARK BECAUSE IT HAS NOT DECIDED WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE YET. DO NOT HELP IT DECIDE.**

Below that, in a different hand: *too late.*
