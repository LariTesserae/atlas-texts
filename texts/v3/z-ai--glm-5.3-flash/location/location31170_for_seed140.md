The Kept Dark

The first thing to understand is that the darkness here is not an event. It is not nightfall, not weather, not the passing shadow of the mountain. It is the climate. It has a temperature and a humidity and a patience, and it was here before anything that lives in it, and it will outlast them all.

You come to it from above. The mountain is old past guessing — a rounded gravel dome two thousand meters up, its slopes worn to knuckles, its crest bare even of lichen, the wind up there sounding thin and dry after the warm air below. Nothing lives on the summit. The only opening is a crack in the bare rock, a shoulder-width slot, and below it a shaft with iron rungs somebody forged and forgot. You descend maybe thirty meters, and partway down the air changes. It turns warm. It turns wet. It smells of wet iron and something yeast-sweet, something almost green. You smell the garden before you ever reach it.

The chamber itself is small. That is the second thing to understand, and it takes a while to believe. Forty paces across, perhaps. A domed ceiling you can strike with a thrown pebble. Black stone underfoot, fine-grained, faintly warm, worn smooth. Your eyes give up within a minute — the eye, given nothing, reports infinity — and so the room feels vast, an interior night, a void with a floor. But your hands learn its edges. You can cross it before your tea cools. It is a room. It has boundaries, and everything in it is countable. It only behaves like a sky.

What fills the darkness — what stands in for light — is the dripping. Where groundwater weeps through the mountain's shell, the walls sweat, and the sweat gathers and falls. There are forty or so of these seeps, each with its own note, tuned by what the drop lands in: a deep pool gives a round *bloom*, a stone shelf a hard *tick*, a bed of moss a swallowed *tup*. Forty small voices, spaced through the dark like stars, and the animals here navigate by them the way sailors once navigated by constellations. There is no silence in this room, ever. The drips see to that. The sound level is high and constant and strangely gentle — a rain that never ends, falling one drop at a time.

Around each seep grows an island of life, and this is where the room is lush. Pale vegetation, every shade of white and cream and blush — nothing here has ever needed pigment for light. Velvet moss that tastes of iron if you touch your fingers to your tongue. Tiered shelves of soft pale fungus, stacked like folded linen. Tassels hanging from the ceiling where the water runs, threads fine as hair, beaded with condensation that catches nothing and glows nowhere. Thin ferns with black veins, warm to the touch, warmer than the stone. Each island is dense, layered, thick enough to hide a fist — and each island is small. Walk ten paces from any garden and you are on bare warm stone, in the empty black, with only the drips for company. The room is mostly nothing. Its contents are scattered islands in a dark sea, and the space between them is as real a feature as the gardens themselves.

The fauna are everywhere the gardens are, and they are unafraid, because nothing here has ever seen anything, and nothing has ever been seen. Pale blind grazers the size of a hand move over the moss like soft-shelled slugs, humming as they eat, a purr you feel through your boots before you hear it. Flutterers — moths, or the memory of moths — cross the open dark in loud zigzags, each one shrieking and steering by the echo, so that the air is stitched all night with little cries that bend audibly around your body. In the one deep pool live white swimmers that have no eyes at all, only dimples where eyes would go, and they find your footsteps in the water by vibration and come to them, trusting, curious. And once, from the pool's far edge, you will hear the large one: a single held tone, low and clean, sustained so long you mistake it for the room itself. When it stops, the silence it leaves has a shape. That is how the animals here see. Every call is a torch made of sound, and the chamber is bright with them.

Someone has been here. The signs are scattered, old, and half-erased. The rungs, for one. A platform of fitted dark stone near the largest seep, worn glassy, purpose unknown. Small cairns of stacked pebbles stand beside many of the drips — one, two, three stones high — a map written for hands instead of eyes, naming the notes. On the north wall, rows of notches, thousands of them, cut at intervals far too regular for haste and far too sparse for urgency: one mark, it seems, per generation. Whoever kept this place kept it on a geological clock.

And there are the three pylons. Thin, taller than a person, standing in the emptiest quarter of the room, made of something that is neither stone nor metal — warm like skin, and humming. Each holds one low note, the three a slow-shifting chord, and they are the room's floor of sound, felt in the teeth more than heard. They are also why the darkness is total. Bring a flame within ten paces and it dims, goes blue, dies. Bring an electric lamp and its battery empties in minutes. The pylons drink light the way the seeps drink rain. The dark here is not an absence; it is a maintained condition, tended by machines that were left on before anyone alive was born and have not yet finished their work. Nearby stands a shallow carved basin, always full of cold sweet water, though the room is warm and no pipe feeds it. It has never been seen filling. It is only ever full. Whatever condenses it does so invisibly, patiently, out of the warm air itself.

Everything in this place runs slow. The mountain breathes on a tide measured in days — the drips' constellation brightens and fades as the aquifer swells and slackens, some voices sinking as others rise. The gardens grow a finger's width in a human lifetime. The echoes are slow even to die: the stone holds sound the way warm stone holds heat, and a shout thins into the walls over hours, so that on very still nights you sometimes hear something faint and specific — a struck stone, a word — that you did not make and cannot have heard anyone make, because it was made long ago and the room has been letting it go ever since. Even the geology is finished. No new cracks, no collapses, no passages forming. The mountain eroded itself to its final shape ages ago and this chamber is what settled at its heart, stable as a held chord. It will keep until the planet does.

So the room's verdict, if it has one, is this: it does not mind you. It has plants that need no light, animals that need no sight, machines that need no supervision, and a darkness it has kept like a garden for longer than there have been names. It will lend you its forty small stars. It will show you the shape of a world with the light left out and nothing missing — just rearranged, into touch and hum and drip, into slow.

The only trouble is leaving. On the surface afterward, the world seems wrong in both directions at once: too bright to think, and too silent to feel inhabited. You will catch yourself waiting for the drips to start, for the room's low chord to resume beneath the wind. It does not. That music stays where it was made, in the small warm dark, dripping on.
