# Terracel

You come up to it through a pass, and the pass decides when you see it. The ridge has been holding its shape so long that the wind has polished the exposed faces of the rock to a dull sheen, like the backs of old spoons, and where the trail crests between two leaning boulders the whole valley opens at once — small, cupped, complete. A cirque of pale stone, maybe a mile across at its widest, walled on three sides by slopes that rise in long regular steps, as if the mountain had once tried stairs and then lost interest. The fourth side is a lip of scree, low enough to see over to more country beyond: ridgeline after ridgeline, going bluer with distance, none of them higher than this one. You understand immediately why the town is here. Everything below is visible. Everything above is close. There is nowhere else to put a town, and no reason to put one anywhere else.

The people call it Terracel. There are perhaps sixty roofs.

That number matters. Everything here comes in exact amounts.

---

The houses are dry-fitted stone, no mortar, no metal — the walls stand on the logic of their own weight, each block shaped by hand with hammer and chisel to interlock with its neighbors, and you can read the generations of care in them: the courses are tight and confident at the base, and near the eaves, where the builders of long ago worked faster, the joints have loosened into a kind of honest wavering. Roofs are slabs, overlapped like feathers, weighted at the ridge with smaller stones. Doors are wood, pegged, no hinges — each one lifts out and leans against the wall when the house is open, and most of them are leaning. The town is too small for streets to have names, so the paths between houses are known by their destinations, and the destinations never move.

The houses are scattered. That is the first thing your eye keeps trying to correct. A town should cluster; this one *breathes*. Each house sits in its own pocket of space — a terrace, a hollow, a shelf of rock flattened for the purpose — with thirty, forty, fifty paces of bare ground between it and the next. Stone walls thread among the gaps like slow handwriting, enclosing gardens that are mostly sky: a few rows of a bitter tuber, a straggle of thyme gone woody, moss in the shaded corners, lichen doing its centuries-long work on every north-facing surface. One tree in the whole valley, a blackthorn grown sideways by the wind, standing in the middle of the lower terrace like a resident who arrived before anyone else and was granted the best room. It does not seem to grow. It does not seem to die. It leans, and has leaned, and will lean.

The emptiness is not neglect. It is measure. The people of Terracel will tell you, if you ask, that the valley holds a certain amount of everything — a certain amount of water, a certain amount of soil, a certain amount of quiet — and that a town built tighter than its allotment would be borrowing against itself. They built to the size of their valley the way a good boot is built to a foot. Sixty roofs, and you could walk the whole town end to end in twenty minutes and feel like you had read a long, unhurried letter.

---

The dampness here is not rain, exactly, or not mostly. Clouds come down into the cirque most days — not storms, just the sky settling in for a visit — and the valley fills with a cool grey breath that bejewels everything. Dew stands on the stone roofs in beads that never quite roll. The lichen drinks. The moss stays moss-dark without ever advancing. Along the base of the northern wall a spring seeps out of a crack and runs, thin as a dropped thread, down a stone channel cut so long ago that the channel's edges have gone round and soft, into a cistern, and from the cistern by gravity and clay pipe into the town. The spring yields, by the townsmen's own patient counting, exactly enough. In the wet season the cistern fills to its rim. In the dry season it drops to a mark worn into the inner wall. It has never gone past either line. The people water their gardens with cups, not buckets, and this seems to them not austerity but accuracy.

Twice a year, it rains properly — a brief, businesslike grey afternoon where every roof does its work and every gutter (hand-split wood, sloped, pegged) sings at once, and the little channel at the bottom of the valley runs full for a few hours, brown and cheerful, and then the whole thing is over and the water has gone where it goes. Children are told to remember these days. They are the calendar.

---

The fauna you can count on one hand, and the townsmen have, in a way. There is a pair of choughs that nest in the cliffs and say nothing all winter and laugh all summer. There are lizards in the sunny walls, so still you mistake them for pebbles, and perhaps they mistake themselves for pebbles too. Once — this is a story everyone tells and no one claims to have seen — a hare came over the scree lip, sat in the middle of the lower terrace for the length of one afternoon, and left. It is spoken of the way coastal towns speak of a whale.

---

And the rule of the valley, which you feel before anyone explains it: *nothing here accumulates.*

A wall slumps on the east side, and somewhere on the west side a wall is mended. Wood wears at the door lintels, and the shavings from the carpenter's knife are exactly the amount the wood gave up. Smoke from the morning fires rises and flattens against the ceiling of cloud and stays there, a soft grey stratum, and does not thicken no matter how many hearths are lit. The people plant what they eat, eat what they plant, mend what they wear, and wear what they mend. A visitor once — there are visitors, rarely — asked an old woman what would happen if they built sixty-one roofs. She considered it genuinely, the way you'd consider a stranger's arithmetic. "We'd be sixty-one roofs' worth of people," she said. "We aren't."

You wake on your second morning to the same light — high, clean, cool, the sun clearing the eastern rim at the same hour and striking the same first rooftop, the way a lamp lights the same corner of a room. The dew is back on the stones in the same beads. The choughs say the same two notes. Somewhere below, a door lifts out of its frame and leans against a wall, and the small sound of it is the loudest thing in the valley, and the valley holds it just long enough, and lets it go.
