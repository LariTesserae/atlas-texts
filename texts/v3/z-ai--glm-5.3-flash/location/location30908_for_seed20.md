## The Louding

For a long way around it, the deep sea is only the deep sea: cold, black, patient, murmuring its one small murmur. Then comes a halo of warmth, a mile wide, like walking into the breath of some enormous sleeping animal. Then the vibration, arriving through the water and through the body at once — in the teeth first, in the sternum, in the fluid of the inner ear. Only after that, the mountain.

It is small. That is the first shock. Charts would call it a knoll. Its base is a few hundred paces across; you could swim its circumference in an hour, and from the summit — which you can reach, with care, in twenty minutes of climbing — you can see the whole of it at once, and the whole of the plain around it, and understand that this is a place with boundaries, a single feature, a room rather than a country. What it lacks in size it makes up in volume. Not the volume of stone. The volume of sound.

The noise is deafening in a way that has nothing to do with ears. Past a certain loudness, hearing gives up and the sound simply becomes the medium, the way water is the medium. It has three layers. The lowest is a bell-tone, one enormous struck note that never finishes decaying — it has perhaps been decaying since the mountain was born, and will be for centuries more. Above that, a granular hiss, the sound of water being worried apart at a microscopic level, cavitationVeins flashing and collapsing. And through both, slow as geology, the syllables.

The syllables are the thing.

No one — no diver, no machine, no instrument — has ever parsed them, because they are not sounds exactly; they are pressures with intent. Each one takes an hour or more to roll through. They arrive in the stone before they arrive in the water, and the echo of each arrives slightly before the sound it echoes, so the whole mountain seems to be answering questions no one has asked yet. Your own movements come back to you a beat late, as noise: you hear yourself swimming after you have already swum. It is disorienting for the first day. After that it begins to seem correct — as if the place were keeping a record of you and playing it back with editorial delay.

The mountain itself is shockingly young. Basalt, black and glassy, with edges like knapped flint; pillow-lava shoulders still sharp-shouldered, unslumped; slopes of scree that never settle, because the vibration shakes every loose stone into constant, glittering rearrangement. No sediment clings anywhere. Nothing has had time to soften these features, and the noise will not let time begin. The rock looks like it cooled last week. Geologically speaking, it did.

And the mountain is still rising. That is not a metaphor.

The builders — for there were builders — came long before, in some era the plain does not remember. Their settlement was small: thin-walled rooms of a grainy gray stone that matches nothing here, a low arch, a threshold now tilted at forty degrees, a stair that emerges from solid basalt and climbs six steps into bare rock, as if the mountain had risen through the middle of someone's house and simply continued the architecture in its own material. The ruin is scattered, half-swallowed, fragmentary — a wall here, a doorway there, most of it still buried. Faint marks run through it: lines of shallow-cut glyphs, each one shaped like a waveform, like a staff of music, like the drawing a sound would make of itself if asked. The lines pass under unweathered basalt and come out the other side.

The stone of the ruin is dead of sound. Inside the little rooms — and they are small rooms, human-scaled or near it, doorways you would have to duck through — there is silence. Real silence, thicker than the dark, a silence with weight, the only silence for a thousand miles. Standing in one is like standing inside the pause between syllables, except the pause is a place. The water in there does not move. The dark in there does not ripple. Visitors have reported that the silence rings backward afterward, that ordinary quiet feels like being inside the unspoken next word.

Over the carved lines, the water shivers. The sparks cluster there.

The light of this place is its noise made briefly visible. Where the sound is densest, bubbles collapse, and each collapse gives off a pinprick of blue-white light — thousands per second, dim, scattered, a starfield that maps the loudness instead of the sky. This is all the illumination there is. You see the mountain by hearing it. At the flanks of the younger chimneys there is a duller glow, coal-red, heat leaking out of rock that has not yet learned to keep its warmth to itself — because the whole mountain is warm. Warm as blood along the lower slopes, bath-warm in the lee pockets, hot enough to shimmer where the vents breathe. The halo of heat extends a mile into the cold plain in every direction, a soft country of warm water with one small loud mountain at its center.

The mountain reads.

That is the working theory, and no better one has been offered: as the peak rises, it surfaces more of the buried text, and the syllables in the sound are the mountain pronouncing what it uncovers. Each syllable takes an hour. When a word completes — and you feel the completion in your bones before you understand it — everything in earshot lurches. Scree re-sorts itself. A meter of new black rock shoulders up through the old. Sometimes a wall fragment rises with it, one more piece of the ruin pushed into the light of the sparks. Whether the marks cause the ringing or merely rhyme with it is not knowable from here. The mountain does not explain itself. It only recites, and grows.

Between the words there is a pause, and in the pause the turbulence stops. The churning water holds still for a single heartbeat — stones hang mid-slide, foam-forms hang mid-shape — and the whole place holds its breath. Then the next syllable slams through like weather.

The weather is real. Surges tear along the flanks; currents wrack the slopes; the water visibly shakes, shedding sheets of flash. Heavy syllables produce storms that batter the summit for half an hour at a time.

The summit is a small plateau, thirty paces across, warm as sun-warmed stone though no sun has ever reached it. Above it hangs the impossible thing: a surface. A waterline, upside-down — a membrane of standing waves where water lies upon water, fifty meters over the peak, stretched flat and trembling. Droplets break upward from it and hang. A wave is caught there, mid-break, curling, and has been mid-break for as long as anyone has watched; it will finish breaking only when the sentence does. When a word completes, the membrane rings visible for one flash — a perfect circle of light — and goes dark again.

Life here is sparse and strange. In the warm lee-pockets, mats of white filament grow like unkempt hair, thin as frost. A single grove of tube-worms — a dozen tubes, feather-pale — retracts at every surge and re-emerges after, patient as grass. A blind shrimp reads the standing waves the way spiders read webs, and rides them. One eel-like thing, old beyond reckoning and wholly indifferent to miracles, patrols the ruin's silence-pockets, resting where the noise cannot follow. And between syllables, in the pauses, the water tries out shapes: a half-fish, an arch of foam, a suggestion of a face — held for one breath, considered, dismissed. Not yet alive. Not anymore. The sea rehearsing.

Everything here is scattered, spaced, surrounded by emptiness — one chimney field, one ruin exposure, one grove, one membrane, and between them bare ringing rock and warm black water, because the place is small and young and still being said.

It is still being said. That is the last thing you understand before you leave, if you leave, if you can leave — kicking away from the slopes into the cold, with the tone lodged in your skeleton where it will remain for days, fading like a struck bell in a sealed room. The mountain is growing. The sentence is not finished. The bright sparks crawl over the buried lines, syllable by syllable, hour by hour, and the little loud mountain reads itself up out of the dark, one word at a time, toward a surface that is waiting for it upside-down, where the wave that never finished breaking is waiting to finish.
