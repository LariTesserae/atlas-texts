**The Weather Cell**

To reach it you would have to know it was there, and no one who walks the long hall above ever looks down. The grating in that hall's floor pretends to be a drain. Underneath it a stair of seventeen steps descends into a cold that arrives before the dark does — each step stamped with its load rating, each one cast, nothing here dug. At the bottom is a hatch, and riveted to the hatch a plaque: *CELL I — ATMOSPHERE, PROTOTYPE. COMMISSIONED. WEATHER FIRST, LIFE LAST.* The letters have begun, very faintly, to tarnish.

Inside: a room you could cross in twelve paces and span with five, arched like the inside of a lung, built of pale cast plates on an iron skeleton, every seam caulked black, every plate numbered. No stone anywhere. No soil. Nothing here was found; everything here was made, and made recently enough that the world has not yet had time to mark it. It is dim — a low amber standby glow, a scatter of small lamps, the brass and glass of a hundred gauges catching what light there is. And it is dense. Within one stride you could lay a hand on the boiler niche, a valve bank, a manometer with its red hairline trembling, the rail of the sump, a cabinet of spare nozzles in a felt-lined drawer. Everything is within reach of everything. The room is packed the way a ship's engine room is packed, the way a jeweler's bench is packed — the way a cradle is packed.

It is cold. Just above freezing in the damp phases, below during the frost ones; breath would show; the metal of the handrails bites. The only warmth is a pilot flame the size of a thumbnail, watching over a small steam plant in its niche — a single drop of orange in all that blue dark, the chamber's hearth by courtesy.

And it is damp, in the way of things that are *becoming* damp rather than being wet. Most of the time the chamber's ordinary weather is condensation: the cooled ceiling membrane sweats, every cold surface beads, the seams gather lines of standing droplets that tremble and let go one at a time. Below the longest seam runs a shallow groove cast into the floor, and into it falls the seam-rain — one drop every four seconds, all day, all night, uncounted. Once or twice a day the schedule calls actual weather: the header pipe exhales a mist, the ceiling harvests it, and for forty minutes it drizzles properly, 1.2 millimeters, stamped on an appointment-plate by the hatch. Then the pumps draw the sump down, the shallow basins in the floor hold their pools for a few hours, the pools shrink to damp, the damp to a memory of damp — pockets of water in a larger dryness, the whole water economy of the chamber no bigger than a bath and recycled to the gram.

The frost comes by appointment too. Every few days a plate on the north arc is flooded with a colder fluid, and ice feathers grow from its edge in perfect silence, cracking now and then like distant furniture. When the heat pulse comes, the feathers do not melt — they sublimate, going straight to fog, and the fog is drawn off through a whisper-blower and returned as drizzle. Damp, frost, mist, drip, pool, drain, damp: the chamber keeps its appointments. It also breathes — every eleven minutes a slow sigh through a felt-packed vent as the pressure cycles, and a barograph beside the hatch writes the pulse onto a drum of paper, hour after hour, a seismograph of weather in a room the size of a parlor.

Because there is a sky here, of a kind. A modest lamp rides an arm on a curved rail across the ceiling — the sun, which over the months swings lower and wider and reddens and dims, then reverses; the chamber has a year, and it is a made year. Faster and higher runs a disc of bright tin: the moon, crossing in under three minutes when its schedule calls, casting the only quick shadow the room has ever owned
