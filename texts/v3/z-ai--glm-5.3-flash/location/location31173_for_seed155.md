You come up to it by switchbacks cut into the last of the old slopes, and then the land simply stops arguing. The Wold is a plateau worn so long and so completely that relief has left it — no peaks, no valleys, not even hills worth the name. Whatever mountains stood here have been sanded down to a suggestion, and the sanded dust of them is somewhere in the ground below your feet, pressed under a hundred generations of growth. The horizon is a clean circle in every direction. The air is thin and cold and very bright, and the light falls flat across a surface that runs unbroken to the rim of the visible world.

That surface is the Sward. Not grass, not forest, not moss — a single continuous growth that has replaced the crust of the land entirely. Underfoot it is dense and slightly yielding, like felt laid over stone, and a hand pressed to it finds warmth, faint and animal, a compost-heat a body-length down while the wind above cuts through wool. Cut into it anywhere and the grain of the cut matches the grain a hundred miles away. It is one thing. The old observers' texts agree on this and disagree on everything else: the Sward no longer grows. It reached its final form before anyone arrived to name it, and now it only holds — though it holds actively, at the pace of millimeters per day, so that a causeway will drift a hand's width off true over a winter and have to be re-cut each spring.

It is also loud. The cold does this: as the thin sun goes down, the whole plain contracts, and the Sward creaks and groans like a ship's hull for hours. Sharp reports crack across the empty miles — the crews call it barking — and at full dark it settles into ticking, a vast slow clicking, as if something enormous were counting in its sleep.

The water here comes from the sky by way of the plant. Each night the Sward sweats, and by dawn every ridge of it carries beads of cold water that gather and run — not randomly, but down channels the Sward grew for itself long before anyone needed them: hardened living conduits that feed still basin-pools the crews call mirrors, so clear and still they look like holes cut in the ground. The works tap the channels with brass spiles, the way you'd tap a tree, because that is in effect what they are doing — the entire plateau is one tree, and its roots go down past the deepest shaft anyone has sunk. True rain comes three or four times a season. When it does, every whistle is tied down, every press is stopped, and the whole population stands out in it. It smells of resin. Nobody hurries through a rain here.

The works themselves are scattered — four, maybe five clusters visible from the rim, each a smoke-line standing perfectly straight in the cold air, forty miles from its nearest neighbor, with nothing between them but empty Sward for hours of walking. They are urban in their organization and strange in their construction, because half of each yard was not built but grown. The Sward anticipated rooms. It raised fluted towers with hollow centers that serve as chimneys and stairwells both, and wind organs sound through them at dusk. It threw vaulted halls of living timber. Older works lie inside the newer: a doorway grown shut with a wall still hinged in it, a bridge deck embedded in a buttress like a rib in a body. The crews build onto whatever the Sward leaves exposed, and their construction is layered the way a shell is layered — around an original no one remembers making.

Inside the yards, it is an industrial place, and a loud one. Great screw-presses squeeze the Sward's fiber into building plate and cordage; spinning houses draw that fiber into cable strong enough to hoist engines. Resin stills burn amber around the clock, and their steam stands in white columns in the cold. Dynamos charge racks of brass accumulators; foundries stamp and ring; a bell marks every hour and is answered by the next yard's bell, faint across the miles. The metal itself is borrowed. Nothing here is scrapped — a dead engine is carried to the returning grounds and buried, and within a season the roots bring it back up as clean ore-nodules, which the foundries smelt into new machines. The Sward recycles everything, including, eventually, its guests. The crews know this. They sign their tools.

Fauna is a matter of events. There are the chimney wrens — grey, silent, few — nesting in the hollow stacks and singing only at first light. There is the moth-fall that accompanies true rain: for an hour the whole plateau sheds pale moths off its surface in the millions, rising together, and then they are gone, and no one has found where. And once a year or so, the wanderer crosses open ground — a grazer the size of a barn, mottled like the Sward itself, moving so slowly you could outpace it at a walk, leaving a wake of smoothed and shining sward behind it. You do not approach the wanderer. Nothing says so. You simply don't.

The people are few — waxers who seal the growth back where it presses against rails and conduits, knockers who sound the ground before every drill, couriers who walk the long walks between yards. The couriers navigate by smoke, not by compass, because compasses here swing slowly toward the roots. The rules are short and all learned the same way. Knock and wait before drilling: sometimes the ground answers, and once a survey team heard their own knock returned from a depth no survey had reached, corrected, in a pattern they had not used yet. Never sleep on the open Sward: you wake settled, subtly remade, and the waxers have to be sent for. And mind the long walks — the distance between two yards is fixed, but the time it takes is not, and the crews schedule by bells and not by miles.

At the rim, the world's edges give out before the Sward does. The plateau ends in a seam of layered strata, old and finished, the last page of the region's geology — and the Sward pours over that edge and continues, green and continuous, down the outside of the world as far as the eye can take it. Ropes sent down come back frayed, and the frays are counted, like a message. Out past the ninth milestone, loads weigh less by a breath; haulers know to schedule the heavy runs there. And every few days, the Deep Note comes up through the ground — not loud so much as total, a single tone lasting a minute, during which cups ring, teeth ache, the foundry stamps fall silent, and the bells cannot be heard at all. The crews stand still until it passes. Some say it is the Sward turning over in its sleep. Some say it is the Sward counting the works, the way you'd count lice on an old animal you had decided to keep.

Night closes cold. Frost blooms along the rails, the braziers are fed, the last whistle goes, and then the plain is louder than the yards — the creak, the tick, the far bark rolling in from the empty miles where the mirrors hold the stars. It is a place at the end of its own history, worn flat, finished, holding still — except that it isn't still at all, and never was. It sweats, and shifts, and answers knocks, and keeps its small machines warm in its skin the way skin keeps a heartbeat that isn't its own.
