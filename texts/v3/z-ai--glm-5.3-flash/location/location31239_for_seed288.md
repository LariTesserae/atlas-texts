# The Underhalls of Verge

Long before you reach it, you hear it. The sound comes up through the stone itself — a vast, layered drone, like a cathedral organ sustained for centuries, rising from somewhere below and ahead. It is not machinery. There are no machines here, and there never have been. It is the sound of ten thousand throats, wingbeats, calls, and the deep resonance of hollowed rock acting as a soundboard. The city is loud. The city has always been loud.

You descend by a ramp, not stairs — erosion at this scale rounds everything, and whoever cut this passage did it so long ago that the cutting has become geology. The walls bear chisel marks only where overhangs sheltered them; everywhere else, ten thousand millennia of slow mineral drift have polished and softened the stone into flowing forms, like wax, like driftwood, like the cheeks of something very old.

This is a city beneath the earth, and it is vast — chambers opening into halls, halls into caverns the size of valleys, all of it worked once, all of it now half-reclaimed. The coolness is constant. There is no weather here, no seasons, only the one temperature the deep rock keeps: a cool, mineral stillness, somewhere between autumn cellar and spring cave. Nothing here has ever known sun.

And yet: green.

The vegetation is the first shock. Lush does not mean wet, here. The plants of the Underhalls draw no water from any sky — there is no sky — but from the stone itself, from slow mineral seeps and the deep humid breath of the rock, condensed in darkness. Great pale ferns with fronds like parchment spill from the tops of ruined colonnades. Moss grows in velvet ramps on stairways worn to curves. Fungi rise in tiered, shelf-like groves from the sides of walls, some broad as tables, all of them faintly luminous — not bright, never bright, but enough. The light of this place is dim and organic: a blue-green glow distributed across ten thousand caps and fronds, like a sky turned wrong-way-round and hung low. The old city, in its ruins, became a garden. The garden became an ecology.

The city itself is real, and was real. Walls in long regular courses. Foundations of districts. The remains of an aqueduct — for they had water once, though that is a story the stone has forgotten — arching overhead in a curve the erosion has thinned to a ribbon. Doorways without doors. A plaza, sunk and polished, where the glowing groves grow thickest. There is no glass, no metal, no artifact finer than shaped stone and cured hide. Whatever the builders had, they took it with them, or it became part of the dust.

The fauna are the noise, and the noise is everywhere. Colonies of pale, eyeless creatures roost in the upper vaults — swimmers in air, they seem, wheeling in flocks of thousands, their calls layering into the great drone. Larger grazers move through the open avenues in slow herds, moth-like, browsing the moss ramps, their low rumbles felt in the soles of the feet. Small things skitter in the ferns. Predators exist, somewhere, patient. Everything is pale, or soft-colored, or faintly glowing. Nothing hurries.

Nothing hurries at all, in fact. This is the deepest truth of the place: the slowness. Change here happens across ten-thousand-year arcs. A wall falls once an age. A grove spreads a foot a century. The flocks circle on circuits so long and stable they might be orbits. The city is not decaying so much as *settling*, relaxing into its final form the way sediment settles, the way a chord resolves. It will be a hill of stone with gardens on it, eventually, and the sound will soften from a roar to a hum, and then to almost nothing.

Almost nothing. Because as long as there are throats and wingbeats, the Underhalls will not be silent.

The dryness is the quiet miracle under it all: a lushness with no rain, no river, no well. Only stone, breath, and patience. Everything here drinks the rock.

You stand on a worn plaza's edge, in cool dim blue-green light, under a ribbon of ancient aqueduct, in the middle of a deafening gentle roar, and the place accepts you the way it accepts everything — slowly, permanently, without surprise.
