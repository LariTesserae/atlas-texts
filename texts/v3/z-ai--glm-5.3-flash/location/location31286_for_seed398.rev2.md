# The Collar

The mountain was not here within living memory. That is the first thing, and everything else follows from it.

The crust split — quietly, by all accounts, no eruption worth the name — and the Newcomer came up through it like a needle through cloth. Not violently. *Arriving.* It climbed in a single season past the weather, past the last ragged cloud floors, until its tip stood in air too thin to trouble anyone, and then it kept going. It is still going. An inch a month, the surveyors say, and they say it the way you'd mention a tide. Everyone in the town keeps their doors on slip-joints.

You reach it by climbing through the cloud decks the way you'd climb through floors of a building — grey level after grey level, each one dimmer — and then the clouds stop and the sky does not turn blue. It turns a deep bruised brown, thinning to black, and the sun shrinks to a white coin you could cover with a thumbnail. This is why the light is so poor: not cloud, not dusk. Altitude. Noon up here is a kind of early evening with better shadows.

And you expect cold. Everyone expects cold. What you get instead, an hour's climb above the last ice, is warmth rising out of the ground — because the Newcomer is young, and it came up from the deep hot, and it has not finished cooling. The heat lives in the stone; the cold lives in the sky; the dim sun manages neither one. The town is built in the negotiation between them.

---

The town is called the Collar, and it earns the name. At the spire's waist there is a single shelf — a place where the rising rock wrinkled and folded back on itself, a lee hollow the size of a generous courtyard, two at most. You could walk the whole settlement end to end in twenty minutes if the footing allowed. It does not, because the footing is glass.

The Newcomer's crust froze as it rose into thin air, and it froze badly, in pleats and fins and blades of black obsidian, edges uncapped, glittering faintly where the weak light finds them. And nothing up here wears anything down. There is no rain enough, no wind enough, no root to crack, no frost-heave worth the word — so a chipped edge stays chipped for centuries, and a fresh fracture face stays mirror-sharp. The terrain is not old enough to have been softened and not dry enough to ever be. Every surface is a decision that was made recently and has not been revised.

Welded rungs and slabs of quarry-glass connect the structures, because walking the natural ground means bleeding.

---

The town's wealth is the mountain's breath.

The young rock is still degassing — thin threads of vapor rise from vents and hairline cracks all across the shelf, the Newcomer exhaling what it carried up from depth. The Collar catches that breath. Arrays of cold-calling panes — glass faces backed with nothing, angled open to the black sky — radiate their warmth away upward and run colder than the air around them, and when the mountain's vapor touches them it gives up what it carries. Dew beads on the underside of the sky, essentially. Rain that falls upward and is caught at the top.

It comes on a schedule. The thin atmosphere pumps with the feeble day: at first light and again at dusk the breathing swells, the panes fog and silver over, and the harvest crews walk the channels — grooves cut into glass, running slick — while tally-counters click each liter into the cisterns. Twice a day, a town's worth of drinking water, out of a mountain's sigh, in a desert of vertical rock.

Once a month or so, a storm's anvil claw this high and dies there as rime, glazing every fin white. It never survives the morning. The warm stone boils it off and the Collar gets its one loud hour — steam hissing from a thousand edges, the whole spire fuming like a kettle — before the pylons take the sound back and the hush closes over again.

There is one seep. A crack on the shade side weeps true liquid, a thread of water a finger's width wide running down a glass chute into a basin, and it is the only running water for fifty miles in any direction, vertical or horizontal. In its channel lives the film.

---

Nobody knows what the film is.

Gray-silver, slick, slow. It thickens where the drip pools, thins where the sun finds it, and creeps — a nail's width a season, always toward warmth. It is not moss; nothing that organized. It is not mineral; nothing that ambitious. It hums, very faintly, at a pitch the pylons seem to lean toward. The keepers argue about it in the evenings: whether it is the first life being invented here, in the condensation of a newborn mountain, or the last machine still running, left over from whoever raised the spire and not yet told to stop. There is no third theory anyone likes. It has no name. They are waiting to see if it earns one.

No animals have ever come this high. Nothing flies, nothing crawls, nothing grazes on the glass. The only moving things besides the water are the motes — maintenance sparks, faint glimmers that patrol the pane-arrays at dusk, licking the beaded dew down into the channels with a diligence nobody programmed and nobody questions.

---

The Collar was built to a plan for thousands, and this is the strangest emptiness of all.

Rings of foundation studs march across the leveled rock, anchor bolts gleaming, streets graded flat and bare between them — and on those streets, forty-some structures. The cistern terrace. The tally house, its single window the only lit thing in the settlement after dark. The cool-rooms, dug into the shade side where the stone is merely warm instead of scalding. A row of cell-dormitories. The net-masts, and the pylon ring, and the survey pegs. A city's skeleton wearing a village's flesh, and the flesh is not neglect — it is schedule. Higher up the spire's flank, more collars are staked. From the terrace you can see their unlit studs as faint glints across a vertical void: towns that do not exist yet, waiting along a road that has not been strung.

Because the spire was not born. It was *raised* — grown in shapes no eruption makes, its grain flowing around built doorways like stone that was persuaded to leave room. It went up from the deep hot as an anchor pier, a foundation for a hoist to the black, and the Collar is its staging camp: the first town on a road to nowhere yet, inhabited by the people who drink the mountain's breath while they build the road's first mile.

The pylons are part of the same certainty. Slim black needles, they sip vibration out of everything — footfalls, drip, wind, voices — and store it somewhere no one has explained to satisfaction. Lay your knuckles against one and you feel a faint pull, like a drain. Between them and the thin air, the Collar is nearly silent. Sound dies within a few steps; conversation is something you have with whoever is beside you, or you walk to them. The town's hush is not peace and not emptiness. It is being *drunk*.

---

What sound remains, you keep a list of, because there is so little: boot-grit on glass grit. The tally-counter's click. The nets' low hum when the dew is running well. The deep, slow tick of the spire itself, still cooling, still shrinking by fractions, felt through your boots more than heard. And once in a while — a rifle-crack as a glass fin, stressed past letting-go by the noon heat, shears off and calves down the flank, swallowed by the thin air almost before it finishes.

The days have a shape. Harvest at dawn-breath, while the panes are heavy. Rest through the scalding noon, when the stone will brand bare skin and the calving risk peaks. Second harvest at dusk-breath. Then dark, which is when the Newcomer shows its real face: the fissures breathe a red-black glow, faint heat-shimmer more felt than seen, and the whole town moves by the light of the mountain's own warmth. Above, stars. Hard, close, unfiltered stars, and the dim coin of sun nowhere among them.

Once a week the survey line is re-shot, because the ground under the town is an inch higher than it was, and the jacks and grouts and slip-joints take up the rise like a raft riding a slow swell. The stair to the seep basin has been re-cut twice this season. The town does not sit *on* the Newcomer. It rides it.

---

The rules, as the keepers state them, are few:

Move slowly — the air is thin and graceless. Work the dew-hours; noon stone scalds. Water is tallied, never owned. Nothing here wears down, so what you break, you break for centuries. And you do not drill the Newcomer without asking first — which the keepers say is custom, though the way they say it leaves room for the possibility that the mountain has opinions.

Below, the cloud floors go down and down until they become a world. Above, the staked foundations climb toward a road that exists only as intention. Between them, in a courtyard-sized hollow at the waist of a mountain that is still arriving, forty structures, one lit window, one thread of water, and something gray and silver and patient, creeping a nail's width a season toward the warmth.

Everything here is young. The mountain, the glass, the film, the town, the road. The place has the specific quality of something recently decided — all sharp edges, no revisions yet, holding its breath around a future it was built for and has not met.
