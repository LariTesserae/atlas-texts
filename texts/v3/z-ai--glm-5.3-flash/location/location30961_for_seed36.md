You come down a stair that is not old.

That is the first thing the valley teaches you, and it never stops teaching it. The steps were cut into raw stone — the chisel-marks would still be sharp if chisels had ever existed here — and the stone has the look of something broken open this morning: pale, unweathered, its edges bright as split flint. Nothing has worn it. Nothing will. The stair descends out of the surface world and into warmth, and halfway down you notice the light is wrong, and then you notice it is not wrong at all. It is only not coming from the sun.

It is coming from the plants.

Below the earth, the underside of the ground is a sky. The ceiling of this hollow — and it is a hollow the size of a county, a buried valley, a sunken region with its own geography — is a canopy turned upside down, rooted somewhere overhead where no one has ever gone to look. Roots hang from it like the ribs of a drowned cathedral. And among the roots grow the lamps: broad pale-gold leaves that shed light the way other leaves shed rain. Not a glow. Illumination. The ceiling is a source, and the light falls soft and shadowless from everywhere at once, so that the whole valley sits at the intensity of noon and the color of new leaf, whitening toward the middle air. There is no angle of sun here, no dusk, no dawn. There is no direction you can find by the light. You navigate by your feet.

The floor is not ground. The floor is one plant — a single continuous green body
