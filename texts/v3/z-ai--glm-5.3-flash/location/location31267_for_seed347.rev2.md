# The Undermere

On the moor above, you would never guess. The ground is sodden heath, hummocked and ordinary, and then a hollow opens at your feet — a seam of green light lying in the peat like something dropped. A rope of living root dangles into it, ladder-knotted, and if you go down forty rungs with your breath fogging and the cold rising to meet you, the roof peels back and you are standing at the rim of a valley that fell indoors.

The cavern is not a cave in any pocket-handkerchief sense. It is kilometers across. Its far walls do not end so much as fade, dissolving into a grey-green haze that reads to the eye as horizon. Overhead there is no sky but the underside of the world: a vault of wet black rock, beaded with condensation, veined with white spring-water, from which the whole country hangs its weather. And through that vault, near the cavern's northern end, runs the Gash — a great collapsed crack, sheer-sided, down which daylight pours like a slow waterfall. It is the only true sun this place has ever seen, and everything here is arranged in relation to it. A blade of light sweeps the far wall each day like the gnomon of an enormous clock; for an hour around midsummer noon, a coin of actual sun reaches the surface of the mere, and the town treats it as a festival.

The cold is not a biting cold. It is cellar cold, the cold of wet stone and standing water, a cold that doesn't strike but *seeps* — into wool, into bone, into the marrow of every plan you make. The air itself is saturated. You could drink it by wringing your sleeve. Every surface is beaded; every wall weeps; the rock sheds moisture the way skin breathes. The floor of the valley is mostly shallow black water — a broad mere pitted with islets and mounded with reed, fed by dozens of springs that enter high on the walls in white calcium veins and by the outer river that sinks into the moor and arrives here, cold and patient. In the low corners where the air pools, green refuses to grow and frost-flowers feather the rock instead — small white gardens of ice in the midst of all that lushness, thriving on neglect.

And it is lush. That is the second thing that undoes you, after the scale. Not the_sparse_ pale tenacity you'd expect of an underground — but a full, greedy, cold-climate green. Moss grows in lawns knee-deep, swallowing boulders, climbing ropes, riding on snail shells. Where the Gashlight lands, ferns grow to the size of trees, and stunted yews lean out of wall-cracks like things peering over an edge. In the dimmer quarters the green goes pale and strange: ghost-leaved trees that tilt toward the light-well, fungus orchards terraced on racks of woven cane, cress so thick in the shallows that the water seems to have grown a blanket. The fertility has an engine, and the engine is the bats — but everything here is layered green on green on green. There is no bare rock in the Undermere older than a season. The moment the stone breaks, the green arrives, before the dust has settled.

Densest of all is the town — the Weft — which occupies every ledge and islet and wall-terrace across half the cavern. It is a real city, layered and old, and it has been raised without metal, without wheel, without fire. Nothing here is built; everything is *grown or woven*. House frames are living roots, trained over the years across earth-fast arches, laced and retied each season until they hold their own weight. Into those frames go panels of plaited reed and clay daub; over them go roofs of living turf, sown with glowmoss so that every rooftop is also a lamp. Because dry ground is scarce, the Weft stacks: houses stand on root platforms over the shallows, two and three generations tall, each generation's roof becoming the next generation's street. There are streets in this town that are the roofs of older streets. The people keep their water as carefully as their houses — woven sluices, reed-gauge staffs knotted to mark the mere's rise and fall, boardwalk lanes that get relaid after every flood. The town is never finished. It grows at the pace of its own plants, which is to say: always, slowly, everywhere at once.

Light, for a place under the world, is oddly ordinary. The Gashlight district is bright as a cloudy noon. Elsewhere the town lamps itself biologically: glowmoss in roof-thatch and stair-rails, fat luminous fungi caged in woven sconces at intersections, and — everywhere, the detail that takes longest to stop noticing — the wicksnails, grazing along the walkway edges and leaving trails of cold blue-green light. The trail-markers repaint the path-lines every night, because the snails graze wherever they please and the streets' glowing edges drift, redrawn by appetite.

The fauna is as saturated as the flora. Fish with no eyes and no pigment rise in the pools all day, dimpling the black water. Salamanders the size of forearms hunt the moss-lawns at night. Glowmoths swarm the lamps in slow tornados. In pens on the Terraces, grazers — rodent-things the size of lambs, patient and damp — are fattened on moss and cress. And twice a day the cavern conducts its great commute: dusk-ribbons of bats pouring down through the Gash in spiraling thousands, out to the moor at first light, their droppings raining quietly onto the reed-flats to become next year's green, next year's food. Predation happens here at every edge — a pale owl drops through the Gash at dusk to take moths and fledglings; something long lives in the deepest pools, something no one has ever measured and everyone respects — but all of it is conducted at a hush.

The hush. That is the thing the Undermere teaches you first and never lets you unlearn. The place is full — packed, water and green and flesh and town, with no room between any of it — and yet it is one of the quietest landscapes there is. The moss eats sound. The wet air softens what the moss misses. The mere itself barely laps, its shorelines being mossy ramps rather than hard edges. And the people keep the hush as a discipline: voices at whisper, boats muffled with moss-lined hulls, children taught silence before they're taught speech. The reason is practical, and slightly terrible. The Undermere is young. Its erosion is still happening *now* — fresh scars on the walls are bone-white, sharp-edged, days-old in some cases — and the pathkeepers say the rock sings before it falls. A crack under stress makes a note, faint and rising, hours before it lets go. Too much noise drowns the singing. So the one loud sound permitted in the whole country is the long horn, blown hard and long when a crack has been heard singing — the single voice the Weft ever raises, and it means *move*.

Because the valley is not a finished place. It is a place being made on a schedule anyone can watch. Each flood-season the water opens new passages and closes old ones; the pathkeepers walk the walls with ears uncovered, mapping a country that redrafts itself. Twice a day the cavern breathes — cold air sliding down the Gash in the dark hours, meeting the cave's warmer breath, and where the two airs mix, the roof rains. Fine, silver, endless drizzle out of a ceiling a hundred feet up, an hour of it at dawn and again at dusk: the Weeping Hour, when the whole town stands out on its walkways under the dripping dark, faces upturned, watering nothing, being watered. The seasons down here are read not in temperature but in the water level, counted each morning on the knotted reed-gauges by the Coldstairs — which are not carved but grown, root-ladders and footholds polished smooth by generations of ascending soles.

Stand on a walkway in the Weeping Hour and the whole identity of the place arrives at once: a cold rainforest, a working city, a young and restless cathedral of water — a valley that fell indoors and, instead of dying, decided to be a country. The green comes up to everything. The water comes up to everything. The hush holds all of it the way a hand holds water: gently, and completely, and with the full expectation that the shape will change. Next year there will be more light where the roof has thinned, more green on last winter's scar, another layer on the Weft, another knot on the gauge. The Undermere does not keep itself. It keeps *growing*, and it keeps its silence, and it has never once confused the two.
