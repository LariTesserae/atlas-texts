Nothing here was ever built. Everything here runs.

Start with the plain, because the plain is nearly all there is. It is low and it is flat and it has been flat for so long that flatness has stopped being a shape and become a fact. No hill interrupts the horizon. No valley interrupts it. The terrain was worn down in eras too deep to number, polished by ice that moved slower than memory, until what remained was this: a black stone floor, smooth as old sea-glass, stretching on in every direction to where the fog takes over the work of description. The stone has been here since before there were names for stone. It is not granite or basalt anymore — those words imply a youth. It is simply old matter, the kind that has outlived its own categories.

Underfoot, the water table sits like a lid on a pot. Stomp, and the ground answers — a dull sloshing, water a hand's depth down, dark and patient. Every hollow is a well. Every footprint fills before it is finished. The whole plain floats on its own melt, and yet the cold —

The cold is the other fact. It comes off the stone and up through the fog as a wet cold, the worst kind, a cold that touches everything twice: once as temperature, once as damp. Breath freezes into falling rime. Eyelashes fur. Metal takes the skin off your knuckles if you linger. And still, everything is wet. The rocks weep. Every outcrop — and there are outcrops, rounded humps of ancient stuff, the last stubborn knuckles of a once-violent landscape — sweats threads of dark water that freeze partway down their fall, so that each hummock wears a curtain of ice fibers with liquid still moving inside them. Ice with a pulse. The brine pools lie still as mercury, unfreezable in this place, because here the line between liquid and solid has itself been weathered thin. Water flows in places where it should be locked. It flows uphill, occasionally, out of pure habit.

The fog is not weather. It is an atmosphere condensing itself permanently, packed so dense with suspended droplets that it has texture, resistance — you push it aside with your forearms. The fog is crowded. Everything here is crowded. The surface of the plain carries no bare ground at all: ice plates fitted edge to edge like flagstones, machinery shoulder to shoulder, herds pressed tight, the air saturated to its limit with droplet and ash and spark. The world has no gaps. Every niche is tenanted.

And the machinery — here is the thing a visitor must be told gently, because the visitor always assumes wreckage, ruins, some predecessor civilization that built and fled. No. Nothing was built. Look at a flywheel turning out of the bedrock with no axle, no mount, no seams: it *grew*. Given enough time, matter erodes toward certain stable forms, the way rivers erode toward the sea. In younger worlds, flat wet ground wore down to grass. In this one, it wore down to pistons. Fields of them — thousands of flywheels at different speeds, valve-stems rising from cliff faces like ferns once rose from soil, compressor housings breathing slow warm fog out of the earth. The industry is native. The land idles like a factory that was never switched on because it was never anything but a factory. Nothing here has straight lines that hands made. Nothing has rivets a smith drove. The bolts, where they appear, loosened themselves willingly from the rock.

There is no sun worth mentioning. Light comes from below — furnace-glow leaking up through translucent ice in slow amber veins — and from above, where the fog charges itself with static and discharges in soft flat flashes. Everything is seen sideways or from beneath. Shadows fall wrong: they arrive a half-second before the bodies that cast them, or slide off at angles the light doesn't support. Under this dim glow, the green has gone. There are no plants. Almost nothing grows — a black yeast-slick film on the weeping stones, a cobalt scrim of something like lichen, nothing taller than your thumbnail, nothing that remembers being a leaf. The last meadows survive only in the water. Stand at a puddle long enough and you may see it: a ripple of green crossing the reflection, a grass-shadow swaying over the image of the plain. The water remembers photosynthesis. The fauna drink that green. No one remembers it was ever real.

Because the fauna are plentiful — that is the deepest paradox of the place, and the one the plain has organized itself around. Life here feeds on the machinery's leavings, and on older, simpler contracts.

The dew-lappers come first to mind: low, broad-footed herds by the thousand, crossing the flats with mouths like squeegees, licking the supercooled condensate off the stone and sieving metals from it as they go. Where a herd passes, the rock is wiped clean — dry stripes across the wet black, the closest thing this world has to grazing. Their droppings are ore. Their bones, when the scavengers are done, are cast iron.

Above them, the spark-swallows: flocks of small piezoelectric fliers that ride the fog in tight spirals, waiting. When the fog discharges — a soft bolt, a flat of light — the whole flock folds into it at once and feeds, and afterward the fog is measurably darker, dimmer, a little more tired. They hunt light the way hawks hunted warm blood once.

The compressor beasts are the great ones, fog-whales swimming the saturated air on wide placid wings, breathing the atmosphere in and pushing it out as warm snow. They make their own weather. Falls of warm snowfall trail behind them and melt the instant they touch ground, so it rains heat under the herd, and the small life follows them — the scrapers, the rust-nibblers, the frost-garden things that farm the fungal film on their backs. Whole economies migrate with them along the ancient flywheel flats.

And there are the dismantlers. Tall, patient, limbs ending in shapes that a visitor wants to call tools, that were never tools, that simply eroded into this form. A dismantler walks up to a running engine — one of the native ones, a flywheel still turning — and takes it apart while it runs. The machine does not resist. The bolts have always been loose. The dismantler drinks the warm lubricant in long slow pulls, and it is gentle about it, the way you would be gentle with something dying of old age. Then it moves on. Heat is the primary nutrient here. Motion is secondary. Sparks are a delicacy. Grief, if it exists, is a scavenger's behavior, and the scavengers attend every dead engine like undertakers, polishing its housing before they strip it.

The sound is constant, because the engines never stop. A permanent freight-train hum under everything, a heartbeat of pistons, the moan of turbines somewhere out in the fog where nothing visible is turning. Rain hiss from the condensing air. And threaded through it, the cries of the herds — which arrive wrong. Here the plain shows its age most plainly: the rules themselves are eroded. Erosion worked through the stone, then through the water, and finally through the laws of the world, wearing them smooth like anything else. Echoes come before their sources — you hear a boulder fall an hour before it falls, and the herds have learned to graze away from pre-echoes. Heat runs downhill and pools in the hollows. Time pools there too. Sit in a shallow depression too long and you climb out having been there longer than you were. In one famous hollow the accumulated noise of a season sits like a liquid; put your head into it and hear yesterday's whole plain, compressed and warm.

The fossils are of the future. The mud keeps impressions of creatures that will not arrive until next cycle — herds pressing tracks downward through time because the ground no longer distinguishes past from future. The herds navigate by the puddles, which do not show the present. The puddles show a near version of the plain, an hour or a day ahead, and the animals drink where tomorrow shows water, avoid where tomorrow shows a dismantler standing. Once — this is told in the way the scavengers tell everything, silently, with their hands still — a puddle showed a plain with one engine too many. And then, over weeks, the real plain obliged it. The reflections are writing the world. The world is too old to argue.

What this place is, finally, is a terminus. Every landscape erodes, and erodes toward something, and this is what they erode toward: the last stable state, the end-form of ground. A world worn down to its resting shape — a low wet dark plain, idling, running its native engines under permanent fog, crowded to saturation with creatures that graze on heat and memory. The vegetation lives only in reflections. The builders never existed. The machines were never made. The cold and the wet hold each other in a long truce, and the thunder of the pistons is the resting pulse of everything, and somewhere out under the fog a dismantler is walking, unhurried, toward something that has been dying since before dying was invented.
