# Hushrow

You find the town by its breathing.

Follow the chimney-pearls down. They rise out of the dark in single file — small silver spheres, one every few seconds, unhurried — and if you put your face near one as it passes you'll feel the faint warmth of someone's fire, someone's supper, someone's life. The pearls are the exhalation of the dry rooms, air let out through chimney-stacks so the houses can take new air in from the Lungs. Count them as they pass and you can guess the size of the town: not many. It is a small place. The whole of it could be shouted across, if shouting worked here. It does not. Nothing works here the way it does above, and the town has had a long time to make its peace with that.

The water you descend through is cold the way stone is cold — not a cold that nips but a cold that persuades, that asks your blood to slow down and fit itself to the place. And the water is not one water. It comes in layers, four of them, stacked like panes of dark glass: a brine over a brine over a brine, each with its own weight, its own salt, its own slowness. The boundaries between them are invisible until lamplight hits them from below, and then you see them shatter the light into shelves, one glow stacked on another like windows lit in a house with no house. The people here call the layers the weathers, because each layer has one. The street-weather is not the deep-weather. You dress differently for them. You cross between them slowly, because a boundary crossed fast will burn you colder than fire.

The basin itself is the low country of this whole drowned world. Everything drains to it — the silt, the snow, the slow leavings of the slopes above — which is why the town is here, on the floor of it, in a pocket valley no wider than three streets. The valley floor is soft as flour, centuries of marine snow compacted to a paste the color of old bone, and the town sits on it the way a boat sits on still water: settled, patient, exactly where everything eventually arrives.

The town's name is Hushrow, and it earns the name.
