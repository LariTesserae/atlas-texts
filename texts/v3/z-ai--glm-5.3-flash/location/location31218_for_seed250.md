# The Unsurfaced

Begin with the fact that swallows everything else: there is no land here. There never has been. The planet is one material, held in one shape, and the shape is water — a single unbroken sea with a circumference, a weather system of its own, a day length, a tilt. The sun comes up over water and goes down over water, and the water in between has never been crossed by anything that was not water. The horizon is a rumor. From the ceiling of this world — the bright, trembling underside of its sky — you could look in every direction and see the same thing: blue going blue until blue becomes a kind of distance rather than a color.

And the blue is empty in a way that takes getting used to. This sea is young and starved. It has taken nothing from the rock beneath it — no silt, no salts, no stain — because this is the wrong kind of water, water that does not dissolve. What it touches, it builds with. So the ocean is clear past the point of comfort: forty kilometers of visibility, a hundred, until the light itself gives out from sheer geometry, and the mid-water becomes indistinguishable from void. There are hollows here — vertical emptiness, columns of nothing four and five kilometers tall between the summits and the plains — and you could fall through one for an hour, arms out, watching the brightness shrink above you and never once see a thing go by.

The floor is under construction. That is the only honest way to put it. Vast plains of fresh rock lie down there with edges still sharp from the laying — basalt faces you could cut yourself on, unrounded, unbattered, every corner exactly as it came. Ridges run in arrays. Plateaus tile themselves in polygons too consistent to be accident, and columns rise from the plains in fluted spirals, kilometer after kilometer of them, each flute the same as the last. No one has ever found a tool here, or a wall, or a hinge. But the ridges meet at angles that arithmetic likes, and the new stone is riddled with pores that align — all of them, across a whole district — like the cooling fins of something. Whether there is a builder is not a question this world answers. It only keeps building.

Where the work is newest, the floor exhales. Warm fields shudder and give off mineral smoke, and because the sea refuses to dilute anything, the smoke doesn't disperse — it rises in visible ribbons, cooling and stiffening as it climbs, and hardens into chimneys that grow while you watch. The warmth is local and startling. Everything else in this world is cold: a thin white sun, water at six or seven degrees that takes the heat out of you politely, without malice, the way a stone does. The hearths are the only generosity the place offers, and you can feel one coming long before you see it — a shiver in the water column, a trembling stair of rising warmth, kilometers before you reach the rock that makes it.

The sound is constant, and it is architecture. Cold clear water carries a blow a very long way with almost no loss, and this whole planet rings like a struck bell. Somewhere beyond the horizon, a laying event is under way, and it arrives to you first as a shiver in the teeth, then, half a breath later, the boom itself — clean, doubled, followed by the long diminishing tail of a world resonating. The blows come in threes. They come in threes from every direction, day after day, as if the crust were being laid to a rhythm, and it is difficult not to notice that the rock grows thickest along the paths where the sound is loudest — as if the noise were not a byproduct of the building but its scaffold, the blueprint carried in the water at the speed of sound. Between the blows there is the sea-quiet, which is not quiet at all: a deep hiss, a resting rumble, the unrest of a crust that has not yet decided where it lives. You feel the lowest of it in the sternum. It never entirely stops.

And the mountains — this is the strange heart of the place. They hang. Whole mountain ranges stand on the seabed and rise, and rise, and their crowns come within forty meters of the ceiling — of the light, of the sky's underside — and there they stop. Every one of them. Always the same gap. If you hung at one of those crowns you would see, in a single view, the sky wobbling overhead with clouds moving across it, and below your feet a fluted summit falling away for three kilometers into the dark. Mountains, upside down in every feeling but the literal one. And the rule, learned the hard way by watching: when a summit crowds its gap — when the laying brings a crown within a few meters of where it is permitted to be — the whole district falls silent. The knocking stops. The warm fields cool. And later, when the work resumes elsewhere, the summit is simply gone, and a version of it is being laid lower, in a place where the tolerance can be kept. Nothing in the history of this water has ever surfaced. The draft is not ready, and the sea holds its margin.

The life is almost nothing, and it knows its place. On the youngest sunlit rock — the crowns, the upper flanks, the rims of the warm fields — dark gardens grow: mats with a metallic sheen, blades of glassy filament a hand's width tall, translucent fronds that stand perfectly still except when the deep slow currents comb them. Sparse is almost too generous a word; the gardens are islands, separated by hundreds of kilometers of bare blue stone and then by more blue with no stone in it at all. Where they grow maps the construction's timeline exactly — flora is the diary of what rock is newest. And nothing eats them. Nothing has ever eaten anything here. When a mat dies it does not rot; it mineralizes in place, stiffening over months into a crust of itself, and the crust is taken up into the next laying. Even the death is construction. It is the least sad thing about this world.

What it does not have is eyes. No shadow has ever crossed these gardens. No mouth has opened in this water since the water began.
