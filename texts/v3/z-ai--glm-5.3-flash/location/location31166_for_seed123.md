# The Kettle

Behind the sea wall there is a strip of ground that nobody beautifies: stripped shingle, a slack run of chain-link, the concrete shoulder of the embankment itself. And at the foot of that shoulder, half-buried, the curved back of a steel cylinder breaks the surface of the ground the way a whale's back breaks water — a meter of rust-dark metal, then shingle again. You would take it for a boiler some contractor abandoned in the sixties, and you would be right about the boiler and wrong about the abandoned.

The district that ordered it is defunct. The switch, if there ever was one, is lost — bricked into a wall somewhere, some say; never existed, say others. The marsh it was built to drain is a car park now, its foundations resting on ground this machine quietly holds dry, though nobody connected to that car park suspects it. It runs. It has always run.

A crack runs along the casing's flank. The crack is young — frost found an old weld a few winters back, and the metal gave. Its edges are bright, sheared, sharp enough to take skin off a careless wrist; no rust on the new lips yet, only a fan of fresh rubble below it, grains still angular, nothing living in it except what is only beginning. The crack is new. What it opened is old.

Before you see anything, you hear it: a tick. One every seven seconds or so, patient as a clock that doesn't care about minutes. And you feel warmth on the backs of your hands — the crack exhales.

Go in low, on your knees, the way children do.

Inside, it is a room laid on its side — garden-shed sized, ribbed like a boat. Daylight comes through the crack in a single bright blade that crosses the floor plates with the hours, and a grating in the collar above lets down a soft gray wash the rest of the time, so the light is entirely ordinary — no glow, no strangeness, just sun behaving normally in an unusual room. The air is warm, dry-smelling of rust with a thin briny edge.
