# The Loud House

You hear it long before the marsh gives it up: a rhythm under the wind, patient and unhurried, like something counting.

The reed-beds on the way in are cut to stubble. They have been cut for longer than anyone remembers — the whole marsh cropped like a meadow, because every stem that grows straight here ends up woven somewhere. What's left is sedge-edge and mud-flat and open cloud-colored water, and a causeway of laid logs going out across it, each log worn pale and slippery on top, toward a thing standing over the far pool.

It is smaller than the sound suggests. That's the first strangeness. The shell is a woven hall — basket-work grown enormous, or a building that never learned to be anything but a basket — maybe forty paces across, standing on a mound of packed brushwood and clay, a made island in a pool of black water. It rises tall, taller than any willow this marsh has been allowed to grow, but it is mostly wall and air. In places the weave is open enough that you can see through it: slivers of light, and birds moving inside.

The rods tell its age better than any chronicle. The oldest are black and smooth as eel-skin. Above them, silvered ones; above those, pale last-year's withies still yellow-green at the joints. Every rod in the shell has been replaced, some of them many times, in an unending patchwork — the hall is older than anyone alive and made of nothing older than twenty years. It stopped settling long ago. It took its shape, the water took its channels, and now the place only renews itself, which turns out to be a kind of stillness.

One door, sized for people, hangs heavy on cord hinges. Smaller openings stud the wall: a slot near the top worn glossy by wings, a gap down at water level rubbed wide by something that walks. The gate swings, and you step through into weather that isn't the marsh's.

The air cools at the threshold — several degrees, all of it borrowed from evaporation, the pool breathing cold up through the floor. It tastes of willow-water, wet clay, and animal. The light comes in through the weave as fine ruled lines, thousands of them, slanted low, so the interior is dusk with a grain to it, like dim cloth held up against a gray sky.

Underfoot, the floor is hurdles — reed bundles lashed side by side over open water. Between the bundles you can see straight down: black water, moving, and the slow S-shapes of eels crossing under the light-lines. The whole hall is built over the pool's lowest hollow. The land slopes toward here so gently that no one could say where the slope begins; the water gathered, the builders gathered over the water, and the drips have been falling in the dark beneath the floor for so long that they've worn smooth cups into the stones someone once set there to receive them.

The stones are the least of it. Set into the weave along the walls, like cups on a shelf, are hide membranes stretched over clay pots and hollow gourds. Split-reed gutters run weeping water to them, and the gutters are notched, sequenced, tuned. Someone tuned a roof. The water does not drip randomly here; it plays. Ticks and cracks, a rolling dry rattle, then a low boom from a deep pot, and the shell hums with it — the whole basket resonating like the body of an instrument the size of a room, which is what it is.

Twice in the day, and more hugely at the moon's swell, the spring under the platform rises. You feel it in the sound before you see it: the drips quicken, thicken, then arrive all together, and the hall plays its long movement. At the same moment the building exhales — a visible breath of mist pushing out through every gap in the weave, the entire structure fogging and clearing like a lung. And from the dark under the floor, the frogs answer.

That is the part no one will settle. Whether the frogs learned the pattern from the gutters, or whether the weavers knelt at the pool centuries ago and transcribed a frog-chorus into notched reed, is past finding out. The call and response is seamless now. The design and the ecosystem have worn into each other like two stones in a current.

Because the animals keep the place, in every sense. Swallows by the hundreds mud their nests onto the inner weave, and where the weave loosens, their daub seals it — the roof repairs itself by being lived in. Voles run polished paths along the floor-bundles. Bats hold the topmost dark and pour out through the bird-slot at the dim hours, a smoke of them. Caddisfly larvae — the marsh's other weavers, silk-and-gravel cases on every wet rod — hatch in drifting clouds that make the ruled light snow. Spiders string the open volume, and the condensed mist beads their webs into rows of pale pearls: the only jewels in the place, lit one pulse at a time when a light-line crosses them. On the lower, wettest north wall, glow-worm larvae burn as small green sparks, steady, the only lamps on that side. A heron comes in at the water-level gap — a gray animal nearly as tall as a person, standing for hours in the shallows under the floor, fishing the eels' pool while swallows scold it and give up. Everyone seems to know the rules. The heron fishes only below. The bats exit only by the slot.

The rules, in fact, seem to be the whole law here, and they are maintenance laws: replace every rod you cut; take no nest; stop no drip; scrub no moss; leave the gate open at the pulse. The green obeys them too — and green is scarce, here and everywhere. The wet north weave is furred with moss, the channels hold reddish algae, and a few live reed shoots come up tender through the platform's joints, cropped down constantly by voles. A basketful of green in a loud dim room. That's the entire garden.

At the center is the one dry thing: a small watertight cell, woven and clayed, a room within the room. Inside it a fish-oil lamp burns no bigger than a cupped hand — filled recently; the oil is fresh. The cell's walls carry handprints, pressed into the clay while it was wet: small hands, large hands, children's hands, generations of them overlapping, a crowd frozen mid-greeting. Above them, notches are cut into a rod — tallies of something. Pulses, probably. Or years. Or swallows' returns.

As for why: three answers are offered, all of them old.

That it is an ear — built to listen to the water under the marsh, and the water, flattered, has learned to speak up.

That it is a calendar — the pulse keeping lunar time when the sky is all cloud, the drums marking months for people who planted by them; the tallies the count of everything the water has said.

That it is a promise — a nursery, a loud hollow shape deliberately stocked with water so that something would always live inside the loudness; and looking up at the slot dark with wings at dusk, it's hard to say this one failed.

There is a fourth reading, that it is a habit which outlived its purpose — but the fresh cord on the gate hinges, and the filled lamp, and a pale new withy or two in the black weave, say someone still keeps the count.

Leave at the dim hour and the sound follows you out across the stubble, thinning, until the marsh's own quiet closes over it — and you understand then that the outer marsh is quiet because all its water's voice is spent here. The hall is where the marsh does its talking. Behind you, through the weave, the cell's lamp shows as a single held firefly in the basket's heart, and the bats stream out overhead, and under everything, patient as ever, the water goes on counting. Whatever it counts, it has not lost its place.
