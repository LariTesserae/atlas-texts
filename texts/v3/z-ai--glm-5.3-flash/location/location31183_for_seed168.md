# The Table

You come up onto it out of a country of cloud, and the cloud does not follow you. The plateau stands above weather the way a shelf stands above a tide. Everything that falls — rain, snow, the gray breath of storms — belongs to the drowned half of the world far below the rim, and has never once thought of climbing. Up here nothing has ever been rained on. Nothing has ever been worn.

That is the first thing the ground tells you. The rock has the finish of cut stone — not dressed, not worked, but *unweathered*: the difference between a thing never worn and a thing never used. Strata end in edges you could shave with. Minerals glitter in their faces because nothing has ever dulled them; feldspar sparks under the sun like sugar. In the near flats the surface still shows the ripples of whatever stirred it into being, frozen mid-gesture, a sea of stone that was just learning to be a sea when the learning stopped. It is geology in its first hour. And the strangest part, the part you stop trying to explain by the second day, is that it stays in its first hour. Nothing here has ever been old.

The air is warm and enormously dry. The sun is an honest sun, a normal sun, but at this height the light comes down hard and unfiltered, and the plain throws it back, so that by midmorning you are being heated from above and below at once, the way bread is. The sky is pale and immense and absolutely empty. Nothing crosses it. Nothing crosses anything. Movement in this place is a property of machines and of light, and of nothing else.

The machines are there, and they were not built.

They come up out of the ground like whale-backs of dark metal in a stone sea: cams the size of barn roofs, pistons that rise and fall through a slow-breathing cycle measured in hours, shafts and teeth and great ring-gears half-emerged from the rock that hosts them. There is no seam between machine and stone — or rather the seam is a geology, not a weld. The metal veins up out of the strata the way quartz does, and the strata fold it, as they rise, into the shapes of function. Cams, cranks, long Lever-arms reaching over the plain. It looks designed because geometry wants to be useful. There were no builders. There have never been builders. The design, if it is one, is the plateau's own habit of shape — and yet everything about the engines says *industry*: they stand in ranks, in a loose half-buried grid, receding by fives and tens to the haze of the distance, and each one is doing a job.

The job is breath. On a schedule too long to watch but too regular to doubt, each engine exhales a warm gas from some deep furnace of the world — you smell it before you understand it, hot stone and a ghost of mint, faintly electrical. The gas spreads low across the plain and sinks into the porous rock, travels, seeps, and surfaces again elsewhere as *dew*. At first light, every day, the plateau sweats a dew that is not water: a fine mineral condensation beading on every surface in pale dry glass, warm to the touch, gone by noon. The engines make it. The ground carries it. The plants drink it. That is the whole hydrology of this world, and it has never needed a single drop of rain.

The plants are the reason you came, if anything here has a reason, and they are worth the walk.

They stand alone, most of them — one great crown to the horizon, then another — so far apart that you can walk an hour between them and hear your own boots the whole way. But *lush* is the only word for what each one is. Each tree is a garden by itself: a canopy the size of a village square, layered and layered with leaf, glassy green-black, drinking light the way desert things drink rain. Where there is no water, the wells are of light, and these are the wells. Their leaves are thick as horn and cool on the shaded side, warm as skin on the sun side. Their flesh, if you cut it — and you don't — would bleed a resin like amber. Around each one lies the only shade for a hundred hectares in any direction, a pool of dark on the glare, and the air under it smells of resin and warm mineral dust, and it is the closest thing this world has to shelter.

Nothing has planted them. Nothing sprouts from them. There are no seeds, no seedlings, no leaf ever fallen — nothing is born here and nothing dies, and every leaf you see is first-generation. And every stem, every single growing tip, ends in a bud one breath from opening. Dense with them, the whole crowns are, thousands of buds at the verge of blooming, held there, all of them, as long as the plateau has stood. The blossoming is a thing this world keeps almost doing. At noon, when the heat moves through them, the trees creak — a slow glassy groan, like the timbers of a ship that never sails.

The sound of the place fits inside a thimble. The plateau allows itself one sound at a time. A tick from the nearest engine, when some great component passes a node — one tick, then minutes of nothing. Once an hour, a long exhalation, so deep you feel it in your molars before you hear it, a chord of moving metal that lasts as long as a slow breath and is over. The creak of the trees at noon. And underneath all of it, the hush — the hush is the base note, the sound of a world with no animals in it, no wings, no calling, no small business of lives. You could stand at the exact center of the Table for a day and count its sounds on your fingers.

Walk to the rim, two days out, where the table simply ends and the young cliffs drop away in clean unfractured planes. Below you the drowned half of the world keeps its weather; cloud stands on the lower lands like a lid, and somewhere under it, presumably, water does what water does. Not up here. Up here the only clock is the sweep of shadows and the ritual of the dew, and the ritual never varies: dew at dawn, drunk by noon; one tick, one breath, one creak, in their hours; sun down over an emptiness so spacious it feels less like barrenness than like a hall built to hold instruments very far apart.

And then night, which is the engine's shift. The sun goes and the ground takes over the warmth by itself — the whole Table is a hearthstone now, faintly feverish, and you can sleep on the bare rock and be comfortable. In the dark the engines hum at the edge of hearing and do their inward work: the plain closes over every footprint, every scuff, every grain of dust you raised, and re-fuses it into the same crystal lattice it came from. Not blown away — *reabsorbed*. The world cannot be marked. That is why there are no ruins up here, no roads, no artifacts, no trace that anything has ever passed: there is nothing here that could pass and leave a name.

Every dawn the dew comes back, the buds keep their verge, the ticks keep their count, and the Table is one hour old again. A finished world, kept. It has been finishing for an age and will finish for an age, and in any single day of it, nothing moves — except the light, and the deep slow arms of the engines, turning for no one, in the silence they were made in, or made themselves in, or simply woke up owning.
