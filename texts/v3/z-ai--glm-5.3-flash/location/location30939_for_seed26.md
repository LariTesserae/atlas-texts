## The Weep

The way in is a throat, and it means that: you are swallowed politely, on a man-lift, in the company of crates and one silent crew chief, while your ears announce each fathom of it. Two hundred meters is nothing on a map and everything in the head. At the bottom the doors open onto a platform where the air is old but moving — cold, wet, and alive with its own weather — and the first thing you understand is that the cold down here is not the absence of weather. It is a weather's home address.

Officially this is Deep Works, Seam Nine. Nobody calls it that. The crews call it the Weep, and the name is accurate the way most official names are not.

## The vault

There is no ground under the ground. That is the second thing you learn. The natural world ends somewhere behind the liner — permafrost, old and grey, pressing its cold inward through two hundred meters of overburden like an intention — but everything you will ever touch here was made. The floor is poured. The walls are shotcrete over lagging over rock that no one has seen since the first year. Even the rubble is screened and graded, even the gravel is engineered. The whole valley is a casting.

And it is a valley. That is the only honest word for it. Three kilometers end to end, a kilometer across at the widest, vaulted overhead into a darkness where the cable trays run like river systems seen from orbit. The floor is laid in numbered plates, twenty meters to a side, a coordinate sea you can walk for an hour without meeting anything. Because the machinery is scattered — that is the design. Compressor houses stand like blocky mesas with warm flanks, far apart, each on its own apron of empty concrete.
