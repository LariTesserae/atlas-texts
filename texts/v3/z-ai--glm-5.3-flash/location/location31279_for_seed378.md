## The Weeping Stair

You climb out of the lowlands for a full day — through haze the color of dirty pearl, past salt sinks that shimmer and breathe — before you understand that the mountain is sweating. There is no moment where the water begins. It was always beginning. The rock simply gives up its moisture everywhere at once: a cliff face a mile long dark with seepage, gullies that trickle without ever having known rain, terraces stepping down the flank of the massif, each one rimmed in stone the water itself has grown. The pools between the lips lie flat as poured metal, warm as skin, faintly steaming under a sky that offers no mercy.

Because the light here is total. High altitude, thin air, and nothing anywhere that stands up — no trees, no shrubs, no stalk, no shade in the entire world except what hands have made. At noon the wet stone glares back at the sun and the glare bounces until the air itself seems lit from inside. The only vegetation is a skin: the bloom, a living film of rust-pink where the water runs iron, bone-white where it runs lime, olive-black where it is old. It grows in hours in this heat, coats every damp surface, thickens in slow channels into rubbery pads a finger deep. It is the base of everything. Nothing here has ever grown a leaf; the whole food of the mountain is this stain, farmed and grazed and dried into felt for hats and sandals and saddle-cloths. Wood is unknown. Fire is unknown — there is nothing to burn, and no one on the Stair has ever heard a fire crackle. Cooking happens in spring-heated stone pots and in mirror ovens that fold the sun onto a plate of pan-bread made from pressed bloom and brine.
