# The Cold Foundry

You come up the cone from the deep side, and the light finds you before the land does.

It comes down in columns through water so clear it feels like an absence — cold, blue-white water, winter held all year in a sea that has never once thawed. Above you, the underside of the ocean is a bright trembling ceiling. Below, the young mountain rises in black terraces, its pillows still sharp as broken crockery, unsoftened by a single century of current. Nothing has worn this rock. It was thrown up from the planet's furnace recently, stood above the sea for a while as a raw black island, then settled back under, and the sea poured into its engine rooms and stayed.

The evidence of the island-days is still written at the rim: a wave-cut notch, a ring of sun-bleached pebbles near the crater's lip, a tide-line frozen into the glass — the mountain's diary of its brief acquaintance with air. And at the northern rim, a single fang of basalt still breaks the surface at the lowest tides: the only dry point in the world, streaming and dark, marked by nothing and known by everything. The fauna below orient to its moving shadow the way compass needles orient to iron.

## The Room

The summit is a crater of perhaps two hundred paces across — a village square, a threshing floor, a grove — ringed by a rim that drops away into hard blue on every side. It is a room, and the sea is its roof, and the sun comes through the roof in slanted nets of caustics that slide over the floor like slow hands.

The floor is new ash, fine and pale, barely compacted. You could press a footprint into it and it would last a season, because nothing buries anything here; the world is too young to have sediment, too slow to forget. In fact the floor is already written on — lanes of chuffed prints from the plow-creatures, months old, crisp as type — and this is the first law of the place, learned with the eyes:

**Nothing erodes here faster than it happens. Time is visible. The plateau is a ledger.**

Almost nothing grows. That is the second thing you understand. The sun pours down all this light, all this warmth-by-proxy, and nothing green rises to meet it. There is no forest, no weed, no mat. There is only the varnish: a thin, colorless film on the stones that shivers when the current shifts, chemistry not yet committed to being alive, metabolism still an experiment — and here and there a fuzz of crystal bloom, white aragonite threads standing up like frost-flowers, growing by pure precipitation, fracturing at their tips on no schedule but their own. grazed at their edges by things that eat stone. It is the ecosystem's entire claim to vegetation: a ghost, a rehearsal, a coat of varnish. The light lands on machines.

## The Chimes

The chimneys stand in the middle of the room — dozens of them, near-touching, thin-walled towers of white and grey mineral, cool smokers, barely warm. This volcano spent its fire fast and the sea drank every ember at birth; what heat remains lives in seams, in threads, in a hand's width of shimmer above each vent. Put your hand through that shimmer and for a moment you know what summer is. Everywhere else, the water holds its cold like a vow.

The chimneys ring. That is the first thing you hear, and the last thing you will stop hearing. The mineral is thin and hard and taut, and the fauna brush it, knock it, toll it — deliberately, idly, constantly. In cold water a struck chimney carries a mile. The whole summit is a grove of bells that never finishes ringing: overlapping tones in long slow decay, a storm of chimes running at one-tenth speed, so that the soundscape is less like noise and more like weather.

And under the bells, the idles.

## The Fauna

There is no shelter here, no weed to hide in, no burrow to duck into — nothing is built; nothing in this world has ever built — and so the animals have made an economy of sound. Every engine broadcasts its idle. A running engine is a claim; silence is broken. So the plateau never stops droning, and you read the crowd by ear before you read it by eye:

**Boilers** carpet the stone at the rim's sheltered hollows — vast bivalves, pressure-vessels, their shells plated in tarnished pewter and riveted in growth-lines, rumbling as they calcify. Twice a day, at the turn of the tide, they vent in unison, and the entire room exhales one long white sigh.

**Stokers** plow the ash: armored isopods the size of boots, hydraulic-legged, each step a slow stroke with an audible chuff, sorting metal from glass with mouthparts like a stamping mill. Their shed hulls lie along the rim in rows — a scrapyard that assembled itself — and the young of every species wear the empty castings like hand-me-downs, because in a world where nothing is made, everything is re-embodied.

**Spinners** hang in the midwater: torus-bodied medusae with vanes, transparent as struck glass, rotating at a crawl. You can watch the machinery of their guts go around. Each rotation creaks. They are the room's hanging lanterns, and where they pass through shade their pilot lights show — small blue flames of cold combustion burning hydrogen they have stripped from the water itself, fire that never got hot.

**Ram-eels** cruise the rim in long patient laps, intake mouths working, throats glowing faintly blue with their low flame, bubbling in slow chains.

**Iron-moths** drift in the open water by the thousand — small copper discs, all facing the same direction, the whole crowd aligned to the young rock's magnetic memory like filings on a plate. When the magma far below shifts and the field leans, the entire community turns with it, over days, a clock hand drawn by ten thousand moving parts.

And near the roof of the room, just under the bright trembling ceiling, swim the **ceiling-fish**: the upside-down school, skimming the surface-film, grazing the sky from beneath.

The density is the thing. There is almost no open rock. Every surface is occupied, every layer of the water column staffed — floor, forest, midwater, ceiling — presences near-touching, and between them the clear cold water stands like glass panes laid edge to edge.

## The Event

Once — you are told by no one, and understand by watching — the leviathan crosses.

It comes up the south flank: whale-scale, boiler-hearted, a power station that was born rather than built, running so deep an idle that you feel it in your sternum before you hear it — a boom that knocks fine spicules of glass off the chimney walls. Above you a permanent snowfall begins: shattered chime-fragments drifting down through the light, each shard taking an hour to reach the floor, so that the leviathan's passage falls as glitter.

As it crosses the summit, the resonance walks the chimney field from south to north — one bell triggering the next, a wave of tolling that takes a full minute to cross two hundred paces. Every idle in the crowd leans toward it for a moment. The whole room briefly syncs, then lets itself fall back out of time.

Then the leviathan is gone down the north rim, its heartbeat fading by fathoms, and the plateau resumes its sum.

## The Breath

The tides are the day here. The summit sits high and shallow enough that the sea's breathing matters: at low tide the room rises toward the light, brightens, warms by a degree, and the crowd climbs; at high tide it cools and settles back into its own acoustics. Twice a day the plateau inhales new water through its dense packed streets — the tide is the lung — and twice a day the boilers exhale their great unified sigh.

Everything here is slow, and this is not laziness but law. The third law of the place:

**Fire is borrowed, and slow engines keep it.** Everything that works runs on the mountain's chemistry — hydrogen, sulfide, metal — and every quick animal has already spent its flame and gone cold. Evolution here trades speed for stamina. The idle is the anthem. Nothing hurries, and nothing stops.
