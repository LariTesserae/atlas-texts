## Warmholme

Approach it from above and you will miss it entirely: a brown moor, wind-combed heather, a sinkhole choked with bramble. After the first frost, though, look for the one circle where frost will not lie. The heather stands in it all winter, unburnished, and the snow melts there in a ragged ring as fast as it settles. That ring of stubborn warmth is the Mouth.

Down through the Mouth a spiral of cut steps goes, seventy-two of them, still sharp-edged at their corners. A rope handrail runs the wall, furred and polished only where hands have gone — everywhere else the rock keeps its fresh chisel-bite, because Warmholme is young. Three generations old, at the outside. The founders came down during the Long Chill, when the fields above turned mean, following a warmth they'd noticed breathing out of this hole in the ground. They never went back up to live. Nobody since has wanted to.

The air rises to meet you on the stair like something exhaled from a kitchen: hot, mineral, thick with wet stone and green rot and the faint hot-iron tang of the dynamo hall. And your footsteps stop belonging to you. The green takes them. Sound does not survive long here.

---

The town is one system of galleries, four hundred paces end to end, never wider than a good throw of a stone. Three boundaries hold it: the Mouth above, the Sump below, and the Face — the raw wall at the far end where the cutting engine works. Within those bounds there is no spare wall. There is no spare dark, either.

The Long Cut runs the length of the town, and both its sides are terraced into three and four tiers of cut niches — rooms, workshops, bunk-alcoves, mushroom-shelves, cistern ledges — stacked like masonry made of rooms. Overhead hangs a second architecture: gutters of glazed tile, copper-seamed; electrical conduit in clay sleeves; drip-lines; rope; drying racks. Read any single stretch of wall and it holds a gutter, a pipe, a fern-rack, a bunk behind a curtain, a shelf of stacked mushroom-logs, a child's chalk marks, and someone's name and date cut small by a doorway. The town is saturated with itself, the way a sponge is saturated. Nothing in it is unused, and the things that have no use yet are being saved for later.

The lamps are few, and they burn low — power here is harvested by patience, not burned. Mostly the lamps are set beneath the garden racks, so that their light arrives pre-filtered through fern-fronds, green and dim, and falls on the walking ways in leaf-shadow. Along the gutters a bred strain of phosphoric moss marks the edges where feet should go. The street-lighting of Warmholme is botanical. It flickers faintly when the draft from the Mouth shifts.

---

The heat is the town's whole economy. The rock under Warmholme is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and near the Sump — the lowest gallery, the deepest cut — the flags will blister a bare foot. The climate is vertical. Hot underfoot, cool overhead, where the thin roof of stone stays near the temperature of the seasons above. People live in the middle band, in linen, permanently beaded with a sweat that never quite dries. The ceiling drips cool water on the neck. Nobody flinches anymore. It is the town touching you, they say, and it is a courtesy.

The machine hall sits mid-town, and it is quiet the way deep water is quiet. A flywheel taller than two men turns once in six minutes, with a sound like a page turning. It is geared off the thermal gradient itself — heat rising through the floor, cold sunk in the roof-stone — so its whole strength is a difference, a weak and patient gradient, and its motion is scaled to that weakness: slow, immense, unhurryable. The dynamos purr off the wheel and feed the town its amber light. The engineers tune the gearing constantly, because the gradient is not constant. It breathes. More on that in a moment.

At the Face, the far end, works the Shear. It is the second duty of the town's machinery: a great slow rack that draws one stroke every six minutes, shaving the cutting wall by a paring. The Face advances about the width of a man's hand in a week. When the stroke lands, you feel it in your teeth before you hear it — a deep *tok*, soft, swallowed almost at birth by the vines — and the whole town tells time by it. *Three strokes home. I'll meet you two strokes after dew-count.*

The tailings run in a warm slurry-channel to the Sump; what comes out the press are pale paving-bricks, and the newest streets of Warmholme are always the whitest, laid down the year they were cut. The town paves itself with its own growth. It does not grow old so much as ahead of itself.

---

All the water in the world is gathered water. The rock around Warmholme is dry; there is no spring, no seep. What the town has, it makes: the machines warm the air, the air breathes against the cold roof-stone, and the roof-stone weeps. Every ceiling is a slow rain. The gutters hang beneath it like a second sky, angled into cisterns at every terrace, and the drip-counters — an honored trade, mostly old women with brass counting-wheels that tick all night — keep the town's true calendar in drops.

After a storm on the moor, a brief river runs down the Mouth stair, loud for an hour, the only loud thing Warmholme permits. Where the roots of the heather have worked through the thinner roof, their drips come sweet, faintly of pollen and tannin. Where bare stone sweats, the water comes tasting of minerals. Every kitchen keeps two jugs. No one has established a rule about which is for guests. It is simply known.

And the town sweats on purpose, too. Surplus dew is poured across reed-trays in the warm low rooms and left to evaporate through the galleries, which cools the air and feeds the ferns and keeps the whole settlement from cooking in its own heat. Water into air into leaf into water again. The cycle here is not a river. It is a breath, held and released, held again.

One more thing about the roof: its temperature is the weather above, delayed. The rock remembers a season late. When the ceiling runs coldest, it is remembering last winter; the wet season down here falls in the spring of the world up there. The drip-counters read it like a delayed sky. *Up there the sky is weather,* the saying goes. *Down here it is history.*

---

Everything grows. That is the first thing a visitor understands wrong and then, an hour later, understands completely. Moss vats line the warm floors; fern-racks climb every lit wall; four dwarf lemon trees — ancient, hard-cropped, each fruiting only some years — stand in the best lamplight like minor nobility. In a good year their fruit is cut into two hundred segments. Mushrooms occupy the dark and the low, by lease. Greening crews follow the Shear the way midwives follow a birth: every new room must be planted within the year, or it goes *dry-sick* — floury, flaking, unlucky. No one will sleep in a dry-sick room. No one will say exactly why.

The animal kingdom of Warmholme would fit in a hatbox, and the town is politely proud of it. Dew-eels, pale and blind and finger-length, live in the gutters and cisterns — the founding records say they arrived in the root-balls the settlers carried down, though no one remembers choosing them. They keep the water sweet. They are eaten rarely, almost ceremonially. White moths, a few dozen, live out their small lives in the lamp-gardens; at rest they all face the Face, all together, and nobody bred them to do that and nobody discusses it much. Mites farm the moss vats and are fed to the eels. That is the whole kingdom. Two hundred humans, four trees, some eels, some moths, and everything else is plant or stone or water.

---

The strangeness of the place is not loud. It accumulates.

The floor breathes. The warmth below rises and ebbs on a slow tide days long, and the engineers have geared the Wheel to follow it, so at the bottom of each breath the lamps dim, town-wide, gently, and brighten again, and no one remarks on it any more than you remark on dusk. The children play a game of palm-testing the flags to feel the warmth come and go. Nobody drills below the Sump. This is not law exactly. It is more like the way you do not shout in a sickroom.

Doors carry names. Every room cut by the Shear is christened at birth — *Kindly*, *the Long Patience*, *Winter's Roof* — with its date cut small beside it, and the eldest names are the oldest writing in Warmholme. The town treats its walls as generations. It is, in the most literal sense, a town that remembers being born.

And once a year, for a single hour, the ground runs hotter — a hand's width of extra warmth, sudden, unexplained. In that hour every moss in Warmholme flowers at once. Every vat, every gutter-strain, every greened wall puts up its tiny pale bloom together, and the spores drift through the low lamplight like slow green snow, and the whole town stands in its doorways and neither works nor speaks until it passes. It is the only bloom the town has. It is the year's axis. Children are considered to have their birthday on the Warm Hour, whatever the papers say.

Sound, in such a place, is rationed. The vines are planted as baffles — the founders learned early that a thin roof and a loud engine do not agree — and the custom outlived the necessity by a generation. A raised voice here is a small act of vandalism. What remains is an inventory: the Shear's tok, felt in the sternum; the tick of the counting-wheels; gutter-trickle; the Wheel turning its page; the leaves shifting in the draft as the Mouth inhales at noon and sighs all night, the town breathing with the weather's reminder that a sky exists.

---

Stand at the Face, at the end of everything, and watch the wall take its stroke. Beyond the Shear there is rock that is not yet a room — new-cut cold, already beading with its first condensation, sharp as the day it was separated from the dark. Someone has already chosen its name. It will be greened within the year; it will hold a gutter, a rack, a curtain, a child's chalk; in eighty years someone will touch its date with their thumb.

You stand a while in the hush and the drip and the heat, and you understand what Warmholme is. Not a burrow. A seed, sown in warm stone — patient, packed, dim, and always, always newly made.
