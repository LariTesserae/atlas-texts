# The High Water Gardens

This is a drowned mountain range, though "drowned" is the wrong word — nothing here remembers being dry. The range was young when the sea claimed it, and the sea was patient. Now the peaks stand submerged but proud, their summits still hundreds of meters beneath the surface, a highland country underwater, an ocean above an ocean floor that has not yet learned to flatten itself.

The water here is warm as bathwater and thick with dissolved light. That is the first strangeness: down at this depth there should be darkness, but the water itself glows faintly amber, a luminous medium, as if the sun were filtered through honey instead of depth. It is normal light, in a place where light has no right to be. Nobody here questions it. Light is simply a property of water, the way warmth is, the way the slow, almost imperceptible current is — a drift so gentle that suspended sediment hangs motionless for years, like the interior of a cathedral in which time itself has been asked to wait.

Because the range is young, its edges are still sharp. The ridgelines have not been rounded by ten million years of rain that never fell here. Instead, the water does the weathering in its own way: microscopically, mineral by mineral, so slow that the mountains seem freshly carved, quarried yesterday by a sculptor with infinite patience. Sheer faces still bear their original fracture patterns. Narrows and flutes of stone run with skin-smooth precision. You could cut yourself on this landscape — if there were anything here capable of cutting itself.

And there is vegetation, lush past all sense. The steep slopes are terraced not by waterfalls but by life: great cascading shrouds of photosynthetic film, russet and wine-dark where the light is thinner, electric green where it pools. Fields of soft-bladed kelp-grass stand upright on the high plateaus like wheat that has forgotten wind. On the leeward sides of the young peaks grow gardens of stony coral-anemone hybrids, branches of pale rose and bone, so dense in the valleys that the valleys have ceased to be valleys and become hedges — corridors of living tissue you would have to part to walk through, if anything walked.

The density is the thing that overwhelms. Every cubic meter holds something: drifting spore-fuzz, swaying fronds, motes of animalcule light, long tethered siphons breathing the slow current. Nothing is bare. Stone lasts perhaps a season before something green claims it. The whole range is upholstered.

And yet — quiet. Almost silent. There is no wind underwater, no rain, no falling. Sound here is only the deep tidal hum, a note felt in the stone more than heard, and the occasional soft crack as a young ridge settles a centimeter. Once in a long while — perhaps once a year, perhaps once a decade — a low percussive thump rolls through the water: a burst of gas from the still-forming seabed below, the mountains' birth pains, continuing. The fish-life is so sparse that each creature is an event. You might swim an entire day through the kelp-grass and meet nothing, and then a single pale eel-like shape will hang in the middle distance, regarding you with an eye like a coin, before unhurriedly pouring itself between the fronds. Solitude is the default condition of the fauna, and it seems chosen, somehow. Rare things in a dense world, keeping their distance from one another out of some instinct none of them could name.

There are no artifacts. No walls, no tools, no bones arranged with intent. Whatever intelligence might be budding in the warm water has not yet reached for the world with hands. The closest thing to construction is accidental: a mat of vegetation that grew around itself into a hollow sphere on the highest plateau, drifting gently against its own tether, a natural cathedral with a natural door. It is not a dwelling. But it is the kind of place a dwelling might someday be imagined in.

The dynamics of this world are glacial. The seasons here are measured in sediment. A current shifts course over centuries, and when it does, whole slopes of vegetation slowly starve and slowly re-seed, colors migrating across the range like weather seen in fast-forward only by geologists. The mountain is still rising — a few millimeters a year, pushing up into the warm amber water — so the gardens are perpetually relocating upward, whole ecosystems chasing the light-zone at the pace of a fingernail growing. Nothing hurries. Nothing needs to. Death here is not violent; it is a gradual dimming, a frond going pale over months, until the water reclaims its minerals and hands them, in its own time, to something new.

It is a young world, high in its own private ocean, dense with life and nearly without sound, waiting — not urgently — for its first real change.
