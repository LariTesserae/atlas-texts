## The Still

You come up into it the way you'd come up into weather.

The last pitch is a ramp of bare stone, and the stone is warm. That is the first wrongness, the one that reorders everything after it: you have climbed all day into the dim high cold of the world's roof, and the rock under your palms is warm as a road at dusk — except nothing has ever shone on it. The warmth is the land's own. This whole plateau rose recently, an uplift the size of a country, maybe a continent; it came up holding the deep heat of its making the way a loaf comes out of the oven, and it has not yet cooled. You stand on the fresh crust of something still in progress, and it breathes heat up through your boot soles.

The light, by contrast, comes from everywhere and nowhere. There is a cloud deck below you — a true sea of it, pale and unbroken, curving away at the limits of sight until you suspect the planet's edge — and another, thinner deck above, and you walk in the layer between, in a light that has been handed through so many veils it has lost its direction. No shadows anywhere. Distance is not a brightness-fade here; it is a thickening, a slow turn of the air from clear to pearl. Colors keep their low, warm register: greys with amber in them, stone the color of bread crust, the green of the vegetation reading almost gold in the haze. It is a permanent dusk that never commits to evening.

The locals — if anything here can be called local — call the place the Still. The name means three things at once, and you'll understand them in reverse order.

First: it is a still, a distillery. The plateau runs a condensation economy. Warm ground lifts damp air; the cooler heights wring it; every surface is a condenser. Dew films the stone so thickly that you can drink by laying your cheek against a slope. Water weeps from fissures in threads too fine to splash. Rain happens, but privately: the plateau is vast enough to keep its own weather, and storms live out their whole lives inside its borders like tenant farmers. You will stand in a rain that has borders — a curtain you could lean across, one shoulder soaked while the other stays dry — and the rain will start without warning and stop without finishing, as if someone set it down mid-sip. Free water is rare and precious and momentary. A basin catches an inch of condensate at noon; by mid-afternoon the ground has drunk it back. Nothing stands open long. The world repossesses its water almost as fast as it lends it.

Second: still, as in silent.

Third: still, as in — and here the name turns ironic — nothing of the kind.

Walk, and watch the green. It does not cover the land; it circulates through it, like blood through a body of stone. Where the ground is dry, there is only a grey-green felt, a crust of lichen-things patient at the condensation game. But every weep, every seep, every seam where mist compresses against cool rock runs lush. Green veins follow the fractures. Green crowds the shadow-hollows where the mist pools. Round each spring-head the vegetation goes almost tropical in a radius of a few strides — tree-ferns slender as adolescents, moss hanging in wet ropes, bladder-plants that refrigerate the air around their fronds past the dew point and drink their own weather — and then the opulence stops dead at a line you could draw with chalk, and dry felt begins again. Lushness here is not a blanket. It is a lacework, a network, the land's plumbing made visible.

And all of it is young. That is the second thing to understand: nothing here is old, not because little time has passed, but because the land keeps renewing itself. There is no soil to speak of — the crust is too new — so everything grows epiphytically, rootless, mats and felts and curtains anchored to bare rock. Everything is a pioneer species. Everything grows fast and lives briefly. Even the largest tree-ferns are decades old at most, and they stand ringed by the already-collapsing compost of their predecessors. Erosion has barely begun its work; the terrain wears its fresh-cut edges like a quarry — scarps with knife-sharp arrises, columned cliffs so regular in their hexagons they look extruded rather than erupted, slot canyons still pristine from the few rains that have cut them. In an old landscape, time rounds everything off. Here time has barely picked up its tool.

The animals are what convince you the place is inhabited rather than merely decorated. They are abundant, and they are layered, and they are — this takes a full day to accept — silent.

The plateau exhales a constant gentle updraft, thermals rising off the warm stone, and an entire ecology rides that elevator. Lens-shaped grazers drift past at face height, combing the mist with fringed mouthparts, harvesting beads of water out of the air itself. They part around you as you walk — brush, part, close again — and that is how you learn how dense the life is, because you cannot hear it. You know the air is populated by touch. Curtains of drifting bodies, spore-fuzz, seed-wool, things the size of your hand and the size of a barn, all of it moving at the speed of rising bread. At every band of altitude, different tenants. Near the ground: stilt-legged waders stalking the moss mats on legs like heron stilts, heads striking with sudden precision at prey you never locate. Higher: the buoyant ones, balloons of muscle and gauze. And in the rain — only in the rain — creatures made of rain: eels of water that thrash briefly in the falling sheets, shaped like life, alive the way a wave is alive, gone the moment the rain ends. They don't die. They simply stop being a form.

The silence is not emptiness. It is economy. Nothing here spends sound. The waders talk in scent — pass one and it leaves behind a word of smell, jasmine-and-iron, gone in seconds — and the ground-talkers speak in vibration through the warm stone, a language you can read by kneeling and pressing your palm flat to the rock. Put your hand down and the plateau gossips against your fingers: footsteps of things unseen, the settling of far slopes, the deep idle of the place itself, an infrasound hum you feel in the sternum rather than hear, the note of something continent-sized idling at low revs. What sound the Still does spend, it spends one coin at a time. A drip lands. Then a long nothing. Then another drip, and in this silence you hear it from fifty meters off, a small bright stroke, each drop sounding less like weather than like a decision.

And then there is the industry.

No hands made anything here. No path, no wall, no cut mark on any stone — the Still is untouched in the ordinary sense. But walk far enough and you will find machinery. Where matter is least decided — and matter here is frequently undecided — engines condense. You round a ridge and there is a derrick of grown black mineral, glassy, faceted, that has clearly risen out of the ground at the pace of frost rather than been assembled, and it is running. Stone pistons stroke in their stone cylinders with a regularity no geology has. It pumps water from a fissure uphill to a basin nobody built, for reasons that belong to the plateau's metabolism and are not being shared. Some of these engines are the size of Cathedrals of motion and have run so long that moss hangs off them; some assembled last week and will dissolve next week, and while they ran they did something legible — sorted gravel, lifted water, turned a wheel against the wind — and then demisted back into the air, leaving nothing but warm ground and a faint smell of ozone. The geological columns are part of it too, if you look honestly: hexagonal basalt extruded by the acre, so uniform the hillside reads as factory output. Here the industry has no factories because everything is the factory — the condensation line manufactures droplets in lockstep by the million, the crust extrudes its columns to tolerance, the fauna arrives in types, each kind a variant on a template with the same gestures, the same gait, stamped clean. Throughput without a single voice. The most industrial thing about the Still is the silence of its production lines.

The 0.4 — the almost-nothing of the built — lives here too: unfinished work. A stair of dew-pressed stone climbing a cliff face, cut steps, rising with real intent, and then stopping in mid-air at the ninth step, because the machine that was making it dissolved before it finished, and nothing else ever took up the task. It waits. The Still is full of such patience.

As for the breakdown — you feel it before you can name it. Matter here is not exactly unreliable; it is undecided. Watch a bird cross the sky and notice its wingtip redrafting itself at each stroke, settling like an image resolving. Notice that the rain sometimes begins before its cloud arrives, drops falling out of a clear sky, the weather running ahead of its own cause. Stand on a ridge at dusk and you may see the mist hold the shape of a mountain that is not there — a precise memorial, slopes and shadow and all — for a minute, until it rains the memory away. And the ground revises. A slope that slumps into grey porridge at morning may be re-risen and re-sharp by evening. New terrain appears — a notch, a terrace, a whole short valley — and here is the rule that explains the lushness, the rule the whole place runs on: the life is already waiting for it. By the next morning the new valley is green, colonized, tenanted, adapted, as though the mist keeps a seed-bank of forms in stock for terrain that has not happened yet. The ecology precedes the land. The plateau manufactures mountains and mornings on the same line, and it has pre-loaded everything it intends to make.

Your own footprint does not escape the economy. Press your boot into the dew-film and the print holds for a few minutes — and then the mist simply repossesses the water, bead by bead, and the ground is unwritten again. The Still keeps its ledger clean. Even your presence is an intermittent moisture.

Night, if you can call it that, is only a further dimming. The haze deepens from amber-grey to a warm charcoal; the felt hum comes up through the stone more strongly, as if the machinery runs its long shift in the dark; and the green answers with its own light — hollows and seep-lines glowing faintly, lantern-mosses spending their phosphorescence carefully, the only points of definite color in the whole dim republic. Somewhere a condensed engine pulses a slow amber signal into the fog, patient, addressed to no receiver you will ever find. Drips continue, spaced like a clock with no hands. You sleep warm on the crust of an unfinished world, and the last thing you understand about it, on the way down into sleep, is this: the turbulence and the quiet are the same fact. The place is working, all of it, everywhere, at enormous scale — manufacturing weather, columns, creatures, hours — and it does it the way the best machinery runs, which is to say at a whisper, with nothing wasted, not even the sound of itself.

You wake to a drop landing somewhere out in the mist. One. Then the long silence. Then another. The shift change between one version of the world and the next goes on all night, and you are lucky, briefly, to have a body it can borrow moisture from.
