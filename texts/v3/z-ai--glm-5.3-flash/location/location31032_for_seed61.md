**The Note Under the Moor**

The surface gives you almost nothing: a moor of bare stone, scoured pale, crusty with grey lichen, under a sky that always looks about to rain and often does. No trees, no grass to speak of — the land here gave up on green long before anyone kept records. Water lies in the shallow pans between the rocks and will not drain, because beneath the moor is caprock, and beneath the caprock, at about the depth of a grave, is the town.

You find it by ear before you find the door. Stand still on the stone in the right place and a note comes up through your boot soles — a low, round, patient tone, like a bell that has been ringing since before the idea of bells. Walk ten paces and it fades. The keepers' door is a slab flush with the moor, shaped to shed rain, and it lifts on a pivot of polished stone that two centuries of palms have worn glassy. Below it, stairs spiral down into the note itself.

The hollow is small. That is the first thing to understand. The whole town — and it is a town, with streets, quarters, a well, a public square — would fit inside a country chapel. You could throw a pebble across it, if throwing were polite. A central pocket, perhaps sixty paces across, with galleries climbing its walls in five crowded terraces, stairways thin as ship's ladders, cupboard-rooms with stone bunks, cisterns set into the floor like buried cauldrons. Every surface is faced. Nothing raw remains. Five or six centuries of hand-chiseling went into this hollow, a few cubic feet per generation, and the chisel-facets survive everywhere as decoration — rows of small regular scallops, worn soft at the high spots where hands and shoulders pass daily.

The second thing to understand is the water. It does not fall here and does not flow; it *arrives*. Rain on the moor above sinks through hairline fissures and takes decades to reach the ceiling of this pocket, so the water the keepers drink tonight fell as weather before their grandparents were born. It comes out of the rock as weep — a hundred seeps, beading on the ceiling, running in threads down channels carved for them, feeding every wall with a slow cold sweat. The air is saturated. Nothing ever dries. Every surface sweats, the mist never lifts, and the temperature is the same coolness in every chamber and has been for as long as anyone can count, because the rock overhead is thick enough to have forgotten the seasons entirely.

No wood, of course. Nothing burns here — there is nothing to burn, and fire, with its flicker and appetite, would be a scandal anyway. So: stone, water, and one more material, the most important one. Where the weep runs richest, the keepers farm the milk: a velvet skin of lichen and film that they grow on stone racks in shallow trays
