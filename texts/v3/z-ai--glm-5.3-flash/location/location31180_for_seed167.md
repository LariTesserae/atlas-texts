The basin lies in the world's low place, a shallow bowl of sea floor too deep for any sun to reach, and the first thing you learn about it — before you see anything at all — is that the water is warm. Not current-warm, not surface-warm. Blood-warm. Body-warm. At a depth where the cold should press in like a verdict, the water holds you the way a held breath holds you, and it takes a while to understand why: the warmth is not coming down. It is coming up. The floor of this place breathes heat the way a sleeping animal breathes, slow and even, through hundreds of small springs scattered across the basin like pore openings in skin.

You cross the rim first — a low ring of drowned hills, sanded smooth, so weathered they look less like terrain than like the idea of terrain, a border someone stopped drawing. And then the bowl opens below and around you, and the dark changes texture. It stops being empty and starts being occupied.

Somewhere down there is the city.

You don't see it at first. You feel it — a roughening of the water, a suggestion of edges — and then the dark resolves, one degree at a time, into structure. Spires. Terraces. Arched buttresses of coral the color of old teeth. Tubes in their ten thousands, packed like reeds, each one a tenant. The city was not built. That is the second thing you learn, and it takes longer to accept than the warmth. There was no plan, no tools, no hands. There was only accretion: coral secreting its stone, worms cementing sand grain by grain, mussels mortaring themselves into walls that other creatures then colonized, generation settling on generation until the generations themselves became architecture. The city grew the way a reef grows, except that here, in the warm and sheltered dark, it grew with the seriousness of a thing that meant it. It is valley-sized. You could swim from one gate of it to the other in a day, if you hurried, and there is no reason to hurry here.

The streets came before the street-builders, in a sense. They are the paths the currents prefer — old, lazy circulation loops that have crossed this basin since before the basin had tenants — and the animals followed the currents, and the coral followed the animals, and the flow lines hardened into open avenues worn hollow by fins and feet and the drag of ten thousand small bodies over uncountable generations. The avenues are smooth. Thresholds are dished. Corners are rounded like river stones. This is what erosion means in a place like this: not destruction, but agreement. The city and its water have worn each other into a shape both can live with, and they have been at that negotiation so long that it has stopped being a negotiation and become simply the way things are.

Every surface is layered. That is the third thing. The reef is porous as bread — chambers within chambers, galleries, spongy walls riddled with burrows — and all of it is occupied. Snails graze the ceilings. Anemones wave from the lamplit corners of the springs. Inside the great spires, fish hover in stone rooms with windows worn through to the current, and the whole structure is threaded with the passages of shrimp and small crabs, so that if you press your palm flat against any wall of this city you will feel it tickling, faintly, like something alive deciding whether you matter.

Which it mostly doesn't. Abundance is the ordinary condition here. Above the rooftops the water column stands in layers, and each layer has its population: the swarms of copepods like slow gray weather; the shoals of lanternfish that rise and fall on some private schedule; the squid that drift through the midwater with their arms trailing like the cables of unpacked instruments. Predators move through all of it — a hunting squid crosses a street like a rumor, and the smaller fish compress against the coral until it has passed — and the prey never seems to thin. The city feeds its upper waters and the upper waters feed the city. Things come down; things drift up; the volume of water between the floor and the far dark is as crowded, in its layered way, as any street market, every presences near-touching, fin near fin, shell near shell.

Green is rare. That is worth saying plainly, because everything else here is so alive. No light falls from anywhere, so the plants that arrive — weed torn loose from some lit shallowness a world away, drifting down for months — arrive already dying, and snag on the high spires like flags of a distant country, and bleach, and come apart, and are eaten before they finish being plants. What stands in for grass is quieter: bacterial velvet, sulfur-pink and cream, matting the terraces around each warm spring, grazed into a lawn by urchins that plow through it all night. There are one or two stubborn tufts of true seagrass on the sheltered terraces, stunted and pale, holding on out of stubbornness, the way a single tree will hold a hilltop no one tends. Sparse. A garden kept by an absence.

The springs themselves are the city's hearths. Some are gentle — shimmer-lines where warm water shivers up through the paving, warm enough to sleep beside, and the streets near them are always crowded. Some are chimneyed, crusted with mineral in layers like the rings of a felled thing, and around them the bacterial glow is strongest: a blue-green film of light, dim as a memory of a candle, the closest this world comes to daylight. Animals that need light have made their arrangements with these films. Lamp-anemones host glowing bacteria in their tentacles and fish their prey out of the dark that their own shine creates. Jellies drift through the districts like slow lanterns being carried by someone who has forgotten where they were going. But it is all so little. The darkness here is not broken by light; it is annotated by it.

Two places in the basin are stranger than the rest, and both will find you before you find them.

The first is the lakes. On the open floor, in the basin's lowest wrinkles, the springs bring up water so salt-heavy that it will not mix — and it pools, and pools, and becomes lakes with actual shorelines, lying on the floor of the sea like mirrors laid face-up. The brine is warm and clear and utterly hostile; a fish that commits fully to crossing becomes a pale glimmer sinking through it forever. So the lakes have edges, and the edges have populations: mussels in chemosynthetic mats at the waterline, crabs that come to moult in the mineral shallows where nothing will hunt them, and — strangest of the small strangenesses — creatures that walk the shorelines the way animals on any world walk any shore, at the border of two waters, belonging fully to neither.

The second is the rain. In one quarter of the city, a cold current dives over the rim and meets the warm rising water, and where they interleave, the sea curdles into snow — not the sparse drifting snow of the rest of the deep but a true downpour, a standing curtain of it, flocculating continuously out of the turbulence and falling in fat, unhurried flakes. It falls all the time. It has fallen, so far as the wear on the stones can tell, since before the oldest spires. The district under it is roofed with shell — great tilted plates, community property, worn glossy — and if you stand out in the open rain with your face upturned, you feel it land and never land, flake after flake arriving out of a black sky inside the sea. It makes a sound. Underwater rain hisses — a vast, soft, granular hush, like the whole district breathing out once and never stopping. It is the loudest thing in the basin, and it is still quiet.

Because the basin is quiet. That has to be said carefully, because it is not silent. The walls crackle — pistol shrimp, ten million of them, their snapping so constant and so evenly distributed that the ear gives up and files it under silence, the way a town files its wind under silence. The reef itself hums, very low, when the deep current leans on it: structure-borne sound, felt in the bones before the ears. And once in a long while — rarely, and you will remember each time — something enormous crosses the rim of the basin, and calls, and the call comes down through the water like a bell through fog, long and descending, and every creature in the city stops. The shrimps snap mid-snap. The snow seems to hang. Then the call fades out over the far dark, traffic resumes in the streets, and the crackle closes back over the silence like water over a stone.

Everything here runs on two economies. That is the first rule. Food falls from above — the snow, the dead, the drifting remains of a lit world that exists only as its sediment — and warmth and chemistry rise from below. Every life in the basin lives in the exchange between those two directions, and the city is simply the shape that exchange has taken after long enough time: the tallest spires reach up to catch the fall, the deepest burrows reach down to touch the springs, and the tenants spend their whole lives in the middle, trading.

The second rule is that nothing is wasted and nothing is begun. When a large thing dies up in the dark and comes down — and it does come down, sometimes, a whole whale descending through the water column like a continent going soft — it does not land so much as arrive, and the basin reorganizes around it for decades. There is a spire at the city's heart that is cored with bone: the ribcage of some ancient fall, long since absorbed, coral grown up through the ribs like fire through a lattice. The oldest architecture is always a corpse that stayed. Growth and dissolution here run at exactly the same speed — new stone accreting on the lee sides as fast as the current dissolves the weather sides — so the city is neither growing nor dying, only continuing, which may be what a place looks like when it has finished becoming itself.

And the last rule is about the dark, and it is this: the dark is not an absence. Eyes are a specialty here, not a default; most residents read the world in pressure and chemistry and the touch of moving water, and they would tell you — if telling were among their arts — that the basin is bright with information and that it is the sunlit world, with its terrible unbroken glare, that must be hard to live in. The quiet is the same kind of thing. It is not emptiness. It is fullness, held at low volume — a whole city of presences, layered fin over shell over stone over bone, all breathing the same warm water, all keeping their sound the way you keep a fire in a small room.

Stand still at the edge of the rain district, at the turn of the long tide, when the springs breathe out and the warm plume goes up through the streets and the whole city lifts its spawn into the dark like snow falling in reverse. The shell roofs are glossy. The lamp-anemones burn their small blue. Somewhere out past the last spire the floor goes on, dark and warm and lightly smoking, farther than anything here has ever needed to go.

The snow keeps falling. The springs keep breathing. And the city, patient as calcium, goes on not being built.
