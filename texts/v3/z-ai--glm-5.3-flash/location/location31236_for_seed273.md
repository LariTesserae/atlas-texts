**The Mountain**

No one who lives there calls it anything but the Mountain, which is either the oldest joke in the place or the oldest act of tenderness, because it is not a mountain. It was made. Course by course, cast and stacked and fused, a tower-massif the size of a small country's hill country, built so long ago that the question of who quarried its first stones has become a theological matter. The lowest stairs descend into the permanent cloud-floor and never come back; someone went down once, generations ago, with rope and lamps and forty days of provisions, and what they reported has been argued about ever since. Within the lived world of the place, natural rock is a rumor. Everything you will ever touch here was made by hands, and most of it was made by hands that have been dead for five hundred years.

You arrive through the deck. The cloud-floor lies below the structure like a white sea with weather in it — storms are born down there, live their whole lives down there, and die down there, and the thunder reaches the upper terraces as a muffled, felt thing, a knuckle knocking on the underside of the world. The approach stair climbs through hours of gray blindness, condensation beading on your shoulders, and then it breaks the surface of the deck all at once, into thin, enormous brightness. This high, the light has an edge to it. Shadows are cut clean. The air holds almost nothing — no dust, no haze, no water worth the name — and the sun comes through it like it has somewhere to be.

The crown of the Mountain is condenser country: fields of fins, acre after acre of copper and glass vanes gone green at the joints, racked in long grids that catch the wind and the cold of altitude and give the thin air something to give up its moisture to. There has never been rain here. There will never be rain here; the weather happens below. All the water this world has ever known arrived as dew, as frost-melt, as the deliberate sweat of cold metal — and, most of all, as exhaust. The great engine halls are sunk into the crown like a heart sunk into a chest, and their breath is water. Every chimney weeps. The keepers collect it all.

The engines themselves are not quick. Nothing here is quick. They run on the difference between the sun-hot stone and the near-cold of the high thin air — vast, slow, heat-drinking machines, flywheels turning with the patience of millstones, one deep stroke every forty seconds that you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear. That heartbeat is the ground note of everything. Above it: wind through the condenser fins, which is a low organ-tone that shifts with the wind's angle, so the crown is always faintly singing. Below it, all the way down: water ticking in pipes. Tens of thousands of pipes.

Because here is the secret of the place, the thing that makes it possible at all: the Mountain is warm-blooded. Left alone, this altitude belongs to the cold. But the engines' waste heat is banked into the stone by day and given back by night, and the flue mouths open among the terraces on wax thermostats that sigh open and shut like the whole structure breathing. The result is an air that belongs to late spring and never leaves. Near a flue mouth it is almost tropical; on an open south terrace, warm and dry on the leaves; on the crown itself, cold-fingered, brisk, bright. The climate is graded by height the way a hillside is graded by slope, except here the slope is made of rooms.

And every level of it is garden. This is what the place is *for* — or became what it is for, which may be the same thing after enough centuries. Hanging gardens spill over every parapet. Weeping walls seep on schedule. The cold mains are routed deliberately through the planted districts so that they will sweat, and the roots are planted against them on purpose — the condensation on a pipe jacket is a crop. Grain on the open terraces, broad-leafed food crops crowding the flue-warm districts, vines on the south rails, ferns in every shaded stair-well, moss on every north face, fig trees old enough to have their own legends. No rain has ever fallen on any of it. None of it has ever gone thirsty, either. Water is counted here the way memory is counted elsewhere.

The whole system runs as a staircase of uses. Water condensed at the crown is pumped down the spine and begins its career: drinking first, then kitchens, then baths, then the dye-works and the paper-mills and the steam presses of the workshop districts, then the wall-gardens, then the settling tanks where fat carp keep the water sweet, and then — only then — the lowest cisterns, from which the slow pumps lift it all again. A single drop, arriving as dawn-frost on a condenser fin, may labor for a year before it is allowed to rest. Nothing is wasted; there is no *away* to waste things to. The place has been in balance so long that the balance itself is the oldest structure here, older than any wall: water in equals water used, warmth rationed by vent, repair done in the same styles as the original so that nothing new has been built in living memory and nothing old has been lost. The steps are dished in the middle by feet that stopped being counted centuries ago. The copper is verdigris-green where it is green and polished bright only where a thousand hands a day still take hold.

The animals arrived by air and stayed, and there are more of them than the keepers ever planned for. Swifts nest in the flue mouths in their thousands and pour out at dusk in rivers. Bats hold the pipe galleries. Frogs live in the cisterns and set the night chorus going so loud that conversation on the lower terraces pauses for it. Lizards sun themselves on the warm pipe runs. Bees live in the old chimney flues, half-tended, half-feral, and the orchard terraces hum from a mile away. Eagles and carrion-birds ride the updrafts along the flanks, where the heated structure pushes a standing column of warm air into the cold — the Mountain exhales, and the birds live inside its breath. And there are the finches. The finches have learned that certain low-pressure pipe joints will weep if disturbed, and they peck them, deliberately, tap tap tap, until a bead of water swells, and then they drink. The keepers grumble and repair and have never once tried to stop them, because it is understood that the birds were here first in spirit and the pipes can spare it. It is one of the place's small laws, unwritten like all the rest.

Twice a year or so, a rag of the cloud-floor will climb the flank and thread the gardens — the great event, the closest thing to weather. Mist in the vines, everything beading, gutters chuckling, the swifts gone silent, the whole structure holding still as if listening. And once in a lifetime, the top of a true storm will break the surface of the deck, and you will feel thunder through the stone under your feet, from below, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

At night the terraces come up lamplight by lamplight — flame mostly, a few sullen electric globes along the spine — and the dew-work begins. Every cool surface sweats. By dawn the drip is everywhere at once, ten thousand small channels ticking, and the keepers walk the sluice-lines opening them in order, top to bottom, an ancient sequence, the day's water set moving like a labor force clocking in. At dawn also comes the underlight: the sun catches the deck below and turns it to hammered gold, and the lowest terraces are lit from beneath, warm on their undersides, as if the whole Mountain stood inside a lamp.

Nothing here is quick, and that includes its death. The deepest law is not spoken as a threat but as a schedule: *the engines must not stop.* Not because anything would explode — because everything would cool. The thin air's true cold would return over a season, quietly, the gardens greying out terrace by terrace, the frogs going first, the water staying in its cisterns, everyone given ample time to understand. The end of this world, if it comes, will be patient, polite, and thorough. Which is why the heartbeat never falters, and why the tenders at the crown listen for it the way farmers listen for rain.

Far below, under the white floor, in the damp loud world where weather is still permitted, people point up into the cloud and call it a mountain, because it looks like one from there, and because there is no one left who cares enough to correct them.
