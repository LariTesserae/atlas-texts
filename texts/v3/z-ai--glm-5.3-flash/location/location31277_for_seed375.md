# Stillwater

The sea is overhead. That is the first thing you learn here, and it revises everything that follows.

Where sky should open above the basin, there is instead a ceiling of water — a sea that rose, some generations ago, and never came down. It hangs at perhaps three hundred meters, a pearly, slow-circulating mass the color of the inside of a shell. All the water this land ever had is up there, and none of it falls. It casts no rain, no snow, not even a heavy dew. What it casts is light: dim, directionless, filtered through fathoms of suspended sea until it arrives at the ground as a soft gray luminance, shadowless and sourceless, like very late dusk held permanently in place. Beneath the hanging sea, the world is lit the way the deeps once were.

The basin itself is low — the lowest land for a hundred kilometers in any direction, a shallow bowl a few kilometers across, ringed by a rim of new rock. The rim is young in the way a wound is young: sharp-edged, unweathered, still bearing the fracture-faces of whatever upheaval lifted it. Nothing has had time to round it. You can stand on the rim and see the entire world at once — every street, every tower, every pale figure moving below — because the whole of Stillwater fits in a single view. It is a small place. You could cross it in half an hour. You could know it in a week. Whether you could ever understand it is a different question.

The city stands on the basin floor, and it is made of salt.

Not brick, not stone — salt, crystallized out of the old seabed into architecture. The streets run in a strict, regular plan, radiating from central plazas, and the buildings rise along them in towers, walls, arches, arcades — but almost nothing here is solid mass. The structures are lattice. Crystal grown in open frameworks, in filigree, in great delicate grids that are more absence than substance. This is a city built around emptiness. The plazas are vast and bare; the towers are hollow shafts of braced prisms; the blocks between streets are mostly air with a skeleton through it. Walk its lanes and you spend most of your time looking through the town at other parts of the town, framed and refracted, so that Stillwater always appears roughly twice its actual size — once directly, once in reflection.

And it is silent. Not quiet — silent, in the way the inside of a sealed jar is silent. The dry, cold air carries almost nothing, and the crystal eats what little remains; sound that touches the salt lattice simply disperses into its facets and does not return. Wind moves through the city as a visible thing — you watch it arrive, a sheet of cold air bending the frost-flowers on the sills, stirring the dust in a slow silver churn — and you hear nothing at all. Drop a shard of crystal on the paving and it breaks without a sound. The loudest thing in Stillwater is your own pulse, and after an hour you stop noticing even that. The inhabitants do not speak. Nothing here ever has.

Here is what the salt remembers, if you ask the pale ones who tend the town — and they will answer, in their way, by taking your hand and pressing it flat against a wall, where under your palm you can feel a slow, fine vibration like a word being begun and never finished:

There was a town here once, on the shore of a real sea. One year the sea rose — not in flood, but *up*, pulling itself out of its bed and into the sky, taking its fish and its fog and its weather with it, and leaving the bowl dry and the town meaningless. But a sea spends its life looking at what stands beside it. Every day for centuries, the town's image fell on that water. When the water went up, it could not take what it had seen. So the salt of the dead seabed — the salt that had lain there under the reflection all those years — began, slowly, to grow the town again.

That is Stillwater: a rebuilding, in mineral, of a drowned place, worked out from a memory of light. The copy has errors, of course — a copy of a copy, corrected by nothing — and so there are stairways that end in air, doors set into blank walls, arcades that imitate streets that never existed. There is a bell tower with a perfect void where a bell should hang. The copy is young, decades old at most, and every facet of it is sharp as the day it grew. Nothing here has been eroded by anything. It has only ever been added to.

It is being added to now. That is the strangest habit of the place: it is *active*. Each night, in the coldest hours, the ceiling sea breathes downward — its underbelly descends into the bowl as a thin, silver fog that flows through the lattice streets like a tide, filling the plazas to knee height, and then, before dawn, withdraws. The fog is the only moisture Stillwater receives, and the salt takes all of it. Where the tide reaches, everything fattens. Crystals gain a hair's breadth of new growth in a night; frost-flowers bloom on every surface in the tide-zone and are transmuted by morning into permanent, cloudy mineral; the whole city adds its daily invisible increment of itself. Around the rim of the fog's deepest reach grows the only vegetation the basin supports — a sparse, stubborn ring of glassy reeds and gray crust-moss, things that are not exactly plants, that drink fog once a night and mineral light the rest of the time, and that grow, at most, a leaf a season. Above the tide-line, the lattice is bare.

The tenders keep the city. There are not many of them — you might count eleven on a crowded day, tall, slow, pale figures wrapped against the cool, their faces weathered to the matte finish of the salt itself. They have no tools. Nothing here has ever had a tool. What they have is their hands and their husbandry: they cultivate, in the hollows of the towers, small translucent grazer-creatures that eat the excess off growing crystal, and worm-things whose brushed secretions seed new growth exactly where growth is wanted. The tenders guide; the organisms build; the salt agrees. Each night, while the fog stands in the plazas, the tenders walk out among the new growth and lay their palms against whatever has appeared since yesterday — greeting it, steadying it, or, with a slow deliberate pressure, stopping it.

Because the city must never be finished. That is the rule under all the other rules, and the tenders keep it the way other peoples keep a sabbath. As long as the copy remains imperfect — a door uncapped, an arch unfinished, the bell forever uncases — the original stays hanging. Complete the reflection, and the sky will remember what it is. Finish Stillwater and the sea comes home. So the town grows and is un-grown, grows and is trimmed, in an endless renovation that has no end as its entire purpose. The basin floor itself assists in the work, heaving and tilting by slow degrees — plazas drift a finger-width off level each year — and the city re-levels itself through new growth, so that nothing in Stillwater is ever still, and none of it makes a sound.

The fauna are few, and you will rarely meet any of them directly. The grazers are known mostly from their reflections, caught skimming along the polished facets. Something long and low comes down to the tide-line on fog nights to eat the crust-moss; you will see its tracks at dawn, pressed into the frost in total silence, and perhaps a pale shape withdrawing through the lattice, and nothing more. And in the sky, sometimes, there are shadows — slow, large, wrong-shaped silhouettes moving through the body of the hanging sea, whether creatures of that water or only its drifting banks, no one living knows. The dry wells tell you what the tenders believe. Stillwater's copy includes the old town's wells, faithfully rebuilt, bone-dry. Lean over any of them and you look straight down into a disc of reflected hanging sea, complete with its slow shadows passing. The water is always visible here. It is always somewhere else.

Once or twice a season, a current thins the ceiling, and a window opens — a patch of true, bright, directional sunlight that slides across the basin like the beam of a distant lighthouse. When it comes, everything stops. The tenders turn from their work and face it. The grazers lift their translucent heads. The frost on ten thousand facets catches fire all at once, and for a few minutes the whole silent city glitters like a held breath. Then the window closes, the dim returns, the fog-breath comes again on schedule, and the salt takes up its work: another hair of growth, another door left deliberately unfinished, another night in the lowest, coldest, emptiest, most inhabited quiet in the world.
