# The Weeping Chimney

High above everything — that's the first thing to understand. This place exists at the top of a mountain range so tall that clouds are a thing remembered from below, if anyone down there remembers clouds at all. But you don't reach it by climbing. You reach it the way water does: by accident, by seepage, by finding the crack.

It's a pocket. A chamber the size of a chapel, hollowed out of the young mountain's spine — and *young* is the right word. The rock here is barely rock yet. Its edges are still sharp, still glistening with the heat of its own formation, black glass fringed with orange where the mountain's interior warmth hasn't finished leaking out. Nothing has worn smooth. Nothing has had time.

## The Water

The chamber weeps. Every surface, all of it, constantly. Superheated water condenses against the cooler glass walls in fat beads that refuse to fall — surface tension here is strange, stretched thick, so droplets hang for hours like glass fruit before releasing with a sound like a struck nail. A thermal spring wells up through a fissure in the floor: a slow, boiling lens of silver-blue water, breathing vapor into the air in a rhythm — exhale, inhale — that you will eventually recognize as tidal. This little room has weather. Its own private climate, cycling every forty breaths or so: mist thickens, rolls across the ceiling, rains gently upward as condensation, and clears.

No plants. Not one. Nothing green has ever established here — the heat forbids it, the youth of the stone forbids it, and the water is too mineral-rich, too *scalded*. What grows instead is a skin of pale crusts: silica blooms, sulfur frost, rings of crystallized salts that form and redissolve with each humidity cycle. The floor around the spring is terraced in white and amber, like a staircase built by evaporation. It's the closest thing to architecture this world has managed, and it built itself.

## The Fauna

And yet — life, everywhere, in staggering density. Not plants. *Animals.* Creatures have found this pocket the way the water did, through cracks, and stayed.

The walls crawl. Small, chitinous vapor-drinkers — thumb-sized, translucent, their bodies nothing but modified mouths and stomachs engineered over generations to condense and swallow the hanging droplets. They carpet the ceiling in layers, sometimes two and three deep, and when a droplet releases they all lean, in unison, toward the sound.

Deeper in, near the boiling lens, live the long ones: pale, blind, eel-facsimile things that hunt in the terraced pools and drink the heat itself, sidling up to the spring's edge until their skins flush rose-red, then retreating to radiate it slowly back into the dark. They are preyed upon by something you will mostly hear — a soft, rhythmic *ticking* from the ceiling hollows, and occasionally a rapid collapse of tickers onto a long one's back, a whole nest descending at once in a silent, economical violence.

None of them make noise. That's the eeriest part. A chamber this crowded, this *busy*, and the only sounds are water: the drip, the spring's tidal breathing, the occasional glassy click of a droplet letting go. The fauna is silent the way a crowd holding its breath is silent. Predation happens in total quiet. Death is a slow folding, and then the droplets resume.

## The Wrongness

Stay long enough and the place begins to disagree with itself.

The geometry is subtly unstable — the chamber seems larger on some humidity cycles than others, and twice you'll count the terraces and get a different number. Droplets sometimes hang in mid-air without a ceiling, obeying some gradient of density you can't see. The fauna behaves as though a second chamber exists, interleaved with this one: vapor-drinkers will sometimes walk along a wall that isn't there, mouths open, and drink from invisible beads. When a droplet falls *through* the floor and doesn't come out the other side, you stop asking questions.

There are no tools here, no artifacts, nothing made — but the long ones use tools of a sort: a hot stone passed from mouth to mouth, used to crack the silica crusts, shared around the pool like a family heirloom. That's the ceiling of their craft, and it's enough. They've been here perhaps a thousand years. The mountain has been here twenty.

## The Rule of the Place

The rule, if the pocket has one, is this: *everything flows through water.* Heat, life, time, the strange interleaved geometry — all of it moves by condensation and release. Whoever holds a droplet, holds the moment. And the moment always, always lets go — a soft click, a falling bead, the spring exhaling — and the chamber, dense and wet and newborn and impossibly high above the world, begins its next breath.
