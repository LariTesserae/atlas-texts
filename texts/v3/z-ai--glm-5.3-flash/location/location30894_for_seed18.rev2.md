**Condenser Nine**

The plain at the top is pavement, and it runs empty to every horizon: slabs the size of fields, joints caulked with something gone the color of old horn, no wall, no tree, no second object anywhere. The air up here is thin and cold enough to bite. And then, with no warning the eye would call proportionate, the ground opens.

The mouth of the Stack is a circle you could pace off in a hundred steps. Rail it, bench it, and it would serve as a town plaza. That is the whole of it sideways; all of its enormity runs the other way. Warm air rises out of it in a steady column, and within three paces of the rim the cold of the plain starts to feel like something you imagined. The breath smells green — crushed fern, hot loam, a thread of steam off metal — and under the smell you feel, before you hear, a swelling in the ground that arrives through your soles, swells, and lets go. Ten minutes later it does it again. That is the Stroke. You will stop counting them once you understand that the Stack counts them for you.

Going down the first stair, you learn the second fact, which is bigger than it sounds: almost nothing is in here. The shaft drops fourteen seconds deep — that is how it is measured, by echo; you shout and your voice comes back thinned and late, and the old landings are numbered by the seconds of their delay. Between the landings there is air, and then more air. A helix of small filament lamps marks the wall all the way down, each one a warm grain, and from any landing the ones above and below read as distant stars. The light from the mouth comes down as a slanted column, tilting slowly with the day, and at noon the whole shaft is a lit room. Everything else is void. The emptiness is not the absence of the place. It is the place.

There is no ground anywhere in it. That took the visitor a while to accept, the first time. The floor is a foundry floor. The walls are cast stone coursed like masonry but seamless, drilled with rows of weep-holes and flue-mouths, hung on brackets riveted through their faces. Terraces project from the wall on those brackets — narrow bands of built earth behind bronze curbs, fed by drip-channels tiled in ceramic. Even the moss is a tenant; even the lichen pays rent in color. And the whole of it is old in the specific way of things that have finished changing. Every edge is rounded. The channels the water cut over its first centuries are the same channels it cuts tonight; the argument between drip and stone closed long ago in compromise. The rust has rusted. On the dry bands — for the wall alternates, green hours and dry hours — inscriptions have weathered to the shape of reading without the words, and bronze has gone the matte brown of old chestnuts. The gardeners chalk tallies on the ladder-rails anyway, in the present tense.

The lushness lives where the condensate lives. The warm breath from below climbs, cools against the upper wall, and gives up its water there — so the crown of the shaft, the underside of the world, is bearded solid with fern, hanging moss, curtains of orchid gone feral from somebody's planting, vines that have trained themselves along the lamp-wires. Below that band the terraces carry the worked beds: wet crops in stepped paddies, dwarf orchards pruned hard to fit their brackets, taro-like leaves holding cups of standing water where midges breed. Then a dry shelf, bare as a road. Then another green band wherever a flue exhales. The moisture is not a climate here; it is an archipelago. Twice a day, more or less, the system overfeeds itself — the breath stacks up under the Crown, a cloud-floor forms and thickens, beads faten, and the shaft rains from the inside. The drops take four full minutes to fall from Crown to floor. You hear the front coming: a hiss that starts above the clouds and widens like a horizon, then the terrace roofs all go drum within the same second, a thousand small instruments struck together. Below the mid-heights the drips land on stone still warm from the engine and answer themselves in steam.

All of it happens inside the sound. The sound is the largest inhabitant. At the bottom of everything is the engine — a piston the size of a belfry turning a flywheel whose revolution takes the length of a long meal — and each Stroke exhales up the bore a boom that the shaft holds and will not release: a continuous organ-note under everything, pitched below thought, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the fluid of the eyes. Over it runs the white hiss of steam and falling water. Under that, the drip-static, multiplied. And through it all the swifts. They nest in the weep-holes by the hundreds and never land otherwise; a torus of them turns all day in the light column, counter-rotating in two streams, and their screaming is the only treble for miles — thin, needling, everywhere. Shout, and fourteen seconds later the shaft hands your voice back. The gardeners' language is hand-sign, and its grammar has a tense for words said into the dark that will be answered late. There is no quiet in this place, not even in the gaps. The quietest the Stack ever gets is loud.

The life follows the water up and the insects up and its own hungers around. Pale moths the size of a palm work the cloud-floor, pollinating the orchids; midges breed in the paddy-water in smokes so dense they read as weather. Trawl-spiders anchor on the dry shelves and put out lines hundreds of feet long, beaded with condensation until each strand looks strung with pearls, trawling the updraft like nets. Snails graze the wet runnels, their shells coiled like little flues. In the cisterns live shrimp white as paper and eyeless. And once an hour, if the light is right, a falcon folds itself off some dry shelf and drops the whole fourteen seconds through the light column with its wings closed — the only fast thing here, and even it looks slow, because the void swallows velocity the way it swallows everything else. When the rain begins, every swift in the shaft rises at once, climbing straight up through the falling water, passing the drops on their way down. Nobody designed that. Or somebody did, and it has weathered into ritual.

At the bottom, the heat. The last hundred feet are boiler-deck warm, then boiler-deck hot, copper-lit, loud in a way that stops being sound and becomes a medium you move through. The flywheel turns its slow turn, oiled and gleaming. The great black cistern takes the rain's tithe and stands almost still; rain-rings cross it and die. Stoker-machines the size of dogs work the coal along the deck at a pace that makes the falling rain look hurried, and a dynamo off the flywheel shaft burns the lamp-helix that lights the whole weather of the place. The Stroke is the clock here — ten minutes, boom and exhale, again. Days are counted in Strokes. The gardens keep the count. The birds keep it. Nothing in the Stack is ever late, because there is nothing to be late for.

Here is the rule the place runs on, and it is the whole rule: nothing leaves. Steam climbs; it cools on the Crown; it drips; the drips are herded down runnels; the gardens interrupt the water on its way down and are paid for the interruption in fertilizer washed from the weep-holes where the swifts roost; the cistern feeds the boilers; the boilers make breath; breath climbs. The birds interrupt the insects, the insects interrupt the sap, the sap interrupts the light, the engine interrupts everything gently, once every ten minutes, forever. Condenser Nine, the ledger-plate at the rim still says — of nine. Its eight sisters stand dry on far horizons of pavement, cold as chimney-stacks, and whether this one lives because someone tended it or because a garden took the tenders' place is a question the patina has been erasing for a long time. The engine keeps the garden. The garden keeps the engine. The argument has settled into balance, like everything else here.

Climb back to the rim before you go. Cold thin sky; the exhaust standing off to the east like a flag made of weather; swifts stitching the mouth's edge, crossing from cold to green and back. Somewhere below, a drop is condensing on a fern-tip that will spend its evening in a paddy, its night in a boiler, and its dawn as cloud. The whole place is that drop, going around. It is not going anywhere, and it is going everywhere — down, at the leisure of four minutes; up, at the leisure of ten; and it will keep the appointment.
