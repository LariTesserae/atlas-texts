# Sump Nine

You come to it across the flats, and the first thing you understand is that the land has no age to it. Not old-new — young-new, like a wound dressed an hour ago. The marsh shelves down from nowhere to nowhere, a gray-green skin of sediment only inches deep over water that has always been there but has only just decided to be a surface. Spoil banks run beside the drainage channels in raw, unweathered shoulders — mud the color of wet liver, cut as recently as this morning, with no softening on their edges, no slump, no vegetation colonizing their crests. Nothing has had time to arrive and establish itself. The place is three days old. It has been three days old for as long as anyone can remember, and this is not a contradiction anyone here worries about.

The town is small — you could walk its full extent in twenty minutes, and the walk would take you past everything twice, because the streets are laid in one compact grid, four long and three deep, bordered on the south by the pumping station and on the north by the water itself. But *small* is not the right word for what the town contains. It is dense with intent. Every structure is put there on purpose: the pipe racks on their trestles, the storage tanks in their fenced row, the refinery shed with its sawtooth roof, the houses — identical, clapboard, galvanized roofs — set out in ranks so regular they read as another kind of equipment. This is not a settlement that grew. It was installed, the way you install a pump, and it runs.

The heat is the second thing. Wet heat, working heat, the kind that makes the air feel solid at the edges of your lungs. Everything sweats here — the pipes weep condensation in long silver beads, the concrete of the pump aprons wears a permanent dark stain of damp, the windows of the administrative building never once hold a clear pane. Fog sits low over the flats in the hours you would call morning, if the hours had a habit of moving. The air is full of water that has nowhere better to go.

And the light — this is the strange bargain of Sump Nine. It is *hot* under a sky that is almost dark. A low, brown-orange gloom, like the light through a closed eyelid, like a furnace room at rest. The sun may be somewhere behind the perpetual cloud deck; nobody has a strong opinion. The refinery's flares and the sodium lamps on their stalks do more work than the sky does, and they never rest either. Shadows here are soft and directionless, and things glow: the pilot lights, the instrument panels in the control shack, the eyes of the goats that live along the levee.

The fauna has taken to the place with a enthusiasm the vegetation never managed. The plants are sparse — a few salt-toughened grasses along the channel lips, one stand of mangrove shoots on the newest mud, a single row of planted palms by the barracks that have not grown a centimeter — but the animals have arrived in force. Fiddler crabs work the mud in waving thousands. Mudskippers flip and settle along the spoil banks. Herons stand in the outfall channels in perfect rows, and no one knows what they're eating, but they're fat. At dusk — the permanent dusk — the frogs in the settling ponds start up, and above them the cicadas, and above *them* the machinery.

Because the sound never stops. That is the third bargain of the place, the one you learn in your chest before your ears. The pumps run at a single deep unvaried note; the flare stack adds its breathy roar on top; the diesel generators in the powerhouse carry the middle register. The whole town hums in one sustained chord, and the frogs and the cicadas have tuned themselves to it. Stand still at the levee at what you'd call night and you can feel it in your molars: the exact pitch of a world holding its breath without breathing out.

Because that is the deepest fact about Sump Nine, the one underneath the rest. Nothing changes here. Not slowly — *not at all*. The tide never advances past its mark on the pilings; the mark is a clean dark line, not a history of lines. The rain, when it comes, comes at a single steady rate, the same drops, the same duration, and then the same pause. The smoke plume off the refinery stack rises in a column so unbroken it looks drawn. The herons stand in the same spots. The mud, fresh as it is, does not erode and does not heal. You get the feeling that the place has achieved some kind of equilibrium so complete that time has lost its grip on it — not frozen, exactly, because everything is running at full output, but *held*. A sealed machine at operating temperature. A note being sustained.

The people who work here adjust to it the way you adjust to any job. They talk about the weather and are answered with silence. They don't celebrate anniversaries; nothing here has ever been older than anything else. They keep the pumps oiled, and the pumps run, and the frogs sing their one chord, and the fog condenses on the pipes and drips at its one unvarying drip, and out on the flats a crab lifts its claw and holds it there, mid-wave, the way everything here holds everything — mid-motion, forever, at full steam and going nowhere.

It is, in its way, the most restful place you will ever be unable to leave.
