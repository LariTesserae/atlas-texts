# Graftwick, on the Lode

Out on the plain you would take it for a wooded hummock and consider walking past: a long low dome of green, half a mile across, hardly higher than a barn, rising out of wet meadow where the sedges stand in standing water and every hollow holds last night's rain. Nothing about it announces a town. The sound does. From a mile off you can hear the Stamps — a deep wooden heartbeat, lift and fall, lift and fall — and beneath it a thinner music, *knock… knock… knock…*, the ram-pumps at the waterline keeping their own time. The plain is quiet. The dome is not.

The water comes first. All around the base of the dome the ground seeps — not springs exactly, but a long weeping line where the root-mat meets the river silt, hundreds of small outfalls braiding into rills that find the Lode together. The people call it the Weep. It has run without pause for as long as there are records, never swelling, never drying, and the water that comes out of it is cold and faintly sweet and tastes faintly of bark. A path of laid boards crosses it on the north side — corduroy, replaced plank by plank for so many centuries that the walkway itself has become a laminate of the town's whole history. You cross out of the plain's weather and into the Stock's.

The Stock is one plant.

Everyone in Graftwick knows this the way a family knows it shares a blood: not always, but at the moments that matter. Every stem in the dome is grafted to every other; the cambium runs continuous from the outermost whip to the heart of the oldest wall. Load one limb and a wall two streets away eases its weight. Tap the north face and the sap can be tasted at a weeping-point on the south. Four centuries of grafters bent the whips and bound them and waited, and the plant grew into the bindings and forgot them, and what stands now is a town that was planted as a promise and has been kept, limb by limb, ever since.

Inside the canopy the air changes. Warmer, wetter, greener. It smells of wet bark and bruised cress off the rills, of the sweet-sour breath of the vat-rooms, and under it all the smell of a stem newly cut — which is to say, the smell of the place itself. The warmth is the town's own doing as much as the weather's: the fermentation vats breathe heat, the heaps of prunings and spent tan steam gently at their edges, and the great walls hold the noon like ovens hold bread. Rain lands on you here already warm.

The lanes are floored in root. Surface roots were drawn flat and pleached across the mud generations ago, then worn by feet into something like old church flags: dark, damp, faintly figured, quiet underfoot even in rain. Gutter-channels are grown into the paving's edges, callused glassy-smooth, and every shower goes off along them toward the rills with a sound like applause that never quite finishes. There are three streets proper and a dozen lanes, a bridge of living arc over the mill-leat, a lock-up in a dead stub, a Counting Room, a school, and about two hundred and fifty souls. It is a town the way a ship is a town: everything within earshot, everyone in everyone's ledger. From the Weep on any side you can see the whole place at once, and at night you can stand on the boardwalk and count every lamp in it.

A house in Graftwick is not built. It is *begun*. A grafter ties whips into the shape of the intended rooms — a cage of living wattle the size of a cottage — and then the town and the plant take over together. The ties rot on their schedule; the stems thicken on theirs; the shape holds because from the first year someone prunes the weave open, and keeps it open, and in fifteen or forty years there is a room: walls of grained living wood, a doorway that was an arch of bent whips and has forgotten it was ever bent, shelves grown as stubs, pegs of peg-wood, furniture grown to its own floor, a window left open in the weave and glazed with a sheet of dried sap that turns the daylight the color of cider. At every door the jamb-wood is polished dark and glossy by generations of hands, so that the town's age shows not as ruin but as shine. That is what *old* means here. Nothing crumbles. Things wear in.

The town is crowded vertically the way it is crowded in plan. Workshops sit at the ground level where the weight is, living rooms a tier up in the thick of the weave, drying racks and airing lofts up in the small branches. Seasoned pipe runs through the walls — hollow stems, bored to a fair circle, married with sap-glue and tarred twine — sweating slightly at every joint, dripping into little grafted cups that overflow one into another down to the rills. The water-wards walk the mains each morning with a listening-horn and can name a failing joint by its drip-count. There is a cistern at the crown of the dome, three yards above the winter flood, standing on a frame of gray timber, filled by the rams and feeding the gravity mains. Everything leans on everything, stems osculate with stems, ladders are living, and a hand laid flat on almost any surface in Graftwick is touching the same body as the hand on the other side of the wall.

The loudness comes off the wheel. The Wheelhouse stands on the leat, a broad-breasted wheel in a house of gray timber, turning all day and driving the Stamps: timber pestles that lift and drop on tan-bark and shingle-stock, a cadence you feel through your soles anywhere in town. Off the same shaft run the bark-brakes and the long auger that trues the pipe-bores. The spinning gallery hums; the press-screws groan and once in a while shriek; the pit-saws rasp; adzes ring from the seasoning sheds; the rope-walk on the outer bank gives out a low steady growl as its top turns. It is a wooden loudness, all knocking and groaning and dripping — the town sounds like one very large instrument played very slowly — and once a day it stops. The Nooning, when the Stamps are stilled. The quiet is so total that babies wake and visitors laugh, and for a quarter of an hour you can hear the Weep, which is the truest sound Graftwick has.

What leaves the town is patience made salable: lacquer by the barrel; cordage by the mile; pipe seasoned seven years and proven under a season's head of water in the proving-tanks; bark-cloth oiled against rain; dyes; ink-galls; seed-stock bundled and labeled for stands that do not yet exist. What comes in rides up the Lode twice a month on the barge: iron, salt, whetstone, blue cloth — the entire mineral budget of a vegetable town, small as a coffin. The ledger in the Counting Room is a thing of years. An order sits on its page with a date like *cut ready, ninth year*, and no one chases it, because chasing is not a thing time does here.

Graftwick keeps two clocks. The first is the day's: Stamps, Nooning, rams at night, rain at its usual hours. The second is the generation's: the tying, the thickening, the closing-in of a roof that will be closed by the grandchildren of the woman who tied it. A grafter plants the walls of a house she will not live to roof, and says so without grief, the way a farmer sows an orchard. Children learn graft-cuts in school and keep their first ties, carefully dead and carefully kept, to be shown at their weddings. The laws are few and old and all of them concern keeping time with the plant: a count of taps allowed per stem; rest-years when a stand goes dark and untapped and its leaves come in a deeper green; the fire-law, which keeps every hearth on a clay platform in an open court with sand and a watch beside it, and makes the water-wards a kind of priesthood who are never wholly dry. Nothing is wasted, because waste here has a face: it would be next year's wall, or the one after.

The Stock keeps its chemistry to itself. Under the canopy the ground is strangely empty — few weeds, fewer beasts. Whatever the roots put out, most of the plain's small life declines the invitation, so that the greenest place for miles is also the stillest, once you tune out the machinery. What lives here lives by permission and in small numbers, and the town knows each of them. There are the gall-wasps, kept on the Ink Stand, grown specially for them. There are the sap-moths, ribbons of them at dusk around the vat-room vents; their larvae are let into the tap-lines on purpose to graze the crystallized sap from the bores — the only pest on the payroll. There is the heron — one heron, old and gray, named the Gray — who holds station at the proving-tank overflow and is pensioned in rejected test-water. There are leeches in the retting pits, sworn at with feeling. And twice a year someone takes an eel in the leat, and by supper the whole town has heard, because news here travels the way the water does: along the rills, door to door.

The river keeps its own slow treaty. On the Lode side the mat faces the current with a living cliff of woven root, a man's height, gray, threaded like rope-end; the river, which undermines every other bank in the world, has never got through this one. It undercuts; the mat answers with a foot of new growth; and so the two have stood at arm's length, equally matched, for as long as anyone has watched. Some springs the Lode comes up over its bars and stands among the outer stems, and Graftwick lifts its thresholds and works on, the Stamps going under their sheds, the town riding the flood on the hill it made itself — because the mat, feeding on its own centuries of leaf-fall and root-death and caught silt, has raised its own ground, a few patient yards at a time, above the plain that made it.

Rain comes often and means nothing by it. A shower crosses, the gutters applaud, the lanes stream — and then the sun returns and the Stock begins to smoke. Every wall breathes its wet back out; the warm air takes it up; and the whole town stands in a rising veil of its own breath, gold-green, dripping, loud, lit from inside its leaves. In that half-hour Graftwick looks least like a town and most like what it is: one enormous plant, warm, in balance, exhaling.

At night the lamps go up — little cups of sap-oil in horn panes, a slow constellation along the lanes. The wheel is stilled, the shops are dark, the rams keep their double knock. And underneath, unheard, the real industry of the place goes on: the thin work of the cambium, an inch of new wood laid down in the dark across the entire town at once — every wall, every jamb, every gutter and main. The town grows while it sleeps. In the morning the ledger is a day closer to every promise in it, the doorways are a hand's-breadth of a hand's-breadth closer to the shapes of the hands that hold them, and the Weep, which never pauses, has moved the river a measurable nothing — as it has every morning, without complaint, for four hundred years.
