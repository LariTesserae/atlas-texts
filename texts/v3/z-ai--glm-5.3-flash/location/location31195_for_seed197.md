The river flattens here. It has been flattening for a long time, patient as everything else in this basin, spreading brown and slow through a country of reeds and fog. The land is low — low enough that the water never fully commits to being land or river — and in the middle of this indecision stands the mound.

From the reed-flats it looks like a granary built by someone who had never seen one: a rounded green-black hulk, maybe the size of a threshing barn, no bigger, its rind smooth and faintly waxy, its base swallowed in reed-shadow. It breathes. Watch for a minute and you'll see it — the whole mass swelling and settling, once per long breath, and each exhale pushes a curl of warm air out of a seam on the landward side. The seam is a lip. The lip is a door. The smell coming out is green tea and animal musk and, underneath, something like a struck bell that hasn't finished ringing.

Push through.

The sound arrives first, and it arrives as pressure — a note so large and so constant that for the first several steps you don't hear it so much as wear it. Then the hollow opens up, and you understand where you are: not in a building, not in a forest, but inside a single living thing.

Everything here is the plant. That is the first rule and it admits no exception. There is no stone, no soil, no wood, no metal — there is only the one organism, playing every part. The walls are its tissue, a hand's-breadth thick in places, forearm-thick where generations of growth have layered, pale green under the rind, warm as skin, pulsing very slowly if you press a palm to it. The floor is its root-mat, grown into a shallow bowl and paved, by nothing but its own habit, with moss that feels underfoot like old velvet. The ceiling is its crown, turned inward, hung with filaments that weep constant beads. Even the light is its: oval panels where the wall has been sung thin — more on that — glowing an even green-gold, no harsh shadows anywhere, a working daylight with the faint lag of a thing being carried in by hand. The panels brighten with the outside morning a few minutes after the outside morning has already begun, and nobody here finds that strange, because almost nothing here is strange to them.

The hollow itself is an egg-shaped chamber you could cross in four hundred steps. This is worth noting, because from outside the mound cannot be more than eighty paces at its widest. Both figures are true. The first is truer indoors. No one who lives here has measured twice, and a visitor who measures twice stops being trusted with anything sharp, if they were trusted at all.

Most of this world is air. That's the second rule. The town — and it is a town, though no hand ever built it — clings to the walls in sparse tiers like swallow nests scaled to houses, and a few woven pods hang free in the middle of the void, each on a single cord of saliva-cured silk, each an isolated island connected to the rest by one trembling aerial street. The hollow is crowded the way a cathedral is crowded by its choir: many voices, immense air. You can stand on the floor and see nearly everything at once — every roost, every reservoir-cup, every grown knuckle where a line is anchored — and still feel that the place is full, because the sound fills it edge to edge, floor to crown, and does not stop.

The builders are the singers. Child-sized, leathery, bandy, with throats that unhinge like a frog's and hands like oiled rope, they have never held a tool and have never needed one. Their architecture is growth and their material is agreement.
