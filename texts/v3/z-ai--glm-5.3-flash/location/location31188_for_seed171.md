There is no ground here. That has to be said first, because standing on it you would swear there was — a surface, gently ridged, faintly springy, dark with water — but it is not ground. It is the current top of something that has been growing for a very long time and has grown into the shape of land. The surface gives underfoot like something muscled, holds, gives again. Grip it and water rises between your fingers. This is true everywhere. The entire place — and the place is continental; horizons here are a kind of local custom — the entire place is the top of a living mass that extends downward farther than anyone has plumbed, and it is rising. Too slowly to feel. Too fast to ignore, once you know.

At the margins, where the green thins enough to reveal its foundations, you can see what it is growing on: stone that is embarrassingly young. Black basalt with glassy edges, unscarred, unpatinated, still faintly warm in places, sweating constantly in the saturated air so that every face of it weeps a thin film that beads and runs and never gets anywhere. The rock looks like it cooled last week. Some of it did. Nothing has ever worn it, because nothing here is allowed to wear. Rain lands and does not carve. It beads, runs a few meters, and is taken up by root-mat before it can learn to cut. Water in this world has never been permitted to finish a valley.

That is the first rule, and once you see it you see it operating everywhere: nothing erodes because nothing gets the time. The place is geologically embryonic and biologically ancient at once, and the biology outruns the geology by whole geologic epochs.
