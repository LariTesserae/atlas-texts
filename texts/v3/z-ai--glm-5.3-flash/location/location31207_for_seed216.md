## The Garden

You come up the last of the stairs onto a terrace and the first thing you understand is that you are indoors. There is no horizon here. Above you, the wall continues upward until it fails into a soft grey luminescence — a ceiling of fog and glow that the tenders call the under-sky — and below you it falls away into a darkness where detail gives out, not sharply like night but gradually, like memory. Between this wall and the far wall — which you can see on still days, a faint green smear, a rumor of masonry at the limit of sight — there is air. Miles of it. A country of nothing.

That emptiness is the dominant fact of this place, more than the green, more than the stone. Everything alive here clings to the edges of a vast hollow volume, the way lichen clings to the side of a jar. The garden has the volume of a sky and the habit of a wall.

The air is warm. It is warm the way a mouth is warm, heavy and humid and slightly scented — wet loam, crushed leaf, the mineral smell of stone that sweats. No cold has ever been recorded here, not on the upper terraces where you now stand, which are the warmest of all because the deep exhales its heat upward through the gulf in slow, deliberate currents. The drizzle that beads on your arms is body-temperature. The water in the cisterns is warm as skin. Frost is a word the tenders use only in stories.

And everything is made. This takes longer to believe than the void does, because the making has been absorbed so completely into the growing that the two can no longer be told apart. There is no natural substrate anywhere. No bedrock, no wild stone, no soil that was not laid. The terraces you walk on are courses of fitted stone bedded on rammed earth, and the earth itself, if you dig, comes up in strata — seams, joints, a laid quality, as though the ground were brickwork all the way down. The great buttresses rising above you were dressed by hands. So were the channels that carry water along their flanks, moss-lined now, older than any memory. Split a branch of the wall-oaks and look at the grain: it runs in courses, like masonry. Cut deeper and you may find a mortise already waiting in the wood, a tenon grown to match. The vegetation here does not merely grow on the building. It continues it. Whatever the hands began, the plants finish, in the same patterns, with the same seams, as if the architecture persists in the living tissue like a song in a throat. The tenders have a saying: *made all the way down*. They do not know what it means. They use it the way we use the word *ground*.

The wear and the building have long since agreed on terms. Stair treads are dished like spoons by uncountable generations of feet, and the hollows have healed over with turf so fine it feels like velvet. A crack in the terrace below has been closing for what the tenders say is four hundred years — its edges stitched annually with wire-grass, the gap narrowing a finger's width each lifetime, patient as a wound. Nothing here is new and nothing here is ruined. The place has settled into its own state, a long equilibrium between the hand, the plant, and the slow rub of water. Repairs made this season are indistinguishable from work done a thousand years ago, because the same tools are used: stone, bone, wood, fiber. The tenders' greatest technology is rope. Rope bridges span the channels; rope ladders hang into the dark of overhangs; the ropes have grown into their anchors until no one could say which was holding which.

Water is everywhere and none of it hurries. The under-sky sweats a fine rain most days — not a fall but a condensation, droplets gathering in the high dark until they let go, drifting down through miles of air so slowly they arrive as mist, as dew, as a soft darkening of the moss. The tenders call it the weep. It stops for days at a time, and then the whole wall seems to hold its breath. Where the weep soaks into the masonry it travels, unseen, and re-emerges at the joints in springs the builders placed generations ago — water coming out of the wall exactly where hands decided it should, still running true because the plants maintain the channels as faithfully as they maintain the buttresses. Every terrace has its cistern, and every cistern has a tide. The water rises and falls on a rhythm no moon governs, slow swells in step with the brightening and dimming of the under-sky, and the tenders speak of the building's pulse without metaphor. The wall drinks. The wall breathes. Whether it is alive is not a question anyone here can settle, because everything you would test it with is also, in some sense, built.

The light deserves its own accounting. It does not come from anywhere. The under-sky glows the way deep overcast glows at dusk — a grey-gold luminescence with no source you can stare at, no point of failure, no warmth of its own. On the water, lantern-flora compensate: cisterns freckled with glow-moss, spore-clouds rising like slow sparks, flowers that burn faintly cold to call the moths in. Your shadow, when you have one, is soft-edged and doubled and does not quite agree with itself about where the light is. The tenders live inside a day of their own — the under-sky brightens and dims over a cycle longer than any outside day, a tide of visibility, and they measure their hours by it without believing anyone knows what hangs above the glow. It is one of the quiet jokes of the place: a world with a sky, and no one who has seen what the sky is.

Life is abundant here and almost perfectly silent, and these two facts are the same fact. Sound travels unreasonably far across the gulf; the void carries it the way a bell carries a strike, and everything that lives on the walls learned, long ago, the local law: **nothing makes an honest sound**. To be heard is to be caught, or to be courting. So the herds that graze the vertical meadows — pale, wide-footed beasts standing at ease on near-sheer faces, their fur beaded with drizzle — move in total quiet, thousands of them, a migration along the wall that you can watch for a full turning of the under-sky and never hear. The gliders that cross the gulf are enormous — wingspans the tenders measure in rope-lengths, though no one has ever gotten close — and they pass overhead like weather, like cloud-shadow, muting even the air.
