# The Sumpworks

There is no ground here. That's the first thing to understand, and the last thing anyone accepts. What looks like terrain — low, flattened, stretching to a horizon that is itself an artifact — is floor. Plate upon plate of riveted iron and verdigrised bronze, laid down so long ago that the memory of any substrate beneath it survives only in the engineering: the whole plain is built to *float*, to ride on water that has no bottom anyone has ever sounded.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It comes up. It weeps through ten thousand seams in the plating, silver threads in the dark, gathering in shallow pools that never deepen because the drainage channels — ancient, worn smooth as river stones — carry it off somewhere. Nobody knows where. The channels predate everything else, which is the strangest part, because everything else is very old indeed.

**The dark.** There is almost no light and what there is does not behave. No sun has been seen here in living memory, or in dead memory either — the archives, such as they are, mention a *sky problem* in their oldest strata and then stop mentioning sky at all. Instead there are work-lamps: sparse, widely spaced, great caged filaments burning a sullen orange at intersections of the causeways, each one alone in its own puddle of visibility, each one surrounded by kilometers of nothing. Between the lamps, the darkness is total and wet. Condensation forms on your face in the dark. The air itself is close to dewpoint always — the whole world breathes moisture.

**The sound.** This is the thing that undoes visitors, though there are no visitors. The Sumpworks never stops making noise. Deep beneath the plates — somewhere below, in the flooded understructure — enormous engines turn. They have always turned. They pump, or circulate, or compress; no one alive knows their purpose and no one has ever proposed finding out, because the machinery is the oldest thing here, older than the floor built over it, older than the archives. The sound is not mechanical in the way of pistons and gears. It is a pressure. A slow sub-bass heartbeat that you feel in your teeth, in the water standing in the puddles — the puddles ripple in standing wave-patterns, geometric, patient. Above the heartbeat: hissing steam from a thousand relief valves, the drip and trickle of weeping seams, and every few minutes, from somewhere in the dark, a long metallic groan as some vast plate settles a fraction of a millimeter into its final position.

**The settlement.** It took ten thousand years and it is nearly finished. That is the mood of the place — not decay but *completion*. The builders are gone or have become indistinguishable from maintenance. What remains is a sparse population of keepers — human, or close enough — living in small lamp-lit stations scattered across the plain, hundreds of meters apart, connected by causeways with raised railings against the water. Their work is not construction. It is *tending*. They grease bearings they've never seen. They record water levels in ledgers whose earliest pages are the oldest writing in the world. They replace lamps. The whole culture is a watch-shift that has forgotten what it watches for.

Sparse vegetation: thin mats of pale filamentous growth — engineered, someone insists, everything here is engineered — clinging to the wet plating around the weep-seams, drinking condensation, glowing faintly blue-white where it is thickest. It is the only thing that grows. Nothing else lives here. No birds, no insects, no fish in the black water. The keepers say the engines sing at a pitch that living things decline, and perhaps that is the purpose.

**The activity.** The place is in motion, but on its own schedule. Weather happens indoors, so to speak: pressure cycles move through the enclosed dark like fronts, raising mist off the pools in slow banks that roll across the plain and past the lamps, and when a mist-bank meets a lamp-cage the light turns into a soft pulsing column, visible for kilometers, and the keepers mark it in their logs — *column at Station 9, third watch* — the way farmers once marked rain.

Once a cycle — roughly every nineteen days by the keepers' count — the engines change key. The heartbeat drops, the puddles' standing waves reorganize into new patterns, every relief valve on the plain exhales at once with a sound like the world sighing, and the keepers stop what they are doing and stand still until it passes. They do not know what the change means. Their oldest instruction, carved into the lintel of the first station, reads:

**THE WATER MUST KEEP MOVING.**

So it moves. It has always moved. The floor rides it, the lamps burn over it, the pale growth drinks it, and far below the hearing of anything, the engines turn in the dark, pumping the oldest work in the world toward a finish that has almost, almost arrived.
