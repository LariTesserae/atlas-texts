You hear the mountain for three days before you see it.

The plain you cross is what remains of a range — hills worn to knuckles under a blanket of dry grass, old riverbeds full of nothing, stone so old its edges have given up. This whole country is a graveyard of elevation. Everything here has already been ground down to its final patience. Except one thing. On the horizon, in the direction of the sound, stands a single cloud the size of a small nation, and it has not moved in anyone's memory, and it does not intend to.

The sound arrives first because it is already everywhere: a note so low you don't hear it so much as notice your molars holding it. Children in the villages down on the plain grow up with that hum in their teeth and assume every place in the world hums. It takes travel to learn otherwise. The mountain is not making noise. The mountain is holding one long note under everything, the way a bell holds its pitch after the strike, except this bell has been ringing since before there were ears it could have frightened.

Walk toward it. The cloud stays where it is; the sound grows.

Near the mountain's skirt the terrain changes character. The dry flanks are scored with flutes — vertical channels worn into the stone by wind, thousands of them, all leaning the same way like combed hair. Put your hand into one: glass-smooth, warm, worn like driftwood. These are old pipes. Old storm-tracks. The wind that carved them has moved uphill, generation by generation of storms, and these lower fields are abandoned — a dead organ standing in the sun, silent, dry, its pipes pointed at nothing. You can read the mountain's biography in these fields the way you'd read a tree's rings: here the storm lived ten thousand years ago; here it lived when the stair was cut; here it has never lived at all. The stone keeps every address the weather has ever had.

Climb. Somewhere on the flank there is a stair — steps pecked by hand into a corridor between two shoulders of rock, each tread dished in the middle like a worn spoon. No one knows who cut them. The keepers, if you can call them that, use the word inherited. The steps climb out of the sun into the cloud belt, and somewhere in that climb the world's rules change.

You enter the roar.

This is not wind as you know wind. The summit has been carved by its own weather for so long that the two of them — stone and storm — have grown into each other, and the sound is their shared body. There is a bass note, the fundamental, alive in the great hollows under the plateau, and it does not enter your ears; it takes you by the sternum. Above it, the flutes: the living ones, near the cloud line, each spire a pipe, each pipe a note, and the wind swings and selects, plays arrays of them like a hand running along a keyboard, so the whole mountain changes key as the storm reorganizes — and the storm reorganizes constantly, hour by hour, like a sleeper turning over, like something dreaming hard. Over everything, thunder that walks the amphitheater basins in long loops; a single clap comes back seven times, each return fainter and later, until the last echo arrives so long after its cause that it feels less like sound than like remembering. And through all of it, the hiss of fog moving through stone teeth at speed.

Speech is impossible out here. Conversation is hand-sign. You feel your own voice in your jaw when you try. After a day in the roar you stop being deafened and simply get handled — the sound is a weather you live inside, and your body, being older than you are, adapts, the way a body adapts to cold.

Then, at some unmarked moment, you cross a line you could draw with chalk, and everything stops.

Not gradually. The wind rounds a shoulder of stone and the roar stays on the other side of that line, the way water stays behind a weir. Inside is a pocket of still air — a wind-shadow, a lee, a hollow where the storm's fingers can't quite reach. And because still air at this altitude holds whatever drifts into it, the pocket is full of fog, and the fog is condensing on everything, and everything is green.

This is the garden. Moss deep enough to swallow your knee, dense as pile carpet, drinking straight out of the air. Ferns unrolling in the drip of the overhang. Small orchid-things clinging to stone with the grip of old men holding railings. One or two cloud-trees, stunted, older than any human institution, bent permanently away from the roar — their growth rings are calendars, fat rings for calm decades, thin rings for storm years. The tree is a seismograph of the weather's patience.

Here is the first rule of the mountain, the one everything hangs on: green is not a place, it's an acoustic condition. Silence is a substance here. It pools where the air pools, and where it pools, water condenses, and where water condenses, life crowds in. The gardens of this summit are islands of quiet — fifty paces wide, a hundred, sometimes less — set in a roaring stone sea, separated by hours of bare fluted rock. Walk the plateau and you can go the whole morning seeing nothing but wind-carved stone, and then round one spire and step, without transition, into a dripping cathedral of green.

And the silence itself is strange, though the strangeness is gentle, almost domestic. Sound behaves like liquid in these pockets. Shout into a pool of hush and the shout doesn't echo — it lingers. A word spoken at dusk is still faintly present at midnight, thinning the way a puddle thins. The keepers know which hollows hold sound longest and treat them with the care other people give wells. A stone dropped in the deep hollow under the plateau is reported to still be falling, acoustically, for a very long time.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. No rain worth the name, no rivers, no standing pools. The whole hydrology of the summit is condensation: fog beading on stone, running in threads, seeps that splash out of a wall and vanish into moss within ten meters. Everything drinks the air. Everything lives downwind of something taller.

The people who come here — and a few always have — understand this economy perfectly, and their technology is exactly equal to it: stone, wood, fiber, patience. Their fog-walls are half-circles of dry-stacked rock set against the spires, opening downwind, and they work not by catching water but by widening quiet — a stone's throw of artificial stillness, and behind it the green answers within a season. Their drip-lines are twisted cords hung from fluted overhangs, wicking condensation drop by drop into basins ground out of solid rock with river cobbles carried up from the plain. One drop at a time is the only speed water travels here, and the basins have accepted that; some have been filling so long they have moss of their own. There are cairns across the plateau spaced at what the keepers call a pause apart — the distance you can cover in one stretch of quiet — so the whole summit is surveyed and waymarked in units of silence. There is one shelter: a flute's mouth roofed with slabs, its walls blackened by old hearths, a polished stone tool left point-up, which is the local custom for *I will return*.

Whether anyone will is the open question. The mountain's population is zero, or one, or two, depending on the season and whom you believe. The basins are tended. The nets are mended. And a few years ago, someone set a fresh stone on the cairn below the south stair — you can tell fresh stone by its lack of lichen — and no one in the valley admits to the climb.

The animals are few, and all of them are punctuation. Fog-midges swarm in the pauses, so dense over the gardens they look like slow smoke. In the drip-channels lives a small amphibian, skin clear as wet glass, its heartbeat visible, its whole biography contained in one seep. And once a day, usually during a pause, a large dark bird crosses the plateau without a single wingbeat — riding the storm's standing waves the way a surfer rides a wave that never breaks — gone in a minute, crying once, one note, and the note is the same note as the flutes, one octave up. The keepers say the mountain taught it. The bird would say, if it spoke, that it taught the mountain.

The storm has a rhythm inside its chaos, and the rhythm has a floor: once a day, deep in the night, the whole engine eases. The flutes fall out one by one. The bass drops below even its usual threshold. For the length of a held breath — twenty minutes, sometimes an hour — the mountain holds its breath, and in that held breath the torn cloud cap drifts open and shows the sky: ordinary stars, plainly burning, which after all that roar is the most extraordinary thing on the summit. In the quiet you can hear each drop condensing out of the fog and striking the basins. Tick. Tick. From far across the plateau, someone else's drip-line, also ticking. The mountain drinking. Then the breath ends, the roar comes back like a tide that never learned to leave, and the stars close over.

The light, by contrast, is the plainest thing here. Pearl overcast under the cap; honest sun in the pauses; nothing strange in it at all. You come to be grateful for that. It's the light that convinces you the rest is real.

The stone is finished. That is what the geology of the plain means, and the driftwood wear of every ledge, and the fat old rings in the bent trees. The stone has nothing left to become. The storm is not finished and never has been — it reorganizes itself hourly, kills one garden in a season, opens a new pool of quiet behind a spire the next, plays its organ, forgets songs and learns them again. And everything that lives on this summit lives exactly on the line between those two facts: on a stillness carved by something ancient, kept wet by something that never rests.

Two days down, on the plain, the roar fades from your ears but not from your bones — you will hear it in your teeth for a week, fainter nightly, like a bell rung somewhere below the floor of the world.

And the keepers have one word, one only, for the mountain, for the storm, and for singing. Their gesture of greeting is a raised palm. So is their gesture for *quiet*.

On this mountain, they have always been the same sign.
