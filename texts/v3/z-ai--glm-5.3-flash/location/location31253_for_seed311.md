# The Cistern

You do not arrive here. You condense into it — one moment you are pressure and cold elsewhere, and the next you are inside a space that could not be more than forty meters across and yet insists, through some fault in the geometry, that it has streets.

This is a drowned room. Not a sea — a *room*, sealed and full, filled to its ceiling with water so clear it seems embarrassed to exist, as if the liquid itself hasn't decided whether it's water or glass. The cold here is not the cold of the deep ocean. It is the cold of a cellar, of stone that has held the same temperature for as long as it has held anything. Cool, patient, slightly mineral against whatever part of you registers temperature.

The city is small. That is the first thing the strangeness teaches you: everything here is scaled to something. The towers — and they are towers, slender hexagonal spires of a material that is neither metal nor ceramic but some self-assembled lattice, grown rather than built — rise only a few body-lengths from the floor before terminating in capped florets, like coral that studied architecture. Between them run boulevards four meters wide, laid with a precision that feels less like planning and more like crystallization. This is a young place. The erosion score of the landscape is nearly zero: every edge is sharp, every seam still glows faintly with the heat of its own manufacture, and the sediment that should be settling — isn't. The water refuses to carry dust. The city is being *kept* new.

And it is packed. There is no vacancy anywhere. Every surface carries structure: conduits ribbing the undersides of archways, filaments strung between spires like harp strings, walls of latticework so fine you have to be within arm's length to see the individual cells — each one holding a bead of light, a droplet of stored luminance, waiting. The whole chamber hums at the edge of audibility, a chord composed of a thousand small pumps and resonators and things you have no noun for, and the sound doesn't echo so much as accumulate. It gets louder the longer you stay. Not because anything turns up its volume — because the room is full of sound the way it is full of water, and you are simply now part of the saturation.

There is vegetation here, barely — a permission granted sparingly. Along the boulevards, in deliberate rows, grow ribbons of dark green frond-grass, ankle-height, each stalk rooted not in soil but in a socket of engineered substrate, fed by capillary channels you can trace glowing faintly beneath the pavement. Someone — something — decided exactly how much life this place could afford, and planted precisely that much, no more. The fronds sway in the current of the room's circulation, a slow tide that moves through the chamber on a cycle: gathering at one wall, breathing along the ceiling, pooling at the far corner, then reversing. You learn its rhythm the way you'd learn a neighbor's footsteps.

And there are no animals. Not one. No fish, no worms, no drifting larvae, no bacteria-fog. The city is inhabited by *processes* instead — and this is where the tech exceeds any vocabulary you brought with you. Watch a spire long enough and you'll see its skin migrate, ribbons of material flowing upward like slow flame, repairing a scratch before the scratch is finished forming. Watch a boulevard junction and you'll see it reconfigure — the street widening by a meter, a filament bridge extruding to a neighboring tower — as if the city is solving a problem you can't perceive, in real time, at a pace slightly faster than comfortable. The whole settlement behaves like a single organism thinking with architecture. Perhaps it is one. Perhaps the "urban" is a misreading, and this is one machine the size of a chapel, and the streets are its circuitry, and you are standing inside a processor that dreamed itself a city because it was lonely.

The light is good, ordinary light — warm-white, seemingly sourceless, falling evenly from everywhere the way light falls in an overcast noon. That ordinariness is the most disorienting feature. The tech, the density, the humming geometry — all of it lit like a pleasant kitchen. Somewhere in the lattice there must be emitters; you never find them.

The tide turns. The frond-grass all leans left together, then rights itself. A spire near you finishes growing and caps itself with a soft, bell-like *tong* that joins the accumulated chord. The water carries the note to every wall at once.

Nothing here eats anything else. Nothing here dies. It only builds, and circulates, and hums, in its drowned pocket of kept-new stone, waiting — you suspect — to be given a larger room.
