# The Weep

The mountain is young, and it has not yet learned what to do with its water.

You know a young mountain by its edges. Old ranges have been licked smooth by time; this one is all knapped flint and fresh scars, slabs split along their hearts and left open, the exposed faces showing the color of the mountain's interior — a blue-gray that no weather has yet dulled. Frost-shatter works on it constantly. At night, in the stillness, you can hear it working: a tick, a tick, a longer crack, as if the peak were quietly finishing itself. The talus fields beneath the crags are angular as dropped crockery, none of it rounded, all of it sharp enough to bite through boot leather. There has not been time for anything here to soften.

The climb is long. The place itself is not.

The Weep is a bowl at the very crown of the mountain — a shallow hollow a few hundred paces across, walled by a ring of unweathered stone fangs that stand up out of the summit like the buttresses of some unfinished cathedral. You can see the whole of it from the rim. You can walk its circumference in under an hour, and most of that hour is spent stepping carefully. Everything the place contains fits inside that ring: the water, the green, the animals, the one stone hut. It is a room. It happens to be a room set on top of the world.

And it is nearly all empty. This is the first arithmetic to understand about the Weep: it is abundant by count and vacant by volume. Bare, luminous rock fills almost every yard of it — pale feldspar and quartz that the sun gets into like a lamp. The green occupies perhaps a tenth of the floor. The animals gather in perhaps a fifth of that. Between presences there is nothing but space and light, and the light is the dominant substance of the place. Above the rim, the sky has begun to darken toward the blue-black of altitude; the sun stands in it like a nail of fire. Below, the glare comes up off the stone so hard that shadows go thin and mean, and the whole bowl seems lit from beneath as well as above. Look long and the world develops a second, glittering copy of itself.

The cold and the brightness do not cancel each other; they coexist. That is the second arithmetic. The air is half-breath and it is killing — any skin left out too long goes wooden, breath crystallizes audibly in it — but the dark stone in the sun is warm. Every boulder is a small hearth. Frost that forms everywhere by night is gone from the sunward faces within minutes of dawn, and the rocks steam. Crouch against the south side of a stone and you are almost comfortable; stand up into the wind and the moisture in your eyelashes stiffens. Everything here is decided by warmth of stone, not warmth of air. Life has organized itself around this fact completely.

So has the silence. The air is too thin to carry much. Beyond ten paces, sound becomes gesture: a finch's wings are a flicker, its song nothing at all. What you hear instead is the near world with terrible intimacy — your own pulse, the creak of your collar, the crystalline fizz of your breath, the thread-thin ring of water finding a pool. The quiet is not vacant; it is granular, made of a thousand tiny adjacent sounds, each one arriving as if inside your own ears. And when danger crosses the bowl — the falcon's shadow, the white stoat threading the moss — the silence does not break. It deepens. The hares freeze mid-chew, the finches pull their heads in, the insects settle, and the hush becomes so absolute and so textured that you can *hear* a predator the way you'd hear a hole in a piece of music. Stillness is the only long-distance language this place allows.

The water comes out of the top. That is the strangeness no one has explained. Springs should not live at a summit, but the mountain is still rising, its bones still settling, and the water has not found its level yet — it weeps from the crown itself, seeping from hairline fractures along the highest seams, running in veins no wider than a finger down the inside of the bowl. It gathers in pools the size of tabletops and never deeper than a hand, because the young rock drinks it back through cracks almost as fast as it pools. Dampness is therefore everywhere and standing water almost nowhere. Frost is the true rain of the Weep. Every night the bowl hoarses white — feathers of ice on every grass tip, rime building woolly on the windward faces of the fangs — and every clear dawn the sun swings over the rim and the whole place exhales at once, a slow smoke lifting off the green. Because the air inside the fangs is utterly still, the vapor rises in a straight, thin column and hangs there through the morning. The valley people far below know the Weep is "on" when they see the white thread standing over the peak.

Rain does come, occasionally — sudden, vertical, violent, hail mixed in, over in a quarter hour, the sky wrung out and blue again — and after it the bowl blooms as if scalded.

Because the green here is genuinely lush. Not alpine-sparse: lush. The seep-lines are bordered by moss deep enough to swallow a fist, dense and almost black-green, warm inside its own insulation, scalloped at the living edge where it advances onto bare stone a finger-width a year — impossibly fast, for this altitude. Cushion plants sit on the mats like loaves, and in the bright season they detonate with white and violet stars. Dwarf willow creeps along the wettest veins. Ferns crowd the spring-mouths in the cracks. The trick is the fog: on most afternoons the cloud layer below climbs the mountain and *grazes* the crown, passing slowly through the fangs, and every leaf and moss-tip combs water out of it. The windward side of each stone goes beaded. The mats drink for an hour and bank it for a day. Even the fangs carry life — map-patches of lichen in gray and sulfur-yellow furring the lee sides, along with the mineral salt-blooms the valley climbers come to scrape.

And the animals. By any count, the bowl is crowded: hundreds of pale stone-hares grazing the flower mats in loose silent herds; a colony of finches — rust and snow colored — nesting in every crack of the fangs, who do not sing but *display*, rising in spirals and flashing their wings, their courtship and their arguments conducted entirely in light; midges beyond numbering, dancing in the sunlit air, their faint sizzle audible only when you're inside the swarm and dead silent ten feet off; the white stoat hunting the moss-edges like water finding cracks; and the falcon, pale as the feldspar itself, perched motionless on a fang for hours, then dropping without one sound. Predator and prey are in constant, quiet motion — the hares' ears swiveling, the stoat's pauses, the falcon relocating twice a day. The whole bowl runs on attention.

The numbers don't add up, though. The bowl cannot have seeded all this life; there is nowhere for it all to have come from. Yet each morning, at first light, a few new animals appear at the rim — a hare, a pair of finches — coming up from directions no one has ever traced. There are no passes that high, no ledges that route. They simply arrive, walk down into the bowl, and join the others. The climbers have looked for the paths for generations. The paths decline to exist.
