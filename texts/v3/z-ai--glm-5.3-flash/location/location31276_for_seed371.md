Start at the top, because there is nothing above this.

The sky here is already black before night properly falls — the air is too thin to hold much light, and the stars don't twinkle. They burn steady, unwinking, like pinholes in a curtain, and the cloud deck lies far below the summit like a second sea, faintly gray under starlight, motionless from this height. The mountain stands up out of that sea into a darkness with weather nowhere in it. It is cold in the way that is difficult to think through. The wind at the rim makes a thin dry hiss across the scree, and below the hiss, under everything, the mountain is breathing.

This is the strange arithmetic of the place: it sits near the crown of an enormous, recently grown volcanic spire — the summit region is young enough that its edges are still sharp, still cut, still unsoftened by a single season of real weathering — and yet the living heart of it is small. A pocket. A room. Two hundred meters across, perhaps, ringed by black glassy blocks frost-shattered into angles, and you could walk the entire country of this place in ten minutes. What fills those ten minutes is another matter.

Because the mountain has grown a new head, and the new head runs hot.

Deep in the pocket, a field of fumaroles breathes through the floor of young rock — dozens of mouths, most of them no wider than a fist, a few like open furnaces with a dull coal-red glow far down their throats. Steam pours out of them in ribbons and columns, and where the steam meets the high cold air it condenses instantly, so the whole pocket lives inside a slow churn of fog that is being manufactured from below and destroyed from above, endlessly, every second. The air smells of struck matches and hot stone, with a cold ozone edge above it. This is the industry of the place: no artifacts, no structures, nothing made — and yet nothing about it is idle. Fissures draw air in; the buried magma body boils water; mineral-choked channels carry it, sealing their own walls with dissolved silica until the mountain grows itself plumbing, rigid white pipes that whistle at their constrictions like valves. Pressure builds, releases, builds again. The vents chuff on cycles — some of them seconds long, some slow, freighted exhalations you feel in the sternum before you hear them. It is a factory with no hands. An engine with no engineer. The machinery is the mountain, running since the eruption that raised this crown a few years ago, and it has never once been serviced, and it does not need to be.

The water here exists only in transit. That is its whole condition. It arrives as vapor, spends a few seconds as fog, a few minutes as condensate, a few hours as rime — and then the ash drinks it. Outside the steam plumes the ground is kiln-dry, dusty as old bone; a droplet that lands there vanishes into the slope within the width of a hand. There are no streams, no puddles — except where the vents have built tiny terraces of porcelain-white sinter, plates and saucers of mineral that hold milky tepid water long enough for life to halo them. Pockets of water in a larger dryness: the only standing water on the summit, each one smaller than a dinner table, each one steaming faintly, each one ringed with color.

And the life — this is where the place contradicts itself beautifully. Vegetation so lush it qualifies as jungle, in darkness, in cold, with no rain, at a height where almost nothing on Earth should bother growing. But the garden here does not garden on sunlight. It gardens on the steam.

It is not green. That is the first thing. Under the starlight and the ember-glow of the deepest vents, the pocket reads in bone and butter and rust: white bacterial mats flowing like broth down the drainage lines, orange and cream crusts, sulfur laid on thick around the vent mouths in yellow waxen slabs. Above the chemistry, the plants. Moss first — mats of it a meter deep in the warm seams, springy, steam-soaked, so dense a hand pushed into it finds warmth like something living, because it is, acres of it in miniature. Then ferns, threading the warm fissures the way they do in volcanic ground everywhere, their fiddleheads slick with condensate. Then the epiphytes stacked on the epiphytes — curtains of drip-moss hanging from overhangs, crusts over crusts over crusts, every surface occupied, nothing bare, everything near-touching, layered four and five deep. And rising out of the middle of it, the great rosettes: cabbage-giant plants with felted skirts of dead leaves insulating their stems, hollow trunks holding towers of water that freeze as slow slush on the coldest nights instead of bursting — living water towers at the top of the sky, drinking fog.

Because fog is the whole water supply. The plants comb it out of the air. Condensation drips from every curled leaf-tip, a constant irregular percussion, the world's slowest rain falling upward from the ground. The vents even act as couriers: their updrafts lift spores and scatter them through the pocket, so the mountain's machinery does the gardening too. No animal has ever pollinated anything here. No animal has ever crossed the frost line. Nothing flies, nothing crawls, nothing gnaws. The lushness has no defenses at all — no spines, no toxins, no camouflage — because it grew up entirely unwitnessed. Everything is soft. It may be the softest landscape there is.

The whole garden may even be one creature. With no pollinators and no seed-carriers, it spreads by spore and runner only, and the likeliest truth is that this is the descendant of a handful of arrivals, possibly of one — a single mindless body wearing ten thousand costumes, rooted in a hundred seams, feeding on one heat.

Then there is the sound, and the sound is loud, and none of it has a voice.

The hiss-choir of the vents, each mouth a slightly different pitch, so the pocket drones in chords. The chuff-cycle of the larger ones, rhythmic as pistons. The tick and crackle of rime ice growing feather by feather along the rim — ice roses, frozen smoke, thickening until they calf off the rocks in small calving events, little glaciers the size of bread loaves slumping with a clatter into the scree. The drip-percussion. The pop of sporangia drying and splitting. And beneath all of it, in the moss, in the rosettes, the sound almost nothing else on Earth makes audibly: growth. The plants here grow fast enough to hear — creaks, ticks, the dry click of a frond unrolling in the warm fog — the whole garden faintly crackling with its own expansion, like a bonfire made of patience. Put your ear against the ground and there is the plumbing too: a gurgle, a knock, pressure moving in the deep pipes. The mountain runs its night shift loudly.
