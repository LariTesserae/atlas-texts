You hear the plateau through the soles of your feet before you hear it with your ears. That is the first rule of the Stave, and everything else follows from it: the air up here is thin, cold, and all but useless for carrying sound — a voice spoken into it dies within a few arm-lengths — but the ground itself is a live wire. The whole plateau hums. Step onto it and the hum comes up through your bones, a deep organ drone that never quite resolves, and your footsteps, absurdly, squeak. The living surface flexes under your weight like the deck of a ship.

The sun is low and pale and does not appear to move. It skims the horizon the way it has for as long as anyone has been here to watch — a white coin in a dark dome — and because the air is dry and high and thin, the stars never fully leave. They stand in the noon sky like dust on glass. Shadows are long at every hour. No one here navigates by sight; sight is the poor relation. The dark on the Stave is not an absence so much as the visual register of a place that has invested everything in the other channel.

Up close, the ground is a hide — the locals call it exactly that — a continuous skin of overlapping bark-plates and dry leaf-scales, glazed like old ceramic at the joints, hairy with gray lichen-fuzz at the seams. It creaks and shifts with the thermal cycles, tick-tick, tick, as the cool air re-tensions the wood beneath. Cut into it anywhere and you find the sap: not water in any sense, but a bright silver resin, dense as quicksilver and slow as cooling glass, welling up in beads that harden into dark, glassy pitch-stone. Trace chemists will tell you the sap holds a ghost of wetness, some dilute memory of solvent in its long molecules, but that is ancestry, not need. Nothing on the Stave has ever been wet. Whatever water the world once owned, the deep roots drank the last of it before anyone here learned to speak.

What replaced it is the second rule, and it is the load-bearing one: **nothing drinks; everything eats sound.** The membranes of the wood are pressure-engines. Vibration drives them through their chemistry the way sunlight drives a leaf elsewhere — flex into sugar, ring into growth. Loud is not a nuisance here. Loud is rain. The scarce substance on the plateau is silence, and both the ecology and the town are organized around that scarcity.

The drinkers stand scattered across the open ground at wide intervals, because the plateau is vast and mostly empty — a region of distances, not a tangle. These are the Pipes: hollow spires of coaxed wood, some slender as chimneys, some broad as silos, their mouths angled like lilies. Wind enters and each pipe gives its one note, and the notes do not blend so much as coexist — a chord the size of a county. The greatest of them, the Bourdon, is so large that its note is less heard than weathered; it passes through the chest cavity of every living thing on the plateau and is felt as a slight pressure, a gravity with an opinion.
