# The Level

You hear it for two days before there is anything to see. The sound comes up out of the fog like weather, except weather arrives, and this has already arrived — it is simply the condition of the air, the way cold is a condition of the air. You stop trying to locate it. There is nothing to locate. What you took for silence out here in the fog was only distance, and the distance is running out, and when it does, you are there.

The old word for this place, in the few mouths that still keep words, translates as *level*. Not the adjective. The act.

Begin with the water, because the water is the first thing wrong. The greatest sea in the world is knee-deep. It has no reason to be deep. Everything low in the world is already here — every river was a courier, every flood a shipment, and whatever the high places were made of has been brought down and spread and brought down and spread for longer than building takes, until the bringing and the spreading are the only story left. You are standing on the answer to every mountain. The ocean lies across that answer like varnish: a hand deep, a continent wide. You could cross most of the largest water there is in a good pair of boots. Its authority was never depth. It is area, and patience.

Because the land is flat in a way that flatness stops describing. Nothing vertical survives here. Whatever the earth ever raised, the water has spent the whole of time talking back down — hills to hummocks, hummocks to sheets of mud — and the only things that stand are things that grew up *after* the lowering learned its work: reeds, roots, the knees of drowned trees, and the fires, of which more later. The horizon is the same in every direction, which means it is no direction. You will look for the middle of it. Don't. Flatness like this has no middle, or every point is the middle; the Level has never needed to choose.

The warmth is a fact you wear. The air is a bath you swim through while standing still — you-warm, blood-warm, warm as broth left out. Your breath does not show, because the air and your breath have agreed on a temperature. Nothing dries, ever. Everything weeps: the reeds weep, the black posts weep, your own eyelashes run. Rain happens constantly and is nearly invisible — it does not fall so much as *settle*, arriving as a hiss before it arrives as a wetness, so that you hear weather the way you'd hear someone entering a room behind you.

And the light — there is no sun, exactly. There are brightenings. The sky is the underside of a pearl; noon is a rumor; day and night differ mostly by degree of amber. The light is old, too — it has been bounced back and forth between the cloud lid and the warm water so many times it has worn itself vague. Which brings you to the thing you will not stop noticing, once you notice it: you have no shadow. None. You will find yourself looking down for it and feel, for the first time in your life, what shadows were actually *for* — checking. Here nothing checks. Here the eyes are demoted. The ears rule, and the ears are drowning.

The sound itself is the strangest arithmetic in the world: there is no single loud thing. Not wind, not surf, not beast. It is the sum of a billion soft events — drizzle touching water, gas escaping the mud in pips and belches, a trillion droplets ticking off reed-tips into puddles, the slow slide of sheets of water over sheets of mud. Deafening, made entirely of quiet things touching each other. Stand in it long enough and it stops being a sound and becomes a substance, a medium you move through, and then some nights it *resolves* — for one breath the roar organizes into something that could be a crowd, a market at dawn, someone calling a name across water, a name you half know — and then the arithmetic takes it back and grinds it down to seething again. The Level keeps every sound it has ever been given. It pays them back at random, with interest.

The water breathes. That is the calendar here. Once or twice a day the whole shallow sea remembers it is one thing and moves as one thing: a front of water, ankle-high, silver-edged, horizon to horizon, advancing at a walk or slower, and where it has withdrawn, the flats steam and seethe in the interval, mud boiling quietly with escaping gas, every crevice exhaling. The returning water does not splash. It *presents itself* — a decision arriving over kilometers. Nobody asks what hour it is. You ask where the sea is in its breathing, and you plan your life accordingly: cross the pan while it is bare, get off the mat before the sheet comes back, let the fish sleep.

For the fish sleep. The water is warm and utterly full — that is its problem, and the key to most of what lives here. Full water has no room for air; the chemistry of this sea is crowded, used-up, half-rotted; so the fish that remain have learned to breathe the sky. There are lung-things sealed in mud cocoons, napping between the water's sentences, waking only when the sheet-tide returns and touches them like a hand. Birds arrive in pulses — shell-swallows that come from beyond the cloud lid in a single ribbon, skim the advancing silver edge, and are gone, never landing, never staying, as if this were only a corridor to them, which it may be. Once in many days the swarm passes: one midge-cloud the size of weather, a living fog bank that moves over you for an hour like slow black rain made of touching, and then nothing — no bite, no buzz, no wing — for days. Life here does not spread itself evenly. It detonates and abstains.

And there is the wader. Grey, absurdly tall, standing at the far edge of what counts as far, doing nothing you can call eating or drinking or waiting. You will not get closer to it. No one gets closer. It is always exactly as far away as the last time, and since there are no shadows to corroborate anything, you cannot even say it moved. Someone once found a single print where the bird had stood, and the print was deeper than the mud should allow. After that, nobody went looking twice.

The ground itself is a rumor. Mostly there is no ground — there are grades of mud, from broth in the channels to cream over the flats to leather where a rare brightening has touched it, and under all of it, shell compacting patiently toward stone, taking its time the way everything here takes its time. Where the water lingers longest when it leaves, it writes in salt: white lace crusts, the one brightness the Level permits itself, glowing faintly after dark like spilled sugar. There are pans of fused shell you can walk on — the only honest flooring in the world — and there are the quaking mats, drifting islands of packed living-and-dead root that roll like waterbeds when you step, so that crossing one is an act of humility performed on all fours. The mats migrate with the sheet-tide. The islands herd.

The trees are cypress-cousins gone black, mostly dormant, ranks standing in knee-deep water on buttress legs, breathing through wooden snorkels — thousands of knees around each trunk like a congregation at prayer. The ranks thin, and then a single tree stands alone for a day's walk, and then the ranks resume, and you will never know if you passed one forest or one tree repeating. Dead timber buried in the airless mud for millennia comes out black and dense as iron; strike it and it rings.

Now the marks. Because there are marks, though almost no one speaks of them. Arcs of shell, laid with eerie accuracy, going nowhere and yet being exactly where arcs should be — no one claims them; the pale crabs that live nearby do not look capable of meaning, and yet. Reeds braided into weirs that catch nothing, woven with a patience that wants a word like *devotion*. Worn posts standing in rings, round as thumbs, older than wood should stand. And at the retreat of every sheet-tide, the flats give things back: a comb green with age; a door, lying flat, its edges worn round; a tooth the size of your forearm from nothing you can name. Whoever left these had hands and patience and nothing else — no metal, no wheel, no argument with the world. They are not messages. Or they are, in a grammar of place: *here*, repeated, for thousands of years, by people who knew the one law of the Level:

Everything arrives here. Nothing leaves.

That is not a threat. It is the plumbing. This is the lowest place; everything the world sheds comes down to rest in it — sediment, shell, seed, sound, time itself, all settling into mud that goes down and down without any floor that anyone has cared to find. The deeper mud is not deeper. It is *earlier*. Which is why the one law has a corollary, and the corollary is never explained, only repeated, with a shrug, by whoever still shrugs:

Don't dig.

Ask why and they'll say: everything down there was put down for a reason, and the reason was *down*. The Level lends. It does not give. Go back the next morning for the comb, and the mud has decided otherwise. The half-sunk boat you passed yesterday you will pass again tomorrow, more rotten each time — either a very large coincidence or a very small one. The Level does not move things, exactly. It revises them. Distances disagree with themselves here; a tree a day away is sometimes reached in ten minutes, and you learn not to celebrate. The fog was not hiding it. The fog was only delaying it.

The storms, at least, are honest. The flat makes its own weather and keeps it — cyclones born over the warm shallows that wander for months and die of old age without ever making landfall, because there isn't any land to fall on. Some storms out here are older than the people who describe them. They come as brightenings and as pressure in the ears and as the seiche sloshing harder, the whole world's shallow sea rocked in its bath, and then they disperse into the pearl sky and become weather-that-isn't-called-anything.

At night the wisps come up. Blue-green flames, knee-high, wandering without wind, burning cold where the mud exhales — the only fires, the only blue, and when they dance, small white flowers open in the black water around them, blooming for whatever it is that comes to the flames, a bargain nobody witnesses being kept and yet the flowers keep opening, night after night, out here where nothing is for anyone and everything is nonetheless tended.

Stand still long enough and the Level stops being loud. Not because it quiets — it never quiets; quiet is not one of the things it keeps — but because you stop being an exception to it. You were the loud thing, all along: one more drop arriving, one more sound the ground will hold. And once, most nights, from directly below — too low to hear so much as to undergo — a single note, the size of weather, something the age of everything turning over in its sleep. The water leans toward it first. Then the reeds. Then the mats, the black trees, the salt lace, you.

The whole flat listening, the way you listen to someone about to say your name.
