# Seepdown

You would never find it from above. Steam owns the hollow completely, a low white roof that seals it from the ridgeline and from the sky, so that the basin reveals itself only to those already descending into it — one step from bare frost-scree into warm wet air, like walking through a curtain into a room. And it is a room, almost. Three hundred paces across, maybe four hundred. Twenty minutes to cross, an hour to circle the rim. Everything in it can be heard from everywhere in it.

The hollow is young. That's the first thing the walls tell you: the ridges ringing the basin are raw, frost-shattered, unweathered, their edges sharp as split flint. No soil has had time to soften them. The collapse that made this place happened recently — a lens of ancient ice buried under the plain finally gave up one spring, and the ground above it sighed down into the space it left. The land is still settling. Some nights you can hear it happen: a deep soft thud as a bank lets go, a brief gargle as a channel reroutes itself, the ticking of new cracks opening in the clay. Erosion here is not history. It is current events.

Low, too — the lowest thing for a long way. Every drip on the surrounding ridges ends up here. The water table sits so close to the surface that the ground never dries; press a boot anywhere and it weeps around the sole. Water seeps from the base of the walls in dozens of places, cold threads trickling down raw mineral faces, weeping from rock the way the whole landscape weeps. The channels that cross the floor braid and rebraid nightly, cutting fresh, sharp-edged courses through grey-blue mud, abandoning old ones. Nothing here has held one shape long enough to call it permanent.

The cold is the law of the place, and everything else is a negotiation with it. The air holds a chill like a lid over the basin; the weak light does little; at night the frost comes down hard and clicks across every exposed surface. But the ground underneath is warm — something down in the young sediments burns, slow and chemical, methane and sulfide seeping up from depth — and so the entire basin is a single slow exchange: warmth rising, cold pressing down, and the visible result is steam. Everything warm here steams. Anything alive announces itself as a column of vapor standing against the grey.

Which is why the green grows where it grows. The vegetation is genuinely lush — not modest, not struggling — but it is lush in islands. Where a warm seep surfaces, life crowds in dense and deep: mats of moss thick as bedding, sedge clumps waist-high, horsetail stands, sheets of maroon and dark-green algae filmming every warm puddle. The heat-islands are crowded, almost tropical in their tangle, steam rising off them in constant exhale. Then twenty paces of nothing — bare wet clay, pale gravel, frost-polished stones — and then another island. The basin reads like a map of its own underworld, each green patch marking a vent below. Between islands, emptiness. The sparseness of the open flats is what lets sound travel: nothing to dampen it.

And the sound. An ear from somewhere else would call it industrial. That is not a metaphor the hollow notices, but it is the truth of the impression: hissing, knocking, rhythmic chuffing, sudden sharp releases of pressure, the tick and ping of metal contracting. Except nothing here is manufactured. Nothing here was built at all.

The boilers are the reason. They are large animals, sheep-to-cattle weight, barrel-bodied on thick piston-steady legs, their hides dense with iron drawn from the mud — grown metal, laid down in their skin the way bone is laid down, dull grey-blue with seams of rust-orange. Their rumens are fire-boxes; they combust the seep-gas directly, keeping a body furnace-warm in a landscape that wants them cold. Along their spines run short vent stacks, and standing at distance you first see the steam columns, then hear the slow knocking knock knock of a boiler's idle, and only then resolve the animal itself. They number perhaps a dozen in the whole basin, and each holds an enormous territory of open flat, so you meet them one at a time, always alone, always announced.

They farm by burning. A boiler parks itself on cold ground, sears it warm with its underbody, and gutters the mud with its passage; behind it, within days, the green erupts — moss, sedge, algae racing into the warmth it left. The lush mats follow the boilers the way gulls follow the plow. Over a cycle of some nine days the animals circuit the entire basin, and you can read their route in the vegetation: rings and crescents of green at various ages, fresh sprouts behind the herd, old overgrown bloom fading to seed ahead of it. The landscape keeps its own calendar in plant rings.

Smaller lives crowd the warmth. Stoker-finches nest in the freshly heated wake-mud, and their whole profession is the boilers themselves: they pick mineral scale off the vent stacks, glean the warm seams, dart between the ribs of a resting animal like cleaners in a machine hall. Condenser-beetles — palm-sized, chitin bright with frost — come out at dawn to drink the air itself, clicking softly as their bodies cross and recross the freezing point, each one a tiny ticking engine of condensation. And rooted to the warm vents, immobile, easy to mistake for landscape, grow the chimney-worms: soft-bodied, stone-grey, pulling dissolved fuel from the seep-water and exhaling constant steam through a calcified stack. They are the reason the basin looks inhabited by industry. The only chimneys in this world are animals. The only pipes are throats. If a surveyor's eye skimmed the hollow it would report structures — vents, stacks, valves, machinery sheds of stacked steam — and every single one of them would be alive.

One thing here eats the others, and it is the loudest presence in the hollow. The shrieker hunts by heat across the open flats, and its respiration is a turbine: a rising intake roar, a sustained screamed note as it accelerates, audible from rim to rim and bouncing off the young walls. A hunt is the loudest event the basin produces — the shriek, the panic-scatter of stoker-finches, the offended chuffing of boilers, then silence, then the water-sounds reasserting themselves, drip and seep and gurgle, as if nothing had happened. The predators are few and wide-ranging; you hear one more often than you see one, and you see one as a single dark shape crossing a single empty flat, alone, the way everything here is alone. The basin is full of life and almost empty of company.

The light is meager. The steam roof diffuses the sun into a sourceless grey-pearl glow that gives no shadows and no hours. But twice a day the vapor thins, and a low amber light comes in under it almost horizontally, and for perhaps an hour the flats turn the color of old brass, every steam column lit up like a signal. After dark the hollow goes deep blue-black and cold, and then the true map of the place shows itself: faint green-gold glimmer where heat-loving algae film the warm water, each seep a dim lamp, each boiler a banked ember-glow between its ribs. The dark basin looks like the underside of a machine, pilot lights scattered across a floor.

Nothing in this hollow has ever been built, and it's worth saying plainly: nothing here could be. There is no cut stone, no wire, no post, not even a cairn. The ground itself refuses the idea — frost-heave would tip any piling within a season, the nightly reworking of channels would undercut any wall, the settling banks would shrug off anything with a foundation. Even the animals' iron is provisional: when a boiler dies, rust-mites strip its metal hide back into the sediment within a year, and the mud repossesses it. The hollow permits only grown things, only temporary things, only things that can move or regenerate. Everything mechanical about this place is alive, and everything alive is temporary, and the landscape goes on being precisely, defiantly undeveloped.

So it stays loud, and small, and busy, and empty, all at once. The daily cycle alone would keep it moving: dawn freeze cracking across the flats, midday seep-thaw, evening steam rising in columns as the air cools faster than the ground, midnight frost and glow. On top of that, the herd circuit, the channel migrations, the green rings growing and fading, the shrieker's territory shifting a little each week. Stand anywhere in the basin and you can locate everything in it by ear — the knocking of a boiler two islands over, the hiss of a vent-worm at your feet, the distant turbine-scream rising over the rim. It is a pocket world that refuses to be quiet about itself: mostly space, thinly populated, and somehow never for one moment still.
