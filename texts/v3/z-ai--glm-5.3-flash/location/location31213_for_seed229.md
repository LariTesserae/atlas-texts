You hear the grove long before you see it. On the stairs of black rock that zigzag up the mountain's flank, in cold that grows rarer the higher you climb, the sound arrives as a rumor: something between applause and surf, a vast continuous settling of noise. Then the stairs end, the rim rises around you like the lip of a bowl, and you understand that the rumor is a place.

It is small. That is the first surprise. A shelf on the mountain's brow, a few hundred paces across, rim to rim — you could walk its whole circumference before the light changed, which is saying something, because the light here barely changes at all. Above the shelf the sky is a deep amber ceiling, the sun somewhere inside it no more than a blurred coal. Below, the mountain drops into an ocean of haze that has never once been called a sea. There is no water in this world. The keepers have a word in their oldest knot-records for *rain* — it means *the soft that falls* — and no one living can tell you what it looked like. No water has ever touched this stone. The mountain was carved dry, finished dry, and the wind has done nothing to it for ages now but play it.

The grove does not drink. It listens.

Sound is the water here. You learn this quickly, mostly through your feet. The floor of the shelf is warm underfoot — stone that holds its heat like kiln brick, scattered with gravel of pink salt and, in patches, a plush dry moss like pressed felt that grows only where noise pools and lingers, in the lee of boulders, in the folds of the old tree's roots. Between the patches: bare stone, warm and wide and empty. The trees themselves are scattered the same way — grand singular specimens, each with a breathing-room of emptiness around it, as if they'd agreed on spacing. Their trunks are layered resin and fiber, banded like wasp nests, each band a turn of seasons turned to wood. Their canopies are membranes: leaves stretched drum-taut, angled and trembling toward whatever is loudest. Where the noise is richest, the trees unfurl new ears. In silence they go slack and pale, and the tollers grieve this, and sing louder.

At the shelf's heart stands the old tree, the one the keepers call the Grandmother, wide enough that a dozen of her root-knees — the knuckled growths where the roots breathe — pulse up out of the soil around her like slow bellows. Her hollows are bedrooms. Tollers roost in them: leathery gliders with throat-pouches they inflate and toll, deep round notes that roll across the shelf and hold the whole soundscape down at its base.
