You hear it before you see it. From the low country the massif is nothing but cloud stacked on cloud, a gray wall with no edges, and then the sound arrives — a standing roar laid across the land like weather. Not one sound: a broad insect shimmer at the base of hearing, frogs over that, birds over that, and through all of it a low organ note that changes key by slow degrees. The note is the wind finding the shafts.

The fog opens — it never stays open long — and shows the face of the place. Steps. A wall of steps rising out of the cloud deck, terrace over terrace, each riser the height of a man, each tread wide enough to plow, and set into the terraces like teeth, square towers: dozens, then hundreds, each with black window-holes ranked in courses. No rock shows anywhere. That is the first thing to understand, and the ordinary thing, the thing the herders down on the plain never bother to say because it goes without saying: nothing up there is ground. Every surface was placed by hands. They call it the Work.

The Work lifts nearly a thousand meters above the plain and every meter of it is labor. The stone is not native — there is no native stone. It was carried up, basket by basket, before anyone's oldest grandmother's grandmother was young, by people who had no tools worth the name: earth, water, fiber, patience, each other. The oldest walls are rammed earth the color of bread crust, warm even to look at. Higher up, dry stone, fitted by eye, no mortar, so tightly set that after all this time you still can't slip a leaf into the joints. Where water seeps through the old courses it dissolves the lime and lays it down again further on, and so the mountain is slowly gluing itself — dripstone hangs in the dead cisterns like melted wax, white and clean, and wounds that would kill a lesser structure close over here at the pace of centuries.
