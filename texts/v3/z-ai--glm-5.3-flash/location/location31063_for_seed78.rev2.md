There is no horizon here. There is the dark, and threaded through the dark, lines.

Stand, and you stand on a braid of living stems no wider than a road, taut as rigging, running off into blackness in both directions. Look outward and you see strands — some thick as a child's body, some hair-fine — crossing at long angles, each one catching a faint sheen from a glow somewhere far above and losing it again into the dark. Beyond those strands, more strands. Beyond those, more. The eye quits before the place does.

The first thing to understand is that you are not *on* this land. You are *inside* it. The tenders who live here call the whole structure the weald, and the weald is vegetation all the way down — no rock, no soil, no ground in the ordinary sense. It is a lattice of living thread grown to a height no one has measured, standing above an ocean of fog, and it is mostly air. That is the second thing to understand. A single chamber of this world can be a kilometer of empty dark crossed by three strands. You can see far here and see nothing. The weald is crowded the way a night sky is crowded: abundant, and spread so thin that every living thing keeps its own immense volume of darkness, and reaches the next one by voice.

**The material of the world**

The vegetation comes in a handful of kinds, and each kind is a trade.

There are the cables — old stems, taut, thickening for decades without ever going soft. Knock one and it answers with a single low note; put your ear to it and you can hear water moving inside, a faint throat-sound, traffic in the dark. The cables hold the spans. Some run farther than anyone has walked. Where two cross, they graft, and the graft-swelling hardens into a knot like a knuckle.

There is the fur — filament-moss, millions of hairline threads dressed over the cables and strung free in the voids, every strand beaded with condensation. The fur is the weald's skin and its gullet. It does not merely drink the fog; it *eats* it, pulling its living chemistry out of the mist itself, which is how a forest can total itself across a world that never sees the sun. When the light above brightens, the fur silvers, and for a few hours the whole country looks like frost that has decided to be alive.

There are the throats — cistern-bladders hanging from the cables, translucent-walled, swelling as they swallow fog, creaking faintly as they fill. A full throat sweats spare water down its stem. The tenders tap them with thorn-spigots, carefully, the way you'd bleed a beast you intended to keep.

And there is blackfall: the dead. The cold here forbids rot. Nothing softens, nothing composts, nothing rounds off. A dead strand stays brittle and black and true until the day it snaps — then it parts with a crack, falls whistling through the voids, and drops out of the world. The weald does not erode. It sheds.

**Terrain, for a thing that has no ground**

Walking surface is whatever has matted thick enough to hold you: braided paths where cables have fused, hammock-meadows of moss grown across an intersection, pleated leaf-roof spanning a gap. Everything else is avenue — open air, sometimes hundreds of meters of it, spanned by single lines going who knows where. Travel is as much climbing as walking; a cable is a road that goes up. And the great anchor-trunks descend through the mist below and keep descending, past the reach of your ears, singing deeper notes the farther down they go.

The weald is a continent. Fog moves through it in herds, in fronts with seasons of their own. The whole structure breathes — swelling at some hours, slackening at others — and it makes weather the way a sea does, out of its own mass. No one has found an edge. Maps among the tenders are not maps of geography, because the geography drifts: growth outruns death along one margin of the weald and dies off behind the other, and the entire lattice walks itself across the world at the pace of a tree. A tender's maps are knot-records of lineages — which cables descend from which — because lineage is the only address that stays put.

**Water**

Beneath the lattice lies the undermist: a cold ocean of fog, its surface flat and dull as milk, shoreless. This is the weald's reservoir, and it moves in a tide. In the dark hours the cold drains downward and the mist *rises* — floods up through the lower lattice in a slow silver swell, and the fur drinks the front as it passes. A rising tide sounds like rain that never lands: a droplet-hiss moving through millions of threads, traveling upward past you in the dark. In the bright hours the mist withdraws, and the weald slackens and grows thirsty again.

True rain is rare and arrives from above. The sky here is a luminous haze-layer, far up, called the Bloom — thickening and thinning over days, a perpetual dusk that never manages to become a morning. Whether the Bloom is weather or organism, no one alive can say; the tenders speak of it the way farmers speak of a moody animal. When it thickens, it sweats, and its condensation falls the full height of the weald — an event, a curtain of drops dropping through kilometers of dark, drumming on every throat and cable and leaf, the loudest thing that ever happens here. Pools stand open in stem-hollows for days afterward. Everything drinks. The pitch of the world climbs.

Between tide and rain there is dew, the daily bread, wrung out of the fur by hand. And there is the dry waist — a middle altitude where the air runs clear, where the fog never reaches and the Bloom's sweat never falls: pockets of outright drought suspended inside all that damp. Travelers cross the waist carrying gourds.

The cold, when it comes down hard, works in frost. At the coldest hours hoarfrost blooms along the windward fur, stiffening every thread, and the whole harp of the world detunes at once — a chorus of sagging notes, a continent going slightly flat. When the Bloom brightens again the frost lets go and falls, glittering down through the voids like slow sparks. Nothing catches it. Nothing catches fire, here, ever — which the tenders have made into law.

**Light**

The Bloom gives a dusk and only a dusk. Its glow reaches the lattice's upper stories, where the fur runs pale and hungry toward it; below that the light gives out, and the deep layers are simply lightless, darkness pooling around the great trunks like a second ocean. What lamps exist are alive: glow-moss cultured in gourd-water, fed, thinned, sung to. Light among the tenders is something you keep alive, not something you burn. It goes out if you neglect it.

**Sound**

The weald is strung, and the tide plays it. Every cable is a string; every thread of fur is a string; the moving fog draws a chord across the whole continent, and the chord never stops. Pitch is tension and tension is water — a wet line sings sharp, a dry line sags flat — so the weald reports its thirst in key, continuously, and the tenders tune it. They twist, they resin, they re-stretch. Over generations the drone has drifted into something half-composed; whole regions of the lattice have a signature voicing, and a home is recognized by its tuning before it is seen. An address is a chord.

And under the drone, everything calls. Sound is how the voids are bridged — sight fails at a hundred meters, but a voice carries for kilometers down an open avenue of air. The weald is loud the way a night forest is loud, with the volume turned up by all that empty space. To be loud here is to be present. To go silent is to vanish.

**The animals**

The weald is crowded the way the sky is crowded — many kinds, each scattered thin over the immensity, all of them talking.

Sifters hang in the fog-channels like living nets: gelid, slow, mouth-wide, combing droplets from the mist as it passes. A fog-front that enters a sifted channel comes out the far side thin. Sifters drink weather.

Strand-grazers eat the world that holds them — long, patient, caterpillar-slow creatures that spend their lives working along a cable, swallowing it a mouthful at a time. You hear the wound before you see it: a note going flat, a local sag in the great chord. Tenders hunt grazers by ear, moving along the line toward the damaged note with bone hooks in their belts.

Gliders ride the tide on membrane wings, utterly silent, riding the rising mist the way sea-things ride a current, and take sifters off their moorings in the dark hours. Click-hunters read the loud dark by echo, clicking and listening; their prey answer by muting, or by mimicry — some small things learn to imitate the drone itself, a creature becoming weather in order to survive it. Dew-thieves tap the throats at night, quick and light, gone before the swelling creaks. And beneath the surface of the undermist, occasionally, a light rises — slow, formless, without pattern — and sinks again. No one has followed it. The tenders do not fish.

**The people**

A dwelling every few days' travel, no closer. Grown, not raised: living cables grafted and trained over years into walls, a roof of pleated leaf, a door that grows shut overnight and must be cut open each morning. A family keeps a span — tunes its cables, taps its throats without killing them, combs dew from the fur and wrings it into gourds, dries food in the waist's clear air. Their tools are bone, thorn, resin, bast, and clay; there is no metal because there is no mine, and no flame because a spark would run the fur like a fuse across a continent. Fire-law is the first law: coals only, in closed stoves, a license held by grandmothers and rarely granted.

News travels plucked. A coded knock on a main cable runs along it as vibration, wet line to wet line, faster than any runner — the weald is also an instrument of record, and the tenders are its players, and on a quiet bright hour you can stand on a path and feel a message arrive under your feet from a day's distance, tick by tick.

The dead are wrapped around a young cable and grafted in. Years later there is a smooth swelling in the line, polished by touching hands, and that is the grave: not a place set aside from the world, but a place where the world thickened.

**The cycles**

A day here has two waters: the tide rising through the fur below, the Bloom sweating above. A week has the Bloom's moods. A season has its rare true rains, when the whole height drums and every throat fills and the pitch of the world climbs for days — the weald goes sharp when it is full. Growth and death run against each other at all times, taut and brittle, nothing allowed to age: the young wood keeps its pale, sharp, freshly-made look no matter how old it is, because nothing here erodes. Things are either alive and tight, or dead and black and falling.

And at the dark hours, standing on a path with the drone all around you, you will hear it: a crack, somewhere above — a blackfall strand parting — then the whistle of its falling, longer than you would ever expect, and then the silence where the sound of its landing should be, and isn't. The depth of the world, said out loud, in one long not-arriving note.

Then the mist keeps rising through the fur beneath your feet, the cables keep their tension, and the continent holds its shape — as it always has, as far as anyone here knows — by thirst alone.
