The country above the hollow is unremarkable — low grass, a dry wind, the ordinary business of light and weather — and it gives no warning. The rim appears as a low ring of pale stone, waist-high at most, continuous all the way around, worn smooth as a banister. You could step over it without effort. But the air changes at the lip. It cools by ten degrees in a stride, takes on weight, goes quiet. The wind that was moving your collar on the outside does not follow you in. Sound on the far side of the ring is another country's sound.

Down inside, the whole place is smaller than its effect. A hundred and fifty paces across, a farmyard, a threshing floor — you can stand at the rim and hold all of it in one look, every terrace, every machine, the far wall with its curtains of mist. That is part of the strange contract of the place: nothing is hidden from you, and yet nothing stays the same from one look to the next.

The hollow descends in terraces. Each shelf is a hand-span to a knee high, rimmed with stone the color and texture of tooth enamel, and each shelf holds a shallow lens of water the color of the sky's underside. The water is never quite still. It is fed from above — the walls of the hollow weep, all of them, everywhere, a fine unceasing sweat — and it leaves below, through channels that the water itself has cut and keeps re-cutting. The channels change. This is the first thing you learn to read: the walls keep a diary in damp. Streaks of fresh dark wet mark this month's plumbing; pale dead streaks mark last season's, abandoned mid-slope like roads on an old map. A terrace that was dry in one memory is brimming in the next. The whole hydraulic system of the hollow is redrawn constantly, and gently, without violence — a channel overtops, finds a softer stone, cuts two fingers' width of new course, and the water simply goes that way now.

Scattered through all of this, and crowding its center, are the machines.

The first one you notice is a pump the size of a milking stool, bolted to a plinth of the local stone, its paint long gone, its metal glazed with a thin mineral skin like a fired pot. It is running. It has the manner of something that has been running for a very long time and intends to continue. Its overflow feeds a channel; the channel drops through a small turbine; the turbine, presumably, feeds something else. Then you notice the second, and the tenth, and lose count around forty. Pumps, condensers, banks of cooling fins so beaded with water they drip in visible rows, float-arms nodding like slow grazing animals, gauges on stakes — a thicket of small dials, each one confidently pointing somewhere. Pipes run between them in braids of three and four. At the heart of the hollow the works crowd so tightly you could not set a boot between them; at the edges they thin to lone machines on lonely plinths, half-encrusted in flowstone to their elbows, still working. Leaks have sealed themselves here with stone. Bolt heads are worn to thumbs. One cable comes down over the rim, glazed like everything else, and where it passes, mist beads on it and runs down it in rows, and the hum of it is not heard so much as felt — in the sternum, in the teeth — a note below hearing that never stops.

The maps give the place a number. The few who come to it call it the Still, which is a joke with two true halves: it distills, and it is not still in any other respect. What condenses here comes down almost pure — the trays of the condensers hold the only soft water in the region, cold and clean and tasting of exactly nothing. What the water touches on its way through picks up the ground's salts. So the hollow divides everything it receives: softness above, mineral below. The floor between terraces is a pale crust, faintly glassy, that rings under a boot heel. A dropped fitting does not lie on that ground; it fuses to it. The crust takes a print and cures it — there are boot-heels standing in the paths from some earlier decade, worn smooth now, kept the way amber keeps.

Almost nothing grows, and the almost is a short list. A film of rust-pink algae on the warm trays, the only warm water in the hollow. A pillow of black moss in one sheltered notch where spray lands softly. A tussock of sedge at the rim, exactly where the runoff turns ordinary and the ground forgives. That is the entire census; you can walk the whole hollow and check it. Everything else — seeds, spores, the ordinary ambitions of plants — arrives on the wind, sprouts in a drip-line, and starves in the crust, which is a menu with nothing on it.

The sound of the place deserves an inventory, because it is nearly the whole of what happens here. There is the gravel of drips — hundreds of them, unsynchronized, each machine and each weeping wall keeping its own tempo, so that from the rim they merge into one continuous tick, like a cooling engine the size of a landscape. There is the hum. There is the tap of a mineral flake letting go of a wall and the far creak of a float-arm. And there is the siphon. Once an hour, more or less, some terrace fills to its lip, a siphon charges, and the hollow swallows — a deep gulp and a gluck-gluck-gluck receding, the loudest thing that ever happens here, startling enough that your own pulse notices. The machines do not react. And through all of it, the mist eats echo. A shout arrives at the far wall half-gone, old, dimmed, as if it had been wrapped in wool for the journey. The hollow is quiet at any volume. All of its turbulence is motion, none of it is noise — fish seen turning under ice have that quality.

The light is ordinary, which is its own strangeness: plain grey-white daylight, flat and sourceless under the mist, spent gently by the air. The stone reads pale, the water reads as brightness, the machines read as darker shapes with wet highlights. On the rare clear day a single blade of sun gets down into the hollow and the whole anatomy shows at once — the terraces, the braided pipes, the steam lifting off the warm trays in visible threads — and then the mist closes the shutter again. The cool has an engine: it is the chill of evaporation, of stone beside a spring, of laundry on a line. Stand still long enough and your own skin begins, politely, to weep. You become one of the surfaces.

Fauna is a matter of patience. The residents are small grey amphibians, thumb-sized, stone-colored, that live in the condensers' warm trays — the soft water, the only warmed water, the only food that the mist carries down and settles. They do not call. No one has heard one call; their water leaves no room for song, or they have simply never needed it. You find them by their eyes, wet pebbles catching the flat light, and you might see one in a whole afternoon. A bird visits — one kind, grey, unremarkable — drinks at the pure trays, never lingers, never sings inside the rim, and is loud again the moment it clears the stone. The hollow quiets its guests without appearing to try.

The built traces are as scattered as the machines are dense. One hut at the rim: doorless, its floor a slick of flowstone, a rusted stove, a wall of tally-marks gone glassy under mineral, a chair that has grown to the ground. A dead hand-pump beside it, fused. A stake, a hinge, a boot-heel. People passed through here the way water passes through the machines — used, transformed, kept. And here is the hollow's quietest puzzle, the one that keeps you at the rim longer than you meant to stay: the machines are maintained. Washers are newer than their housings. A pipe section shines where its neighbors are glazed. There are no footprints to explain it. Whoever tends them does not leave prints, or the crust cures them the same hour. The count of machines, someone noticed once, does not change. It is the same number every visit, and no one has said aloud what that implies, and everyone who visits thinks it.

The rules of the place, as far as they can be written, are these. The hollow condenses; air that crosses the rim sheds its water here, the way a cold cellar wrings a coat. Soft above, hard below — the still divides what falls. The machines belong to the geology now; they are weathered as the terraces are weathered and they are maintained by no one in evidence, and the border is exact: outside, mist is weather; inside, mist is a resident. What enters leaves in another form — water becomes stone, prints become stone, visitors become quieter.

Sit through one cycle and you understand the last rule. The mist thickens; the gravel of drips becomes a fine continuous hiss; a siphon charges and the hollow swallows; the mist thins; the terraces appear again, redrawn — a new channel where the dry streak was, a brimming lens where last week's water ran elsewhere. A pump changes its note by a third. In a tray, a grey frog opens its eyes. Nothing has changed; everything has moved. The hollow erodes and builds in the same gesture — every grain dissolved above is deposited below, and the terraces rise as the walls retreat — so it is neither wearing out nor silting shut. Its turbulence is its balance. It would go on exactly like this without you, and it will. At the gate-stone where you climbed out, your boot has left a print in the crust. The hollow has begun, already, slowly, to keep it.
