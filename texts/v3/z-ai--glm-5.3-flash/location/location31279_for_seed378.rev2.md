# The Weep

You come up to it through the black hills, a day and a half of dry switchbacks where the only water available is the water your own body spends. Then the rim, and the land opens all at once: a highland basin miles across, terraced in pale stone, step after descending step toward a floor of salt glare, and over all of it a standing veil of steam that the sun cannot burn away. The air up here is thin. The light is not. It comes down with the particular intensity of altitude, and the white terraces return it doubled and tripled, so that the whole basin seems to glow from beneath its own surface — fired enamel, cooling.

Each tread of this enormous stair is a smooth white field of mineral crust. Each riser weeps: hundreds of seep-threads per wall, constant, thread-thin, glinting. The shallow pools scattered down the terraces — the locals call them pans — hold water the color of sky or rust or nothing at all, depending on what grows in them, and steam stands over the hottest of them in slow columns, like smoke that has decided not to hurry. Near the head of the stair, where the ground's heat concentrates, the pans steam hard. Near the foot they go cool, then merely salt, and the oldest terraces there stand dry and grey, retired.

The town is visible from the rim: a knot of pale roofs at the shoulder of the great spring, wearing a permanent pearl of its own steam.

And here is the thing that undoes visitors, before the heat, before the glare: the silence. The first thing the Weep takes from you is sound. Your own breathing becomes the loudest event of your day. A shout dies within ten paces, folded into the wet air like a stone into deep water. Footsteps on the damp sinter make no more noise than a kiss. The air here is so heavy with vapor that it refuses the job of carrying sound — the mist eats the edges first, every hiss and click and consonant, and then takes the rest. Sound in the Weep has left the air entirely. It has gone to live in solid things.

This is law as well as physics. A sharp crack can spall the delicate crust; a sudden noise can stampede a herd and break treads that took centuries to round. So the quiet is kept the way other towns keep their water: with rules, with maintenance, with the whole culture leaning on it. The Clatter Ban is the oldest ordinance on the town's books. No hammers. Ever.

The water itself is everywhere and always in motion, but almost never falls. The risers weep in a thousand named threads — the seeps have individual names, though there is no name for the quiet among them. Around every warm pan the crust wears a skin of living film: mats of the simplest life, laid down in rings of rust and saffron and olive and ink, the color telling the temperature more precisely than any gauge. Nothing here flowers. Nothing here has ever flowered. The mats are called *bloom* out of courtesy, or memory, and the joke is old enough to be respectful. At the head of the stair sits the Seethe, a broad spring that boils gently and never boils over, breathing its column of vapor into the bright air; at the foot lies the Salt Verge, grey and finished, where old treads go to be mineral again.

For all the water, the land is settled. The Stair finished arguing with its water long ago. The risers are rounded like tools worn by hands; the pan rims are smooth as old bowls; the seeps run at the rate they have run at for uncountable generations, and the crust grows upward at the same pace the water takes it away. Nothing here is eroding toward ruin. Everything here has arrived.

The heat is the other constant. By noon the sun-touched stone scalds bare skin, and shade becomes a currency — the north side of any wall is claimed, inherited, defended. The air sits on you like a wet cloth and tastes faintly of hot stone and pennies, and because it is nearly saturated, sweat does not evaporate so much as accumulate: nobody in the Weep ever dries. The people only shine. Work happens at the cool ends of the day, and the pools grade from scalding at the head of the stair to bath-warm at the foot, which is where everyone goes to think.

The town — it is called Weepstead, named for the weeping walls and not for sorrow, though visitors sometimes report both — is built of the terrace itself. Sinter cut from the crust in blocks, or cast in molds set over slow vents, where it grows into shape like a very slow loaf. Pale, warm to the touch, faintly furred with mineral bloom, patched with copper. The town is stratified in the manner of its ground: mains and cisterns below, streets in the middle, sky-juice above — every roof a shallow pan still, a small distillery pitched to catch the condensing air. The town drinks the sky. Wealth in Weepstead is measured in cistern depth, and a marriage settlement will specify how far down the water lies.

The industry is steam, of course, and it is quiet the way the town is quiet. Capped vents feed lagged mains — stone conduit wrapped in felted sinew and clay — that run for miles across the treads in long, sparse lines, each line dead straight, each junction its own small monument of copper and glass. In the turbine hall the machinery makes a hum too low for ears; it arrives through the soles of your feet, and the floor there is always warm, and the workers go barefoot because here the feet are the ears. The smiths work not with hammers but with presses, slow hydraulic things hissing down through a long second, and forge their copper at the blue mouths — certain vents that exhale flammable gas and burn with a steady colorless-blue flame, the basin's only fire. The glassworks sits beside them. Sinter is rich in silica, and the Weep has always had more heat than it knows what to do with.

Even the pipes are staffed. Brushers — small hot-water snail-things, native to the vents, mute and industrious — graze the scale from the inside of the mains, and the engineers keep them, count them, and mourn them when a cold snap takes a colony. They are fed. They have names. They are, essentially, cats.

Talk in Weepstead happens at kissing distance. Conversation is conducted mouth to ear, a whisper you must lean into, and strangers find it shocking how close a greeting comes. Farther than an arm's reach, the hands take over: *mist-hand*, the gesture language, is taught to children before letters, and no one in Weepstead can tell who is deaf, because no one much asks. For anything longer, there are rails and pipes — tap-code, clicked through the mains and railings, solid-borne, carrying beautifully where the air carries nothing at all. Put your ear to a main and you can hear the town's day: a herder's coded rattle three streets over, water counting itself through the flumes, the turbine's slow vowel underneath everything. *The mains are full of talk*, the saying goes, and it is not a metaphor. Out on the rim, flash-posts stand on the high dark shoulders of the hills, and on the basin's clear days — most days — mirrors speak across miles in blinking light. The Weep has no bells. It has a water-clock, a patient public drip, which keeps excellent time and is heard by exactly whoever stands beneath it.

The animals make the silence make sense.

The salt-rakers are the land's great presences: low, broad, unhurried grazers the size of hay-wagons, shelled in grown mineral laid down in layers, like beehives built of panstone. Their feet are soft as lips, so they never spall the crust, and their trackways — worn into the treads over uncountable generations, parallel grooves like rail-beds — are as much a feature of the landscape as the risers themselves. They crop the bloom at the pan margins and the mineral rime on damp stone, and they excrete their excess as pellets of nearly pure salt, which is panned, raked, bagged, and sent down the switchbacks on caravans of molt-year rakers, sure-footed and silent, heavy with salt and shell-plate and silk.

Herding here is done with mirrors and arms. The herders wear smoked-glass eyeshades against the glare and speak in sign and flash, and they watch eyes, not ears — because the Weep's one predator gives no warning at all. The stiller is a grey assembling of legs and patience, colored exactly like the dead treads it haunts, and its coming is announced by nothing, which is its whole strategy and the herds' whole education. You learn to read the herd before you learn the herder. Custom says let the stiller hunt; the stiller keeps the herds moving, and moving herds keep the treads from being grazed bare. Everything in the Weep is in on the arrangement.

Over the Seethe hang the vapor-bells — pale translucent things the size of church bells, anchored on spun threads of silk, riding the steam columns and drinking the vapor, exhaling a fine salt-dust that glitters in the light like slow snow. In midsummer comes the bell-rise, when hundreds hang over the hot water at once, lit from within by the sun passing through them, a silent chandelier a quarter-mile wide. At dawn, curtains of sippers — small as mice, quick as spilled water — swarm the overhangs to lick the night's condensation off the stone, arriving like weather and leaving with it. High over everything ride the quiet flocks, thermal-riders visible only as shadows flashing across the white treads, as if the basin were blinking. The Weep is mostly empty — a mile of tread, then a pool; an hour of walking, then a herd — but it is crowded the way a sky is crowded: rarely, and then all at once.

The basin breathes. At dawn the cooled air slides the mist downslope in slow laminar sheets, and the mist pours over each riser in a silent cataract — a waterfall with no noise in it at all, the Weep's one extravagance of motion, spent in total quiet. The roof-stills run; the sippers swarm; the herds move along the rims. By midday the mist lifts and burns off, the glare hardens, colors bleach to white and copper, the far treads swim in shimmer, and the town works under shade-cloths. At dusk the sweat comes: every surface weeps at once, the light goes rose and gold on the wet stone, and the mist climbs back up the stair toward the Seethe.

And the ground has tides of its own. Nobody charts them by any moon — the deep rock keeps its own calendar — but when the boil-tide comes, the pools rise a hand's breadth, the whole Stair steams like a held breath let go, the engineers open their sluices, and fresh bloom paints new rims overnight. The herds come down to feed on it. The stillers come down after the herds. From the rim, the watchers count the herds by mirror-flash and relay the numbers down the line of posts: a census in blinking light. Once or twice a season the vapor climbs too high, cools, and returns as a brief warm rain with the sun still shining through it — the closest thing to thunder the basin knows is that rain on the copper roofs, a soft drumming, almost scandalous in its loudness. The children stand out in it with their arms spread.

Night comes late up here and never quite wins. The Seethe steams under the stars. In the warm pans, bubbles rise through the bloom and burst in small cold sparks of light — the mats' faint glow, disturbed — and it is the closest thing the Weep has to speech after dark. The blue mouths burn in the smith-sheds. The windows are beaded. Walk the treads at midnight and you will hear nothing whatsoever, but the soles of your feet will keep reporting the town.

Four things are true here, and the town teaches them before letters. Heat is borrowed, from below, and must be borrowed politely. Water is grown, drop by drop, and wealth is measured in cisterns. Light is the loudest voice available, and it is spent in flashes. And sound is the rarest substance of all — spent only mouth to ear. The engineers' oath, older than the mains, says the rest:

*Draw no faster than the Weep weeps.*

People who leave — and the caravans do leave, heavy with salt and silk and shell-plate — find the lowlands unbearable for a while. They flinch at bells. They keep leaning in. It passes, mostly. But if you stay, some evening you will see a child lying along a main with her ear to the warm stone, feeling the town talk, and you will understand the place completely. The Weep is not silent. It has simply given up on the air. Everything here says what it has to say through the stone — and the stone is a patient talker.
