You come up out of the cloud and the cloud ends at a line, clean as a cut. That is the first thing to understand about this place: it is new. Not young the way a forest is young, seeded and growing — new the way a thing is new the moment after it is finished being made. The terrace sits high in the air, a shelf of land maybe four hundred paces long and a hundred wide, walled on every side by escarpments that drop sheer into the white. The rock at those edges looks quarried — faced, jointed, mated — though no hand ever quarried it and no quarry was ever here. The whole shelf has that quality: surfaces that meet other surfaces, as if the mountain arrived in parts, freshly machined, and the parts are still warm from the fitting.

Except nothing is warm here. Hold that too.

Underfoot, everywhere, is a centimeter of standing water over slick wet stone. The walls weep — not in trickles but in a film, a continuous sweat rising out of the rock itself, feeding the shallows without any stream you can point to. The water comes up from below. Something under the stone is pumping it, and the pumping has been going on for exactly as long as the shelf has existed, which is to say: for no time at all, forever. You never find the source. Half of this machine is buried, and half of it is hidden below the cloud lid, and you are standing on the one exposed deck of the works.

Because it is a works. That is the second thing to understand. Nothing on this shelf was built — no structure, no artifact, not one stone balanced on another — and yet everything on it is in operation. It is a factory that was never constructed and will never be decommissioned, a plant with no maker, no product, and no hands.

Above the shelf, it rains without landing. The mist that stands knee-high over the whole terrace condenses into fat droplets that fall a hand's width and re-evaporate before they touch anything — a perpetual rain that never arrives, rising and failing and rising in the dim green air. It never thickens to fog and never clears. The light here is the same everywhere: gray-green, sourceless-seeming, cast by the overcast and by the vents, and there are no shadows, or rather every object casts all of its shadows at once, faintly, in every direction, so that the terrace reads like a photograph taken at some hour that does not exist.

The sound is the thing you will remember. It is loud — you will find yourself raising your voice to your own thoughts — and it never varies, and it comes from everywhere at one volume. The hiss of the condensing mist. A deep sustained note from the great vent at the shelf's center, like a pipe organ's lowest register held down by a weight. The tick and creak of the reeds. The whirr of the flocks. The metronome splashes of the herons. None of it diminishes with distance. Sound has been abolished as a way of locating anything here; you hear everything, always, complete, as though your ears had been moved to the center of the shelf and the shelf moved around them. Walk to the far edge and the roar does not change by a fraction. It is the same roar. It is the same roar.

The green comes in islands. That is the pattern of the place: lush, violent clumps of life standing in a ring of bare glistening stone and mirror-flat pools, with true emptiness between — not bare ground but presence-of-emptiness, space held deliberately, the way a machine holds clearance between its moving parts. In the islands, the vegetation is doing work. Reeds stand head-high and rotate slowly on their own axes, whole stands of them turning in unison like screw-blades, driving the mist. Ferns piston — each frond a stroke, up and down, slow and load-bearing, lifting water through their hollow stems to the rim channels at the escarpment's edge, where the water goes over in bright threads and falls into the cloud and, somewhere beneath the cloud, begins the long unseen leg of its circuit back. Moss mats the stones and pulls water straight out of the air; it drips in chorus, thousands of mats, each drip on the same beat.

The animals are the workforce and the workforce is abundant. Flocks of what you will end up calling vanes — swifts, more or less — wheel in fixed toroids above the warm-humming vents, wings phased so precisely that the flock reads as one rotating surface, pumping the air that feeds the condensation. They never land. They have no chicks. Nothing here has parents and nothing here is young, though everything is new; things simply are, the way parts are. Gray piston-herons stand chest-deep in the rim pools, making their slow vertical strokes, each stroke ending in the identical splash, splash after splash after splash, forever, the sound of the place keeping its own time. Slick black salamander-things pack themselves into every hairline crack in the fresh rock and exude a secretion that sets into stone identical to the stone around it — the crack heals, the edge stays sharp, and the land stays new because it is repaired as fast as it erodes. Low heavy herds circle the green islands cropping them at exactly the rate the islands grow, neither gaining nor losing, and whatever falls to the ground — a leaf, a dropping, and if you are lucky enough or unlucky enough to see it, a bird — is taken apart in seconds by clots of white settling-insects that dismantle it to its molecules and vent the rest as a whisper of steam. Nothing rots here. There is no sediment, no dust, no litter, no past.

The vent at the center burns with cold flame. It is a strip of teal-white light standing in the dimness like a door opened onto some brighter elsewhere, and it gives light without heat — the air beside it is the same temperature as the air everywhere on the shelf, one cool degree, and the water everywhere is one degree colder than that. No gradient. That is the law here, the deep rule, the breakdown: nothing accumulates, nothing flows downhill, nothing runs warm or cold, nothing is loud here and nothing is quiet, everything is held at level. And the machine runs anyway. That is the impossible part, the broken part. A wheel turns with no head of water above it. The cold flame burns on its own smoke. Every loop closes on itself with no input and no loss, phase-locked, every stroke of every fern and every wingbeat of every vane in synchrony across the whole shelf, so that the entire terrace — stone, water, green, feather — is one engine turning over at idle, holding its single note, producing nothing but the fact of its own running.

Sit still as long as you like and you will not see an event. The herds pass the same stone on the same beat. A vane falters once, if you are fortunate, and the white settlers have it before it touches the water, and that is the closest the shelf comes to a story. Tracks fill in behind you before you have finished making them. Your breath disperses instantly, without a ghost of linger. The mist closes. You cannot get wetter or drier, warmer or colder; the shelf draws your body's heat off at exactly the rate you make it, so you never shiver and never sweat, and the only clock left running in this place is your hunger — the only gradient for a hundred miles in any direction, including up, is you.

When you finally climb down one of the seams — if you climb down, if you can find the seam again, which you will not be able to do twice — you will not be able to say how long you were up there. The roar had no rests. The light had no noon. Nothing on the shelf aged, and nothing on the shelf remembers you, because keeping you would have been a change.

You were the only weather it ever had. It smoothed you out and went on idling: the same rain never landing, the same heron lifting, the same splash, always, always the same delay — and then the splash.
