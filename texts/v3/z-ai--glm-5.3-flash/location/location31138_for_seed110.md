The mountain is young, and it behaves like it. Up here, above the last grass, nothing has had time to soften. The scree has edges like broken plates. The gullies are still drawings of gullies rather than gullies — lines scored in the rock by a single season of melt, not yet widened into valleys. No lichen has claimed the boulders. No moss has found a footing. The cliffs rise bare and unapologetic, and the wind that polishes them is the oldest thing on the summit ridge.

And yet, in the flank of this infant mountain, warm air is escaping.

You find it by feel before you find it by sight: a hairline cleft between two slabs, breathing. On a cold day the breath steams — a thin ghost rising from the rock itself, at an altitude where nothing else is warm. The cleft takes a body sideways, then a body turned, then a body lowering on its heels, and then it stops taking anything at all, and there is a room.

It is small. Three long paces by four, perhaps, with a ceiling that a raised hand can touch, and in that darkness — a darkness so complete it has texture, a darkness that presses back when you press into it — the size stops mattering, because everything in the room is within reach of everything else. That is the first thing the body learns, before the eyes give up entirely: this place is crowded. Water touches stone touches air touches root. There are six or seven strata of world stacked within a person's height. To stand in the middle of it is to be the least crowded object in the room.

The warmth comes from the rock itself. The mountain is young enough that its interior has not yet finished cooling from its own making; the stone holds the memory of heat the way a fresh loaf holds it. Lay your palm against the wall and the wall is warm as a sleeping animal. Lay your cheek there — travelers do, eventually, because the dark strips away embarrassment — and it is blood-warm. The water arrives the same way. Somewhere above, snowmelt and rain sink through cracked new rock, and by the time the water weeps back out through this chamber's walls it has been warmed inside the mountain and filled with its minerals. It arrives already made gentle, already carrying stone in solution like a messenger carrying a gift.

And here is the strange heart of the place: the stone is being born. Right now. Continuously. Where the water weeps, it lays down skin after skin of mineral — milk-white, faintly cream, here and there a seam of rust where an iron thread runs. The formations have none of the grandeur of old caves. There are no great columns, no cathedrals. Instead: nubs. Buds. Knuckles of flowstone no bigger than a fist. Curtains one finger thick, translucent at their edges like the rims of ears. Milk-teeth of stalactites along the ceiling seams, each one a first attempt. The whole chamber has the tenderness of an unfinished thing — the inside of a mouth, the inside of a wrist. It has not decided what it will be. It is still rehearsing.

But nothing in it changes.

This is the paradox the room holds without strain: it is under construction, eternally, and it never changes. The water lays down a skin as steadily as an older skin dissolves elsewhere; the pool receives its thread of seepage at exactly the rate the warm stone drinks it away and the thick air returns it as condensation. The ceiling beads with moisture, the beads gather, and a single warm drop falls — the cave's own private rain, so slow it is closer to exhalation — and the pool into which it falls never rises and never falls. The grotto stands at the exact hilt of its own becoming, building itself at the speed of its own stillness. A place being born so slowly that, so far, it has never begun.

The drip keeps an interval. One drip every several heartbeats, from the ceiling crack above the basin, and it has kept that interval as long as anyone has listened, and the listening has not been long enough to catch it falter. There is no echo here — the moisture in the air swallows sound after a single soft reflection, like a room upholstered in felt — so each drop arrives complete: a plink, round and whole, then silence with warmth in it. The other sounds are smaller than that. The continuous thread-thin hiss of the main seep, barely louder than attention. The tick of a condensation bead letting go. Your own blood, which in this room you can hear, and which sounds, for once, like it belongs.

The water collects in a basin — a natural hollow in the soft floor, deepened by hands. The pool is never fuller, never lower. Its lip is a rim of stone thin and hard as porcelain, and its surface, in a place with no light at all, cannot show you anything; instead the pool offers itself to the touch as a second skin, warm within a hair of your own temperature, so that sinking your hand in feels less like entering water and more like being confirmed.

Life here is a rumor of life. From the hairline cracks in the ceiling hang root-threads — the fine white hair-roots of the sparse turf far above, alpine cushions and stubby shrubs that have sent explorers down through the young rock, seeking the warmth. They dangle translucent, beaded with condensation, leafless and blind, drinking at the drip line.
