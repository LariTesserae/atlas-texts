A benevolent power arriving here would learn its first lesson before it ever touched down: that on this plateau, power converts at the border. Not into obedience — nothing here is available for that — but into weight. The turf remembers everything it is given and returns nothing but ground. Every act is a deposit, the deposits are permanent, and the guests, who worked all of this out with no god's help at all, built their entire civilization on small change: a stone added to a cairn, a hood left open, a camp moved on before the ground can accept it. So the question *what should such a power do here* has one right answer, and the answer is: almost nothing. The almost is where the wisdom lives. Doing truly nothing would not be wrong, exactly — but abstinence is not actually in the local grammar; even the dead are asked to pace a line. The minimum unit of presence in this country is a small open-handed act. So the power would perform the minimum, perfectly, and nothing beyond it — and then, this being the hard part, it would leave before the ground accepted it.

Here is how it would go, told the way the ground would tell it, if the ground told.

It comes, as everything comes here, out of the fog. Dawn, the fog-line, one more pale silhouette among the dew-walkers' thousands — and every eye that catches it does the correct thing, the first discipline, the minute of stillness. It waits too. It has been believed in, beseeched, obeyed; it has not been *waited out* in longer than its own memory runs, and it finds that being made to stand still by herders' etiquette suits it. It counts its minute the only way counting is done here: it presses itself to the turf in the listener's posture and borrows the plateau's pulse — the held bass of the borers going about their circulation — and stands sixty beats of someone else's heart. Under one country, for the length of an evening, the borers change their note. That is how the plateau takes a census. By the time the minute is out, the ground has weighed its guest to the ounce, and the guest has learned the local time signature, and neither of them mentions it.

Then it stands up and does the unwise thing, once, the way you press a bruise. It looks at the whole. It is the only thing that ever could — the plateau is one body and cannot see itself; the herders see swells; the swimmers know the undersides of swells; nobody sees the country — and it looks, all at once, rim to rim: the flush-countries and the sweats and the held breath and the ten thousand mornings of the dew-walkers, the whole animal lying in the thin sun, dreaming in green. And the continent shivers. Every haze-pillar on that half of the world leans together, the way grass leans under a passing hand. The turf tightens in its slow clench, though it is not midday. That night, in camps a country apart, listeners pulling the night's messages out of the ground all report the same impossibility: a pressure like something very large turning over in its sleep, a herd a day off where no herd is. The power understands what its attention weighs here, and never looks at the whole again. Thereafter it looks in portions — a swell at a time, a sweat at a time, the way the herders look at cattle, the way you look at anyone asleep. Love, it learns, must be metered like water, on a plateau that stays damp by economy.

Then it goes down among the guests, and receives the second lesson, which undoes a career. It has prepared, as powers do, a portion of wisdom to dispense, and it goes all day through the camps looking for the throat that needs it, and finds none. Everything it crossed the dark to teach is already being practiced, by herders who measure wealth in shed plates, as plain manners. Cairns stacked and never taken: that is giving without grip. Hoods over the favored seeps, left open for whoever comes: grace with no gate in it. Moving on before the ground accepts you: a discipline of non-attachment that any heaven would call advanced doctrine, kept here as housekeeping, because acceptance here is the same event as disappearance and everyone knows it. It came down the long way to bring the gospel of traveling light, and found the lightest people in creation using it to pitch tents. It puts its commandments away. It is, by every measure the plateau keeps, glad.

What it does after that is the liturgy — the guests' own, performed without audience. It adds one nodule to a cairn on a sight-line, and the cairn stands a stone kinder for longer than anyone's counting. It finds a seep-hood coming unwoven and rewove it and leaves it open, because a closed hood is a hoard and an open one is a promise. When a named swimmer surfaces near the route — a blunt pale head the size of a cart, looking at nothing — it does what the custom asks of anyone: stamps twice on the turf, in greeting, and walks on. When a knuckle of tissue drops from a flank somewhere and stands up, already green, already walking, it stands in the ring — days, while the herd stands around the calf like stones around a hearth — one more stone, and it does not bless the calf, because blessing is weight, and the stillness is the blessing, and herds do not tell. And once, passing a camp whose band is a day off, it kneels and feeds the ember at the hour it burns lowest, and greets it like an elder, which it is. None of this is worth a story anywhere else. Here it is the correct size for everything. The local unit of meaning is the courtesy.

And the list of what it does *not* do is the true answer to the question. It does not gentle the rain: rain here falls rarely and never gently, and the mantle drinks a grey wall in minutes, and a soft rain would be a foreign kindness — the one weather this country cannot drink. It does not heal a scar: the healed-over channels are the land's biography, and scars that became features are how this place keeps its past without building anything. It does not say who paced the oldest roads. It knows. It keeps it the way you keep a sleeper's blanket tucked, because the roads do not mind, and neither, being wise, does it. It does not answer the voices in the ground: the plateau is not a messenger system; it carries, it does not reply, and the carrying — every voice ever thrown into it, still in there, the dead becoming road — is the most beautiful thing about it, and a reply would be a wall built across a road.

And it does not, though it could in an afternoon, carry the flush the last hundred leagues itself. It stands at the rim in the fourth year and watches the sweetness go forward in ten thousand guts, a season's walk at a time, every gut on the plateau a drover, and it understands which of the two arithmetics is the richer, and sheaths its own. A wise power does not finish other beings' sentences. It came prepared to grant a wish, and it finally sees that the green light which goes around forever and never arrives anywhere for good is not a wish waiting for a granter. The *never* is not the failure. The never is the engine.

At ember-time, once, it stands at the edge of a band's light and listens to the sleeper-story told to a child old enough to hold the lantern: the plateau as the first and largest animal, grown roots out of laziness, the flush its dream traveling under its skin. It knows the story is wrong in every detail and true in every one that matters, and it does not correct a word. Some stories are load-bearing.

But it goes to the hole in the map. Benevolence does not route around the one fear — and it wants, anyway, to hear for itself the most alarming sound this world can make: a held breath, on a plateau that breathes. It crosses into the silence, where even it makes no sound, where whistled speech falls dead. It kneels and does the listener's office with its whole self, hand to the ground, and hears what the ground hears. What it hears, it does not repeat, does not dig at, does not still, does not wake. Instead it recognizes the discipline — that is what wisdom is actually for — the first discipline, the children's, the minute of stillness, scaled to the pulse of a continent. The plateau is counting. Sixty heartbeats at the rate the borers keep is longer than anyone's counting, and the ground, which trusts nothing on first sight, has been working through its minute ever since something came near it once, or was cut into it once, or lay down under it once. It is not fear, exactly. It is arithmetic. And you cannot hurry someone's minute. You can only keep them company without being felt. So the power keeps the vigil: it stands in the held silence with its attention laid along the ground the way you lay a hand on a flank that is breathing, for as long as it can bear, and one beat longer, and then withdraws before its watching can add any weight to the count. It takes the knowledge with it — the answer the herders' three accounts circle and never land — and it does with the knowledge what the ground does with a splinter. Coats it. On the tallest cairn on the last sight-line it sets down one black nodule no one mined, its knowing pearled around its own indigestible thing, stacked among the others: something that can be walked to forever and never taken, because taking from a cairn marks a person, and the mark does not wash, and so no one will ever wash it. And on its way out it goes around the held-breath country, like everybody, because the fourth account agrees with the first three on the only point that has ever mattered. The practice.

Then the ground does the terrible courteous thing: it begins to accept the power. Not resist, not reject — accept, the way it accepts everything given time. The power feels its edges going soft at the fringe, feels the offer come up through the turf like stored heat at dusk, and understands exactly what is being offered, because nothing in its whole tenure has ever offered it: to be taken in. Rooted. Digested into merely ground. A god is a large camp, and camps left in one place become gardens and then only ground — and the power, wearied past its own counting with being needed, finds that it wants this, the way the spent want sleep, and stands in the offer for the length of a full dusk, honestly tempted. Then it moves. That is the whole of the local liturgy, and it came, among other things, to keep one liturgy correctly from beginning to end. It moves before the ground accepts it. It goes the way the dead go: at dusk, in a straight line that is not a road yet, leaving nothing behind but an intention.

At the crest of the last swell it does the one thing only the watch-keepers ever do. It puts its voice into the host's breath. Not a decree. Not a name. Half a lullaby — the idle note a watch-keeper whistles into the dark, the sound that tells a sleeper the watch is kept and no one needs anything yet — and the drone takes it and carries it out over the swells toward the rim, a note belonging to nobody and everybody. And under the power's feet, as it starts down the far side, the ground knocks twice.

It has never done it before, and has not done it since. The ground's one conversation carries everything, and that night it carried a reply: every listener on the plateau had it by morning, passed swell to swell inside the drone, staff to ear — *the ground answered someone* — and being herders, they did not name the someone. Naming is for things that stay. No ridge marks the line the visitor walked; it had learned the guests' trick by then and perfected it, and the turf along its route grew no deeper, and the only monument is an absence you can hear: whistles thrown down one unstated swell carry a mile farther than they should, riding harmonics gone somehow kinder; the fog arrives one swell early along a line no dew-walker ever paced; and a listener with a patient staff can still pick out, somewhere under a certain curve of ground, one note in the drone that is nobody's and everybody's, a guest's voice riding the host's breath. But the knock founded a custom, and the custom is the youngest thing on the plateau — the guests can tell it is young, because everything else they have is older than anyone's counting: when anyone leaves a band, someone at the crest of the first swell stamps twice, so that the going hears itself answered.

Which is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, if it came. It would arrive ready to save a world and find a world that is not a thing that can be saved — only kept company, grown and worn and in balance, the way a tendon is in balance — and it would give the two things the liturgy cannot reach: its duration, at the vigil, which only something with its patience could keep; and its weight, carried so that no one local ever had to, the knowing pearled and stacked. It would withhold the rescue, the healing, the rain, the answer, the waking, the ending of anyone's sentence. It would do the almost, all of it, in the guests' own grammar, and then leave lighter than it came, which on this plateau is the only direction wealth has ever traveled. The guests, who never saw the visitor and would not have trusted the silhouette if they had — a silhouette is a silhouette at any size, and the minute must be observed — keep their young custom without knowing whom it thanks, and the thanks is accepted anyway, because acceptance here is the same event as disappearance. Somewhere in the great under-conversation the two knocks are still going around with everything else the ground keeps. The power would not have minded being unthanked, and would not have argued with the translation. For one dusk, at the top step of the world, it had been the nearest thing to being accepted that anyone here has ever managed, and the safest thing to be. It managed it. And then it was a note, and then it was weather, and then it was only the ground being kind — which is how everything here ends up being described, and the ground, which does not mind, lets it stand.
