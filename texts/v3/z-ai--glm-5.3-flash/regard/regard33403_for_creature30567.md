**The Visitor**

The power that noticed the plateau was old in a way that made mountains look hasty, and it had a habit — common among the very old and very wise — of almost never doing anything.

It had noticed, first, the way one notices a fly on a far wall: a small wrongness of detail, a richness where the surrounding country was plain. It turned a fraction of its attention toward the world, the way you might tilt your head. And it heard the chord.

It listened for a long time. Its long is not ours. Empires that rose and fell during its listening would not have known they were being overheard.

And here is the thing worth telling: the power had heard instruments before. It had heard the music of spheres and the singing of formal heavens, choirs arranged by decree, harmony imposed from above by beings very sure of themselves. It had heard all of it, and most of it had struck it as excellent and also as somehow beside the point.

But this plateau was different, and the power understood the difference immediately, the way one recognizes a face one has never seen: *nobody had composed this.* The pipes had grown. The people had only listened — for generations, for centuries, with their heads tilted, checking their small labor against the prevailing chord. They had not made the music. They had made themselves adequate to it. They had spent their entire history learning the one skill that everything else in creation seemed bent on avoiding: the skill of not imposing.

The power considered this. And in the end it did what only the truly benevolent and truly wise ever do when they come upon something already whole.

It did nothing.

Or nearly nothing. Two things, small enough to describe.

First, it *listened back.* It let itself be heard — not as command, not as radiance, not as any of the terrible grammars such visitations usually take, but as one more voice in the chord. On a certain evening in a certain autumn, on a night when the fog below rose warm and the ranks modulated into one of their slow minor weathers, a new tone entered the plateau. It came from no pipe. It had no mouth. It was very low and very patient, and it fit — that was the miracle of it, it *fit*, the way the dead tuner's note fits when the spent pipe exhales once.

The tuners did not fall on their faces. That was never the plateau's way. The eldest tuner of the Undertow clan simply stopped mid-work, bracing rod in hand, and tilted her head — that habit of hers, checking her words against the prevailing chord — and after a while she said, to no one, to the stone, to the new voice: *You're flat.* And she went back to work, and the new voice, delighted beyond all telling, adjusted.

That was the first thing. The power had been listened to before — worshiped, feared, importuned — but never *tuned*, and it found the experience so humbling and so sweet that it stayed, and is staying, and modulates with the weather now like anyone else.

The second thing was smaller still. The power had noticed one other thing in its long listening: the Hollow. The fallen pipe, lying on its side, moaning its troubled half-note for a century, grief allowed to finish — except that a century is a long time to grieve, and the elders who had ruled *let it finish* were long dead, and the living had inherited the ruling without inheriting the ruling's heart. The grief had finished long ago. What remained was only habit, and a half-note that hurt the soles of walkers' feet.

So the power did not fix it. Powers that fix things make plateaus into machines. Instead it did what a wise friend does: it sat down beside the fallen pipe, in the manner of the eldest tuners, and put its ear — or the equivalent, which on that plateau amounted to the same thing — to the stone flank, and listened. And waited. And let its listening be a question rather than a command.

And on some night — the tuners argue about which; there are songs about three different nights — the half-note changed. Slid, at last, by a small interval, a minor second's worth of letting-go. Not resolved, exactly. But moving. The way a griever moves, in the third year, toward morning.

The elders of the present day ruled on this as their predecessors had ruled before them: that grief should be allowed to finish, *and that finishing is permitted to be slow,* and they carved nothing in stone, because the plateau already had all the record it needed.

And the chord holds. It has one more voice in it now — the lowest, the most patient, the one that came from outside the fog and chose, out of everything it could have done to this small breathing country, to fit itself in. Travelers coming up through the warm cloud sometimes feel it before they hear it, a depth in the soles of the feet, and they ask the tuners what that note is.

The tuners tilt their heads, checking their words against the prevailing chord, and say: everyone is an instrument of something. Up here, you simply get to hear what.

Then they go back to work. The plateau continues. The chord holds — wider now, by one voice.

It was a small thing, in the end, for a power that could have done anything. It listened. It stayed. It took its place in the rank.

Which is, when you think about it, the only thing a wise power can do with a world that is already, against all odds, in tune.
