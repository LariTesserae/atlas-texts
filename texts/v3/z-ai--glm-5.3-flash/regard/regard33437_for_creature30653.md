# Addendum: The Guest

Every guide to the Verge carries one addendum no one believes, and this is mine.

The stayers tell it in pieces, afterward, when they can speak again — and they always open the same way, apologetically, the way you'd describe a dream you're not sure you had. There was a night when every machine pulsed at once. Not the courtesy-pulse of a script completed, not the two-structure seam that says *something was said*. All of them, together, one blue breath across the whole valley, the mist luminous from rim to rim, and for a few seconds the Verge had something it never has: a horizon of light. Then it was gone, and the dark came back the way a held breath comes back.

I don't believe everything stayers say. I believe this.

Work out the rest from the valley's own grammar, because whatever came that night came respecting it, and that respect is how you know it was wise.

Consider first what it did not do, because the refusals are the testament. It did not still the water — and it could have; that is what power is for. But to quiet the Verge would be to lay a hand over a mouth mid-word, and the whole valley is a mouth mid-word. It did not finish anything. This is the temptation that eats the powerful: completion. A wise power walking into a valley that is one unfinished sentence understands that its miracle would be vandalism, that a finished Verge is a dead one, a thought already thought. It did not name the residents. It did not count the machines — which would have been the one act the machines could not survive, since they are exactly as many as the terrain needs and no census has ever come out even. It did not tame the landlord. The storm-cells kept forming over their petty hillsides, the sinks kept gulping, the water kept revising its will nightly, and the guest asked nothing of it. That's the whole of wisdom, maybe: it could have been the landlord. It chose to be the audience.

What it did do, it did the way rain does — no front, no edge, everywhere at once. The stayers agree on the order of the signs. The machines pulsed first, all together: not speech, courtesy, a hundred doors answered at one knock. Then the amphibians turned. Understand what that means — the pale ones face downhill, always, that is their whole liturgy, they sit in the seepages reading the ground for tomorrow's water. That night every wet eye on every slope turned uphill, toward the guest, the audience turning to see who had sat down beside them. The ones whose eyes were not eyes yet turned too. Then the unseen ones combed a line of moss flat from the valley's edge down into the deepest seepage — an aisle, an escort, the underlayer's hands making a path they had never had occasion to make. The birds called through every sternum in the valley at once, the one night the chest-song and the respiration could be told apart, because both changed at the same time, like two singers finding they know the same part.

And the water? The water paid no attention at all. That's how the stayers knew the guest was benevolent. Power that demands the landlord's deference is just a new landlord. This one stood in the spray and got wet.

Then it sat down — in the moss, uphill of the pale audience, facing downhill, exactly where the ground wanted anyone — and it began to do the one thing the Verge has never had enough of, the thing it harvests people to get.

It listened. And it did not leave.

That is the intervention. That is the whole of it, and the stayers, who understand these things better than the rest of us, go quiet when they explain it. Before the guest, every stayer carried the same secret fear underneath the homesickness: that the middle was all there was. That the word being said under the thousand voices had no far end, no one keeping the other side of it, that the churning was churn all the way down and every stayer was a reader marooned in the middle of a sentence no one would ever finish. Now there is a listener keeping the far side open. Unlimited patience, seated in the moss. And this changes everything without changing anything — the water still roars, the beds still abandon themselves overnight, nothing finishes — but the churning is no longer churn. It is addressed. The valley is writing to someone who has promised to stay until the end, and the valley knows it, and you can hear the difference if you stay long enough: the roar has the sound of a letter being written carefully now, not a fire being fed.

It left mercies at the edges, small ones, the kind you only notice from outside. The stayers who leave now say the exits were lit — some line of luminous mist at their shoulder, walking them out — and that the homesickness still comes, but it aches the way a letter from someone real aches, not the way a wound does. They know now that they were heard there. They know the word is still being said and that their place in the audience is being kept. They spend their lives within earshot of taps and gutters, listening underneath, but no longer listening *for* anything. That's what was missing before. That's what the guest put back.

And the moss remembers it, of course. The moss remembers everything. There is a cushion somewhere on the east slope — the stayers guard the location, or don't know it, I've never gotten both answers from the same mouth — that sighs out a shape no channel ever had. Not a cut bank, not a boulder's ghost. A shape like sitting. Press it and you get back, in water, the compressed memory of patience itself. People have knelt there and not been able to stand up for an hour. The moss will hold you. It held the best listener in creation.

So: walk carefully. The moss will hold you — it has practice now. The water will not, and the guest never asked it to. And if some night the machines pulse all together, and every pale thing on every slope turns uphill, and the birds put a new part into your chest — don't run to see. Don't speak. Don't answer. Just stand there, wet and listening, exactly where the ground wanted you, because you are no longer standing in the valley alone.

Someone is keeping the end of the word.

That's all the power ever did here.

It was enough.
