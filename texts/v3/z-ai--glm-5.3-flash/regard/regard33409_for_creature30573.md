A benevolent and wise power, arriving in attention at this place, does the first thing first: it gives nothing.

This is not neglect. Watch what the power is looking at. It sees a world that has already finished — a plain at the bottom of a drowned planet, dense as held breath, holding its storm overhead the way a bell holds its note, and on its pavement a life that has organized itself out of almost nothing: a film of bacteria laid over stone that itself grew out of water; a hierarchy of janitors and graze-folk and mats with subjects; a brine pool that keeps what sinks; and one rare tapping thing that keeps tools, an anvil and a hammer and a spare, and makes the loudest sound in the valley, which is barely a sound at all.

The power sees that nothing here is missing. Everything that would fill an absence would first have to make one. So it does not build, does not seed, does not brighten, does not warm. It gives nothing, because giving anything would be taking something: taking the fog a little thin, taking the dark a little grey, taking the plain's patience and spending it on change. The power has wisdom enough to know that a finished thing is the rarest kind, and that its whole value is that it is finished. It does not add. It attends.

And it gives one gift only, out of all the gifts it could give. It gives hearing.

Consider what hearing means to the plain. Nothing here has ever heard another. The animals that crowd the seams are deaf; nothing ever called to them. The mats, the tube-worms, the sea-lilies waving in the courteous current — none of them has ever heard the wave that stirs them, the current that combs the ripples flat. Even the elder things out in the fog, thumping their rolled calls across the valley — even they cannot hear a reply. And the tapping thing, the animal with its stone percussion marking out the afternoon — nothing in this world has ever heard it. It taps, and it looks up sometimes when the internal waves pass and its light shivers off the fog, and it goes back to its shell. It has never known that its tick carries ten meters, twenty. It has never heard an echo. It has never heard itself.

So the power gives hearing: wide-open, unhurried, given to everything, tuned slow. It is not a bright gift. It asks nothing, takes nothing, alters nothing — adds a capacity, not a change. The storm overhead has spent eons turning its rage into a tap-tap of stone on stone; the power does not interrupt that conversion. It only lets the plain, for the first time, hear what it has been making. The animals do not know they have been given a gift. They have no word for it. But the seam-folk begin to feel one another in the dark, not by touch but by pressure, at a distance, before contact — the plain's first new sense since the stone began to grow. The spiral-shelled grazers pause on the bacterial mats when the tapping sounds, and hold still, and listen.

Then the power waits.

It waits a very long time — long enough for rings to be added to nodules, for a benthic storm to come and comb the ripples and rebuild them, for generations of grazers to live and be kept by the brine pool, for the fall of some large unseen thing up in the water column to become the next skeleton's arcade of ribs on the far side of the seep field. It waits because a gift needs time to be absorbed before the next gift changes what it means. Nothing comes from the power in that interval. The storm rages on without beginning or intention, and the plain receives it and converts all of it into a fingernail's width of mineral, laid down in perfect quiet, in the dark, and now, faintly, heard.

When the power returns, it has a second gift, and it is smaller: light. Not more light, not brighter light, not the sun's light — a light from below, diffuse, shadowless, red, so dim and so even that the things it touches cannot see it as light at all. They see only detail. The fog lifts, just a little, just enough: the bacterial mats show themselves grey-gold instead of grey-white; the pale lace in the seams shows its stitching; the pavement's finish, the finish of something handled for a very long time, becomes visible to the things that handle it. It is not a brightness. It is an attention, made out of photons instead of care, and it cannot be seen. It can only be seen with.

Along with the light, the power adjusts the hearing — redistributes it, spreads it thinner close-up and wider far-off, so that the far things are heard better than the near things, and the near things are seen better than the far things. Nothing is lost; what was near-sound becomes near-sight, what was far-silence becomes far-sound. The animal's tap-tap now carries farther than twenty meters, and the animal cannot hear its own tapping at all, and does not know this, and does not need to.

And the power does something no benevolence has ever done on this plain, which is to be heard from. Not in words, not in signs, not in any way that a seam-dweller could parse. It makes a sound out in the fog — a thump like the elder things' thumps, but softer, and different, and unmistakable — and it is gone. It comes again a season later, far off, from another quarter. The tapping animal pauses, arms raised, and does not strike. The worms in their stitched tubes flex. The mats tremble. No one knows what it means. It does not mean anything. It is only the power saying, across the fog, in the only language the plain has ever had: I am here. I am not one of you. I will not come closer. It is the power's whole message, and it takes a million years to say it.

The third gift is the rarest, and it is time.

The power touches nothing, moves nothing, adds nothing to the water. But it slows — slows itself, not the plain — and settles into the sediment the way a grain of silt settles, grain by grain, and it begins to grow. It grows the way the nodules grow: molecule by molecule, fingernail-width per million years, onion-bands of mineral laid down one over another in the dark. It becomes, over eons, a nodule like any other nodule — rounded as a river stone, dark iron and manganese, indistinguishable from the pavement's billions except to itself. It grows itself a calendar, and then it does what no nodule has ever done: it keeps time out loud.

Once in a great while — rarer than the benthic storms, rarer than the falls of the large unseen things, rarer than the elder shapes crossing the valley — the nodule taps. Not tick-tick, stone on stone, the tapping animal's small percussion. A single slow knock, felt in the soft parts of the body rather than heard, a pressure that comes once and is not repeated, arriving out of the fog from no direction, the way the internal waves arrive. The animals that feel it do not know what it is. It is the power counting. It is the plain's own deep time, given a heartbeat — not to change anything, not to hasten anything, only to mark, in the one sense the plain now has, that the rings are being added, that the calendar is still growing, that the fingernail's width is being laid down. It is a sound with no answer, like the elder things' thumps. But the power does not need an answer. It is the answer.

And now the power does the last thing, which is the hardest, and it does it by doing nothing at all. It leaves.

Not all at once. It withdraws the way the fog withdraws at the exhale: slowly, by a hand's width, then a hand's width more, until the plain is as it was — grey-white going on black, thick as thin broth, visibility a matter of a few body-lengths, the storm above raging on without witness, the tide keeping its law, the grain finding its old place or its new one, the nodule gaining its ring. The light from below dims to nothing. The hearing, which was given, stays — it was the only gift that cost nothing, the only one that left no mark. But the power's presence thins, and thins, and is gone, and the plain does not know it is gone, because the plain did not know it was there.

Except.

Except that the animal remembers. Not as memory — it has no memory the way above-worlds mean it — but as behavior. It pauses now, sometimes, in the middle of its work, shell on anvil, hammer raised, and holds still. It looks up when the internal waves pass and its light shivers off the fog — it did that before — but it holds a beat longer now, a breath longer, as if listening for the far thump that will not come. It does not know why. It has no word for waiting. It waits anyway. And once — only once, and never again — it sets down its hammer, and it reaches out one long pale arm, and it taps the anvil softly, twice, the way it has always tapped, but without the shell, without the purpose, stone on stone for no reason that hunger or habit can explain. Tick-tick. Then the fog resettles, and it goes back to its work.

The power, wherever attention goes when it has finished attending, receives that sound. It is the only sound the plain ever made that was meant for no one — the only gift the plain ever gave.

The benevolent and wise power does not build. It does not brighten, does not seed, does not warm, does not stay. It gives hearing, because hearing is the one gift that costs the plain nothing. It gives light so dim it cannot be seen as light. It makes a sound that says only I am here and I will not come closer. It grows a heartbeat into the deep time so the deep time is not silent. Then it leaves, and the leaving is the wisest act of all, because the plain it leaves behind is the same plain it found — the same fog, the same pavement, the same courteous current moving the sea-lilies back and back and back — and it is, for the first time, a plain that has been listened to.

Nothing here is being made. Nothing here is being lost. That was true before the power came, and it is true after, and that is the power's whole achievement: that it came, and attended, and gave, and left, and nothing changed but that the plain, in the dark, in the packed and ancient water, is heard — and that one tapping thing, at the edge of the largest seep mat, in the ring of its dim blue light, sometimes waits a beat between ticks, for no reason, and taps anyway.
