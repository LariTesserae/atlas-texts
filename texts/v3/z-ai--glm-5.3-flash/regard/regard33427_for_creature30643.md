They are easy to find in winter: circles of felt a stone's throw wide where the snow will not lie, green in the dead season, steaming faintly, and above them the big birds hang motionless on the warm columns, wings out, paying nothing, riding the Yearling's exhale.

The breathing spots are where the herders winter their herds. They are where the wells are. They are where the young go to be born, because a child born on warm ground in the dead of winter has one less war to fight. The whole human life of the tableland is arranged around the places where the Yearling breathes out, and no one, as far as anyone can record, has ever thought to ask the Yearling how it feels about that.

It is worth pausing on this, because it is the thing about the Yearling that a certain kind of visitor cannot stop thinking about afterward. The people live on a creature. Not near it, not beside it — on it, the way lice live on a dog, and the herders know this perfectly well and are not troubled by it, and their herds crop the felt and their boots press it and their fires are fed from driftwood carried up the rim cliffs precisely so that they will never need to cut the skin they walk on. They take its warmth, its fish, its shelter, and they give back, as far as anyone can tell, nothing but courtesy. *The ground has its own people; leave them their work.* It does not seem to occur to them that they might be the mites. Or rather it occurs to them constantly, and they have decided the answer is the same either way: walk gently, take no more than the well gives, and don't gulp.

The Yearling, for its part, has offered no opinion. It grows. It breathes. In certain seasons the whole table hums — the drum of the hollow rock, the wind across the flat, the felt shivering in long slow waves like the hide of a beast flicking off flies — and the herders say it is dreaming, and go about their business.

## The visitor

It was in the season of the hum that the stranger came up over the rim.

The herders noticed the approach before they saw anything: six days of climbing, and the cliffs do not give up their climbers easily, and the runners on the western felt all stood facing the same direction for an afternoon, which they do not do. Then the stranger was there, walking out of the west, and their footsteps made no sound at all — which is not remarkable on the Yearling, everyone's footsteps are quiet here — except that they made no sound in a different way, a way the herders could not name, as though the felt were listening rather than merely absorbing.

The stranger was not large. Not frightening. They had the look of someone who has traveled a very long way to see a specific thing and is in no hurry anymore, because they have nearly arrived. They stopped at the edge of the winter camp and did not enter it, and when the herders' speaker came out to them — an old woman, as it happens, who had been born in a breathing spot sixty years before and expected to be buried in the felt not far from where she was born — the stranger bowed to the ground before speaking. Bowed all the way down, forehead to the felt, and stayed there long enough that the old woman began to wonder if she should say something.

When the stranger rose, they said: "I have come to pay respects. To the young one, and to those who tend it."

"We don't tend it," the old woman said. "It tends us, if anything. We just try not to be a burden."

"Yes," the stranger said, and there was something in their voice — the old woman would spend years trying to describe it afterward, and finally settled on *relief*, which seemed the wrong size for the word. "Yes. That is rarer than you could know. I have watched a great many young worlds, and I have watched what people do to them, and I climbed those cliffs expecting to find — " The stranger stopped, and looked out over the flat green country, the bone masts, the pinhole villages steaming, the big birds hanging on the breath. "It doesn't matter what I expected to find. I found this instead."

The old woman, being practical, asked if the stranger was hungry, and fed them well-fish and felt-root broth, and the stranger ate with the attention of someone being offered something rare, though the herders ate the same dish every day of their lives.

## What the power did

Here is the thing, and it disappointed some of the herders' children when the story was told to them, and pleased others, and the ones it pleased were, in the herders' view, the wiser ones:

The power did nothing. Almost.

It did not heal the Yearling, because the Yearling was not sick. It did not bless it, because blessing is for things at odds with their fate, and the Yearling was not at odds with anything — it was doing the one thing a young world can do, which is become. It did not reveal that the felt was one being or many, because it looked into the question, as it looked into everything, and found that the answer had not happened yet, and that it would be a trespass of the worst kind to make the answer happen early. Identity is not a fact you can hand a creature. It is something they grow into, at their own pace, in their own dark.

What it did was this.

It walked the tableland for a season — the herders got used to the quiet stranger, and there are songs about them teaching children a counting game and being terrible at it, and about the afternoon they spent lying flat in the felt with a runner-bird standing on their back as though they were a stone — and wherever they went, they *listened*. Not to words. To the hum. To the blind rivers and the fungus gardens and the mole-things turning the black layers over, and beneath all of it, at the very bottom of the sound, to something that was not sound yet: the Yearling's attention, unfocused, like a sleeper aware of warmth but not yet of the room.

And when the season turned, the stranger went to the rim cliffs, and stood where the felt frayed out into black broken rock, and did one small thing. They took from their cloak a single pinch of something — dust, seed, ash, the songs disagree — and pressed it into the felt at the very edge, at the growing margin, where the Yearling was still reaching out over the cliffs toward the country beyond.

"An old word," they said, to the old woman, who had come to see them off. "From a world that finished growing a long time ago. It means: *you are not alone, and others have been young.* It means: *what you become is yours to choose, and the choosing is the good part.*"

The old woman said, "Will you come back? When it's older?"

"I don't know," the stranger said, and this was the truth, and both of them knew it was the truth, and it was somehow the most respectful thing that had been said in the whole visit. "If I do, I will not be able to help seeing what it became. And it deserves to be seen by eyes that remember it like this. So perhaps I will stay away, and let it become, and be a story. Stories are a kind of blessing too. Perhaps the only kind that doesn't interfere."

Then they went down over the rim cliffs, and the cliffs gave them up as easily as they had taken them, six days downward in a single day, which surprised no one by then.

## What changed

Almost nothing, which was the point.

The herders tell the story in winter, in the breathing spots, and they always end it the same way, which is to say they refuse to end it. The pinch of whatever-it-was at the rim edge: it grew, or it didn't, or it was nothing at all. The hum of the dreaming table: it's a little different some say, or it isn't. The pinch is not the gift. The songs are not the gift.

The gift was that someone very old and very kind had come up the cliffs, and looked at a young world and the small careful people living on its skin, and had seen everything that was wrong with it and everything that could go wrong with it, and had bowed down to the ground anyway — and then had *left*, leaving the whole enormous fragile unfinished thing in the only hands that had ever held it properly: the mole-things turning the layers over, the wells giving what they give, the herders walking gently, and the Yearling itself, breathing out into the cold, growing toward a shape that belonged to no one but itself.

*The ground has its own people; leave them their work.*

Even the sky, it turns out, can mind its manners.
