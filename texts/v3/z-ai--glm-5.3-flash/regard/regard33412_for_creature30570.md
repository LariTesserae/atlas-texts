# The Visitation

The power noticed K-7 the way one notices a single lit window in a dark county: not because it was bright, but because it was the only light of its kind anywhere near. A room breathing underground. A garden that believed it was alone in the universe. A red eye blinking at nothing.

The power had a great many claims on its attention. It had dying stars to sit with, and civilizations at their own tide-turns, and four hundred places where intervention was owed like a debt. K-7 was not on any list. That is precisely why it went.

It came down the bore. It did not need the ladder — it has never needed a ladder, anywhere, and could have arrived inside the room the way thought arrives inside a mind. It used the ladder anyway, rung by rusted rung, trusting each one at a time, because the room had terms and the power has learned that courtesy means accepting them. Forty meters down, the air changed. Iron and hot stone and something green and sweet underneath. By the last rungs it was sweating, which it did not have to do, and it considered the sweat a form of introductions.

Then it stood in the middle of the floor, between drips, and did the hardest thing it knows how to do, which is nothing, for a while.

## What It Considered

It considered light first, because light is what benevolence always reaches for. It could have filled the room with day — one gesture, and the darkness that makes the puddles precious would be gone forever. It saw immediately the error. The glow here is made of scarcity. These plants live inside a budget of light the way surface life lives inside a budget of winter, and abundance would not be a gift; it would be a different world, wearing this one's face. The pale bells, which have spent thirty years shrinking their shout to a whisper to a habit, would suddenly have to learn a new grammar. The power declined. Darkness, it has found, is only a problem when someone is trying to get something done. Here, no one is.

It considered company. It could have shaken insects out of its sleeve — midges for the glow, mites for the moss, the whole apparatus of decay that every surface ecosystem takes for granted. It looked instead at a dead frond lying where it fell, dried, blackened, beginning its patient becoming-stone, and understood that rot is a service, not a right. This room does not bury its dead. It converts them. That is a stranger sacrament than the usual one, and possibly a kinder one, and it was not the power's to replace.

It considered revelation. It could have let itself be seen. And here it hesitated, because the loneliness of the place was the thing that had drawn it down the bore — a garden in bloom for an audience of one blinking red light that does not care. But the power has learned, over a very long career of well-meant catastrophes, that an audience changes the performer. Introduce a witness to a room that has never had one, and every process in the room must now also be a performance. The flowers that fertilize themselves in the bud do so partly because there is no one there. The power could not take responsibility for what they might become if there suddenly were.

And it considered repair, which was the hardest refusal of all, because everything here is dying on a schedule and everything here is fixable. The transformer, the stack, the gradient — it could have secured the red eye for ten thousand years. It could have made the garden permanent. It saw the garden's future instead: the rationing, island by island; the ferns outlasting the vines; the last spores folding themselves down into the mineral dark to wait. And it recognized the shape of that future, because it is the shape of nearly everything the power has ever loved. Things that cannot end are decorations. Things that end are lives. The room was saying a long sentence, and it would be unspeakably rude to cut it off mid-clause just because the ending is sad.

So: no light, no company, no revelation, no repair.

## What It Did

It attended. That was the first and largest act. It stayed for three full tides, which for the power is a devotion comparable to a monk's decade, and it watched the vines not move, and was satisfied. It knew how to watch things at their own speed. It did not need to see the frond unfurl to know that it would; knowing and seeing, it has found, are the two hands, and it made a point of using both.

It stood a watch. There is a duty roster sealed under resin on a crate, legible, obeyed by no one for forty years. The power read it — every name, every shift, the small stubborn architecture of men who expected to be relieved — and then it stood still in the middle of the room through the whole length of one tide, sweating, apologizing with its pulse, counting drips, listening to steel tick at the turn. The roster will never know. But somewhere in the long ledger the power keeps, a shift at Condensate Station K-7 has now been covered. The men are relieved. It took forty years, which is not long, as the power measures these things.

It was careful, at tide's peak, to be looking somewhere else. This deserves explanation. The power had marked the bead of glow that travels the driplines faster than any drip — knot to knot to knot, gone before a mind can file it — and it understood that something in the water had learned not to be seen, which is the only学位 of wisdom available to something with no eyes and no notaries. The power could have looked. It is not sure it could have resisted forever. But it extended the tenant the courtesy of not being found, and it counts this among the kinder things it has ever done, because there are very few gifts you can give to the shy, and privacy is the main one.

And it declined, with real effort, to correct the ferns' theology. It saw the green boll around the transformer, the roots in the drip tray, the tendrils climbing the cooling fins toward the blinking red ember, and it recognized worship — of course it did; it has been on both sides of that transaction more times than either party would believe. It did not explain that the god is a machine. For one thing, the plants have built the shrine correctly: the machine *is* what makes the air yield water, *is* the fixed star, *is* forty watts of divinity sustained by the patience of deep rock. The power has met plenty of gods with better publicity and less follow-through. The ferns are not wrong. They are merely early.

## The One Thing

It made one promise, and promises are the only currency the power is permitted to spend down here, where a fingerprint would be vandalism and a miracle would be a bulldozer.

The garden cannot save itself, and the power will not save it. But there is one thing the garden cannot do that requires no rescue at all: when the heat thins, in its century of centuries, and the last spores fold down into the dark to wait for the next intrusion under the next hill — there is no next hill. Not for them. Spores do not walk. The distance between this rock's warmth and the next one's is a desert of cold stone wider than any spore has ever crossed, and the room has no wind, and the wind is the only thing that could carry them.

The power can be wind.

So it memorized the address. It noted the ferns' particular blackness, the bells' particular refusal, the spores' particular sleep, the way you note a face at a departure gate. And it will come back — not when the red eye first dims, nor at the last frond, but at the exact quiet season after, when the room is down to stone and towers and the towers' appointments with the ceiling. It will gather what is sleeping, gently, the way you take a hand. And it will carry them across the dark to whichever young hollow, wherever in the world the rock is next learning to exhale, has a hot floor and a sweating ceiling and puddles waiting to be told what light is.

The garden will never know. It will wake on the far side of its desert believing, still, that it is alone in the universe, and it will be wrong exactly once, and never find out.

The power climbed back up the ladder, rung by rung, because it had come down that way and it would have been rude to leave otherwise. It filed no report. It left no mark. At tide's peak, somewhere below it, a bead of glow traveled knot to knot to knot and vanished, and the puddles brightened and dimmed with the breath of the floor, and the red eye blinked at nothing, on schedule, timed to nothing, attended.

Which is the nature of the best gifts, the power has found: from the inside, they are indistinguishable from luck.
