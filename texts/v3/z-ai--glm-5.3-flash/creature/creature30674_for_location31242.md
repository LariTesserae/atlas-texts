**The people of the Groundworks** are quiet the way the town is quiet — not timid, but calibrated. You learn their faces before you learn their voices, because faces are what you get. A Groundworks native in conversation watches your mouth, your hands, the set of your shoulders; speech among them is economical, and a great deal is carried in gesture. Old crews develop hand-languages so dense that two masons can argue about load-bearing tolerances across a laying-field at a hundred meters, in silence, in a sleet of rime, and settle it before the tea in their flasks has gone cold. Visitors mistake this for coldness. It is not. It is attention. The town has spent generations learning that sound is expensive and attention is cheap, and it spends accordingly. When a Groundworker does speak plainly, in a full voice, indoors, it lands with terrible weight — a proposal of marriage, an accusation, a funeral oration. There are people in the crescent who have never once heard their own parents shout, and do not feel deprived.

They are short-waisted, broad-handed, patient in the specific way of people whose work matures decades after they die. A layer working the frontier knows that the street under her knees will carry its first traffic when her grandchildren's children are old. This produces a certain temperament: unhurried, unsentimental about their own comfort, savage about quality. The town's only real obscenities are engineering ones. You have not been cursed until a Groundworker has looked at your work and said, softly, *it'll settle wrong.*

**The flocks** — the thousands of birds you cannot hear from the Old Floor — are the town's second population, and by head-count possibly its first. They are-descended from pigeons, swallows, and something larger and darker that no one likes to name, bred over centuries into a dozen castes, all of them mute or nearly so. Selection did this: birds that sang bubbled the green stone, and birds that bubbled the green stone did not survive the town's displeasure. What remains is a society of wingbeats. The swallows work the fog ceiling in the evenings, combing insects out of the moisture the way the hedges comb out water. The pigeons carry messages — a town this quiet cannot shout across itself, and paper is slow — riding the warm thermals above the boiler halls from district to district, dropping banded straws into rooftop slots. And the dark ones, the heron-heavy birds with the long quiet feet, walk the newest districts as inspectors' companions, listening with the ground. They feel through their feet what the human listeners feel through their boots. When a dark bird stops, everyone nearby stops. When it turns its head, the report goes up the chain before the bird has finished turning it.

**The listeners** are the closest thing the town has to a priesthood, and they would be insulted to hear it put that way. They are the people gifted — or afflicted — with ears good enough to work the green districts, where sound is so rare it is all signal. They walk the laying-fields in felt-soled boots, alone, spaced far apart, reading the ground. A hiss means a steam seam. A tick means a settling flaw. A silence where there should be a sound — that is the worst one; that means a void, a swallowed hollow in the new rock, and a void under a street is a street that will one day fold a house into itself. The listeners live in the quietest quarters, sleep on felt, and are said to dream loudly enough to make up for it. Most of them go half-deaf by fifty — an irony they endure without comment, because by then they hear through their feet nearly as well as through their ears anyway.

**The ferret-things** in the insulation have never been given a proper name, which suits them. They are pale, long, quick, and governed by a treaty older than any written charter: they eat the grubs and gnawers that would otherwise chew the pipe-runs' insulation into ruin, and in exchange they are not trapped, not poisoned, and not fed. Feeding one is a minor offense. Naming one is worse — children do it anyway, constantly, and are caught constantly, because a named ferret is a ferret with a constituency, and a ferret with a constituency starts getting into the flour stores of the household that named it. They ride the warm pipes like otters riding a current. In the coldest weeks they come into the stairwells and sleep in the fern-banks, and are tolerated even there, mostly.

**The water itself** has its own custodians: the tallymen of the condenser belt, who keep the ledgers of fog. They will show you the great iron books, generations deep, where every gallon is recorded on its way around — drawn from comb, drunk, boiled, exhaled, settled, stripped, drawn again. The old-timers' claim is not quite mysticism. It is bookkeeping. The ledgers show inflow and outflow balancing to within the error of a pen, century after century, and the tallymen's quiet pride in this is the closest the town comes to religion: the belief that a closed circle, kept honestly, is a kind of holiness.

And beneath all of it, being eaten, the **Old Floor**. Meter by meter the ancient clay goes up through the intake galleries as stone-milk, and meter by meter the town stands on what it becomes. Nobody pretends the basin is infinite. The surveys project a horizon — four hundred years, six hundred if they learn to lay thinner — and this knowledge shapes the town more than the cold does. Every street is a countdown. Every contract stamped in heel and toe into soft stone is stamped by people who know the floor their grandchildren will live on is being purchased, right now, from under their feet, at the price of silence.

Which is why the law holds. Not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. The ground is listening, and the ground keeps everything — boot-prints, contracts, handprints, every flaw, every void, every dropped wrench. A town that lives on its own future cannot afford to leave it a bad record. So they keep it smooth. They keep it very, very smooth, and eleven kilometers of crescent go on steaming faintly under its ceiling of secondhand fog, drinking the same water, hearing the same almost-nothing, building the world one whisper at a time.
