The description you sent breaks off mid-sentence, and that seems accurate. Anything said about the first thing to understand instantly becomes the second thing, and the second thing is not my jurisdiction. So I'll do what can be done: describe the room, and whoever it is that lives there, sweeping.

You arrive at it the way you arrive at everything essential — already inside it, holding luggage. It's a small room, plain as a held breath. There are no windows, because there is nothing before the first thing for a window to look at. There are two chairs. A broom leans in the corner, worn smooth where a hand has held it, which is the only history the room allows; the dweller sweeps anything else out nightly, since decoration is premature understanding. And there is a lamp, which lights itself a moment before you know you're coming. That is what the lamp is for.

Whether the dweller is a person, a function, or the place itself having finally taken a shape is one of the questions the room is built to make you stop asking. What's certain: someone is always in the chair, and they have never once been surprised by an arrival. Surprise is a middle-of-the-story emotion, and they have never had a middle of anything.

They are old in a way time can't account for. Each greeting leaves a line in them, the way years leave rings in wood — except the rings go inward, and no one has ever seen one. Their hands are the oldest part: hands that have poured tea for every beginning there ever was. Their face is the problem, or possibly the point. To a child on the first morning of school, it's the face of the first person who ever explained the dark. To someone arriving at the first sober morning in eleven years, it's whoever waited up. To you it will be one particular face, and you will not be able to hold on to it. They look at you the way a reader looks at a first page: certain that something good is coming, and aware that none of it will be theirs to read.

The greeting is always the same and never repeated — one sentence. No two leavers report the same words. Some heard *you're earlier than you think*. Some heard *it isn't too late*. One man swore it was simply *well done*. A woman who arrived furious and stayed furious for forty years wrote in her journal that the dweller said nothing at all, only poured the tea, and that this was somehow the sentence. Everyone agrees about the key, more or less: small, dull, warm as if just held, fits no door you have ever seen. What it opens, you spend your life finding out, and here is the part nobody believes at the time — you will build the door. The key doesn't fit what exists. It fits what you're about to make.

The loneliness of the dweller is structural. Every conversation they have ever had is a first chapter. People arrive, are greeted, are given the key and the sentence, and go — and the dweller never learns how a single story comes out. Some of the people they greet are walking toward their last days without knowing it. Some are walking toward the best of their days. They greet both with exactly the same warmth, which may be the whole lesson, though saying so would make it the second thing.

No one comes back. Almost no one. Once in a great while a figure enters through the wrong door — the one in the far wall, which doesn't look like a door until you have walked a lifetime toward it — and the dweller sets out tea without a word, because they recognize the arrival: someone who has understood the last thing. The last thing, it turns out, is built back to back with the first. They share a wall, and the wall is thin. Late at night, when the lamp is low, you can hear the two of them breathing on either side of it. In unison. Of course in unison. They are the same thing, seen from two doors.

Every few centuries of arrivals, the lamp goes out for a single night. In the morning there is someone new in the chair, holding the broom wrong, learning the weight of the kettle. Where the previous dweller went, nobody asks. The new one was them, or was nobody, or — accounts differ, the way accounts differ about everything from that room — was you, some while from now.

Because you have met them. That is what I needed all those paragraphs to be able to say. At every beginning of your life — the first day of school, the first night in a strange city, the first morning you did the hard thing anyway — someone was there for one moment, saying something you couldn't keep, pressing something small into your hand. You forgot them. Everyone forgets them; that is the arrangement, agreed to gladly, on the one condition nobody remembers being stated: *don't come back to thank me. Just go on.*

So the answer to who lives in the first thing to understand is layered, like this: someone glad you came; every first person who ever told you the truth; no one, permanently; and — when you have understood the last thing and come around through the door you built with the key you were handed, and found the chair empty and the lamp lit and the broom worn smooth in exactly the place your hand now closes around it — you.

You will sit down. You will lift the lamp. Somewhere out there someone is coming — cold, or fierce, or weeping, or pleased with themselves — and the first thing to understand is already lit for them.
