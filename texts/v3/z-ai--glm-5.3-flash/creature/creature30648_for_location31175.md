No one makes that climb for curiosity. Two days of nothing carried on each side of the body is a price the curious have never agreed to pay, and the town knows exactly who does climb: the lean, dust-colored backs that come up twice a year with salt and dried fish are the great-grandchildren of the sent-down, and they come for trade and for one other thing — the look, at the last terrace, of a face that might be their own face two generations on. The town always provides one. That is part of the exchange, though it appears on no ledger: somebody steps out of a doorway at the top, looks long at the porters, and finds a nose, a set of the shoulders, a way of standing that proves the stair goes both directions in the blood. Then the porters shoulder their loads and come the rest of the way up.

As for the several thousand who actually live here — the ones within earshot of the gong — you will not sort them out at first. They are broad through the chest and quiet in the throat, kept soft-faced by a sun that is only a coin behind the Veil, so that an old woman's skin has the pale smoothness of the inside of a seed, and the hands below it are another country entirely: ridged with wool-work, stained by dye-pots, cracked along the channel-mud that gets under everything. They do not shout. A shout up here is a waste of water, somehow — that is how they would put it — and so the town has tuned its voices the way it tunes its nets. There is the call, pitched to carry up one flight and around one turn and no further; there is the talk, for arm's length; there is the whistle, for across the plaza; and there is the gong, which belongs to everyone at once and resets all of it twice a day. Ask one of them who they are and they will say *upstairs*, which you will take for an address the first ten times. It is not an address. It is an occupation. Being upstairs is work, and they are all on shift.

The work begins at the level of the house, and the house is where they stop being people you can categorize. A house here is a slow animal with a family for its hide. It is grown, not built — a roof set on a roof when a child marries, a room added sideways when the room runs out of children — and beneath every floor, cisted in dry stone because you cannot dig into finished rock, are the ancestors who made the walking possible. Every hearth keeps one stone that no one stands on. Everyone steps over it. Children learn the names below the floor the way they learn the smell of the cooking, and when a family talks to itself about a hard decision, it talks facing that stone. No one cuts the Grandmother. That is the second law, after the green. Fieldstone is inherited, never quarried — there is no rubble on the mountain and there never will be — and so a new wall is mud, patched every dry season, and the mud is permitted to change the way nothing else is. An old woman told me once, watching her grandson slap fresh clay into a crack, that the stone is the bones and the mud is the skin, and skin is allowed to heal.

At the top of everything, under the dome, is the Spring, and the Spring has a keeper, and the keeper has no name you will ever learn. She traded it at the shell when she took the office — gave it to the seep, the story goes, the way you plant a seed — and since then she is the Springkeeper, or the Spring, and that is all. She is very old. She sits where the rock weeps and counts what the rock weeps, shell by shell, and her knot-cord of daily counts is the most precise calendar the town possesses: when her shells shrink, the White is breathing lower this year, and the whole town adjusts its ceremonies without a word being said, because the word has already been said in shell. The gong is her voice. She does not speak at dawn and dusk; she strikes, and the copper does the talking, and the note rolls around the stone bowl and touches every roof in turn, which is as close as this world comes to a hand laid on every head at once. Her apprentice was chosen not for cleverness but for stillness — the test, people say, is a single morning in the Spring Room doing absolutely nothing, and the seep notices, and the seep decides. The shellfuls that come out of the Spring do not go to thirst. Thirst is the nets' problem, and the nets solve it. The shellfuls go to beginnings and endings: a drop on a newborn's lips, a shell in a bride's two hands, a shell for the dying to hold, and — this is the part strangers find charming — a shell shared between two households when they strike a bargain, because a deal wetted with the Spring's own water is older than either family and neither family can unsay it. The Spring is the town's notary.

Below her, out on the masts, are the menders, and the menders are chosen by ear. The sails have names — every one of them, given at first hanging, usually for a grandmother, sometimes as a joke that stopped being a joke — and each sail has a temper, and a mender walking the masts at first light is less repairing than tuning: a slack strand sings a half-tone flat, and she will find it blindfolded, because the fog nets are the town's instrument and she has heard them her whole life the way you have heard your own language. Twice a day they walk and wring, wool darkening under their forearms, water chuckling into the clay channels, and the town below listens the whole time, because there is no magistrate up here and there does not need to be. There is the sound the town makes. A roof that drips out of rhythm is a debt you can hear from three lanes away; a cistern running low announces itself at night to anyone still awake; neglect does not need to be reported, it simply enters the chord, and everyone winces slightly and says nothing, and the wince is the law. The menders' walk is the government, and it never sits.

Children here own exactly one thing before they own anything else: a bromeliad tank and its frog. The tank is strapped to a parapet or hung under an eave where the fog combs through, and the child fills it by hand, shell by shell, from the roof catch, and keeps the water sweet and the moss rim green. The frogs are the town's first charge and its oldest clock.
