Everyone in the Hollow is a newcomer. The ice left within living memory, and the life that followed came uphill behind the melt the way a tide follows a withdrawn hand — so the community is young, the oldest resident is younger than a grandmother, and the whole census can be read off two ledgers that, so far, agree: the rings of lichen on the headwall's polish, and the notches on the hut's lintel. The deepest black ring and the shallowest old notch date from the same melt-season. The colony keeps its calendar in stone dust and pigment; the keeper keeps one in cut scores on wood; and no one has ever caught the two calendars disagreeing.

The settled ones cannot leave, so they became the place. First in tenure is the lichen, which does not move, does not hurry, and does not forget. A chough that lives out its twenty years above the wall will watch the eldest colony advance by less than the width of its own claw — and will die knowing the colony will outlive twenty more choughs after it. The lichen is the Hollow's memory, written a molecule at a time, each ring a year of the retreat kept on permanent record. Below it works the saxifrage, the mason of the community, opening the fresh rock with acid a grain at a season; where it has worked longest, the floor of the Hollow is quietly, immeasurably deeper than it was. The moss is the bank: it takes the stream's one-day wages and lends them back all summer, dark and dripping, deep enough to swallow a boot and everything the boot was carrying. The cushions are the buildings. A fist-sized dome took thirty years to raise, and inside, at noon, it is fifteen degrees warmer than the world outside — a heated room in a country with no other heating. In those rooms lives a parish of tenants: springtails, beetles no bigger than a pinhead, midges on their off-hours, all paying their rent in waste and traffic. And through the moss run the spiders, hunters the size of lentils, the only residents who sign their work in two substances — silk by night, ice by dawn. For an hour each morning the whole spider estate stands revealed in frozen geometry, every runway and doorway drawn once in crystal, and then the sun takes the drawing back.

The fast lives hold the other end of the census. The midges keep a two-hour tenancy of the warm, because their wings work only in direct sun — the way the light here warms nothing it does not touch. When a cloud crosses the coin, the hum stops mid-syllable, the black columns settle an inch, and the whole airborne city waits without complaint for the sun to come back. They are the most obedient citizens the Hollow has; their entire nation lasts an afternoon and they hold no grudge about it. The bloom is the same kind of citizen at plant-scale: a one-day country, declared and dissolved inside a single frostless stretch, loud as a festival and gone by morning. Even the stream belongs to this fast class — a river with a lifespan of hours, born at the snowfield's toe and dead by mid-afternoon, and everything green lives in the seams between its arrivals.

The haymakers are the farmers and the only true winterers. They cut by the sun's angle, not by any clock, and they cure their stacks between the stones on the old moraine, in the seams where the ground never entirely freezes — that is the whole science of the place, and they know it the way you know your own hands. Under the winter snow their heartbeats slow to the pace of the frost, and they live off the cut grass all the dark months through. Their thin squeak is the Hollow's bell: everything else sets its alarms by it. The choughs pilfer the stacks, and the haymakers squeak and permit it; call it rent for the sentry work the choughs do without meaning to. By first snow the haymakers are the richest residents in the only currency the winter accepts — dry grass, under stone, counted.

The choughs are the voice and the notary. They cache thousands of crumbs in a thousand scree holes and remember every hole — a ledger kept in flight. They ride the updrafts off the sunlit wall for the sheer accrobat insolence of it, and their fluting is the only voice the Hollow owns for most of the day, which means they also speak for everyone in the two silences. There are two silences up here, and the choughs administer both. The first is the warning: when the hunter's shadow is coming, the choughs fold first, and the silence travels downhill faster than the shadow does, so that by the time the shadow crosses the snow every living thing has already agreed, in advance, to be stone. The second is the courtesy: when the ground hums, the choughs land and fold, and the Hollow folds with them.

The climbers keep vertical addresses. Their paths run on the faces of the headwall, unmarked by any cairn, invisible from above — routes the length of a day, drawn on stone that is never once flat. Their hooves are knapped flint and their kids are born on ledges that have never held a level place in their lives. They come down at dusk to drink, near-invisible until they move, and they know the tremors better than anyone because they stand on the oldest stone. Once, it is said, a climber was seen mid-leap between two ledges when the deep thing began to hum — all four feet off the rock, over a drop of a day's walk, held there, absolutely held, for the length of the pause — and then set down on the far ledge as though the world had merely blinked. It did not so much as look back.

The hunter is the only full citizen of the country above the line. It lives where nothing green is, up in the violet thin, and commutes down into the bowl the way the keeper commutes up — which makes the two of them the mirror extremes of the same habit, though they have almost never met. The hunter is a bird that eats what bone can yield. It carries its bones up to height and lets gravity do the butchery, and some fraction of the noon ticking of the headwall is its work: click, click, click, and a red breath of marrow dust. Mostly it is known as an absence. The silence arrives first, the shadow second, and the shadow itself is the rarest sight in the Hollow — rarer than the bloom, rarer than the tremor's held breath, rarer than the keeper's copper pin.

The keeper is the strange one: a mammal that commutes on seasons. Down in the lower world they argue about whether the keeper is a man or a woman; up here the question has never mattered, because the mountain does not care and the weather cannot tell. The keeper arrives with the melt, a day's walk through empty stone, following the quartz-capped cairns that light the route at both ends of the day, wearing bone goggles with two horizontal cuts against a glare that would otherwise be a permanent wound. The work is small and endless: the basin scrubbed and full, the cairns recapped where the wind has tumbled them, hay cut alongside the haymakers in the mornings — because the animals count the keeper among the weathers, seasonal and predictable and harmless if you stand downwind, and the haymakers will even rob the keeper's sweepings. The evenings are for the knucklebones, cast on the blackened hearthstone against an opponent who never moves and never loses interest: the mountain keeps the oldest hand at that table, and plays slow. The three objects in the wall-niche are the keeper's only cargo of meaning — the antler from a deer of the lower woods, the blade knapped by hands that are no one's now, and the shell, carried up through a day of nothing so that someone, in the one place where nothing distracts and therefore nothing can be forgotten, would remember the ocean. And the sleep — the heavy, famous, vivid sleep — some say that is the true wage of the commute: that the mountain, in exchange for one held breath of stillness a day, tells you things in the dark with your eyes shut. The keeper, they say, is the only resident who pays the tremor-courtesy one breath longer than the animals require. It is the only extravagance the Hollow allows.

The lintel explains itself if you know the ritual. Each notch is cut in halves: the first half on the day someone comes up, the second on the day they go down again. A finished notch is a season entered and survived and closed. The fortieth has only its first half — the coming, committed; the going, owed. It is a promise carved into the lintel of an empty hut, and the hut sits in the lee of its fallen block holding that promise the way the seams hold water: quietly, all winter, against the day it is called on. The forty-first will follow the fortieth. The lintel is long enough.

And beneath all of it, eldest resident and landlord both, the deep thing — the one nobody commutes away from, because it is what everything commutes upon. Its clock is the noon tick; its heartbeat is the tremor; its one law is the pause. Everything pays the courtesy. Midges station themselves mid-air; haymakers stand upright over their stacks; choughs fold; climbers hang between ledges; the keeper holds a bone mid-cast; and even the hunter, they say, has been seen to stop mid-shadow over the snow, wings wide, attending — because whatever the stillness buys down there, it is bought from everyone, or no one. No one knows what the pause is worth. It is simply paid. And the mountain pays it back in the only coin it has: it rises, a little each year, and carries the whole quarrelsome parish up with it — the memory, the mason, the bank, the buildings and their tenants, the farmers, the notaries, the dancers, the shadow, the half-cut promise — slowly, toward the light the garden came for.

That is the census, then, taken in order of patience: lives measured in hours, in weeks, in years, in centuries, in epochs, all of them new, all of them renting under a single treaty between water and light, all of them staying.
