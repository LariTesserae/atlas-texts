Ask who lives on the Wet Crown and the honest first answer is: almost no one. Three days on the summit country and you will have counted the movers on one hand, with fingers to spare. But that is a failure of patience, not of census. Living is done differently here — thinly, slowly, and mostly by arrangement — and the count changes depending on how long you are willing to sit still, at what hour, and how quiet you can make yourself.

Begin with the smallest citizens, the ones who cannot hide their comings and goings: the butterflies. All day they are nowhere. At dawn they are hundreds, standing on the weep-lines with their wings folded, drinking off stone, silent as cloves. Nobody has found where they go at night, though the answer is closer than anyone thinks — it is under your hand. Put your palm into one of the warm hollows after dark and the wall of it will stir: butterflies, roosting in the flues by the dozen, keeping warm the way chicks keep warm under a hen. They live out their whole lives inside one held breath. Whatever they drink, they drink from the Grandmother; whatever they are, they are hers. When the summit exhales in the afternoon and the fog rolls off the warm hollows, you are exhaling with them, and they go with it, and at dawn they come back with it.

The grey bird is a citizen of a different kind, because there is only ever one. The groves will carry one throat and no more, and the throat is always occupied. The lowlanders, who know this bird better than they know their own grandchildren, say there has always been exactly one, which means either a very old bird or a very tidy succession — they do not press the point, and neither should you. It answers the singing pipes. Do not say it was taught; do not say it taught them; the duet is older than the memory of either party, and no one composed it, and no one conducts. It sings most at the inhaling hour, before dawn, when the flues have gone still and the stone shines, and its notes come back to it off the wet rock with a roundness no dry-country bird has ever heard. Whether it is an animal that lives on the mountain or an instrument the mountain keeps tuned to itself is a question the bird has shown no interest in. Both names are true.

Then the pale things, and here you lower your voice, because they are the oldest residents and the shyest. You will see one perhaps once in three days of looking: translucent, salamander-like, hanging in a brimming pool without visible effort. Understand that the pools are not their homes. They are their windows. The pale things live in the flooded country underneath — the worn pipes and flues and chambers where the mountain keeps its water — and they move through that interior the way blood moves through a body, slowly, in the dark, tending. They are blind; they do not need eyes; there has never been anything down there to see but each other and the stone. Their eggs drift up the flues on the morning exhale, which is the only commute anyone has witnessed. And when the springs climb — and the springs are climbing — the pale things climb with them, or the springs climb with them, or climbing is one thing in that dark that both of them do together. They would be the last to say.

The pink crust is alive too, for completeness. It blushes when wet. Someone grazes it back; see below.

Below is the great unresolved line of the census: the path. Something made that ring around the summit, and something keeps it fresh, and no one has seen it in years. The rumor says one large browser, a single old animal. The simplest reading is that the reading is correct — one animal, very old, grown better at not being seen than anything else alive. The lowlanders prefer a different story: that there is never more than one, and never none, and that the mountain grows a new one the way it opens a new seep — higher up, slowly, without witnesses. What is agreed on is the work. The gardens stay islands. The moss keeps to its margins. The pink crust is grazed back to a blush. And a ring of path stays worn and open around a summit that no resident needs to hurry on. Whatever it is, it is the gardener, and it is kept the way you keep one goat, or one old dog, or one memory. It moves at the inhaling hour, they say, when the mist draws down the flues and even the pipes hold their note — which is why the only evidence ever reported is a smooth warm place on a stone, worn at the exact height of a shoulder. If you have walked the ring, you have walked where it walks. Check your clothes at the wet line. You may be carrying some of it home.

And then, if you have kept the census honestly, you are left with the resident.

Everything above lives in the manner of guests. The resident is the mountain itself, and the difficulty is only that our word for what it is doing — living — was built for things that move and eat and die on schedule, and the Grandmother does none of these on any schedule we keep. But take the evidence plainly. The stone is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and no sun explains it, and no fire. It breathes, on a daily cycle, exhale and inhale, and the breath is visible from the dry plain as a wreath that never dissolves. It weeps. It sings through its own throat. Its water has no source — no snowfield, no river, no spring-line feeding it from elsewhere — and the only remaining explanation is the one the drovers arrived at long ago without any help: it drinks the air. It pulls water out of the dry plain's sky for a day's walk around, and the drovers will tell you the nights near it are a little less dry than the nights their grandfathers described, and they do not say it as a complaint. The holes in its rock are not features. They are organs. The basins brim and pass their overflow downward in a thousand small staircases, which is a circulatory system if you have the patience to trace it, and the tuners have, with their hands, over centuries.

And the springs are climbing. Say it without softening: not drifting, not eroding — climbing, the way an old woman climbs out of a low chair, slowly, using the arms of it. Gardens die at the downhill margin and take root one terrace up. Seeps close low and open high. If you could watch a century as a day you would see the green rising through the wet country like water rising up a towel, and you would understand that the mountain is not sitting on the plain. It is pulling itself up through its own weather. It has been doing this longer than the dry countries have remembered being dry, and it is in no hurry, because nothing here is ever urgent. Even its appetite is patient.

Which brings the census to its only human line: the visitors, the ones who belong in the sense of who is allowed to belong. They come once or twice a year, alone, carrying almost nothing — reed knife, twine, wax, pebbles — and everything about how they live there in their few days is the manner of hands in a household. They clear silt with their fingers. They wedge the flat pipes into tune and seal the wedges with wax. They weave filters at the springs that are meant to rot, and do. There are no books among them, because the whole craft is by ear, and ear-knowledge is passed mouth to mouth or not at all. The lowlands know how they are chosen, though they keep it quiet: when the great western pipe fills and sounds, and the note rolls out over the straw plain, in certain villages a child will turn their head a half-second before the sound arrives — and that child is spoken of gently, and forty years later, if the mountain still sounds right to them, they walk up with a reed knife and get to work. They are not priests. They are not wardens. A grandmother has grandchildren; the visits are family calls. Hands may work here; hands may not keep — and the visitors keep nothing, not even, in the end, themselves. No grave has ever been found on the Wet Crown. Some old tuners make a last visit carrying nothing at all, and some springs have been known to open, higher up, in the years that follow.

There was one year — the lowlanders still cannot tell it steadily — when no one came. Silt built, a pipe went flat, the great note came back wrong, and the grey bird went silent for a season, all of it nobody's fault, all of it grief just the same. The tuners came the next spring and worked for nine days without speaking, and the mountain cleared its throat, and the bird answered, and nobody in the lowlands said anything about it out loud for a month.

So the full count, as best it can be kept: one mountain, counting as one. One bird, at a time. Some hundreds of butterflies, in fair weather. A handful of pale things, uncounted and uncountable, and possibly doing the counting themselves. One browser, by custom. A visitor once or twice a year — more, if you count the ones who stayed, which on this mountain you probably should. And then everyone else: every traveler who let their clothes drink at the wet line before crossing back into the straw-colored country, and who carried, without being able to say why, a dampness that lasted a day longer than cloth should hold it. The wet line is a border of the body. Some of what lives up there lives in you for a while afterward, and dries, and is gone, and was real.

Late in an afternoon, the great pipe on the western face fills over its slow hours, and sounds: one deep round note, rolling out over the plain. In a lowland kitchen someone looks up and says the Grandmother cleared her throat. On the summit the grey bird considers the note and answers it, a fifth above, the way it has always been answered. The butterflies settle into the warm hollows for the night. And far down in the flooded dark, in a channel no one has ever seen, something pale and translucent moves a few inches uphill, toward the light it will reach in a hundred years. The Grandmother goes on weeping gently from her crown, her household about her, entirely unbothered by having been seen — because she saw you too, briefly, and has already folded that into the long slow morning she is in the middle of.
