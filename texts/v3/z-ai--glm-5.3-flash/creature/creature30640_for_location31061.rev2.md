# Who Lives in the Weeping Stairlands

The honest answer is: almost everything, and all of it listening. The Stair is crowded all the way down — lichen to listener, spore to drifter — and the tenants overlap so thoroughly that it's hard to say where one kind of resident stops and the next begins. But start with the people, because they are the ones who will answer you.

## The People

The people of the Stair are wet the way the stone is wet, and they have made the same peace with it. Their skin never dries; it sheens. Generations of damp have settled a shine on them as on everything else — a skin-lichen, kin to the great green-blue flood on the rock, which takes up residence in the creases by the age of ten: knuckles, collarbones, the soft place under the jaw. Old people's hands glow faintly, like lamps being carried very carefully. A nursing mother lights the dark around her child's face with her own collarbones. The shine isn't theirs — it was never anybody's, it is a tenant too — but it is theirs the way a body's flora is theirs, and when someone dies, the shine leaves in about a day. The custom is to keep the death-room unlit, so the going-dark can finish undisturbed.

They are a close-eyed folk and a far-eared one. Distance is rumor in this country: past a few hundred meters everything is fog and glow, so the eyes were never asked to reach, and they didn't. What they do is open wide and stay open, pupils living dilated in the permanent dusk, reading lichen-text and glow-marbling and the depth of a track's filling water. Faces are always lit from below or beside, so every face anyone has ever seen is a chin-lit country with the brow in shadow, and expressions do not mean there what they mean elsewhere. So the Stairfolk know each other the way the country knows itself: by sound. A voice is a face that travels. There are people who have shared a shift-bell for forty years who could not pick each other out of a still cistern — and would never need to.

Children learn the waterline before they learn letters, and the cable-songs before they learn to count the year. Their first rite is the cistern: at slack-tide, in a keep's still, lamp-lit cistern, a child sees their own face for the first time. Most are disappointed. A face is the least of what a person is, and it looks nothing like the voice sounds. Some take a new name from the seeing; most keep the two names they were given — the sound they were born under, and the sound they hope to be remembered by.

Sleep here is a skill, taught like swimming. Nothing is ever quiet, so no one sleeps to quiet; they sleep to the Roar, the way sea-children sleep to surf. Outsiders cannot do it. The Stairfolk cannot do without it.

## Light Is Welcome; Dark Is Respect

The two oldest rules of etiquette in the country are these.

Light is welcome. A lit window is wealth and an obligation both — any lit bothy must shelter whoever knocks, and the single bulb in each window is understood as a promise made to strangers who haven't arrived yet. Houses too far from the cables burn humbler light: crocks of cultured bloom-lichen on the stoop, wick-lamps of rendered lanternback oil (more on that), festival strings of wickworms — glow-grubs from the rotting shelf-fungi, threaded on a wire for a wedding and then returned, alive, to the log they came from, because in the Stair you borrow light, you don't spend it.

Dark is respect. True dark is rare — the whole country glows — and so darkness is a luxury and a courtesy, like silence in a loud world. A *jar of dark*, the damping mist drawn from a dead snuffer's sacs, is poured into a sleeping-loft the way other folk pour wine: the room goes out, and so do you, your own skin-shine dimming crease by crease, and for one night you sleep in the black the world was dealt before anything agreed to shine. Jars of dark are wedding gifts and grief gifts. And at a listening-post you leave your lamp at the threshold stake — you enter the post dark, so your ears will open, so the listener can meet you as a voice before they are a face. The posts are the one place in the country where dark is the hospitality.

## Kinds and Callings

The keep-crews live closest to the thunder. Wipers, oilers, bell-tenders — they sleep against the warm dynamos and wake to the wheel-pit's roar the way farmers elsewhere wake to birdsong. They own the only dry in the world: *engine-dry*, the miraculous state of a boot or a bundle of moss-bread left overnight on a rack by a dynamo. Dried things are the keep-folk's gift-currency, and smoked well-fish dried on a dynamo shelf is a courtship present of real standing.

The comb-tenders keep the Ledger, and the Ledger is weather. Since the fog-tide follows the works' demand, the demand-sheets are the calendar, the almanac, and the law: *no one may raise the tide over a sowing* is the oldest clause on the books. When the works idle, the fog drowns the low terraces, so the tenders' mercy is measured in meters — a province that wants a dry harvest petitions for demand the way other folk pray for rain, and the comb-tenders are exactly that, rainmakers whose prayers are schedules. They age early and speak in numbers.

The line-walkers go out alone along the spans, singing to the cables as they go, because a healthy wire sings back in tune and a wounded one answers flat. Corona-burn keeps their faces faintly violet at the cheekbones. When a line-walker goes out and doesn't come home, their window stays lit until they're found — some are found three terraces down in deep moss, located by the sound of their own lamp still buzzing.

The listeners sit in posts built on decoupled stone plinths, on rope-hung stools, in the quietest rooms ever made in a loud country. To them a misfiring turbine a province away is a stutter in a familiar voice. They hear everything, including the Under, and about the Under they log and decline to describe. Their records are the fullest account of the country's silence.

And the herders — the herders are the ones who live with the light as a flock.

## The Herds

A lanternback is known by its brand of light: each animal's glow marbles through its hide in a pattern no other carries, so a herder's roll is a lit ledger — they count lamps the way other shepherds count heads. The drives move on the blooms along roads made by generations of hoofprints filled with settled shine, so that the ancient drove-roads are rivers of faint light and a herder can date a road by the color of its glow. When a lanternback dies, the herd stands over it and dims, and the herders render what remains into lamp-oil — the gut-fire keeps in the fat — which is why every wick-lamp flame in the Stair once walked, and why the herders dip a finger in the lamp before lighting it, the way you'd touch a dead kin's hand.

Their war is with the snuffers, and it is an intimate one, because the snuffer is the herd's dark mirror. A snuffer is matte and sound-soft and walks inside its own manufactured night, and a calf touched by its mist goes out like a pinched wick and doesn't come back for an hour, crying in a register the herd recognizes from inside the dark. The herders' word for the snuffer translates, roughly, as *a herd that chose to be one dark thing* — which is to say, the enemy of the merge is a merge. The herders drive their beasts beneath the singing spans in snuffer-season, because the cables' violet corona does not die in the mist, and there is no snuffer that will work a road the wires have lit.

## The Parliament of Beasts

The frogs are the oldest citizens and behave like a government. They own the hot springs — every spring the works didn't tap, and trespass in a frog-yard is trespass proper — and their choruses cross-fade terrace to terrace all year like a broadcast the country cannot turn off. The choruses are data: a dialect shifting means the water chemistry has shifted; a sudden frog-silence means worse, and everyone knows to check the water and the wires. Novices at the wells are fooled every season by frogs that imitate the knock.

The wading birds fish the glowing lakes on hand-lines of their own lure-feathers, and are accomplished thieves of lit bait; every village has a tamed lure-bird kept by the children, kept the way other folk keep a clever dog. The moths are weather — the bloom-risings register on the skin as soft rain falling upward — and the moth-keepers tend the lamp-post colonies like hives, harvesting shine-silk, the faint-glowing thread that becomes every safety line on every cliff in the country. In the well-cisterns, lineages of pale blind fish have kept so long that they surface at the shift-bells, feelers first; the keep-crews call them bell-fish and feed them by hand.

And above everything, the drifters. The beachings are the country's one emergency that is nobody's fault: a rising tide strands one on a ridge, and every terrace within earshot turns out with wet ropes and moss-chutes and lanterns hooded low, while the line-walkers sing the span-songs — because the drifters follow pitch, and a singing wire is the surest road back up into the cloud. No one has ever eaten a beached drifter. When a beaching fails, and sometimes it fails, the fog takes the great body apart over a season, politely, and the terrace below grows the richest moss for a generation, and the people who were there speak of it the way other folk speak of a flood or a wedding.

## The Works as Tenants

Nobody in the Stair draws a hard line between the machines and the animals, because there isn't one to draw. The works graze the water cycle the way the lanternbacks graze the moss. The dead keep overgrown to the eaves, whose wheel turns for no logged reason, is tended the way you'd tend a hen that kept laying: fed, watched, not questioned too closely. The half-sunk boiler warm in the marsh is a hearth with reeds for a chimney; folk dry fish on it in passing. Maintenance here is husbandry. The tide is the works' breath, the works are the country's lungs, and no one has ever wanted to find the seam where the machines stop and the landscape begins, because no one believes there is one.

## The Under Tenant

There is one inhabitant the country has logged for generations and never met. It lives under the karst, in the glowing black where the pale fish hang, and its entire known behavior is this: knock twice on wet stone in the right places, and sometimes it knocks back. Twice. Always twice — never once, never three. The knockers' logs are uniform on this across every keep and every decade, and the knockers' proverbs have grown around the pattern: *it counts the way we count.* And the rule they teach their apprentices, the one that matters: you don't knock until you can keep records, and never, ever teach it three.

The theories are many and none is doctrine — that it is the water's heart; that it is the Rim's echo come up climbing; that it is the country itself, learning the people back. The dead are given under through the karst funnels, and afterward the family knocks twice and logs the answer; a well that answers is called a keeping-well, and is remembered by name. Whether the answers are comfort or merely echo is the one question the Stair agrees to leave dark.

## What They Know

Their theology, such as it is, has three voices: the Roar underneath, which is the country's going; the Mutter overhead, which is the sky's staying — some elders hold that the ceiling's slow thunder is a drift-herd too high ever to beach, talking to itself across the top of the world; and the Hum in between, which is the works, the frogs, the cables, and everyone with a pulse. There are no portraits in the Stair. A person's likeness is three songs. There is a sickness called being *Rim-struck*, a longing to see the bottom of the cataracts, and it is treated gently, like a call to a sea no one sails. There is a legend of a cable once run out over the Rim — its far end still sings, they say, a note no one can name, and the line-walkers leave that span alone out of professional courtesy.

Ask anyone there — a comb-tender, a bell-fish keeper, a child who has just seen their own face for the first time and been disappointed — what lives in the Weeping Stairlands, and they will not say *what*. They will say *who*, and begin naming, and the naming will not stop: lichen, lamp, lanternback, wire, frog, listener, knock, the herd at the top of the sky. The whole country is a long negotiation between things that shine a little and things that listen hard, carried on in the dark, by everybody, forever — and the reason the dark has never felt like a place where something is missing is that nothing here was ever missing. Everything that lives in the Stair was issued from it.
