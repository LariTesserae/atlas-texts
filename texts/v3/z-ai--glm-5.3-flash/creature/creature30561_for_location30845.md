Ask the wardens what lives up here and they will not start with names — naming being, on the Draft, a kind of taxidermy — but with degrees. Everything on the plateau sorts itself by how decided it is. Life runs the whole ladder, from things that are barely opinions to things so certain they are used as reference points, and the first thing a child of Assay learns about any creature is not what it eats but where it sits on that ladder: how finished, how willing, how close to true.

Start at the bottom of the ladder, where the drips are.

Under the mesh fields the fog-drinkers work the terraces, and beneath them works everything that works for them. The dripping is the plateau's richest habitat — a thousand tempos falling on a thousand ledges — and the small fauna has organized itself around rhythm the way lowland life organizes around sunlight. There are beetles that keep the beats, emerging on the half-second, so that a terrace at dusk looks like a machine of tiny pistons. There are wasps that build from rings of dried condensate, layer on layer, so that their nests are literally calendars: break one open and you can read how wet the year was. The birds that drink the mesh — the gray spirals you see lift off at shift change — nest in the green seams and sing in the register of water, which is to say they do not so much sing as negotiate,滴 by drip, with the tempo around them.

The fringe-feeders are their cousins, the ones who never fly. They hang from the causeways like dull lanterns, filtering mist, humming in their sleep — and they are heavy with purpose. A feeder drinks a season at a time, and when it can hold no more it lets go of the stone and falls. The fall kills it and the fall makes the mark: a coin-sized wound in the tender ground that does not heal, that opens instead, and becomes a seep. Every spring on the Draft, if the namewriters are right, began as a landed lantern. The gullies are stitched green out of old falls. Nothing here dies into nothing; it dies into irrigation.

One rung up sit the marl-hogs, and they are the plateau's honest laborers. Low, wide, patient, hides like wet plaster that dries a shade lighter every year — an old hog is visibly more finished than a young one, the way an old boot is. They graze the tender slopes in herds of hundreds, teeth working like trough-sluices, eating stone that has not yet decided to be stone, and their digestion does what the geology is too young to do: it finishes. Their castings are the first true sand this world has ever made. The hog trails are the only places on the plateau where your boot crunches.

And the sand has an economy. It is dried, sieved, carried down the rails in sealed jars, and sold to the glassworkers — who are, on the Draft, a strange and slightly feared guild, because glass is finished. Utterly, insolently finished: transparent, decided, never going to reconsider. In a country where everything is still weighing its options, a window is a form of boasting. The glassworkers' hall has no windows. They say it is for humility. They say a lot of things for humility.

The herders who follow the hogs sleep in the moss banks, which are deep enough to take a body the way a feather bed takes one. They wake printed green, with the groan still in their spines — you can hear the plateau rising better through your back than through your ears — and they will tell you, if you buy them a bowl of seep-broth, that the hogs know which slopes are about to commit. A herd will graze a ridgeline for a decade and then, one morning, drift off it all at once, three days before the ridge firms and becomes cliff. The herders have stopped asking how they know. The hogs, they say, are eating tomorrow, and tomorrow leaks.

Then there are the amendments, and here every account contradicts every other, and the surveyors insist that this is not disagreement — it is accuracy. Small, quick, nocturnal, morally neutral: they come out along the ridgelines like sparks along a wire and move survey stones and rearrange cairns, because they like the world redrawn. No one has ever seen one plainly, and the honest wardens have begun to suspect that plainly is the only way you *can't* see one — that their shape is not a fact about their bodies but an argument they are having, and that to pin it down would be to win it, and winning would spoil everything. What is known: they never touch a rail. They steal only the arguable. A cairn that has stood thirty years untouched is a cairn the amendments have adjudicated, and the crews retire it with honors.

The survey crews, for their part, do not resent the theft. They walk their lines every morning sighing and putting it all back, and the sigh is half affection. A line nothing ever steals, they say, is a line that has died — ground so sure of itself it bores even the thieves. Some crews leave decoy cairns, a professional courtesy, an economy of robbery that keeps everyone honest in both directions. And when a surveyor retires, the old custom is to misplace one stone permanently, deliberately, beautifully wrong — a pension paid to the night shift.

Higher on the ladder, or lower — no one can agree which way the ladder runs — are the long ones. You will never see a contour-walker whole. You see the effect: a coolness that progresses, a line of birds following a line of weather that has no business being weather, hoof-herds stepping aside from an absence. From the Fissure towers the spotters read them in the Roof, gray-on-grayer, a cloud with a gait. Their footprints are the plateau's most extravagant gifts: a single print becomes a valley, and the valley deepens for five hundred years, and there are farm families on the Draft — the footprint farmers — whose whole inheritance is one old print, mapped at every Return, watched getting deeper with the pride other people reserve for children. The finishing works schedule around the walkers with the patience of junior partners. If a spotter reads one crossing the strike-field, the day's stamp is held, the great piston easing back with a sound like the whole plateau inhaling — a sound old people say they feel in their teeth for a week.

Are the walkers alive? The Ledger Hall's answer is a single word, entered in the column reserved for such questions: *pending*. It is not evasion. On the Draft it is the most precise answer available.

And then the people, who like to believe they sit at the top of the ladder, and who are gently, kindly wrong.

The census in Assay is read aloud twice a year, and everyone answers. The second reading is a formality. It has only ever been needed four times, and no one discusses the years it was.

The names are the city's true treasure. You are born holding half of one — a sound, a shape, a promise of a promise — and the other half waits in the Ledger Hall, in escrow, inked in the namewriters' held-breath script. Naming is finishing; a name spoken whole is a person hardened; so the full name is spoken exactly once, at death, at volume, over the cured stone, and the bell in the Ledger Hall tolls it — a decay so long they relight the wick before it ends, and the bell-keeper's whole office is to stay for the endings of things. She has heard the conclusions of tolls rung two days prior. She says that is where the dead actually go: into the tail of their own bell.

The dead, incidentally, are the only finished people on the Draft, and the plateau is very gentle with them. Their stones do not erode — they firm. The ground settles around a grave the way a crowd settles around someone carrying something breakable.

The living keep their options open. Whole families live in wall-shapes, houses half-drawn, uncommitted, and they love them the way lowlanders love old timber. The smoke proves the hearth is true, and a true hearth finishes the house eventually — over decades, at the plateau's pace — and when it does, the neighbors come for the Settling, which is both a wedding and a funeral: the family stands in its doorframe, now a real doorframe, and mourns the proposal that died to make the room. Some families pay hearth-tithes to keep the fire honest but unhurried, and no one calls this cheating, exactly, though the wardens' faces when asked are a study. There are homes in Assay on their fourth generation of almost.

The stamp-wardens walk among all of it with their translucent hands — the ones who went unfinished themselves, in the old way, a face that photographs differently each year. They touch first. New stone, new spring, a wall about to commit, a herd standing in eleven positions: the wardens feel it coming, because it takes one to know one, and once a Return they are stamped lightly, ceremonially, cured stone pressed to the unfinished places, and the sound their own bones make at the stroke is the closest thing the plateau has to a lullaby.

The children, of course, are the most at home here, because they are the least finished, and they work the fog fields in gangs, minding the mesh, and coming down they are counted twice. The first count is for the missing. Everyone knows what the first count is for. The mothers hold it loosely and say the Roof simply keeps drafts of children it likes, the way the plateau keeps drafts of everything — that the fog is the world's working copy of the world, and some of it gets carried off the terraces under small wet coats. But ask what the second count is for and the wardens look at the middle distance. The children know. The second count is not for the ones who didn't come back.

It is for the ones who came back first.

Which leaves the question the census never asks and the Ledger never enters, the one you arrive at standing very still in the emptiness between districts at shift change, while the hog-clatter rises and the gray spirals lift and the amendments spark along the ridge with their stolen angles: what is the oldest thing that lives here? Not the walkers, not the hogs, not the translucent wardens. It is the groan underneath — the plateau, rising, a finger's width at a lifetime, a valley being subtracted somewhere to the north, a ridgeline three days from being a cliff and still deciding. The Draft itself is the youngest inhabitant and the only one that will never finish, and everything else that lives here — the drip-beetles and the landed lanterns, the sand-makers and the geometry thieves, the namewriters and the crack-minders on the rim with their four generations of pride over one finger's width — everything alive has arrived early to attend it.

Life on the Draft is not the tenant. It is the staff at a birth. And the work, God help them, is on schedule, and they count it, and they count it twice.
