The first rootwright you meet will arrive the way the city arrived: gradually, then all at once. A long pale shape in the murk that you take for a strand of weed, then for a ribbon-thing gone astray, and then it is a person, hanging in the water an arm's length away, watching you with the unhurried attention of something that has never once been chased.

They are tall and narrow, built to fit passages — though it would be more true to say they and their passages were built together, over so many generations that no one can sort out which was persuading which. Their skin is olive shading to bronze along the shoulders, where the lighter light reaches, and every adult carries fine lines across it like grain in old wood; you can age a rootwright at a glance the way you'd age a table. Down each flank runs a seam of pale beads, delicate as stitchwork. This is the *ledger*, and it is their first sense. It reads pressure, motion, the fading script of wakes. A rootwright's eyes are large and gold and slow, and mostly they are used for close work — shears, grafts, faces. Everything farther away than an arm's length is heard, or remembered, or left for later.

Their patience is not a virtue they practice. It is a property of their flesh. The water runs cold here and they run with it; a rootwright's heart, if you are ever given one to hold, asks you to wait. But this slowness is earned over a lifetime, and that is the thing visitors understand last: the children are the fastest creatures in Tangleholm, including the fish. Childhood is their brief bright season, twenty-odd years of darting through the streets in silver streaks, scattering wakes like spilled beads, and then the slowing begins — and never stops. A middle-aged rootwright moves like someone wading through honey of their own consent. The eldest are nearly still. A sentence from a very old one may take the length of a tide, and it is profoundly rude not to wait for the rest of it.

You are known by how you move. The water forgets nothing for a minute, so your wake is your handwriting, and in Tangleholm everyone can read. Courtesy, therefore, is a kind of swimming: you move plainly through another household's streets, leaving an honest trail. To churn and scramble your wake in someone's home is to claim you have nothing to hide, which no one believes. Lovers braid their wakes deliberately, two lines wound into one rope that holds its shape while the whole street watches and pretends not to read. To apologize, you retrace the other person's path — you swim, alone, the exact route they swam in their anger, so that your wake lies down inside the ghost of theirs. It is understood to be very hard to do with a straight face, which is the point.

The whole city, meanwhile, is their instrument, and they have been tuning it for longer than they have words. The hollow chambers that bellow with the gray tide are not accidents; each one's mouth has been opened, narrowed, curled by centuries of careful cutting, and there are rootwrights — the listeners — whose whole office is to sit inside the great resonating chambers with their chests pressed to the walls, hearing whether the city is in tune. They have names for the tide's voices the way ground-dwellers have names for winds: the Long Breath; Grindstone; Old Sister, who means rain in the upper water; the Hollow Jaw; and the Thin One, the new high note that means a storm is three days out and the shears should be checked. A new voice appearing in the city is an event. The listeners go out into the streets like doctors to a bedside, and the whole population waits, quiet, listening to a noise.

Every doorway is somebody's work, and most doors are somebody. This is the part of rootwright life that takes the longest to explain, and the shortest to understand once you have knocked on one. At the end of a long life — and their lives are long, two centuries and more, slowing all the way down — a rootwright may choose engraftment: to be grafted, body and all, into the living ribweed, and become architecture. An arch. A bench. A door. The grafted do not die, exactly, and they do not stay, exactly. They answer in creaks. A household knows its own door's voice as it knows a grandmother's cough, and questions are put to these elders at midwinter and answered by spring, in wood. When the answers stop, the family performs the last pruning, with shears that have been in the line for longer than the line, and then the passage they became is allowed — for the first time in centuries — to begin closing. Nobody lets it finish. A door that was a person is never let close all the way.

Their dead who are not grafted are given the last swim. The dying are carried, slowly, through whatever streets they loved best, so that the water holds their shape — so the city itself can carry them for one more minute after the heart slows to the pace of the plant. Then the family swims the body to the rim, to the edge where the ribweed thins, and releases it upward, toward the shallows of the world, into whatever is on the other side of everything. And there, always, at a respectful distance, wait the ribbon-things.

They are the one fact of Tangleholm the rootwrights do not pretend to understand, and the one they refuse to simplify. They call them — the translation is not exact — *the ones who listen from outside*. Ten people long, eyeless, dignified, never entering, never quite leaving. They do not come for the living. They come, in ones and twos, when someone is released upward, and they hang at the boundary while the body rises, and then they resume their slow circuit as if nothing had occurred. The water at the rim stays scribbled with their wakes for hours, longer than any wake should last, and the rootwrights say those marks are not wakes at all. They say they are signatures on something. They do not claim to know on what.

Down on the plain, meanwhile, there are other folk — the quiet ones, the ones who live in the still dark where the gray tide never reaches and the shrimp-tick fades to nothing. Traders come up sometimes, moving fast and speaking in whispers, and the rootwrights find them unnerving: people whose wakes say nothing, people who do not hum. The plain folk, for their part, find the city unbearable — a place that screams forever and calls the screaming weather. Both are right. Each side is certain the other is a phase the world is going through.

Law in Tangleholm is a garden. Grievances are painted into the scroll-plant in stained algae, and once a cold season — when the whole city slows anyway, and stillness is not a discipline but a temperature — the law-reader opens the scroll and reads the disputes aloud to whoever has drifted in. By the time a grievance is read, the wakes around it are long folded, the parties are often asleep, and the quarrel, deprived of warm water to live in, usually dies mid-sentence in front of everyone. This is considered a mercy, and it is the entire justice system. What force the law has comes from the water itself: you cannot lie about where you have been, because where you have been is still hanging in the street behind you, legible for a minute to any reader good enough to chain a trail through the churn. So the only privacy in Tangleholm is the crowd. If you wish to be unread, you swim at market-bell, among ten thousand interleaving wakes, and become rain in a storm of rain.

And the ribweed itself? The rootwrights' one unhealed question is whether the whole city — every wall, every arch, every grafted grandmother — is a single being, or a congregation of many grafted into agreement. Two schools have argued it for four thousand years. The argument is maintained the way everything here is maintained: patiently, and by successive generations, and nobody wants it settled. It is considered load-bearing.

Because that is the deepest thing about them, under all the rest: they do not experience tending as labor. The shears are their sacrament; the prunings are the harvest; the trimming of the walls is the feeding of the city and the feeding of the city is the tending and there is no seam anywhere in the loop. When a rootwright takes up her grandmother's shears at the start of a shift — pressing her chest to the wall first, to hear which voice the tide has today, and what the door has to say — she is not going to work. There is no word in their language that separates the garden from the gardener from the gardening.

Above them the light comes down like weather. Above that, the shallows of the world, where the water grows thin and everything falls upward into the other side of everything. It would be very interesting. There is pruning to do.
