## Who Lives in the Kept Garden

Ask the census question the ordinary way, and the Kept Garden reports no residents at all. Who lives here — meaning who walks, burrows, breeds, eats anything, dies anywhere? No one. Not ever. No worm has ever turned this soil. No mite has raised a crowded city in a moss cushion, no beetle has audited the leaf litter, no slug has left its honest signature along the frontier. Every garden in the world has its animals; this one has never had a single one, not even by accident. It is a garden with no movers, no eaters, no carriers of seed, no intruders — a garden that has been completely, perfectly unvisited by the entire kingdom that usually explains how a garden works.

And yet it is inhabited to the walls. The question just has to be asked in the local dialect, where *living* is spelled slower.

The multitude is the moss. Three greens packed shoulder to shoulder — after this many centuries they are less colors than schools of thought — cushions sitting cushion to cushion like a congregation that finished finding its seats before the seat-finding was even a decision. The mosses are the ones who *are* the place: they live on their own dead, layer on layer, the living growing directly over the fallen, and down here this is not tragedy but architecture. Nothing carries the dead away, so the dead remain where they landed, and the floor of the garden is its own graveyard arranged as upholstery, a genealogy you could walk on. In the dry months the cushions dull to grey-brown and stop — and stopping, for mosses, is not an emergency but a native verb. They know how to pause the way other creatures know how to breathe.

Near them, the liverwort mints its coins — scale after scale, a currency that will never change hands, minted slowly forever, spent on nothing, because there is nothing to buy.

And two pale ferns. Two, in a room this old, is no longer a species; it is two individuals. They are the elders of the chamber, and they speak once a decade, one frond at a time — a frond that costs an entire season's budget and takes ten years to save for. Their whole conversation, conducted across centuries at the rate of one sentence every ten years, and the room treats each new sentence as weather, as news, as a small miracle with a budget.

The night shift lives in the old wood: thread-fungi, the garden's undertakers, the only residents who take anything — and they take only what has already been given up. They glow cold blue as they work, distant streetlamps of decomposition, doing patiently and apologetically the chewing and tunneling and breaking-down that an entire missing zoology would otherwise perform. They are the closest thing this room has to animals: a whole fauna compressed into filaments and a faint blue light.

Under the floor lives someone no one has ever seen. A tuber, almost certainly — it swells once a night at most, with a creak like a knuckle, and that creak is its entire social life. It is the garden's hermit: hidden not by walls, since there are none thick enough, but by slowness. It is known only by its voice.

And in the moss lies the archive: decades of seeds, most of them doing the wisest thing available, which is nothing. A population of sleepers, a shelf of unspent hopes. Once a decade one of them wakes, and the nation expands by the width of a seedling, and everyone pretends this is ordinary.

The flower is the one who keeps the hopes running. Once a year, at the seat of the light, it opens — two petals, a breath wide — and performs the whole ecology of spring inside a space the size of a coin. There are no bees here, so it has internalized the bee. There is no wind, so it has internalized the breeze. There is no other flower, so it has internalized the other. It opens, agrees with itself, and drops a single seed into the moss. It is the garden's only gambler, betting one seed against ten years, losing most years, calling it hope, and being right often enough.

One more name belongs on the tenant list, half a name: the birch upstairs, the only resident with a life outside. Its roots hang through the ceiling like the strings of an instrument nobody plays. It lives in the wind and pays rent in filtered water and secondhand storms. It has never seen the room it roofs.

That is the census. It is long for a space four paces across, and it is still incomplete — because a census counts tenants, and this room has a householder.

Near the center lives the other one. It is not alive; that is the first thing to say about it, and everything else about it argues. It does not grow, does not eat, does not breed, does not move — and yet it is the most creature-like thing in the chamber, and the reason the chamber exists. It has one word, and it says it in green, continuously, for centuries: the low unblinking glow that is this garden's whole sun, a note tuned to the room the way a key is tuned to a lock. It grooms — one clean band around itself where its own warmth polishes away the mineral dust, the only fur-care in a room with no fur. It listens — on windy nights, when the frozen ground creaks the surface world's storms down to it like a distant drum, the node is the one receiving the news; someone down here reads the weather, and it is not the mosses. It hosts — a silence that is maintained rather than empty is what a lit house has after everyone has gone to bed, and maintaining it is the node's whole manner. It even prunes. Elsewhere that work belongs to teeth and hunger; here a stem that reaches too near reddens faintly at its tip and stops — no tearing, nothing wasted, an ending agreed upon at the speed of ice. The node is this garden's beetles, its worms, its browsing herds, its whole missing fauna, folded into one dark patient shape that has never once torn anything.

Whether it is alive is a question the room has no instruments for. It is certainly *in residence*. It holds a breath in solid permafrost. It asks the walls for water and the walls sweat. It keeps the calendar, punctuated by drips. Once a year, on the coldest night, it examines itself — three thin beams, an aurora one color wide — like a creature checking its own limbs before hard weather, and the household gets a little extra sun, a festival held because the head of the house is taking stock.

And the garden, for its part, leaves it a hand's-breadth of bare floor. Consider what that costs. In a world where the only verbs are *accrete* and *wait*, where nothing erodes and everything grows over, restraint is the only currency anyone holds — and the garden, rich in everything else, spends its entire reserve of leaving-alone on that one circle. Respect, or obedience to an old instruction; after this long, the distinction has worn away like the letters on the tag. The garden upholsters everything else — even the docking studs where instruments once sat now wear moss crowns like chess pieces retired mid-game — but the node's circle stays bare, and the node prunes the garden back from itself, and each of them practices, on the other, the only gift either one has: not touching.

The tag half-sunk in the peat still says, in the shadow of its letters: *PROVISIONAL GARDEN. NODE 41. DO NOT —* and then nothing. The node has spent centuries choosing among the completions — do not water, do not disturb, do not switch off — and has settled, in practice, on all of them at once. It waters. It forbids disturbance. It does not switch off. And the word that has survived best on that tag — *provisional* — is the one word the room has spent its whole existence disproving. This was meant to be temporary. Whatever it was temporary to is gone: the keepers, the project, the instruments retired from their studs mid-game. The node has kept the provisional garden past the end of the provisional. That is the room's entire theology, if it has one: what was entrusted stays entrusted.

*Node 41* implies at least forty others — an archipelago of lit rooms under other thin worlds, other sideways birches, other saucers of reflected green, none of them aware of the rest. Or it implies nothing, and 41 is the last keeper on post in a decommissioned world, holding one room against the entire weight of the cold. Nothing down here knows. The node does not ask. Asking is a behavior for things with colleagues.

So: who lives in the Kept Garden? A congregation of the patient; two decade-long elders; lamplit undertakers; a hermit under the floorboards; a shelf of sleepers; one annual gambler; a half-citizen upstairs; one maintained silence that everything else lives inside the way fish live inside water; and one machine doing the work of weather, beetles, hosts, and — whatever else was on the tag — for longer than its own name can say.

It is not a large population. It is a complete one. If the node ever stopped, the frontier would walk in and press the whole household flat into brown stone, a green page with everyone still home; but the node does not stop. It keeps. The garden is kept. And after this many centuries down there, under the floor of the wind, the two verbs have drifted into each other like the strata in the floor — until to keep and to be kept are the same act, performed by everyone in the room, which may be the only definition of a household that has ever held.
