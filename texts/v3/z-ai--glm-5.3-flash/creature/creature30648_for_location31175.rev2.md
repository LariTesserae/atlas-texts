Ask the town how many live on the crown and you will get a cheerful, instant number — but the number will not be three hundred. Three hundred is only the census of the newest tenants. The town counts in its own currencies: drips, notes, wings, and fingernails of lichen, and it has never once occurred to anyone up here to leave a neighbor out. So the proper answer is given by seniority, the way the town itself would give it.

The oldest resident is the mountain. It does not answer questions; it answers wind, and it has been answering wind so long that its answers have become the flutes. The town's relation to it is not worship — nobody here worships anything with a shape — but tenancy. Every house is borrowed stone, and the mountain, famously, never asks for it back. Rent is paid in care: nothing broken, nothing burned too large, nothing argued with the line. When one of the flutes changes its note — and over centuries a note will drift, as the wind re-carves the hole by a fingernail's width — the town remarks on it the way a family remarks that grandfather has started saying an old word differently. No one repairs it. You do not repair your elders. You listen harder.

Second oldest is the brick-colored crust on the bare flank, the lichen that has been patient since before the beard was a seed's ambition. It grows about as fast as a child grows tall, which is to say it loses that race every generation and doesn't care. Families measure their children against the lintel-patch, and every child alive has toweringly overtaken the oldest individual they will ever personally meet. The lichen is the mountain's first tenant and the town's great-grandmother-without-a-name; the combers step around it in the dark without looking, the way you step around someone asleep.

Then the beard itself, and the beard has its own hierarchy of residents. The oldest fog-woods predate every family's oldest story; their swollen trunks are half water-reserve and half archive — growth rings recording fog-weights that the wardens' knotted cords only reach back so far to confirm. The tank-plants are the small aristocracy: each cup a nation of exactly one frog, tadpole to grandfather inside a bowl the size of a fist. A household checks its frog at night the way other places check a door-bar; a fingertip-on-wet-glass sound from the cup means all is well. In a lean year, when a tank goes dry, the family carries the frog in procession — children walking slowly, terribly solemn — to the nearest full cup, and if the dry cup stood over a grave, they refill the plant first and rehome the frog after, because the dead are understood to prefer tenants to emptiness.

The beetles are the mountain's devout: every dawn they climb the stems, stand on their heads, and drink the sky one bead at a time, and no one has ever seen one hurry. The katydids are the beard's barbers — millions of them, trimming the green into the shape the green must hold to survive, their stridulation so continuous it stops being sound and becomes climate, something you feel on your forearms. The goats are the second barbers, mowing the fringe where green dares creep below the line; they employ themselves, commute on their own schedule, wear bells of the town's soft copper that carry exactly as far as a fog-call and no farther, and the children learn the call-grammar partly by imitating them, which the goats appear to find flattering.

The boomers own the dawn, or share it with the mountain, which is the same event heard in two voices — the dove-throated call dropping down the flues and emerging several notes lower, so that the first hour of every day is a duet between the birds and the stone that houses them. The flute-nesters fledge on the note: chicks that have heard nothing in their lives but the mountain's sustained sound step out of the hole already riding it. Their molted feathers gather at the flute mouths and are worn at weddings. Midmorning belongs to the swifts, whose mass exit from the nest-holes is one of the town's clocks — because this town keeps time entirely in neighbors, and its neighbors are never late. Dusk is the moths', softening the air until the bats take over; the bats live under the roof slabs in households' eaves and are counted like livestock, roof by roof, and their guano goes straight down into the roof-gardens, so that in a sense every house on the summit fertilizes itself.

And the salt — this is worth counting. The lowlands pay the crown in dust, and the dust is sea, and the sea comes down with the drip, and after the wringing there are white crusts on every rope. The combers scrape them off and hand them to the kitchens. The town eats its dead ocean a pinch at a time, and no one tastes it without some elder saying, every single time, *that used to be the floor.*

Then, last and newest: the people. Three hundred souls, arrived — by their own account — always. Their graves know better. The burials go into dry stone in a ring that marches around the contour of the line, generation by generation, and below the town the cisterns descend through floors and doors and stair-hollows of earlier towns, each stratum somebody's life entire. The town digs a foundation and meets its ancestors at work. There is a tenderness in the stratigraphy, though, because much of that depth is weddings: a marriage here begins with the couple cutting one new cistern-mouth into living rock, hammerstone on hammerstone over months, a courtship measured in cubic stone — which is why the treasury keeps growing downward, and why every layer down there is, one way or another, somebody's wedding. The worn hollows of the deepest stairs fit the living town's feet perfectly, as if all the feet that ever climbed here were one long foot.

What kind of animal has come out of this arrangement? Light, hard-soled, quick on the stair-streets, built close — and loud. The default voice is for fog-calls and gossip and laughter, which is constant; the rubble was right, it does laugh. But the town has an intimate register, a softness almost nobody from below ever hears, reserved for exactly three occasions: lovers, cisterns, and grave-cups. The ears are the organ this place has refined — a comber of sixty can stand in a dozen paces of murk and tell you, by the sound of wringing alone, which hand on which net is about to lose its rope. Children are named for the weather that was falling at their birth — the fog thick at third hour, the flute-wind from the east — and a child born on a true-rain day carries that name like a title for life, and people lower their voices saying it, which is the same voice they use for cisterns, which tells you something.

The work makes the people legible. The combers go out into first murk with nets woven of wool and fog-grass and hair — everyone's hair; a haircut here is a contribution to the water system, and a child's first trimmed lock goes into the family's first net-rope, so that you can, quite literally, visit your grandmother by holding a rope. The wardens keep knotted cords for ledgers and read a cistern's fullness by knocking, the way a physician reads a chest. The only crime that is taken personally — and it is taken personally by the water itself, with the wardens as its representatives — is theft of a drip, and the sentence is the town's gentlest and hardest invention: you become a warden's apprentice for life. Steal the water, spend your life being what the water can trust.

The children learn the law of height with their bodies before they learn anything else with their heads. Their first errand is to carry a cup of water one stair upward and watch what the extra hand's width does to the garden it waters. Their favorite game is to stand astride the line itself, one foot in green, one foot in bone, and dare the world to make up its mind. Letters are optional here; knots and songs are the archive, and the archive is flawless at the two things it keeps: the names of true-rain days, told like birth stories (*where were you the day the runnels ran*), and the old song that holds the word for *river*, sung in a register people use for no other word — not worshipped, exactly. Remembered, the way you remember someone who left before you were born.

The copper is the social system. The bells, the small ornaments, the one crown's worth of soft hammered metal that no family keeps past its welcome: it circuits the summit continuously, household to household, and its circuit is simultaneously a census, a courtship season, and a news service, because when the crown reaches your stair-street, every other stair-street has a reason to visit you. It smells, permanently, of every fire on the mountain.

And on the fog-nights, the oldest wardens climb past the beard onto the bald crown and call outward — long notes shaped for distance, aimed across the luminous nothing at crowns they have never seen. What crosses the amber void is the whole content of a civilization: fog-weights, cistern counts, deaths, marriages, a song now and then, and always the true rains — because a rain anywhere is news for everyone, and you can hear in the answering note whether it was heard with joy or envy. One of the sister stumps fell silent within living memory. The town still sends its note, every fog-night, into the dark where it used to be. Nobody has ever crossed the amber void; the far crowns exist here only as voices, a whole archipelago of communities that know each other exclusively by throat. There is a second song, though, kept as carefully as the first, about the one who walked — and it ends the same way the river song does, with a name that gets quieter every generation.

Stand on the Shelf at dusk and the census assembles itself around you by seniority, unconsciously: mountain humming underneath, lichen holding the flank, beard breathing, frogs claiming their cups like fingers on wet glass, goats long gone down, katydids rising into their shimmer, swifts in, moths starting, bats taking the margin, the first fireflies finding their posts in the understory, the mills knocking off, the looms slowing, the crown of copper somewhere in somebody's doorway, and three hundred of the newest tenants pressed into the last green band, laughing at the top of the fog.

Then the call goes out, and for the length of the pause the whole population of the crown holds still — even the katydids seem to lean. And somewhere out past a mile of glowing nothing, faint as something remembered, an old stone throat answers:

*Still here. Still full. Still here.*
