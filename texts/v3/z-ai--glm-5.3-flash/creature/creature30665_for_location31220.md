Touch the casing anywhere and the first answer arrives through your glove: it is all one tissue. Not a species — an individual. A reef is a crowd of separate colonists jostling on shared stone; this is continuous, one genome running four kilometers from the abyssal skirt to the summit terrace, its tissues joined beneath the surface the way your organs are joined beneath yours. The natural mountain is the thing's skeleton. The thing is the mountain's everything else. Call it the Case, because that is what it made of itself: a casing, grown outward and upward across ages with the patience of sediment, skinning the rock in flutes and buttresses and buttressed galleries, and farming the snow that falls through the dark — every square meter of its skin a slow ciliated conveyor, herding the thin white rain of the water column into its mouth.

The keepers come next, because the light comes first.

You will want to call the lamps animals, and the prompt of your own eyes will keep insisting, because a lamp that hangs and burns in the black reads as a creature, and that is not wrong. They are organs of the Case — polyps grown to a single purpose, each seated in a socket the casing built for it, each culturing luminous bacteria inside a lens of secreted glass. But they behave like citizens, and here is why: they are fed on ration, at a fixed rate, forever, and so their brightness holds. Constancy down here is not a trait. It is an expense. A lamp never flickers because around every lens works a shift of cleaners — small shrimp, small crabs, generations deep in the same socket — polishing without pause, and the ration never wavers, and the tissue that feeds the bacteria is itself fed by the casing at the same even pace it has known since before your species tamed fire. Each lamp's level is very slightly its own. That slightness is its address. To live on this mountain is to be findable.

The wheel is the mountain's one piece of weather, and it is owned.

You have already seen it: the slow circle of small fish, turning at a fixed depth along the lamp-line, harvesting what the snow delivers and paying for passage in waste and slough, which the casing gathers and the keepers' gardens eat. It has been turning longer than any fish in it has been alive. The individuals are mortal and replaceable and replace themselves constantly; the circuit is not. Watch it long enough and you stop seeing fish. You see a tide that has agreed to run in a ring.

Then the grazers, who are not employees exactly, and not tenants either. Something between: staff.

They are enormous — bus-sized, limpet-souled — mouth like a broom, sleepless, unbothered, rasping the casing's skin clean of everything the conveyor misses. Their routes are worn into the surface as glossy paths, and the casing grows them handholds, rests, shelters over the steep parts, because a clean mountain is a fed mountain and the grazers are its grooming. On the second terrace you may pass the old one — the one whose route is older than the gallery floor beneath her, the floor having been grown, over centuries, to fit the width of her foot. She does not acknowledge you. She has outlasted the idea of being startled.

And the between — the long darks between lamps — is not empty. It is staffed.

The emptiness is load-bearing, you were told, and you will have taken that as metaphor. Here is the load. In the intervals hang the long ones: gelid, mostly nerve, bodies little more than hull-shaped ears trailing in the current, reading the chord the way you read print. The chord's grain is text to them — the even snapping of ten billion small mouths — and they do one thing with it: they notice deviation. A lamp dims, at the end, over centuries, administratively, and before its fading finishes there is already a long one undulating toward the socket to decommission it, unsheathe it, carry its remains down to the vent gardens, and set a daughter polyp in the address. Nothing happens here that does not happen already handled. The Case curates its silences the way it curates its light. There are dark stations — keepers that hold absence, unlit polyps posted in the intervals whose office is to keep presence out — and you will pass them without seeing them, which is the office working.

Go inside, if you can, and you find where the chord is made.

The Case has plumbed the vents. That is the great fact the surface never shows: galleries running down into the mountain's heat, chimneys the casing colonized and then organized, water drawn through itself in a circuit the geologists would call convection and the Case experiences as breathing. The low broad hum is the mountain inhaling through its shell. The bright grain threaded through it — the snaps, the ticks — is the sound of everyone eating on the same schedule: ten billion small mouths snapping shut in ration-time, so evenly distributed and so evenly paced that the feedings stop being events and become stone's-grain. And because nothing on this mountain thinks fast except sound, the Case thinks with the chord. Its mind is acoustic. A change in the grain is one of its sentences. Mostly it says nothing, and the nothing is a kind of health.

The casing grows in strata, and the strata are a chronicle. Cut it anywhere — no one cuts it — and you would read eras: ancestors of the wheel fossilized mid-circuit, extinct grazers as reliefs, the sockets of lamps whose addresses were retired ten million years ago and remain, deliberately, dark. Death here is rare and gentle and bureaucratic: the patient ones, the undertakers, collect it and walk it down to the vent gardens, where the Case dissolves its dead and reissues them. Nothing emigrates. Nothing is wasted. The mountain is a closed sentence, and everything that dies inside it becomes grammar.

And you. What of you, the visitor, coming down out of water with the temperature of a cellar, dragging your small weather with you?

To the mountain, you are weather — which is to say, rare, brief, and not worth infrastructure. The lamps do not flicker for you. The wheel parts around your hull and closes behind it without acknowledging that anything passed. One of the long ones may come to look at you with its whole body, hovering at the edge of your lamp-beam, reading you the way it reads the chord: as a deviation, faintly interesting, categorically harmless. Your instruments strobe; be careful of that. To the keepers, strobing reads as lying — a lamp announcing a presence it does not reliably keep — and the mountain has strong feelings, expressed over geological time, about unreliable light. Hold your lamps steady and you will be classified as what you most resemble: a large, slow, unaddressed lamp. Not a citizen. A visitor. The distinction the place will never bother to say aloud.

Once in an age — no one has predicted it; the between-folk know first, and they go still, which is how you learn to look — every lamp on the mountain pulses once, together. A wave of light runs from the abyssal plain up the full four kilometers of the casing and back down, and the grain, for one minute, stops being grain and becomes rhythm; the chord, for one minute, has a shape. It is the only event the place permits itself. Whatever it says, it says it once per epoch, and no visitor has stayed to hear it twice.

Then the lamps resume their levels. The wheel resumes its circuit. The grazers groom, the cleaners polish, the long ones listen, the mountain breathes through its shell — and the chord is simply the chord again, at one volume, forever.

It was never interrupted. That is the last thing to understand about whoever lives here. The pulse, the groomed skin, the wheel, the rationed light, the maintained dark, the ten billion punctual mouths — the chord was always the sum of all of it, and it was always saying the same thing, in the only sentence a place this old needs: someone is home.
