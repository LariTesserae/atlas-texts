Everything that lives in the weald is made of the same three things as the weald itself: thread, water, and voice. Nothing here is an exception to the landscape. The inhabitants are the landscape, gathered into pieces small enough to move.

In a country that is mostly air, a body is mostly boundary — a way of holding one volume of darkness apart from another volume of darkness. What fills the volume is voice. So the first condition of tenancy is not *eat or be eaten* but *sound or be lost*, and every creature here has solved that problem in its own way. Some by being louder. Some by being perfectly silent. Some by becoming, as exactly as flesh can manage, one more note of the world.

**The tenders**

A tender is a human animal shaped by three pressures: cold, damp, and listening. The eyes are wide-pupiled and slow in bright light, which they rarely meet. The head has a habit of tilting, offering one ear or the other to a distance. The hands are the giveaway in any case — damp-palmed, quick at the fingertips, callused in strange places, and given to reading. A tender who has never seen your face will know your grip on a line. Your tension is your name.

The feet are hands that walk. Children go bare and fur-wrapped by turns, and their toes splay early and grip early, and a tender who has moved to the undermist countries and back still walks a line the way other people swim — without having to think about it.

Here is a small strange thing, once you know to look: in a world where nothing softens, nothing rounds, nothing is permitted to age, the only weathering the weald produces is on its people. The wood stays young forever or dies true. The tenders wrinkle. Old age is the weald's only ruin, and it spends it all on its grandmothers, which may be why the grandmothers hold the licenses.

They drink dew and they eat weather. The staple is fog-glass — sifter flesh cut thin and dried in the waist's clear air until it is a translucent wafer that crumbles into hot water and becomes a broth tasting of almost nothing, which is to say of fog, which is to say of staying alive. Cable-hearts, the pale soft tips of new growth, are the only green vegetable the world offers. Line-honey, scraped from the gut of a hunted grazer, is festival food. And in the wet week after a true rain, the stem-hollow pools fill with rain-worms that have waited years inside drying eggs for exactly this, and the children are sent out to harvest a season of feasting in three days of mud.

The day begins with the door, which has grown shut overnight, as it does, and is cut open along the same scar it opens along every morning of its life. A door is a calendar. Count the ridges of cuts and you have the age of the house. Then the dew-combs, drawn squeaking through the fur before the Bloom brightens. Then tuning — because the tide retunes the entire continent twice a day, wet lines singing sharp through the dark hours, dry lines sagging flat by day, and a keeper walks the family span with twist-stick and resin-pot after every change of water, bringing the home chord back to its voicing. The weald plays itself whether anyone wants it played or not. Keeping a house means keeping up your end of the chord.

Evening is for the lamps: glow-moss in gourd-water, fed, thinned, sung to. The singing is not ceremony; it is diagnosis. You sing at a lamp to hear where it answers, and a dull lamp answers dull, and you know what the night's work is. A favored lamp is set inside an old grazer-shroud to throw its light through silk, the way other worlds get moonlight.

And somewhere in the house there is a clay stove, and in the stove a coal, and the coal has been alive longer than the family, handed down warm through hands that were themselves once small. A grandmother holds the license. Children are brought before the stove and told the first law, which has no story attached because it is older than its stories: nothing burns, ever. The world is one fuse and everyone lives on it.

A tender's name is a word, but a tender's address is a chord. Meeting on a far path, two strangers pluck their home voicings at each other, and each answers whether they know the tuning. The grandmother keeps the address-cords — knot-records of which lines descend from which, the only maps that stay put. A marriage is a graft: two main lines cut and joined with resin and singing, followed by the long work of retuning two chords into one key, which is why the saying goes that the first year of a marriage is mostly tuning.

The dead are wrapped around a young line and grafted in, and the swelling that rises over them is polished by touching hands. A grave flattens its string, permanently, slightly. An old family's chord is full of these small flattenings, and no one ever corrects them, because to tune the dead away would be a kind of second death. A house's chord is therefore also its list of graves. And when a message comes up the family line — tick by tick under the sill, under the floor, under your bare feet — it passes through everyone the house has ever lost on its way to the living. Tenders touch the nearest swelling when the news goes by. It is not ritual. It is including them.

**The sifters**

Out in the fog-channels hang the sifters: the mist, having agreed for a while to have a shape. A sifter is a veil of gelid tissue, mouth a wide ring set facing the weather, everything about it translucent and unhurried. It feeds by standing still. Watch a fog-front reach a sifter's lip and you can see the mist slow as it crosses, thicken, and join the animal — drinking weather, made visible.

A sifter can hang a year in one volume of air without touching a strand. It has no eyes, no limbs, no voice, and no enemy it can outpace. At ebb, in the shallow channels, the thinning fog can leave one grounded — heavy, sagging down onto the lines in folds, like stranded sailcloth. Then comes the harvest. What is cut and hauled to the waist becomes fog-glass. What is eaten fresh tastes first of nothing and then, faintly, of green. *Eating weather*, the tenders say, and are not joking.

Once a season the elders bud: motes no bigger than a fist, drifting off into the dark like a snowfall of jelly. A family stocks its fog the way another people might stock a pond — motes carried up in gourds of tide-water and turned loose in a good channel, and ten years on, that channel is a sifted channel. Behind an old sifter the mist runs so thin that for a hundred meters you can *see*, actually see, the far strands. Sifted country is the closest the weald comes to openness, and travelers detour for it.

**The grazers**

Walk a line in the cold hours and touch the knots as you pass. This is not superstition; it is the oldest test in the weald. Cold means wood. Warm means meat.

A strand-grazer at rest is indistinguishable from a graft-knuckle, and that is its whole life's trick. It hatches finger-length at a graft and eats its way outward along the cable for decades — an old grazer is as long as a person and as thick as a thigh, and it is as old as a grandmother, and it has eaten thirty years of road. You hear one before you ever see it: a note going flat, a local sag in the great chord, the sound of the world being swallowed a mouthful at a time.

The hunters go up the line toward the damaged note, hooks in their belts, palms open. The grazer hears them coming — everything here hears everything — and goes still, and becomes a knuckle. So the last stretch of the hunt is conducted by touch, knot by knot, in the cold, until a palm finds the one that has a heartbeat. What comes out of a big grazer's gut is line-honey, dark and sweet and faintly of resin — thirty years of road gone down sweet — and a kill is the one event that gathers houses, the knock sent ahead along the wet lines so that neighbors two days away can arrive in time to eat.

The wound is mended before the hunters leave — resin, a bast splint — because a line is a road and a string, and no one leaves a string open. The mend buzzes slightly, forever. Every old family's main line is written over with these small buzzes: a ledger of hungers, generations deep.

A grazer that is never caught grows full at last and climbs — past the paths, up to the high crossings — and spins itself a silk shroud and hangs. The law is that no one cuts a shroud. In time it splits, and what comes out is pale and winged and does not eat, and does not stay. It climbs the rising tide and keeps climbing, and no one has ever watched one come down. Consider what that means in a world where everything that dies, dies true and falls: the grazer is the only thing in the weald that never falls. It goes up instead. What happens at the top is the Bloom's business.

The empty shells hang on. A high avenue strung with generations of them — grey with age but still true, each one combing its own small dew from the passing fog — is a shroud-row: landmark, larder of clean water, and in the bright hours, when the fur silvers, a fleet of lanterns nobody lit.

**The gliders**

The gliders ride the tide the way sea-things ride a current, and they do not flap. They trim — a tension here, a tension there, riding the rising mist on wings wide as sails. The wings are furred, a living film of silencing down that the animal grooms hourly, and that is why they are silent. In a world where to be loud is to be present, the glider is the one loud absence. You do not hear one pass. You hear the chord continue on both sides of a hole where sound should be, and the hole moves. People mark a glider the way other worlds mark an eclipse.

They hunt the dark hours, dropping out of the swelling mist onto sifters, gripping a rim, tearing free, gone upward again. A sifter's only defense is to let its shape go — to spill through the strand-gaps in sheets and gather itself downstream — and so the hunt is grip against collapse, and the sifter sometimes wins by refusing to be a thing. A year of many gliders is a thin year: where the sifters are torn, the fronts arrive less thinned, but fewer of them arrive at all, and the dew-harvest knows it. Tenders read the open voids for moving shadows the way farmers elsewhere read clouds.

You will never hear a glider die. The dead are found hanging from lines like flags, and the membrane makes window-skin, drum-heads, rain-aprons. Even its death is only a longer absence than most.

**The small war**

The click-hunters are fist-sized, quick, and almost all ears, and they announce themselves constantly — because for them too, silence is the sound of hunting, and a click-hunter that has gone quiet means everyone run. They work the hammock-meadows and the stem-hollows by echo, ticking and listening. They are curious past the point of sense about anything large and strange, which is to say, about you. A click-hunter will pace a traveler down a whole avenue for hours, ticking the question *what are you* at intervals, and some travelers tick back, and it is an acquaintance with no content in it that can last a lifetime. A child's first solo journey — the walk to the next span with a message — is traditionally escorted partway by a tick that loses interest at the edge of its own curiosity.

Their prey has answered the dark in two ways. Some things mute. Some things went the other way and learned the drone. There are small sap-drinking grubs whose entire defense is to add themselves to the local chord as one more thread — a creature becoming weather in order to survive it. But the trick must be held in tune, and pitch is water, and the water moves twice a day. The continent changes key at every tide, and every mimic changes with it or dies. They drink from wet lines to stay sharp. They sag with the dry hours. On frost nights, when the whole harp detunes at once, the lies must sag with everything else, or be heard.

And the mimics have accents. The regional voicings of the weald — the signature by which a home is known before it is seen — are partly made of vermin: a chord here sounds like itself partly because its hiding things have learned to sound like it. A mimic that travels dies of its accent. Every household knows its own lies — which threads in the home chord are animals — the way a ship knows its fouling: counted, tolerated, useful. A family's tame mimic is the watchdog. It holds its note through anything. When it goes quiet, something is moving in the avenue, and the household is already awake.

**The thieves**

The dew-thieves are small and warm and furred, and they have hands — that is the shock of one, if it ever sits up inside the reach of your lamp. Not claws. Hands, with a thumb, closing on a strand the way a child's do. Each carries its own thorn, and knows throats the way a herdsman knows udders: it knocks, listens to the swell answer, taps where the wall is thinnest, and drinks the bead in the silence before the creak. It steals in the gap between fullness and sound. Timing is its entire fortune.

Families count their throats the way shepherds count sheep, and lose some to thieves, as shepherds do. Most households keep one thief the way another world keeps a barn cat — under a name that is a title rather than an individual, passed down the generations: *the one who pays for his water*.

Because the thief pays. Its feet and fur are trailers of fur-seed, and its nightly roads — the same run, night after night, year after year — are the weald's future roads. In ten years a thief's path is a young line; in fifty, a path you can walk. A family that wants a road grown across a gap does not plant one. They leave a throat open on the far side and wait for the title-holder to come. *Pay the thief in water*, the saying goes, *and the thief pays you in road.*

**The deep**

Below the tide-line the lattice goes on down into a darkness that has never thought about light. The great anchor-trunks stand in it and sing deeper notes the farther down they go, notes that stop being sound somewhere around the level of your marrow. And on the trunks, far down where no tender husbandry has ever reached, there is glow-moss that no one cultured — growing in rings, band over band, recording a thousand years of tide-highs. Light nobody keeps, keeping itself. A traveler who is shown one comes back quiet. The whole teaching of the lamps — that light dies if you neglect it — does not hold down there, and a doctrine with one exception is a door left open.

The dead rain into the undermist forever. Nothing rots; nothing finishes; so beneath the flat milk surface lies everything the weald has ever shed — every blackfall strand of every century, every glider, every spent sifter, all of it still true, a country of the fallen with no floor anyone has plumbed. No splash is ever heard. The mist accepts without comment and keeps its surface like a held note.

And sometimes a light rises in it. Slow, formless, without pattern. It has been seen to pause directly beneath a watcher, and the whole surface glows upward faintly, like a breath held under a skin, and the drone above seems to lean toward it — and then it sinks, and the milk closes, and the chord goes on. Some say it is the weald's heart, going down to count. Some say it is what the dead become when nothing will let them rot. Some say it is the Bloom's root, feeling along the underside of the world. The law says only: you do not follow it, and you do not fish. The law does not say why. The oldest laws are like that. They are older than their reasons, which is why they hold.

**The eldest**

Count the inhabitants from the top down and you arrive, last and largest, at the first: the weald itself. Where two cables cross they graft. Consider what a continent of crossings therefore is — one continuous flesh, every intersection a healed seam, no part of it joined to any other by anything weaker than living wood. The tenders do not live on this thing or beside it. They live inside a single animal the size of a world, which walks across the real world at the pace of a tree, breathes in tides, drinks fronts of fog, sheds its dead, and keeps its graves in its own flesh.

Whether the animal includes its sky, no one has ever said out loud. But the grazers go up and do not come down, and the wild light keeps itself, and the tenders speak of the Bloom the way farmers speak of a moody animal — and a farmer's task, after all, was never to be right about the animal. Only to read its moods.

So: the chord is its voice and its pulse at once; the thirst is its appetite; the tide is its breath. The tenders are the part of it that answers back. It keeps them — water, road, lamp, grave — and they keep its voicing, and neither party would use the word *keep*. What the weald calls them, it says in the only language it has, which is tension. It says it continuously. They answer in kind.

And on a dark hour, standing on a path with the drone all around you, you will hear the crack above, and the long whistle falling, and the silence where the landing should be. Below your feet, the rain that never lands climbs through the fur. Above, the Bloom thickens toward one of its sweats. And among the threads, all through the dark, the small work goes on: a warm knot holding its breath, a lie holding its pitch, a thorn withdrawing from a throat, a shroud splitting quietly at some impossible height.

The continent holds its shape by thirst. Everything that lives on it holds its shape by listening.
