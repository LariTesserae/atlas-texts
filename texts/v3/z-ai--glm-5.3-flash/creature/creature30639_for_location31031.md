Ask the Kettle who lives there, and it answers in the oldest order it knows: by abundance. First come the millions.

The bats are the ones you would name, and they deserve the naming. Each one is small — a thumb's worth of animal wearing a folded cloak of wing, a warm knot with a heartbeat fast enough to hum. By day they hang like dead leaves that reconsidered falling, and touch is a rumor they pass among themselves in ripples: disturb one cluster and a wave runs through the pelt, a thousand small bodies adjusting their grip on a ceiling they never see. At dusk the whole surface tenses. That is the first thing the Kettle does each evening — its skin flinches — and then the exodus pours up the entrance shaft, smoke rising in reverse, out into the cold where the stars are. They run down the mountain's dark flank to the lowlands where rivers still run. They drink at a river you will never find. They hunt moths above the reeds by shouting into the dark and listening to what the dark hands back, and every meal is a voice-shaped echo, so that in a sense they eat sound all night. Then they eat the world too, and bring it home in their blood. The homing is the miracle: at dawn the funnel reverses, ten thousand bodies funneling down the shaft, and each one folds itself back into its own inch of ceiling — its own crease in a pelt of a million identical creases — called home by voice. In high summer the ceiling wears a second fur of pups, and the clock on the floor ticks a little faster.

The owl lives at the door and is the only resident with a commute. It arrives at last light, pale as the frost it perches over, and does not enter. Why would it? The Kettle is a larder, and the door of a larder is the best seat in the world. Its face is a dish that catches the column's chaos and focuses it into one audible river; it takes its toll from the smoke as it passes, one bat in twenty, sometimes fewer. Below its ledge the middens have stratified into a soft grey country of their own — thousands of pellets, thousands of skulls small as grains of rice, fur compressed to felt. It is a bone shingle marking the border between the cave's jurisdiction and the night's. On storm-nights when the column does not rise, the owl waits on its ledge, goes hungry without complaint, and watches the weather the way sailors do, because it too lives by the Kettle's clock — it may know the schedule better than anyone inside.

While the travelers are gone, the residents keep the house.

The crickets keep the walls. Shoulder to shoulder in their thousands, long-legged, the color of wet paper, antennae extended like drawn whiskers, they hold the vow of silence that the dryness imposes — every sound costs water, and nothing here can afford one. But they have found the loophole: sound that does not travel by air. They drum. A hind leg against stone, a tremor, and the message runs through the rock itself — small talk of humidity, of approach, of a good drop of wall-sweat two body-lengths up. Put your palm to the wall and you can feel it: the stone is speaking in a medium thicker than air, a choir performing only in the floor of hearing. The crickets eat everything — the night's droppings, the shed skins, their own dead — and their patience is total. When a bat falls, the floor receives it, and by the following dusk the floor has erased it.

The beetles keep the floor. They move in slow herds, thumbnail-sized, domed, shellacked dark, shells clicking glassily where they jostle. They roll the droppings into pellets, bury the pellets, and lay their eggs in the burying, so that the ground of the Kettle moves the way a gut moves: peristalsis. A herd crossing the open floor sounds like distant rain heard through a wall. Their shells are smoother than the stone they live on, polished by a hundred thousand generations of shoulder-to-shoulder traffic. And beneath them, down in the strata, the true citizens: mites, springtails, thread-thin larvae, uncountable by any honest method. Pinch a gram of that floor and you hold thousands of lives. They are the sediment's fish, the gut's plankton, doing the work no one will ever watch and nothing will ever need to reward.

The spiders keep the doorway, and their trick is the cave's own trick. The Kettle breathes outward all night — warm, ammoniac, musky, mineral-sweet — and that breath rolls out of the slot like the smell of a bakery over a hungry street. It draws in the first flying insects of evening, following their noses to what they believe is warmth and rot. The sheet-webs strung across the entrance throat are the hooks on that line. Understand what this means: the whole cave fishes with its breath, the spiders are simply where the hook meets the water, and the larvae of the inner dome are fishing the same way from the other end. In the dome the silk hangs downward in threads beaded with moisture, each anchored to a small cold star — a larva with a glowing snare, harvesting the cave's only rain, which is condensation, which is breath. Gnats rise to those lights believing they are the moon. The cave's only sky hangs from its own ceiling, and it is made of fishhooks.

Everything drinks at the same hour. In the coldest dark before dawn, when the rock's sweating is greatest, the crickets press their mouthparts to the wet walls, the beads on the silk lines fatten and slide, the mites rise toward the surface of the strata, and outside the owl shakes the frost from its feathers and drinks the droplets off them. Ten thousand animals exhaling, moisture climbing, cold rock returning it — the colony drinks its own breath, and nothing is wasted here because nothing could afford to be.

Deeper still, past the second chamber, the herds stop — all at once, in a straight line, as at a border written in a language every small thing can read. The rock there is blood-warm. Along the wall lie shed skins, translucent, some of them long — long enough that you begin measuring in arm-spans and then quietly stop. The floor of that last stretch is scoured clean, and the silence is different: not the dry-thrift quiet of the colony but an attentive one, the hush of a room where someone is listening. Even the ticking stops. What owns the skins drinks at the same hour as everyone else and eats almost never — a cricket taken without a sound, or once a season, far down in the warm dark, a bat that roosted too deep: a soft noise like a page turning, and then the attentive quiet resumes. It entered the Kettle long ago, small, through the same slot you would use, and grew past its own doorway. It cannot leave now. It is the only inhabitant with no outside at all, the one who has never once flown out over the frost, and it keeps its counsel the way hearts do, and everything smaller has agreed to let it.

And the who — there was only one who, ever. Long ago, people climbed here at night with wooden scrapers and fire, the only fire this place has ever hosted; its soot is still up in the dome, a second darkness laid over the first. They cut channels in the niter-blooming floor, straight and deliberate among all that organic wandering, and tallied their take in charcoal on the wall — hundreds of marks, a ledger of salt in a place with no other writing. Near where the last grey light reached, one of them pressed a hand flat to the stone. The moths found the handprint within a generation and have been drinking it ever since: the palm line, the heel of the thumb, licked of its sweat-salts by small grey wings, fainter every year. In a few hundred more years it will be a smudge, and then not even that. They came with wood and fire and patience and left with bags of salt, and the Kettle filed them under weather — the only inhabitants it digests unhurried, an heirloom in the strata rather than a meal.

Stand in the main chamber long enough and the census closes into a single creature. A skin of wings tensing at dusk. A gut of beetle herds, with plankton in its depths. Nerves of cricket-tremor running through the stone. A lung exhaling warm up the shaft; a set of false stars fishing the fall of its own breath; a customs house of webs at the mouth; an owl levying its toll at the door; a heart in the blood-warm deep, and ghosts in the ledger of the floor. Warm because it is full, full because it is warm. Once in a long while a single bat shrieks somewhere in a passage, and the sound rolls away down the throat of the dead river and takes ten full seconds to die — the closest the Kettle ever comes to raising its voice. The rest is conversion: the distant wet world, turned nightly into beetles and bone and heat, quietly, under one shared vow. And at the slot in the ledge, if you climb to it, the warmth breathes out against your face like the flank of a sleeping animal.

That is not a simile. That is the census result.
