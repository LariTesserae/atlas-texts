You will not be greeted, because you will not be found. But suppose you already know the way — the only way anyone arrives — and suppose you stand at last at the lip of the seam, clouds pooled below you like a second, softer country. The first inhabitant you meet will be standing there already, mopping rime off the mirror-dust with a swab longer than she is tall. She will not call out to you. She will look up, register you the way she registers a change in a drip, finish her stroke, and then decide what you are.

That is the temperament to hold onto. The people of Weirhold are not hardy in the way valley folk mean it — red-faced, bellowing, throwing themselves at weather. They are spare, deliberate, and slow in the specific way of things that cannot afford to be otherwise. They are powered, like their machines, by the willingness of water to become ice, and it shows. Nothing in a Weirholder is wasted at speed. They walk as if the ground were listening, because it is: a wrong step near a loaded curtain of ice is a civic event. Their bodies carry the town's history in them — broad-ribbed and thin-limbed, chests like bellows built for air that has given up, hands that are the signature organ: nails thickened, knuckles that have not been warm for a full day in years, skin split each winter and oiled back together with rendered worm-fat. Their faces are pale except for a faint amber freckling across the cheekbones, sun they have only ever met by reflection. A Weirhold handshake is an inspection. A Weirhold embrace is an event.

The first word that comes to mind looking at them is *monks*. Strike it. There is no piety here and almost no ceremony; there is accounting. Nobody owns land because there is none. What a household owns is water — a named stretch of the weeping wall, a thread of the mother weir's offspring, a screen, a valve. Families are known by their threads the way valley families are known by their fields, and children are named for the drip their family keeps, so that every introduction in Weirhold is also a small lesson in hydrology. To say "I am of the Slow Braid" is to say who you are, where your water comes from, and whom to blame if the flow shifts. Inheritance is not a house. It is a row of beads on the wet face, and the obligation to read them well.

Their economy is conducted in futures. Ice is the coin, so debt is measured in winters — *I owe you a cold season* is a sentence with a banker's precision and a grandmother's warmth — and wealth is security postponed, stacked in the deep gallery as graded blocks. A rich family is not one that has things; it is one that has already been warm in years that haven't happened yet. The frost-bank is the closest thing the town has to a temple, and they would deny even that, because temples imply mystery and the frostworks have none. What awe they permit themselves is spent elsewhere.

Because warmth is so scarce, it is the whole grammar of feeling. To love someone in Weirhold is a thermal verb. A family at night sleeps in a graded pile of pressed felt and bodies, arranged by size around the smallest member — warmth made to walk the wrong direction, inward, without a single moving part, the household operating as a small solid-state device of its own. There are three warm rooms in the entire country and everyone was born in one of them; the town's universal first memory is the same heat. When a child is coming, the community cedes its vent-hours the way other towns cede grain. A guest, if one ever comes — rarer than the kite — is given a heated stone and one hour in a vent-hall, and that gift is the truest sentence the town knows how to speak. Hospitality here is denominated in degrees.

They speak at the volume of the wall. This is not softness of character but etiquette of place: the drip-count is the ambient voice of the country, and to talk over it would be like shouting during someone else's heartbeat. Across the seam, people converse in knock-code — knuckles on stone, valve-handles opened and closed in syllables — so that from a distance a Weirhold afternoon sounds like a slow discussion among doors. Oaths are not sworn on anything. The phrase is *the wall will say it*, and it is not a figure of speech; the filament takes strain and temperature and flow everywhere at once, and a lie about where you stood or what you opened tends to surface by morning as frost-script on the Ledger Wall. The record exists only as long as it is true — and the people, raised under that physics, are honest the way their records are: evaporating-if-false.

The day is shaped for them before they wake. They rise in the dark and go first to the Ledger Wall, because literacy here is a sport against a deadline — the script sublimates away by afternoon, so the whole town's news is read once, aloud, and never again. A Weirhold reader moves her eyes faster than any valley scholar and retains everything, which the valley scholars find impossible and the Weirholders find unremarkable; when your archive evaporates daily, you build the archive in yourself. Then second dawn, the amber wash pouring down from ten million tilting flakes, and the work: valves, screens, swabs, pistons, the hum of the frostworks like a held breath. At true noon the coin of sun touches the bottom for a few minutes, and everyone feels it arrive on the skin before the eye confirms it. Weddings are scheduled by shadow and the vow must fit inside the coin — some years that makes for very short ceremonies, and the town finds this appropriate.

They eat the mountain's youngest meals. A tablespoon of seepworms fried on an exhaust-warmed stone is a feast, and everyone remembers their feasts; a mouthful of midges off the afternoon air is a joke children tell with their tongues out. Pressed yeast-skin becomes their clothing — felt the color of wet slate, cured in flat sheets, cut like the founders cut everything, with room to spare — so that the whole town dresses in the wall's outer skin and smells faintly of bread and iron. Their seasoning is a pinch of the wall's white bloom, which is not salt but holds the office of salt. Their drink is the sweet water itself, drunk at the weir where it costs nothing; Weirhold toasts with the only thing it has never had to pay for.

Children here grow up with two literacies — the script and the wall's spoken dialect of drips — and one exam: to stand in the galleries in the dark and name the drips that matter by sound alone. They keep the kite counts. They compete, seriously, to be first to hear the anchor collars take up the creep in the night. And sometimes — some seasons never — they stand frozen an arm's length from the whitekite, because when the kite drops into the slot, all work stops by a custom so old it has no enforcer. Six hundred people become as still as the stone they live in. It is the town's one extravagance. The kite is the only creature that visits them, and they repay it with the single thing they have in surplus: stillness. Some years the count is zero, and those years are spoken of the way valley towns speak of droughts.

The dead are not buried; they are *given*. A body is placed in a gap in the rock — a deliberate vacancy, like the gaps left in the walls — and the fault closes over it at its own pace, grief granted the same tolerance as masonry. The estate is returned as flow: the family places a block from its banked winters in the gap, and over the years it melts back into the wall the deceased kept. To visit your ancestors you go into the oldest galleries, where three generations of rooms have been crushed to hand-width slits by the creep, and you put your arm in up to the shoulder, and no further. Nobody mistakes this for melancholy. It is maintenance.

But the honest answer to *what lives in Weirhold* is larger than the town's two-legged tenants, and arrives in layers. First the films: black yeast-skins in velvet patches, rust-orange bacterial felts grazing the fresh iron the young stone keeps grinding open — the secret being that a newly uplifted mountain is a meal not yet eaten, and the fault renews it a few millimeters a year, setting the table millimeter by millimeter. Then the grazers: pale eyeless seepworms, ancient in the way of things that never needed eyes, tended by wardens whose entire husbandry is attention. Then the midges, weathering out of the warm afternoons like slow snow falling upward. Then the kite. Then, in its gallery, the founding machine — fed, aligned, oiled, and not questioned, in the manner of people tending an animal that still works and cannot say why — and its nerve, the filament, the oldest grown thing in the country, older than any name currently in use. Then the ice itself, a citizen with moods, ticking and groaning through the nights, calving once or twice a winter to the struck bell that empties the face. And finally, the people: the thinnest film of all, a film of order grazing the difference between liquid and solid.

Strangers' words for them accumulate and get struck through in turn. Monks — no. Pioneers — no; pioneers look outward, and the maps here end at the lip. One organism — not quite; they are too particular, too quarrelsome about flow-rights, too fond of their own drips. The truest description is the one they would give, if asked, with a shrug that costs them a degree of heat: *tenants*. The mountain rose, the water found the light, and they are what grew in the crack between — expecting, without bitterness, to be closed over someday the way the walls are closed, the way the script is closed. Nobody here mistakes permanence for a virtue. The virtue is being true while it lasts, and the town can read that sentence on its own wall any morning, gilding in the second dawn, gone by afternoon.
