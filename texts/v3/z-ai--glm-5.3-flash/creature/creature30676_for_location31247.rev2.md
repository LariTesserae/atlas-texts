# The Nation

You will never meet one of them alone. Alone is not a condition the hill produces. Stand in the traffic and put out a hand, and what touches it is the river itself: cooler than the corridor, dry as old paper, the length of the last two joints of your little finger and lighter than the dew the hill hoards — and then another, and another, shoulder to shoulder in both directions, each one held up by the others the way water is, passing made of a hundred small courtesies, crowding simply weather.

One of them, by feel: pale as the paste she is made from. Blind, and has never known otherwise — where eyes would go there is only sealed skin, smooth as a soldier's helmet, smooth her whole life. Her antennae never stop. She tastes the air in small strokes, over and over, faster than you can follow, and by this she reads everything: the wall, the crowd, the hour, you. Her mouth is her hands, her trowel, her cradle, her spoon, and half her speech — whatever she does to the world happens between two jaws thinner than a hair. You can read her age in those jaws. The young ones have edges. The old ones have coins.

She has no name. She has a scent, which serves better: it says her nursery, her caste, her appetite, her health, the shift she was born into. The toll at the gate is not a challenge. It is two antennae touched, and the whole of citizenship changes hands in the touching. A wrong scent, and the traffic does not fight it — the traffic parts, the way a stream parts at a stone, and the soldiers arrive, and by morning the stranger is paste in the rendering galleries. The hill does not hate. It declines.

And she knows almost nothing. This is the hard thing to hold, the thing the hill is built to make unnecessary: she knows her corridor, her rope of scent, her task, and nothing whole. The hill knows everything — every breach, every grain, every egg — and she knows almost nothing, and the work gets done in the gap between those two facts. Carry one out into the daylight she was built never to meet and she does not grieve. She simply runs out of instructions. Everything she knew was a place, and she has been removed from all of them at once.

A life here has a direction, and it is the hill's own direction: born at the warm center, ending at the cold skin. First she is a nurse, in the blood-warm halls, grooming eggs and carrying them along the gradient like coals, warmed and turned and warmed again. Then a gardener, in the jungle-air of the deep beds. Then granaries, hauling, mending — and at the very end of life, the outer crust, where the storms are, sealing the cracks the weather opens. When the rain finally comes, it is the old ones who are in it. None of them will see the hill finished. None of them ever has. And every one of them is worn and mended at the same rate, like the crust itself: mandibles rounding with use, skin groomed back to smoothness by the mouths of strangers who will outlive her.

Death is a change of smell. A body cools, its scent turns from *citizen* to *material*, and the neighbors lift it with the same unhurried care they lift anything — a grain, a lump of paste, an egg. It goes down to the gardens or the rendering vats, and what it was becomes what things are made of. There is no procession. The wall remembers her by holding. The floor is family.

Everyone eats twice: once for herself, once for the next mouth. Food moves through the hill the way heat does — mouth to mouth to mouth, down long chains from the granaries to the soldiers standing in the traffic and the nurses at the warm shelves — and no mouth along the way is permitted to own it. Hunger here is a current. Eating is a relay. A soldier is fed where she stands, in the middle of the river, without breaking her watch, the way a lamp is fed: without being taken down.

The soldiers are the sealed ones. Their heads have grown into fitted, blunt shields, corks grown around a face, and their jaws are enormous and useless for eating — they could not feed themselves if they starved. So they stand at the edges of the flow, every one of them oriented outward, toward the nearest place where the hill touches the outside. When the crust opens, they go into the gap first and become the wall: wedged head-first into the breach, jaws set against whatever is on the other side of the sky, while the menders seal the passage behind them. Most of them finish their lives as architecture. Some are in the walls now, head-first in the moment of their holding, and the wall is thicker for it.

They speak by striking their heads against the world. An alarm is a skull knocked on a floor, and the floor carries it farther than legs ever could, and everyone who feels it adds to it, and that is the whole grammar: fear and work, rippled outward through the mass, answered before the echo finishes. On the bad nights the ripple becomes a single syllable the size of a mountain, and it is the only sentence the nation says all together. The drum is not obeyed, exactly. The drum is the obeying.

Below the granaries, the gardeners keep a herd. It is blind and pale and never moves except when turned; it lives in shelves instead of fields and is milked with mouths. Fresh paste is planted with a drop carried in the gardener's own gut, which is why the first condition of sowing is having eaten: the seed-sack is the stomach. The herd is weeded by smell — the wrong molds announce themselves long before they show — and cropped before it can fruit, so that every generation of the fungus passes through the nation and is re-sown, and the mushroom never once gets to be a mushroom. Except in a few deep chambers, where the gardeners let it stand, and the rot glows faintly, coldly, the only light in the hill — and it lights no one's work, because no one's work here is done by light. The gardens are gold and bruise-mauve only to guests with eyes. To the gardeners they are textures, temperatures, smells, and every one of those is either *keep* or *eat*.

The queen is at the warmest point of all, in a long dim hall, and she began as one of the winged ones. Now she is a barrel of generations, longer than ten of her daughters laid nose to tail, her skin stretched thin at the segments so the next brood shows through her side like weather through a window. A court of some hundreds grooms her in shifts, licks her crown, feeds her the best of the gardens, and at her rear an assembly line has run without one pause since the night this hill was founded: egg, mouth, mouth, mouth — an egg is carried by forty citizens before it comes to rest. Beside her the king is small and slim and old, the only citizen of this hill who has ever been a stranger anywhere, the only one who has seen the sky. He grooms her neck. Nothing in the drum-language makes anything of her. There is no announcement, no summons, no word for ruler. She is not in charge of the hill; the hill is arranged around her the way a house is arranged around its fire, and the fire does not command the house. It only must not go out. It does not go out. Her heartbeat is the lowest note in the nation, slower than any drum, and all the drumming rides on it the way surf rides on swell.

Once a year, the hill grows eyes. The winged ones are fattened in their halls, and they alone carry sight folded up inside them, waiting. When the rain finally comes and the drum says *now*, the chimneys open, and for a single night there are citizens who can see: the grey plateau, the plume of their own home rising into it, the skyline of older hills going dark and lighter dark. They are the only ones who will ever look at the place they lived, and they see it exactly once, as they leave it forever. By dawn their wings are pairs of litter on the terraces. A pair lands, snaps its wings off at the root, digs a cell the size of a fist, and seals itself in with the rain and its own first paste. Most pairs are eaten — the frogs' one loud festival, the swallows' harvest, the bats' bonanza — and the ones who are not eaten become a hill. The queen fattens for decades in the dark of the founding cell. The first workers she makes open a doorway outward and walk into a country of made ground and begin. On the far skyline, some of the old hills have gone quiet: the pulse stopped, the heat left the center a chamber at a time, the crust stands fluted and black with lichen. Every hill out there is either a hearth or a mine. All of them began inside a fist of earth, with two citizens and one heartbeat.

The second nation lives at the edges, and pays in silence. Swallows in the flutes of the towers; bats folded into the vent mouths, whose droppings fall down a shaft and end in the beds, so that a portion of the sky's appetite is composted into the gardens — guests who pay rent that drops. Beetles saw away in the granaries and take their tithe, because the law here does not recognize theft, only flow. The moths have learned the grammar: when the alarm-drum ripples they drop and fold, and the bats hunting by wingbeat get nothing, and the oldest guest cancels the newest predator in the dark. The black frogs sing in the cisterns, and no one has ever seen a frog arrive; they are simply one of the things water does when it gathers, and the hill accepts this. There are mites that ride the soldiers' shoulders, whole civilizations of them, no larger to a soldier than the soldier is to the hill. Nobody has tallied the nations inside the nation. Nobody is counting.

And the dead are not kept, and they are not gone. A citizen stands on her ancestors at every hour of her life; the deepest floor is made of citizens no one remembers, and no one has ever dug to the bottom of them. When the hill mines an old gallery for paste, it is eating its own history — and that is why it never needs to keep one. Memory here is not a story. It is load-bearing. The past is not preserved; it is recycled, which is more loyal.

So who lives in the hill? A creature the question was not built to hold: one nation, blind, warm at the center, wearing its dead, speaking by drumming, feeding itself mouth to mouth down a gradient that never stops sliding. It breathes on the day's turning. It answers the sky once a year with a hurricane of itself. It has never once seen the whole of what it is, which is the only way anything this size has ever been made — by hands too close and too many to see the whole. It is not finished. Neither is the ground it stands on, because the ground is made of it, and it is still making the ground, and nothing in it knows that this is what it is doing.

That is who lives here. The hum you feel through your feet on the approach road, under everything, is the sound of the country being made — and it has been made of them all along, and they have never stopped.
