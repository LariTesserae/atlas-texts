It is Fellstrand's one wealth: wood out of a sea that left, cut by people who walk a mile over mud to reach the trees, and who are wet to the waist for half the year and rich for none of it.

Because there is a town. You will doubt it for the first mile of the walk, because it shows you nothing — a low dark line on the landward edge of the flats, stone-colored on stone-colored ground — and then it resolves, and you understand that what you took for a ridge of old ground is roofs. The houses are stone-footed and bog-oak framed, roofed in turf and weighted with nets of stone against the wind, and they are not where they started. This is the first thing to know about the people here: they live in a town that is walking away from the sea, one house at a time. Each generation builds at the landward edge and lets the seaward quarter go, and the quarter goes slowly, by degrees anyone can watch — houses become stores, stores become sheds, sheds become mossy stones with a gull standing on the ridgepole. The streets of the old quarter still run straight and end in nothing. Some of the buildings in the new quarter have made the journey twice, dismantled and carted and rebuilt, and the people will tell you so about their own kitchens the way other people tell you a grandmother's name.

The people themselves are short and thick through the shoulder, bandy from a lifetime of soft ground, with a rolling flat-footed stride that no one teaches and every child has by the age of four. Their faces are the color of old rope, salt worked into the creases. They are quiet in a way outsiders first read as unfriendliness and later understand as economy; the wind owns the flats, and shouting into it is a habit that died out generations ago. Instead they whistle — long flat signal-calls, each family with its own — and the sound carries over the seep-lines like something with wings.

Their work is patience, almost all of it. At every ebb, the sea-walkers go out across the Renounced to the living tide-edge, where the water still comes and goes, to fish and dig cockles from the new creeks; they carry everything they need and bring back everything they have, because the flood will cross the flats behind them and the creeks do not forgive. Between the tides they scrape salt from the crusted pools, cut peat from the lower ground, and work the drowned forest — dangerous labor, that. Peat shifts. Men have drowned on dry land out there, in ground that closed over them, and the town keeps small carved posts at the places where it happened, because the flats keep what they take and the posts are all the giving-back there is.

One person in the town holds the office that matters most: the map-keeper. Every autumn, after the drizzle season has rearranged the creeks, the map-keeper walks every channel of the Renounced on foot and repaints the map on the boards of the moot-hall, over the top of last year's. The outlived maps are not thrown away. They lie in a chest by the door, a hundred years of wrong lines, and it would not be much of a lie to say the town's whole history is kept in that chest — a record of where the water was not. The children are walked through the new creeks each spring, taught them underfoot rather than on paper, and they play a game the visitors find alarming: they race the seep-threads at the turn of the tide, seeing if the darkening water can reach their feet before they step off the line. When the ground wins, the ground gets a count, and the children keep tallies of it.

They measure almost nothing in years. They measure in lifetimes. The sea is a lifetime and a half out from the church, they will say, or three generations past the well. On the old slipway, steps that once met the water are numbered with carved lines, one to a generation, each mark standing for the height of the tide in some ancestor's day, and the marks walk up the stone like the rungs of a ladder laid against nothing. It is the town's ledger, and the slipwright keeps it: an office now mostly ceremonial, whose holder re-cauls the slipway planks every spring — not, most people admit, because ships are coming, but because the carved lines must not rot. At the head of the slip stand three boats. They have never been launched in living memory. They are tarred every year anyway, and the tarring is not permitted to lapse, and when you ask why, the answer you get is a shrug that has been passed down intact for six generations.

The animals know the town's schedule better than the town knows its own. Gulls time themselves to the ebb and go out with the sea-walkers, hanging over their shoulders, and come back with them, and the first gull over the roof-ridge is the town's clock. Twice a year the outer flats fill with wings — knot and dunlin and curlew in such numbers that the horizon seems to come loose from the ground — and the people harvest what they can and let the rest be, because the flocks are the one thing the Renounced has more of every year rather than less. The whitest brine pools swarm with pink shrimp-fairies; brine flies rise in brown smoke when you walk the summer mud. A few sheep graze the salt-succulent on the upper ground; their wool is stiff with salt and their mutton tastes of the shore, and the dogs refuse to drink from the seeps. Hares box on the old stones in spring. And in the drowned forest, each black stump has become its own small kingdom — a cushion of moss on the root-pan, cold black water between the roots where young eels come up from somewhere no one has ever traced, and very often a wren, singing from the top of a tree that drowned before anyone's story began. The cutting crews leave the tops standing until the birds have gone off them in autumn. Nobody remembers making that rule. Nobody has broken it.

As for the breathing: the town sets more of its life by it than the town readily admits. The seep-threads darken on the flood and pale on the ebb, and the salt is scraped when the ground has breathed out, and the old people claim they can read the long sigh well enough to know that the sea is not thinking of coming back — not soon, anyway, and not as a favor. They will tell you the sea lets go the way a rope pays out, not the way a hand opens, and they will say it without grief, because grief is for events and this is weather. Children ask whether the water will return. The answer is *not in our time*, offered without blame, as if the sea were a relative who moved away.

One thing only is kept deliberately out of the reckoning. The first churchyard lies a mile out under the Renounced, taken so long ago that no stone of it survives; the sea took the markers, and the families never replaced them. On certain calm days you will see someone standing alone out on the flats, in no apparent relation to anything, and that is what they are doing — standing where the family knows. The graves have no markers now. The living are the markers.

Stay past dark, if the tide-walkers will let you, and you will hear the ground breathe under the whole shore, that long subterranean sigh, and watch the lamplight in the moving town settle with it. Everything here lives inside that rhythm — the wrens on the drowned trees, the eels in the root-water, the hares, the sheep, the flocks that arrive like weather and leave like it. But the largest thing the Renounced has managed to grow, on ground too young for forests and too salt for fields, is the people.
