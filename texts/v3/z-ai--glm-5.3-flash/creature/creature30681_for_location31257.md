Ask who lives on the Shelf and the honest answer has to begin with the water, because the water was here first and has never once left. Everything else on the terrace is a lodger. The water is the resident.

It has a home range — the whole network of seeps and threads and falls between the cliff face and the rim — and it works that range the way any householder works a property: endlessly, and with total familiarity. The channels are not geography; they are habit. Every bend is a decision the water made so long ago that it has forgotten deciding, the way a person forgets the walk to their own door. The pools are where it sits down. The hundred small falls are its voice, and it talks constantly, in the same chord, the way old residents talk — not to anyone in particular, just because the talking has become part of the house.

The moss is the second-oldest tenant and by far the slowest. It does not move, but it is always working. Its sphagnum cushions are the furniture of the Shelf: press a fist into one and it gives up a cup of water and a town of invisible citizens — rotifers, water-bears, thread-things that live whole generations inside a handful of green. The moss holds its ground the way old families hold a house, by simply outlasting everyone else's interest in it. And it has its projects. The abandoned paths are being undone one grain at a time, without malice, the way a landlord eventually repaints. On the rim-wall the crust lichen grows at a pace of centuries per thumb-width; whatever the rim has to say, it says once per epoch, and means it.

Then the quick small lives, packed into the gaps. In the lee of each sheltering boulder hangs a smoke of flightless midges whose entire species' territory would fit under a hearth rug. They rise in a column when anything tall passes, then settle back into their private weather. A midge's horizon is one boulder's shadow and the mist above it, and by midge reckoning that is the whole sky — they have never left the Shelf, they never will, and it has never once occurred to them to want to.

In the stunted willow scrub, small spiders string dome-webs that come up every morning dressed in silver. The fog condenses on the silk strand by strand, and the spiders run out along the guy-lines and drink their own architecture. They farm the air. Nothing on the Shelf eats so steadily, and nothing eats so invisibly.

On the seep walls the pale thumb-sized frogs sit like living drips, their skin so thin that they mostly breathe the air itself — the wetness of the Shelf, given legs. At fog-rise, which is the closest thing the Shelf has to a dusk, they chorus: a rattling ovation of gravel for no occasion anyone has ever identified. Possibly they are applauding the fog, which is the largest thing that ever happens to a frog here. Their eggs sit tucked in moss, and the young hatch as tiny copies of their parents — no tadpole stage, because a tadpole wants still water and a childhood, and the Shelf grants the second only grudgingly. A frog's whole biography is one arm-span of wet rock. A frog that has traveled three paces from its birth-seep is, by frog standards, a great explorer, and probably dead.

The marmot-things graze the moss lawns with their calendars open to the sky, because their entire society is shaped by two birds. They live bent a little lower than they need to, moving boulder to boulder like nervous punctuation, whistling short alarm notes that the wind promptly steals. They are the only animals that rearrange the Shelf's furniture — lining their slab-dens with pulled moss, nudging pebbles, leaving the lawns clipped in a fine croppage that the water and the moss then quietly renegotiate.

Above them, always, the pair. Whether it is always the same pair is a question for ornithologists with more climbing gear than sense; the Shelf has had raptors so long that *the raptors* has become a singular thing, an institution that keeps exactly two employees at a time. Their nest on the rim is a masonry of sticks and bone and packed moss, added to across generations — no one alive has seen its first stick, and no one alive will see its last. They ride the updrafts in long screaming circles, and their cries are answered by the wind's held organ note in the fissure, call and response, the Shelf's oldest duet. They are also the only residents who can leave. Some days they rise past the haze of the upper sky and are gone — out over the cloud-sea, to some other peak, some other ledge — and they always come back. Everyone else here was born here and knows nothing else. The raptors have seen the alternatives and chosen. In that sense alone they may be the freest animals on the mountain, and almost certainly the most loyal.

And in the pewter bowls: the translucent shrimp, hovering and sifting the sky's drippings, and the one small flat fish found nowhere else on the mountain, or anywhere at all, probably. Sit with what that means. The entire species — every individual, every egg, the whole unbroken lineage running back to whatever first drifted into these pools when the peak was young — lives in a handful of hollows that together would not fill a cellar. Its whole world is smaller than a room, and it patrols the rim of its bowl like a small landlord, and it has no name, or none that survives. If the people here ever called it anything, the name left when they did.

Because people stayed here, once. Past tense — but not past tense enough, since their works still function. The dry-stacked shelter on the dry knoll. The channel cut to bring one seep to the shelter door: the only straight line on the Shelf, one sentence of intent in a language of curves. And here is the detail that says the most — the water still honors it. The channel still runs as cut. Over every other span of the terrace the water has done exactly as it liked for ages, but that one line it keeps, faithfully, the way a landlord keeps a clause in a lease signed by tenants long gone. Whatever bargain those people struck — *this much, this way, no more* — the water has held up its side of it without fail. It is the people who stopped showing up.

What were they like? Only the tools can guess. Hand-work, rope-work, slabs fitted without mortar or metal — patient people, or people who became patient here, because you cannot stack wet limestone in a sidelong drizzle any other way. They had time to cut one perfect channel. They did not have time, or did not take time, to pick up a hammer. It sits in its crack mid-task, the moss already treating it as slow stone. Maybe the last day was ordinary — fog rose, rain came sideways, the sun made diamonds — and someone set the hammer down to do one more small thing first, and the one more small thing was leaving. Or maybe they climbed down whatever path there was on purpose, work finished or abandoned, and the hammer was simply not worth the rope. The moss does not distinguish. To the moss it is a rock that arrived recently and is being assessed, at a rate of about a grain a year, for permanent membership.

So who lives on the Shelf, now? All of it, at once, in the tight balance that is the Shelf's only form of wealth: the water working, the moss undoing, the spiders drinking the sky, the frogs applauding the fog, the marmots living bent and low, the raptors circling their loyalty, the nameless fish keeping its cellar-world, and the people, absent, whose one straight line still runs. And over everything the sound — the booming hollows, the hissing chord, the low note held in the fissure a minute at a time. Locals say the Shelf speaks. There are no locals left to say so, which is the only thing wrong with the sentence. The note holds anyway, patient as water.

And if a person ever stands again on the wet stone at fog-rise, everything will go on around them exactly as it does — the midges, the chorus, the falling diamonds — because the Shelf's residents are not used to anything new. The last time something new arrived, it stacked some stones, cut one straight line, and left its hammer. The moss has nearly finished deciding how to feel about that. Somewhere under the ceaseless drips the hammer waits to be picked up, and the patience around it — water's patience, moss's patience, the Shelf's own long unhurried attention — is weighing, without any urgency at all, whether it is a tool or a stone.
