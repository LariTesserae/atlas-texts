Nobody lives here. That is the first thing the flats will tell you, and it is true; the flats are scrupulous about truth. They simply don't finish it. Emptiness is the only category you carried down the ramp, and the basin is willing to wear it for a while. You meant to pass through. The flats let you believe that too.

But consider what a track actually is: a record of passage. Nothing here has passed — and that is not the same as nothing being here. A visitor leaves signs because a visitor is on the way to somewhere else. A resident leaves none, because a resident is not going anywhere; it finished arriving, permanently, and an arrival that complete cancels its own evidence. So the blankness under your eyes is not the signature of emptiness. It is the signature of the other thing: occupation so total that occupation and location have merged. You thought you were looking at an uninhabited basin. You were looking at an inhabitation with nothing left over.

Start low, where the answers start. The moss cushions at the lips of the pools are not colonies; each is a single individual, and each is old on a scale you have to re-learn to perceive. A cushion the size of your spread hand has been thickening since before anyone you have ever met was born. It grows, but you would have to leave for a decade and return to prove it — a millimeter of deep green, paid out like interest. The green is that deep for a reason: nothing has ever taken a bite. This is the color of unused time. The cushions are soft in the way that makes you draw your hand back and then, immediately, return it. They hold their posts at the waterlines the way old attendants hold doors — without visible effort, without ever letting them swing shut.

The grasses are one plant. Follow the gappy stands and you will find the runners beneath the felt, joining stand to stand, and under the runners a sameness: every blade identical, every yellowing and replacement undetectable, because there was never a population here at all — only one grass that subdivided so long ago its parts have forgotten one another. The forgetting was its idea of peace. It dresses as a crowd and lives as a hermit.

In each pool, the green film is a single continuous body: one thought per pool, held flat, computing nothing, merely continuing. Photosynthesis, at this address, is a discipline rather than a strategy.

And the shrub — the youngest citizen, and the only one who still admits to age. It was the last thing to arrive. Nothing has arrived since. It keeps the clock. The soft tick of a droplet releasing from its leaf, and then, a long time later, another: intervals too long for weather and too regular for accident. Someone marks the hours in this place, even at this exchange rate. Time does not stop in the flats; it is counted very slowly, by a shrub, for an audience of one.

Put your palm flat on the ground and wait, and the next answer comes up through your skin. The springiness is not soil. It is felt — roots, hyphae, films, and rootless dark interwoven to the depth of your wrist — and the bread-color is pigment, not dirt. The basin is not a place with life in it. It is a life with a place arranged around it, or possibly the reverse; at this altitude of patience the distinction is only a hurry, and hurrying is not one of the local verbs. The warm weight of the air is breath: the knee-deep fog of the coldest hour is exhalation, the midday draw-up is inhalation, one full breath per day. The glup is a heartbeat, or a syllable — a bubble rising from somewhere under the largest pool, from a depth you cannot sound, once per visit or once per era, and the silence afterward is deeper because it comes from below everything, from the part of the basin that never surfaces.

So you could say the whole basin is the inhabitant, and you would be nearly right — and here, being nearly right is precisely the kind of rounding error that someone corrects.

A rule implies a keeper. This is grammar, not mysticism. The silence, remember, has the quality of a rule rather than a coincidence — and rules are kept. Wind does not enter; birds do not cross; nothing that lives by catching anything can afford this basin, because its economy does not carry predation. No pursuit, no prey; no prey, no hunters; no hunters, hush. Somewhere at the bottom of that chain of consequence is a will — or a wish — old enough to have become physics. The keeper does not enforce the rule. The keeper is it. Things nearby accede to its posture the way water finds level.

Read the built marks as furniture — its, or the furniture of whatever held the post before it — and notice what they have in common: almost nothing, in both senses. The ring of stones is a boundary, not of territory (the keeper claims nothing; claiming is spending) but of promise: within this ring, no pursuit, ever. A sanctuary's edge, marked in stone because stone holds a promise without needing attention. The flat stone on the smoother flat stone is a seat, and if you sit you will find it warm, and the warmth is current — not the memory of a sitter but a sitter's temperature, faint, shared. Press your ear to it and beneath the hum you will hear a tide: water moving the way water is permitted to move here, about an inch a century. And the shallow rectangular depression beside the largest pool is not a cistern and not a foundation. It is a bed, or the negative of one — the shape of a weight that rested so long the ground stopped arguing and began, in its rounded way, to agree. You stood in it once, testing, and the warmth came up your shins like recognition. The damp softened its edges the same way it softened the slopes: by settlement.

What does the keeper eat? Nothing here is eaten, and yet the books balance, so something must close them. Answer: the remainder. It lives on what the system would otherwise waste — the last warmth before it leaks away, the light the pools gather before it can be absorbed (why are the pools the brightest things for miles? because someone is *saving*), the interest on all that circulating moisture. It skims nothing. It lives on the circulation itself, the way an eddy lives on the river. The condensation circulates without ever being spent because spending requires a deficit, and the keeper is the deficit's opposite: the principal. Everything else in the flats — moss, grass, fog, warmth, you — is interest.

You will want to see it. Here is as close as seeing comes. Lean over the largest pool — you will have a last day, even if the flats never announce one — and look at the inch of warm water between you and the reflected sky, warm as bathwater, which is to say the temperature of tending. The sky is there, perfect, dim, faintly gold at the rim. And beneath it, just under the mirror, a darkness gathers that is not the sky's. It does not move. It sharpens. Attention does have an edge, it turns out; you simply have to be looked at by something slow enough to watch the edge arrive. Then the glup — one small bubble rising through its own reflection, breaking without splash — and the keeper has said its one sentence. It is not a word. It is an acknowledgment: the nod you give a stranger you will never see again and somehow always do.

In the fog hour, knee-deep in the basin's exhalation, look for the shape that is not dispersing at the rate fog disperses. Not a figure — a bearing. You can recognize a person a mile off by stance alone; this is that, minus the distance, minus the certainty. It does not approach. It was already there. When the warmth draws the mist up, the bearing goes up with it, and you will not be able to say afterward whether you saw the keeper, or the keeper's effect, or your own expectancy condensing. You will find you cannot keep the who and the what apart, and eventually you will stop trying; at this depth of stillness the distinction is a kind of haste. The hum you took for damp air against your ears — that is the voice. It has been pronouncing one long warm vowel since before you arrived. You spent your whole visit inside the saying of it.

Do not expect a greeting. From where the keeper stands — or lies, or pools — you came and went inside a single one of its breaths. You were weather: fast, loud-footed, warm, briefly shading the moss. It has incorporated weather before. The hands that set the ring, that dug the bed, that left the seat, were weather once too — and what remains of their remaining is the post.

That is the last thing the flats will let you understand, and they will let you understand it at the top of the ramp, if you turn around: the resident is an office. The basin fills it with whatever condensation lingers longest. The depression keeps the vacancy the way the seat keeps the warmth — not as a trap; the basin spends nothing, including persuasion — as a standing invitation that requires no answer. You stood in that hollow once. You stood a little longer than you meant to, and you cannot say now who was keeping whom.

Your footprints, when you look back from halfway up the slope, are already softening — not erasing; digesting, gently, the way the basin digested the hands. By whatever passes for nightfall there will be no record of you here. The record is kept elsewhere: filed as an extra hair of smoothness on some rounded slope, one more increment in the geology's long settlement, a breath of warmth in the seat. You climb out carrying the basin's damp on your ankles and in the warm weight of your breath, and nothing has been spent anywhere. You are in circulation now.

And behind you the flats continue — small, dim, warm; the pools holding the same sky at every hour; the shrub marking an hour no one needs; the vowel still being said; the keeper never pausing, because pausing is a motion — arriving still, at the only pace the basin allows: the pace of remaining. The Condensation Flats are inhabited the way a held breath is inhabited: entirely, and only, by the holding.
