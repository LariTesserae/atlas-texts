Higher up, at the crown of the vault, the climbers keep to their own country. They are long-limbed, slate-colored, quiet in the way of things that have never needed to be loud — no enemies have entered this hall in a span of time no word remains for, and so caution has had nothing to do, and has drained out of them like color from old cloth. They run the beams upside down. They drink from the drip-cups, delicately, one bead at a time, and they prune. That is their whole occupation: pruning. They pinch back the growth at the roof-crown where it thickens, they thin the leaf where the light has gone murky, they train the vault's curvature the way a gardener trains an apple over an arbor — except that no gardener taught them, and there is no garden. There is only this. The ceiling of the hall is bright because ten thousand generations of climbers have kept it bright, and they keep it bright because bright leaf is the leaf their young can eat, and so the light in the room is not weather and not miracle but husbandry, self-employed, running on its own forever.

Lower, in the dark seams where wall meets floor, live the sweepers — flat pale things, soft-shelled, that emerge on no schedule anyone has caught and work the root-mat in short industrious circuits, and everything they find is gone. A seed, a feather, a dropped husk, a droppings pellet, a dead moth: gone. The mat is immaculate because the sweepers are tireless and numerous and have been for longer than the cups below the drips have been deep. Nothing in this hall decays in view. Death here is a service performed quietly, overnight, by employees who are never seen at their work.

And between the millers and the drovers and the sweepers and the climbers and the eels in their black cisterns and the pipe-animals sweating their metered rain, there runs — this took the longest to see — a schedule.

The millers grind loudest at what might be morning. The drovers' single-file trains move heaviest then, and again at what might be dusk; between shifts the lanes stand empty, and the gloss of them is the only clock. The eels flash most often in the dark hours, hunting whatever hatches in the cisterns on a rhythm as fixed as tides. The drips never vary — that is their point, their whole character, constancy itself — but the *drinking* varies, and the drinking is the tell. The climbers come down to the cups in waves. The wall-niches empty and refill. The whole hall breathes in a slow twenty-four-hour swell of appetite and rest, and it has done so for so long that the schedule is no longer behavior. It is architecture. You could tell the hour in this room with your eyes shut, by teeth-hum and traffic alone, the way a sailor tells midnight by the feel of a hull.

So the answer to *what lives here* is: all of this. The hall is not a place containing animals. The hall is an animal's body that has come apart into specialists and forgotten it was ever one thing — a stomach of beetles, a lung of pipes, a skin of leaf, a blood of metered water, and the slow polished lanes for arteries. The plain outside finished because everything unfinished in it moved indoors, ten million years of surface life condensed into one room, the way a dying tree pulls its sap back toward the root.

But there is one inhabitant left to name, and it is the reason for the doorway.

The mouth of the hall is worn low and glossy at its crown by uncountable feet. Not paws. Not claws. The polish sits at head-height for a walking figure, and it is *crown*-height polish — worn by things passing through bent, over and over, coming and going, for a span of time that has outlived whatever they built elsewhere, if they built elsewhere, if they were anyone in particular. They may have been the ones who first folded the growth inward. They may have found it folded. No record survives in the hall, because nothing here records; everything here *wears*. But the room is sized for them. Sixty paces, a vault of thirty feet, a light bright enough to read by — it is a human-scale room, a hall in the honest sense of the word, built to the proportions of the creatures for whom a room is worth making at all. The millers do not need daylight to read by. The climbers do not need a doorway they must stoop through. Every comfortable dimension of this place is calibrated to a visitor.

And the visitors still come. That is the last thing the hall will tell you, and it tells you sideways, in the negative space: there is a stretch of wall near the great doorway where the niches are empty and the pipes do not drip and the mat lies unusually deep and springy, and the eels in the nearest cistern hunt there more boldly, unafraid. It is the one part of the room where nothing has been worn smooth. Around it, everything is wear; this one patch is kept, the way a floor under a bed is kept. Something sat there. Something sits there still, on some schedule of its own — rarer now, wider-spaced, a tide with a longer period than the hall's day — and while it sits, the room rearranges its attention around it without appearing to move at all. The drips fall the same. The millers grind the same. But the drovers take a different lane, the climbers keep to the crown, and the sweepers give the sitting-place its width.

Whether the visitors are owed anything by the hall, or the hall by them, there is no way to know. Perhaps they were gardeners once, and this is the garden gone feral around the last plot they tended. Perhaps they were only weather to the hall — a brief bright animal that came, and sat, and left, and whose visits mattered to nothing but the polish on a doorstep. But the doorway stays folded open. It could close; the growth closes gaps all the time, out on the plain, in a season. It has stayed open for the whole of the time the polish took to form, which is to say: the hall has kept a door for a longer time than most kinds keep a species.

That is who lives here, finally. A room that made itself out of its workers, in the middle of a finished world, keeping one low green mouth open and one patch of floor clear — for a guest that perhaps no longer knows it is expected, and comes anyway, less and less often, and sits in the only clean place in a room where everything else has been worn smooth by ten thousand years of arriving.
