...The Snagmoor out in the drowned forest, where the houses are woven *around* the standing dead — old snags left in place as corner-posts and rafters, herons quarrelling in the crowns, fish passing beneath the floors like slow weather. The Snagmoor has no streets, only channels and the memory of channels. And at the eastern rim, the least district and the most argued-over: the Outfall, a shelf of rock where the river gathers itself to leave, kept by a rotating company of wardens who tend the last works of the Hollow — willow trained across the young gorge the way you would train a hedge across a doorway. It is a dam that has never been built. It is fed. It has been fed for two hundred years, and it has bought the lake, by the wardens' own proud arithmetic, a century it would not otherwise have had.

**The people**

Three hundred years ago, the people of the Hollow were the people of the plain. Herders, mostly, and fishers, and they went down into the break in the world to find out where their river had gone — and stayed, because the fall had made a country where the only work was tending, and tending, it turned out, suited them. Their descendants know the rim the way a cellar knows the house: as the cold dark thing overhead.

The vow came early, and it is kept now without much needing its reason, though the reason is kept too, folded in the peat like everything else. The ground cut itself open once, a wound nine kilometers long; the first generation looked at it and decided that a wound that size has said everything there is to say about edges. So: no blade, no fire. Not made, not kept, not borrowed. The rim herders have fire, and the Hollowers treat that the way lowlanders treat a reputation for violence — as something a place can have, if it is that kind of place.

They eat what a cold clean river gives, which is more than you would think. Fish taken by hand and by woven trap, eaten straight or pressed into peat with a stone on top and kept all winter, sour and firm — the same technology that keeps the dead, applied to supper. Cattail rhizomes dug from the hummocks, sedge pith, gooseberries off the Terraces that come warm off the stem, the only sweetness on the plain. Their cord is willow bark retted in the springs until it surrenders, their baskets are sedge, their poles are willows trained straight in the nurseries and harvested whole. Nothing is cut. Things are persuaded to let go.

There is no fire, which means the Hollow goes out at dusk like a lamp, and this shapes them more than any doctrine does. The evenings are dark and long and full of frogs; people sleep in heaps, in houses of living moss a meter thick, while frost silvers the rim a hundred meters up and the springs, at four degrees, keep the sink faintly steaming all winter. A Hollow winter is gray, wet, close, and crowded, and nobody who visits one ever describes it as cold.

Their law is their economy, and their economy is care. Nobody owns wood; you own the feeding of it, and the census of a family is not its people but its dependents — bridges, houses, beetles, mats, each with a name and a keeper. The only crime is negligence, and the punishment for starving a bridge is to be given a hungrier one. You inherit the fed, not the owned. Families court by inspection: you look at what the other family has kept, because a well-fed bridge will say more about a household than any courting song.
