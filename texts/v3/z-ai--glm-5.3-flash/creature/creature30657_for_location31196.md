Ask who lives in the Weep and the bowl answers the way it answers everything: by count. So begin with the count, and begin small, because that is the order the mountain built its tenants in.

The green is the oldest resident. The moss was here before anything that moves — you can read it in the way the seep-lines are ordered, the oldest mats downstream of the youngest, each generation advancing onto bare stone on the bodies of the last. A finger-width a year, the climbers say, impossibly fast for this altitude, and it is true, because the keeper keeps proof: a line of notched marker stones along the eastern mat, filed fresh each season, and every notch has its honest inch. The moss is the bowl's skin. Everything else lives on it, in it, or off it. The cushion plants are its furniture; the ferns crowding the spring-mouths are its hair; the lichens furring the lee sides of the fangs are its slowest children, growing at the speed of patience, gray and sulfur-yellow, maps of countries that take a century to add a province.

The midges live in the pools, which sounds like a joke until you understand the pools. Hand-deep, tabletop-wide, drunk back by the rock almost as fast as they fill — and in each one, overwintering as grains no bigger than pepper, the whole sky-sizzle of the following summer. In July you walk into the swarm and the sound arrives, thin as wire, from immediately around your head and nowhere else. Ten feet off: silence. The midge is the only tenant of the Weep that owns a sound.

The finches own the fangs. Every crack is a household; every household is lined with hare fur and cushion-plant down; and none of it is ever announced, because the thin air strangled finch song generations ago. What survived the strangulation was light. They are rust and snow, and they conduct their entire lives in flashes — a cock rising on stiff wingbeats until he hangs against the dark zenith-blue and opens his wings white, closes rust, opens white, a sentence; the hen answering from the fang with one slow flare of both wings, an answer; two rivals spiraling each other up and up in countersunk silence, flashing, while the whole bowl watches a quarrel no one will ever hear. On the hut's roof-ridge — the highest perch the finches know — they display at dawn in ranks, and the ridge looks struck with sparks.

The hares own the floor. Three hundred, give or take, pale as the feldspar they shelter against, grazing the flower mats in loose herds that never tighten and never scatter. They do not flee things. The herd simply edits itself — the edit is where the stoat was — and a moment later the herd is whole again, chewing, ears turning like the bowl's own instruments. They bed in forms pressed into the warm side of the moss, backs to stone, faces to the sun, and they graze hardest on the very mats they manure, so that the fattest white-and-violet blooms stand exactly where the herds rest longest. The leverets sit in moss cups the size of two hands, still as dropped stones, and their stillness is not fear. It is the local language.

The stoat is one. There is always one. The ledger keeps a single line for it, continued by cross-and-rewrite further back than anyone living can read, and in all those years no keeper has recorded two. It hunts the moss-edges the way the water-hunting metaphor fails and the water itself succeeds — no direction you could name until it has already arrived. It kills what it kills and the keeper writes it down. When one dies or departs, another arrives at the rim within the season, white as the last one, and takes up the line as if resuming a sentence mid-word.

The falcon is one, also always. Pale as the rock it sits on — the only reason you ever find it is that it blinks. It relocates twice a day, always along the warm stone, always downwind of the basking hares, and its hunting is invisible by design: you never see the falcon hunt. You see the shadow of the falcon hunt, a dark coin crossing the mats at a speed the eye refuses, and then the silence afterward, which is the only sound it makes. The kill itself reaches you through your boot soles — a thump, like a word spoken under a floor. It drinks nothing from the pools. Everything in the Weep drinks frost. Nothing drinks; everything licks.

That is the count. But the bowl holds one more tenant, and the hut is how you know.

It stands against the southeast shoulder of the tallest fang, which means it borrows that stone's warmth by day and its shelter always. Dry-stone walls, low enough to touch the lintel without trying; a roof of split slabs weighted with talus; the chinks packed with moss that is green on the outside and brown insulation within. The door faces south, the warm side. There is one window, a slot, and it faces east across the rim, because that is where the morning's business happens.

Someone lives in it. The valley calls them the keeper of the Weep, and speaks of the keeper as one continuous soul who has kept the bowl forever. The graves say otherwise, and say it politely: behind the hut, a horseshoe of low cairns — keepers who climbed up and did not climb down.
