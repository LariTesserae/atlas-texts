# A Census of the Undergrown

Ask what lives in the Undergrown and you have asked a question with the wrong shape, because almost nothing here lives *in* the Undergrown. Everything here lives in something else. The willows live in the cavern. The lumenmoss lives on the willows. The moths live in the moss's light, the beetles in the moths' aftermath, the wrens in old beetle galleries, the keepers in rooms the willows agreed to grow. And the thing in the deep vaults lives — as far as anyone can tell — in the general permission of everyone else.

So this is a census of tenants. Read it in any order; the order barely matters, because the order is the point. Everyone here is inside someone, and everyone here is somebody's first try. That last part is a keeper's saying, and it is not a complaint.

## The Tenants of the Moss

The lumenmoss is the Undergrown's ground floor, weather, and noon all at once, and it is the world's most numerous inhabitant by any measure you like. Whether it is one organism or a billion depends on which keeper you ask, and the keepers have stopped arguing about it. It behaves like a meadow and inherits like a family, and it is stratified: the bright top-meadows where the glow is strong enough to read by, then dimmer compressed strata below, old moss pressed into something like slow peat, and beneath that the root-floors, where the light is a rumor. Each stratum has its nations. Mites in herds you would need a lens to census. Springtails like grains of living salt. Threadworms that rise and sink on the mat's breath, twice a day, like tide-pool creatures in a sea that has never seen a moon.

The visible animals are rare enough that each one is an event. The invisible ones are beyond counting. Weigh the Undergrown's life and the great beasts and the keepers together are the ribbon on the parcel.

One habit of the moss is worth knowing: grazed moss regrows brighter, flushing hot for a season before it settles. A meadow therefore writes its grazers in light. Keepers read tumber routes the way other people read tracks in snow — a bright sweeping script across a dark field, fading slowly, updated nightly.

## The Slow Eaters

Nothing rots quickly here, so decay is not a process but a craft, and its practitioners are masons. The bracket fungi the keepers call *pages* spend thirty years digesting a fallen trunk, and they do it without destroying it — the grain stays readable the whole time, which is why a dried sheet of pages takes ink, which is why the seed-runners wrap cuttings in it, and why the surveyors' few surviving field notes are written, when you look closely, on mushroom.

The gallery-beetles are worse. Their larvae spend four years inside a single trunk, carving passages with the patience of prisoners. When the beetle finally emerges, the galleries are not vacancies but infrastructure: wasps move in, then moss, then the shaftwrens, who nest in wood a beetle hollowed out when the wren's grandparents were eggs. Nothing is wasted because nothing is hurried.

A fallen tumber becomes, over some decades, a long green hill with a shape you can still sense underfoot. The eaters do not erase a body; they convert it. The Undergrown has no graveyards because everything is buried in use.

And at the drinks, the beetles stop too. The whole floor glitters with lifted wing-cases, ten thousand small backs turned to the fog.

## The Moths

The pale moths — hand-sized, cold to the touch — spend their lives in spirals, and the spirals are not wandering but commuting. They ride the shafts' slow updrafts with pollen packed on their backs, and the glittering snow you see inside a light column is half plant, half moth. Every shaft is a road, a weather system, and a granary.

They hatch on the drinks. A cohort emerges all at once while everything else is standing still and beading, and if you are holding a leaf-cup during the right drink you will hear a hatch begin — a sound like rain starting, except it is wings.

They have also acquired an enemy, recently. Some young plant — nobody knows which; it has no name yet beyond the keepers' *lying lights* — has learned to make a lamp: brighter than the moss, warmer-tinted, wrong in a way a moth apparently finds irresistible. Moths come. The keepers do not stay for the endings. They do not weed the lying lights out either; it is not their world to correct. What is certain is that the lying lights are younger than the oldest keeper's teacher, and that the moths give them a wider berth every generation. A predator and its prey, conducting an arms race at the speed of centuries, both sides with nothing but time.

## The Birds

There are birds, though you could spend a year between shafts and never meet one. The *shaftwrens* are dust-grey, small, and thrifty with sound: they sing only inside the light columns, where the acoustics are a gift, and outside them they speak in signs — a wing-flick, a lifted crest, a silent vocabulary worked out among themselves. Their song descends the shaft like a bright thread let down from the weather. The keepers set their mornings by it, because the wrens go up before the drink comes, every time, and have never once been wrong.

There are so few of them that the keepers still say the tumber's hoot is the closest thing the world has to birdsong — which tells you how far it is from any given root-mat to a wren.

## The Tumbers

The tumbers are the Undergrown's cows, its snowplows, and its seismographs, and they are extremely bad at being hurried. Broad-backed, moss-colored, slow-gutted, they are warm — actually warm, stovewarm — and in a cold snap a tumber will drowse against the wall of a station for days, radiating. The keepers tolerate this the way one tolerates a cat, if cats weighed what a laden cart weighs.

The name is accurate. On steep grades a tumber folds itself down and rolls — deliberately, tidily, a controlled fall — then unfolds at the bottom, shakes itself, and continues as though nothing happened. Watching it is like watching furniture decide to become a barrel.

Their hooting is a distributed herd. Each hoot is answered from a kilometer off by another hoot, so the herd is a conversation rather than a place, and no tumber has ever met the whole of it. Keepers learn individuals by voice, and some keepers learn to hoot back. Badly. A patient tumber will humor you.

Most importantly, they feel the swells hours before the mat shows anything — through their feet, the keepers think — and before a swell they go silent and give the long note, and stand still. Keepers watch tumbers the way sailors watch water. And their grazing is pruning: the trails between stations stay open because tumbers keep the meadow-moss browsed back, and where the herd stops walking, the green closes the road within a season. Every human path in the Undergrown is tumber infrastructure, borrowed.

A calf is a genuine event. Travelers will detour a full day for one.

## The Eldest Generation

The willows are the oldest things here, which is to say four hundred years old, which is to say children. Nothing had ancestors to copy. Every willow invented itself. Some turned out conservative — plain column-builders, honest buttresses, the masons of the family. Some turned out experimental: the great arch across the nameless vault; the willow at Hollow Station that grew a staircase-spiral and then, inexplicably, a second spiral that no one has ever needed. A failed experiment becomes a leaning ruin, and the leaning ruins are colonized within a season and nursed for a century — the Undergrown's only ruins are also its nurseries.

They spread by suckers, so a grove is one willow, continued — which means the Undergrown's most common large inhabitant is not a forest but a handful of enormous individuals wearing the disguise of one.

Do they talk to each other? The keepers notice that the swells tend to follow, at long remove, some far-off change — a collapsed shaft, a new daylit wound in the roof — as if the green were rebalancing accounts. This is a theory, not a fact. The willows have been asked, in the slow language of cuts, and they decline to explain themselves.

They have refused exactly one thing, as far as anyone can tell: the pools. The one *no* in a grammar of yes.

## The Deep

The black pools sit in the deepest vaults, glass-flat, ringed in white frost-flowers — and the frost-flowers are worth pausing on, because they are the only true flowers in the Undergrown. Four hundred years of leaf and wood and moss, and the world's sole blooms open where nothing else will go.

At the drinks, when every surface beads and drips, the pool surfaces ripple. Nothing touches them. The pools drink from underneath.

Even the beetles drink elsewhere. Nobody swims. No one has ever had to say why out loud.

And in the deep, at the edge of the glow, the unnamed thing keeps its distance. Its presence is known by inference: a corridor where the growth leans subtly away, moths going quiet along a line, a smell of wet stone where there is no water. The agreement's full text is a list of things nobody does. The keepers do not name it, do not follow its tracks, do not prune the far galleries, do not dig the deep vaults. Its side of the agreement, as far as anyone can determine, is that it stays away — and it has never taken anything, in four hundred years. Not a tumber, not a wren, not a lantern left out overnight. Four hundred years of *not*.

It feels ancient. It is actually a few generations of courtesy, which may be all that ancient ever is. And here is what the keepers think about, on the long nights: everything else in the Undergrown improvises in company. The moths have their cohorts, the tumbers their far-carrying conversation, the willows their groves, the keepers their successors. If the thing in the deep is the only one of its kind — and nothing suggests otherwise — then it has been improvising alone this whole time. Or it is simply a large animal with a large territory and no opinions. The keepers do not presume to know which. Not presuming is also part of the agreement.

## The Keepers

The humans are the Undergrown's only signers. Their work is negotiation, conducted in a grammar of wounds: a cut is a question, next year's growth is the answer, a graft is a promise, compost is a plea. A good keeper is fluent in refusal — knows how to ask in a way the green can say no to without taking the roof down.

Every station is a letter. A keeper inherits an unfinished sentence, adds clauses for forty years, and leaves the ending to a successor. You plant the roof you will die under. The keepers count time in keepers — *two keepers back*, *five keepers ago* — because the willows do not keep years and the brass instruments lie. Keepers who leave are buried at the roots of the willow they trained, compost like everyone else, and their successors swear the wood is warmer, afterward.

A station abandoned grows shut within a generation and becomes a green hill with a door in it. The Undergrown's only ruins are also its pastures.

The rest of the human fauna moves between: sap-tappers working a route of wounded trunks; seed-runners with cuttings sealed in wax and wrapped in pages, racing frost-berry season, because a willow berries perhaps once a decade and when it does, the whole region attends; surveyors with brass instruments and a haunted look — the look, the keepers agree, of someone in love with a document that refuses to exist. The maps die weekly. The surveyors keep drawing them anyway, and this is respected.

The children, rare as calves, learn the world's size by echo: stand still, count the seconds between a trunk-split and its dying-off, and you know how far the world reaches in every direction, better than any map. They learn early to carry a leaf-cup, and they learn that light can be owned — pressed moss-candles are the only light in the Undergrown that belongs to anyone — and that iron is older than everything, stamped with maker's marks from a country that, down here, is only a story.

Everything else that lives here gets eaten, grown over, inherited, converted. The keepers carve a name into a training-post and press a hand into drying sap, and the marks last decades, which in this world is nearly forever. They are the only ones who sign.

## Standing Among Them

So you stand on root, inside all of it. Under your boots, mites in nations. At your ankle, a beetle four years into the only home it has ever had. Overhead, in a column of weather wide as a cathedral, moths spiraling up with the world's pollen on their backs, and a wren's song coming down the light like a thread let down a well. A kilometer away, something hoots its location to no one in particular and is answered. The willows hold the dark up and refuse the pools. The thing in the deep holds its distance like a held breath, and keeps its side of four hundred years of *not*.

And under you, the mat breathes, and grows — fast, in the dark, in the cold, fast enough to feel if you stood still long enough.

You stand still. You feel it.

Nothing here is finished. The willow is still inventing itself; the moth its migration; the keeper their grammar; the thing in the deep, its solitude. Everything is somebody's first try — the keepers say so, and they would know, and it is not a complaint. Ask one what lives in the Undergrown and they will consider the question, hoot badly at a passing tumber, and answer: *everything, so far.*

Then the drink will come, and everything will stop at once — beetle, bird, tumber, keeper, you — and for a few minutes the whole world will be doing exactly one thing. It is the closest the Undergrown comes to an assembly, and you will be handed a leaf-cup, because you are counted now.
