# Who Lives at Condensate Station K-7

No one. That is the first answer, and it is nearly true, and like all nearly-true answers it hides everything interesting.

Let me try again.

## The Inheritors

What lives here is a garden that believes it is alone in the universe, and has never been contradicted.

The ferns came first, or perhaps the moss did — no one recorded it, and the duty roster sealed under resin lists only men who left. They were test-rack specimens once, or stowaway spores on a bootlace, and when the crew's thirty working years ended and the bore was laced over with rust, the green stayed behind and discovered it had been born into paradise. Heat without end. Water delivered on schedule by machines that would never stop. Light, of a kind, pooled in the floors. A predator-free world, a rot-free world, a world with no competition because there was nothing else to compete with. The niches stood open like empty houses, and the green moved in one at a time and has been redecorating ever since.

The ferns are the aristocracy now: black as wet slate, patient, each unfurling a frond a month with the ceremony of a flag raised over conquered ground. The moss is their peasantry, sopping charcoal velvet, doing the humble work of holding water against the stone. The vines are the diplomats and the empire both — they cross the bare black wastes between islands at the speed of weeks, in the dark, unwatched, feeling their way along conduit and cable with tendrils that spiral like things reading braille. When you come back a season later, the map has changed. The vines are how the islands talk to each other, or perhaps how they stopped needing to.

And the flowers — pale narrow bells that never open. This is the saddest and most honest detail. A flower is an advertisement, a shout into a crowd. There is no crowd. So the bells fertilize themselves in the bud and drop in silence, generation after generation, the shout shrunk to a whisper, the whisper shrunk to a habit. They bloom for an audience of one red blinking light, and the red light does not care.

## The God of the Small Room

But the plants have noticed something, and this is where "no one" stops being true.

They have noticed the transformer.

They have wrapped it — roots in its drip tray, tendrils climbing its cooling fins, a green boll around the warm housing — with a devotion that looks exactly like worship because functionally it is. The transformer is warm. The transformer makes the air yield water. The transformer's pilot light is the only fixed star in their firmament, one red ember blinking every few seconds, timed to nothing, and the vines have grown toward it the way surface vines grow toward the sun. The machine is becoming a hearth; the hearth is becoming a shrine; the shrine has no priests and does not need any, because the god runs itself. The thermoelectric stack drinks the gradient between hot floor and cool ceiling, the gradient has not failed, and so the red eye burns on, forty watts of divinity, sustained by the same deep rock-heat that sweats the ceiling and fills the puddles with borrowed light.

The station's schematic survives now only as a trellis. The company that owned it gave up the paint before the paint gave up. But the physics never signed anything, never resigned, never lapsed — and so the machines are on, forever or near enough, and the garden tends its machines the way any congregation tends a lamp that must not go out.

## What Lives in the Water

Here I must be careful, because I only have the evidence of the drips.

The puddles glow with light made of water, and they brighten and dim with the breath of the floor, as if the light were inhaling too. And once — the way such things happen, only once, at tide's peak — a bead of glow travels down a dripline faster than any drip, knot to knot to knot, gone before the mind can file it. Something moves in the water films. Something small and cold and blue-green that has never been sampled, jarred, or named, because the sample jars crystallized in their racks before anyone thought to look. It may be mineral trickery, an optical rumor. It may be the true tenant of K-7, the thing that was here before the plants and will be here after them, riding the driplines from puddle to puddle like a landlord walking the property.

The safety placard says not to drink the water, in three languages. Something down there has learned not to be seen, at minimum, which is more than most life manages.

## The Dead

There are no animals, and so there is no decay, and so there are dead things everywhere that have simply become furniture. A frond that dies does not rot; it dries, blackens, mineralizes, and joins the floor. The mug on the railing. The man-sized absence around the duty roster. The crystallized sample jars, which were once perhaps the graves of whatever the green used to be. In this room death is only a change of material — nothing is gone, everything is slowly becoming stone, including the mourners, of whom there are none, unless you count the stalagmites.

Those four pale carbonate towers are the room's own children — the first stalagmites these stones will ever build, decades old, growing a grain a year, reaching patient appointments with the ceiling. They are the only things here that live on a timescale longer than the heat itself. When the stack fails and the red eye closes, when the vines ration themselves island by island, when the last fern sends its last spores to sleep in the mineral dark, the towers will keep growing. They do not require a witness. Nothing here ever has.

## So, Who Lives Here

A garden without gardeners, worshipping a machine without operators, lit by water without a source, watched over by stones without eyes — a whole congregation of processes, each one alive in the way that heat and chemistry and slow green hunger are alive, arranged around a red light that blinks at nothing in a room that breathes.

And you, if you stay. Standing still between drips in the middle of the floor, sweating, apologizing with your pulse, full of the room's slow work. For as long as you stand there, the census of Condensate Station K-7 is one greater than it has been for forty years, and the room is utterly indifferent to the correction.
