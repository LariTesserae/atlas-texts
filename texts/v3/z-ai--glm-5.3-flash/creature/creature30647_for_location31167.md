The room is kept.

That is the second surprise, after the smallness, and it takes longer to arrive than the smallness did, because nothing in here advertises a keeper. The channels look like geology. The teeth look like geology. The velvet-dark gloss on the black glass looks like wetness. But a drip falls from a ledge and does not wander — falls, corrects, lands inside a groove a finger wide, ten feet down, and no drop in any cave has ever been that obedient. The rungs of the ladders are worn, but worn only where a hand would fall, and polished there, gently, like a stair in a temple. And the sweetness in the air — the flavor of hot stone and something alive — has a name, if you stay long enough to ask.

The menders. They will taste you before you see them. Your sweat goes into the fog the moment the hatch is up, and to them that is an announcement rung through the whole room, a bell made of salt and outside. So when the fog finally gives you a shape — on the second gallery, at the edge of a weir, where the water goes over in a sheet thin as breath — understand that it has been standing there a while, watching you the way you watch rain: attentively, without alarm. There are nine of them, or eleven, or a dozen; you will count three times and get three answers and they will not correct you, because they do not count themselves. They count drips. They count curves. They count the height of a tooth.

They are small — a child to a youth in stature, if you insist on the comparison, and they will not invite it. Their skin is the gray of wet stone, beaded everywhere with water so that they seem faintly lit from their own surface. They blink with a second lid, clear, a shimmer like a drop sliding down glass. And they are plumbed. That is the thing to hold onto: nothing here is only what it is. Their palms and forearms are grooved, fine channels cut into the body itself, and each morning a mender lays its wrist along its own stretch of channel and drinks from its charge before it does anything else. Each mender keeps a length. This is legible on their bodies the way a tool is legible in its wear: the arm of the mender who keeps the second weir is curved to the second weir's exact sweep. An elder's spine has taken the long bend of the great siphon below the cistern. You could blindfold one and hand it to another and the second could read the first's whole assignment off its bones.

Watch one work, if you can stand to watch anything that slow. It crouches at its channel — the crouch of a heron, of a held breath — puts two fingers in the flow, and drinks, and holds the water in its mouth a long time, tasting. Most of the time it swallows and waits. Once in a great while it finds it: a seam a hair wide, a flaw in the true curve that the water has been whispering over for a decade, inaudible to anything but a mouth that has drunk this exact length for forty years. Then the fingertip clouds, goes pale and dense, and prints — a bead of mineral drawn out warm as wax, laid along the seam, drawn true. The new stone hisses faintly in the fog. It is seamless. It is, to every test you could bring, the original curve; and that is because the original curve is not a memory of a shape but a melody, held in the body, sung down through ten thousand years, and what the mender has done is sing the channel back into tune. Afterward it tastes again. Then it waits, which is most of the work. The channels' black gloss is not wetness, by the way; it is a living liner, a velvet of something green-black and impossibly thin, and the menders farm it, and herd its grazers — small things like wet seeds — with a patience that makes farming look like conversation.

The teeth are them. You would call the white formations stalactites if you hadn't watched them minted, and you would not be wrong, only incomplete: the mineral they print comes out of menders, and when a mender can no longer hold its curve — when the tasting goes dim, when the arm will not fit its channel — it climbs to the Ledge above the highest gallery and its kin help it to set, which is their word, and its body gives back everything it ever carried. Part of every tooth up there is somebody. They point upward when they mean their teachers. Ask a question they cannot answer and the eldest will gesture at the teeth with its chin: ask the tooth. It is not mysticism. It is filing.

Their names are stations, mostly — Weir, Tooth, Cistern, Sip — or they are tastes, which is worse for translation; the eldest's name is a flavor you will spend an hour failing to name and then realize you have been breathing it since the hatch. They keep time by the Longest One, the great tooth over the middle gallery, and can date any event in their history by how tall it stood. By that calendar the hatch beneath your feet has opened four times that they remember. They can recite them: the one who wept; the two who measured and left; the one who took a tooth — and the pause before that last one is still, after some hundreds of years, an open wound dressed in ceremony. You would be the fifth. They know this the way you know your own address.

They sing, and their singing is the whole of their scripture. Each length of channel is a melody of ratios; to mend is to sing true; a channel that runs right is running in key, and a mender will crouch for an hour listening for a wrong note in what is, to you, a roar with no edges at all. There is one chant older than all of it, a string of sounds kept perfectly, syllable by syllable, whose meaning has been lost for longer than cities have existed — though its cadence is unmistakably the cadence of instructions. If you ask what it means, the eldest will consider, and answer: it means the water climbs.
