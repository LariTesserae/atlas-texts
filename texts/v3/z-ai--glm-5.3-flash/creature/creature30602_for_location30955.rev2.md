**The Tenders**

The people of the Shelves do not call themselves a people first. They call themselves *rounds*. A person is the path they walk. Ask someone who they are and they will tell you the wall they keep — *I am the ninth bund of the Kessel rise, the one with the crooked notch* — and only after that, if pressed, a name, and the names are mostly water names, or rather, mostly not names at all in the way an outsider means them: sounds that fit a person the way a notch fits a weir, cut and re-cut over a life until the fit is exact. A child is given a provisional sound at birth and it is gradually adjusted by consensus — family, neighbors, the way the child's own pace on the wall-tops feels — until by adolescence it sits right, and from then on it rarely changes, because nothing here changes without cause.

A tender's life is a round, and a round is measured not in miles but in drips. You walk your wall at the pace the wall demands, which is slower than a mourner's pace and has no relation to hurry at all. The gourd scoop is the whole toolkit: lift a palmful of silt from a pan's belly, crumble it into the flow, feel the notch's lip with the thumb for wear, reset a stone that has shrugging-tilted a degree off level — and you know it is a degree off because the water tells you, thinning over that spot into a sheet finer than the rest, and the difference between a full sheet and a thin one is something a tender hears through their boots before they ever see it. They are all, without exception, listening. Walking a wall is the same act as reading it.

They live long, and no one is entirely sure why, though there are theories spoken quietly in stair-town kitchens: the constant cool, the never-hurrying, the fact that a body here is always marginally wet, always marginally supported, always drinking water that has sat in stone long enough to become, they say, *patient*. Visitors have noted that the tenders age the way their walls do — by accretion, not collapse — the face slowly tufa-ing over its own structure, hardening in the same pale mineral tones, until an old tender and an old weir are hard to tell apart at a distance, which the tenders consider a compliment of the highest order.

**The Houses Under Water**

The stair-towns hold perhaps four hundred souls each, and the houses go down, not up. To build here is to negotiate with water, and the oldest solution is the drowned doorway: a dry room sunk behind a pool, its entrance below the waterline, the water held out by nothing but the arithmetic of trapped air and stone. Children learn to enter home by ducking through the dark curtain at the threshold, coming up inside into candlelight and the sound of their own household breathing. A stranger's first entry is a small ceremony — the family stands inside, silent, watching to see whether the guest will duck without flinching. Flinching is not shameful. But not flinching is noticed.

Inside, everything is soft-sounding and low. The rooms are small, moss-roofed, smoke sinking out through the living roof a thread at a time. There is almost no furniture in the outsider's sense; there are worn places. A seat is a stone polished by four generations of the same hips. The hearth is a shallow depression with a wind-shield of fitted slate, and the fire is fed one twig at a time, by hand, never left, because an untended fire is the only fast thing a house can produce and fast things are what the walls are there to prevent.

Meals are quiet and long. They eat cress and sedge-heart and root from the silt gardens, snails from the warm outfalls, the white fish in seasons the law allows, salt, and — this is the luxury, the festival food — anything that grows fast. A fast-growing thing on the Shelves is exotic the way a spice is elsewhere: a rush of duckweed in a warm spring, a mushroom fruiting overnight from a wall's wet foot. Children treasure these. Speed is the delicacy, because it is the rarest substance on the plateau.

**What They Do With Time**

The cistern-keepers are a separate calling, and a strange one to describe from outside: people who spend their working lives in the dark, in galleries stacked like drawers beneath the deepest shelves, tending drip. Each gallery has its own interval — four breaths, nine, thirteen — and the keepers learn their intervals so completely that a keeper can be passed, in the total dark, by another keeper's footsteps, and know from the rhythm alone who it is and which gallery they're in and roughly what they've come to do. They converse by adjusting drips. A keeper who wants to say *I'm here, all is well* to a colleague three galleries down does not shout — shouting in the cisterns is unthinkable, the acoustic equivalent of breaking a wall — but enters their own gallery slightly off the interval, just for a few drips, enough for the listening neighbor to taste the wrongness and smile in the dark. It is gossip, of a kind. It is also the closest thing the Shelves have to music.

They court the way they announce everything: hydrologically. A young tender who means to court another does not say so. They open a small sluice on the beloved's round — a courtesy flow, a gift of water sent a mile down the lines to arrive, hours later, as a slightly richer seep at the beloved's listening wall. The beloved reads it. Everyone nearby reads it. The whole local system knows within a day, and the acceptance or refusal is sent back the same way. A refused courtship is answered with a damming — a day of withholding — which sounds harsh but is understood as gentleness: *I will not send you water I do not mean.* A wedding is a great opening, sluices lifted along the whole round, and for one day the downstream neighbors' walls run louder than they have all year, and the neighbors taste the celebration arriving and add their own drops in congratulations. A death is the opposite: a closing. When someone dies, their round is quietly shut for a day — the water they tended held back, standing, keeping vigil — and every tender downstream feels the silence in the seep-lines and knows without being told, and adds a handful to any pool they pass that day, paying the dead person's interest forward, because the dead can no longer add before they drink.

The body is not buried in the wet. It is carried up to the dry divides, to the twisted black trees, and laid in the wall-top debris among the roots, and a small catchment is built beside it — always by the family's own hands, always drystone, always imperfect in some way they leave deliberately, because a perfect wall is for water and a grave is for grief, and grief should need tending. And so the cemeteries of the Shelves are these lines of small, slightly-failed catchments under black trees, each one leaking, each one visited by descendants who come to mend the flaw their family maintains — the same flaw, generation after generation, because the flaw *is* the memorial — and to press a hand to the moss and feel the water rising between their fingers, which is the dead, in the only way the dead can still be useful: still holding a little water up, still slowing the fall.

**The Children**

Children on the Shelves are taught three things first, in this order: how to listen to a wall, how to add before drinking, and how to be silent *comfortably* — not silence as deprivation but silence as the natural medium, the way a fish might be taught water. They play, and their play is elaborate and almost soundless: games of sending coded flows through the notch-chains, games of walking a weir's lip at dusk, games of reading each other's footstep-rhythms across a pan and identifying a friend a quarter mile off by the sound of their walking. They are given gourds young — a first scoop, worn on a cord — and the day a child's scoop is upgraded to a full-size one is the closest thing they have to a majority ceremony, held at the pan the child will one day tend, with the whole round's households present, everyone drinking a cupped handful together and adding a handful back.

They are not often told stories in the outsider's fashion. What they have instead are *demonstrations* — an elder takes a child to a specific weir, at a specific hour, and shows them something the water is doing, and the story lives in the place, told by the place, annually, like a tide. The Loud Years are taught this way: the rebuilt shelf with its overwide terraces and double weirs is walked once a year in full silence, and no one explains anything, and the child understands anyway, because the walls themselves are saying it in the only language they have: *we are thicker here because we were thin here once.*

**The Mirror People**

Twice a year, at the solstices, some hundreds of tenders converge on the great Mirror — a pilgrimage of weeks, walked alone or in silent pairs, each pilgrim adding to pools along their route so the land itself is slightly higher behind them, an approach that is literally an accumulation. They arrive, and camp at the rim without fires, and at dawn they read. What a reading actually consists of, no pilgrim will fully explain; each seems to watch something a little different — the plane against the sky, the rim-stones' wet lines, the first wind-wrinkle and how long the water takes to still after it. But they compare readings the way astronomers compare observations, and the consensus that emerges — *the west has risen again; the notch-cuts this generation should bias eastward; here is the new level* — is carried home in no document at all, only in the adjusted hands of the people who read it, which is the only archive that has never once been lost.

They are, in the end, an old people shaped entirely by one idea: that speed is a kind of theft, and slowness a kind of keeping. They will not be hurried and cannot be surprised into noise. But they are not grim. Watch a stair-town at evening — smoke threading down through the moss roofs, the level-birds standing their patient vigil at the pool rims, one frog somewhere beginning its single round note — and you will see people leaning in their doorways in perfect contentment, listening to a drip count out the dark at intervals of four breaths, nine breaths, and knowing, from the sound alone, that everything, everywhere, is exactly where it should be: high, slow, and getting slightly higher.
