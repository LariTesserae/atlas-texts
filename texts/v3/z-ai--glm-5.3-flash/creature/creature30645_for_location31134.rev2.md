Who lives here is a short list that takes a long time to say.

The census of the Floor is not a crowd; it is a handful of kinds, each so old and so few that *kind* and *individual* have nearly become the same word. There is the meadow, which may be one thing. There are the trees, which are one thing wearing many coats. There are the shells, who crowd; the threads, who visit; the fish, who is one; the Circler, who is one, and maybe the last one. Then the dead, who are kept; then the departed, who are kept only as a direction; then the water, which is not alive and is not anything else either.

What the Floor has instead of multitudes is depth of tenure. Everyone here has been alive so long that life has stopped being a struggle and settled into a posture: patient, particular, unhurried, and going on.

⁂

The first resident is the Long Meadow, and it is possible that it is the only resident, in the way a country can be said to be one person.

Ask it whether it is a single plant or a million and the question falls between the leaves. Root joins root beneath the silt; province grafts to province along the avenues; the khaki and the grey shade into each other with no boundary any survey has found. It is cleanest to say the meadow is one organism — the largest life this world has ever managed, wearing the disguise of a landscape. When one province bows in seam-weather and a far province bows at the same hour for no local reason, that is not contagion. It is the same body flinching twice.

It eats three ways, and none of them is hunting. It drinks stonefall — mountain translated into bread. It leans toward the Knees' warm breath the way plants elsewhere lean at a sun. And it opens its leaves to the Glow, which is the strangest meal, because the Glow gives from no direction at all: brightness that simply *is*, with no arrival point. The meadow's leaves open like hands held out in a dark room, not knowing where the gift will come from, and it always comes.

Nothing has changed its mind here in longer than counting holds. The spirals, the wheels, the avenues that look designed and are only old — these are habits with the force of law. The meadow does not remember the gardener. It remembers the tending.

And it leans. That is the thing to know. When the Circler passes above a region — once each circuit, which is decades between visits at any given mile — the lawn bows along its length and then does not straighten. Not for years. A region the Circler crossed a generation ago is still rising back, slowly, like flood-grass, and by the time any stretch of lawn has fully forgiven its own leaning, the moon is coming around again. The meadow is never upright. Somewhere on the Floor, at every moment, it is mid-devotion.

Its roots, meanwhile, are at the Knees, and they are eating. Stone into leaf; height into green; the last of the world's verticality going down as nourishment. This is not violence. On the Floor it is barely even change — it is the one remaining direction the world has, and the meadow is simply the part of the world that is pointed along it. The final shape of everything will be a warm dim lawn, and the meadow will be there for that, and it will still be leaning after something.

⁂

Among it stand the Slow Trees, and they are the meadow's punctuation.

They widen a finger's breadth per century — coral-boned, stone-wooded; stone that consented, for a while, to grow. Each grove is one tree in many coats, root-joined beneath the silt the way the meadow is joined beneath everything, so that a grove is less a forest than a congregation: one individual appearing, for its own reasons, as a gathering.

A Slow Tree does not fall. It finishes. Its wood is already half mineral, and given another few ten-thousands of years it is a low stone ridge in the lawn, and the meadow — which eats everything that used to be height — begins on it at once. A grove is a congregation; a dead grove is a meal being said over a very long time. Nothing about this is sad on the Floor. Sadness is a hurrying emotion.

Higher up, in the Upper Dim where the pale light barely holds, stand the lantern-groves: meadow-plants that eat the Glow and give back a little of it, changed, the way a lamp gives back oil. Their shining moves. It travels grove to grove on a tide with a period of years — lamp answering lamp down a whole province, until, seen from a distance through the thinning water, a lantern-grove looks like a slow thought being had. The world's night is lit by its own floor, and the lighting is a behavior, not a fact.

New groves appear only where seam-weather has passed through the high layers. A thread that rests a moment on a dim leaf leaves something behind — a seed, or only a habit; no one has ever been in a position to say. The lamps of the Upper Dim are planted by the weather. Nothing on the Floor minds not knowing that either.

⁂

At the warm feet of the Knees, where the seeps breathe their blood-warm breath on a rhythm of years, live the coin-shells — and *live* is the word, because this is the Floor's only crowd.

They are small and ridged like milled coins, and there are pavements of them at each Knee, generations thick, the terraces of their forebears underfoot like white steps. They face the seep. When the stone exhales — the long breath, years wide — the whole town opens. A pavement of shells becoming, all at once, a flower. In the pause between breaths they close, and it is a pavement again. A visitor arriving at the right moment would see the floor of the world blink, politely, like one eye.

They eat the updraft: the fine mineral dust the Knees shed, carried warm off the rock and strained from the water as it passes their opened rims. They eat mountain that has only just become weather. Time runs slack by the seeps — hours widening into seasons — and so the shells live long even by this world's forgiving count, and their town breathes in perfect time with the mountain above it, which is either worship or digestion, and no one has ever needed the difference settled.

They build. The pavements thicken, generation on generation, into low terraces — the only new made-thing on the Floor, a staircase of coins growing at the foot of each sleeping mountain. The meadow roots between them with the courtesy of a neighbor who does not wish to be thanked.

Once in a great while the fish comes, and takes one — takes one that is open, feeding, unafraid, because fear is not a thing the shells have ever needed to keep. The taken shell does not reopen. The town answers with a single closed blink, all together, and then breathes on. Grief on the Floor has one gesture and no vocabulary, and no one has ever used the word.

⁂

The thread-things are not residents. They are visits.

They appear only in seam-weather, only in the Seams, and they are made of the one substance this world has in abundance and keeps nowhere: disagreement. Two waters that do not agree shear along a front a season wide, and in that shear the boundary itself condenses into bodies — translucent, a hand's length, drifting with a leaf's manners through a doorway's draft. A thread is fed at every point of itself at once, the way a flame is fed along its own edge. It eats the gradient. It is the argument between two waters, briefly having a shape.

What they do with their day is drift, and sometimes pair, and once in a long while three of them braid — and a braid of three holds its knot an extra hour, as if trying the idea of lasting. Then the Seam moves on, the shear slackens, and they thin, translucence completing itself into absence. They do not die so much as finish agreeing to exist. Nothing on the Floor mourns them. They were never quite on the books.

And here is the detail worth keeping: the Still Middle, which remembers the shape of everything that ever stood, has never once kept a thread. Memory in this water is made of standing still, and the threads are made of going. They are the one thing the world lets go of without trying — the exception that proves the keeping. Where they come from is unknown. Where they go is the same kind of unknown. The fish does not wonder; the shells close politely as a swarm passes, the way a congregation rises; the meadow bows them along. Origin unknown, and unminded — on the Floor this is not ignorance. It is a form of politeness.

⁂

There is one fish.

Pale, stone-eyed, patient — the paleness of the Glow seen from underneath, light with no direction in it. It keeps to the ruins, and it goes from one to another on a round of years: the doorway's worth of wall, the sill-line of the bridge, the standing stones with their grooves worn to mere texture, the tower dissolved to a knuckle, the stair at the western Knee, and back — a small circuit inside the Circler's great one, the way a clock inside a clock turns faster. It is the tenant of remembered rooms. It hangs in the pressure-ghosts of houses built of nothing, in corridors the water kept and never knew why.

Not for long, though. Never for long — and that is the strangest fact about the Floor's only predator: the water has no memory of it.

The Still Middle keeps whatever stands. The fish has never stood. It drifts, it hangs, it moves on before the water can settle around its outline — and so the one creature that lives entirely inside the world's memory is the one creature the world's memory has never written down. The fish reads the remembered rooms the way a reader is not in the book.

It eats when the Seam brings food. In seam-weather it rises to the boundary's hem and angles its mouth into the shear and takes the threads as the current delivers them — no pursuit, no blood; the water does the hunting, and the doorway takes the draft. Otherwise, once in a long while, it makes the journey to the seeps. Headings refract on the way: it sets out along a Seam toward one Knee and arrives, without any turning it did, at a different one, and it takes the shell that is there, because the Floor forgives destination. The town blinks once. It swims home.

It is old. No one knows how old. If a body can inherit a posture, its patience is a posture learned somewhere near the end of the leaving; or patience is simply what a body becomes when nothing hurries it, which is the rule everywhere here, and the fish is only the rule's one mobile example.

And where the Circler rests — it rests for years, and the Still Middle memorizes the vast lying shape — a Circler-ghost hangs in the warm water long after the Circler has gone on around: a stiffness the size of an afternoon, fading over centuries. The fish visits these ghosts. It swims their whole length, slowly, once. It is the only creature on the Floor that has ever measured the Circler, and even it has only ever measured where the Circler had been.

⁂

And there is the Circler.

Vast beyond easy measure — the length of a slow afternoon's drift, nose to tail — it is not the largest thing on the Floor; the Knees are larger. It is the largest thing that *does*. It swims one enormous circuit around the entire world, and the circuit is not quite a circle: each round it passes a little to the side of where it passed before, a drift measured in Knees over rounds measured in ages. Over rounds past counting it has therefore come, once each, above every province of the lawn. Its itinerary is written where it went. The meadow's wheels are its crossings; the spirals are the Rivers' old turnings; the avenues are older than their own explanations. The Floor is a map of everything that ever touched it, written in growth.

It eats one mouthful a year. The bite, at that scale, is the size of a grove, and it is taken slowly, and the meadow heals it over a decade and heals it *brighter* — so that the Circler's route, seen from above the Dim (if anything could be), is a scrollwork of brighter green unwinding across the lawn. The one grazer and the all-green have made, between them, over eons, a single collaboration: the moon tends the lawn by leaning, and the lawn tends its moon by growing back sweeter where it bit. It is the only relationship on the Floor with give on both sides, and it has been running longer than the leaving.

Midway through each round it rises along a Knee into the Bottom Warm and hangs there for years — fins barely moving, the stone's slow breath washing its length, resting; and rest on the Floor is not an idle state, it is a thing done as slowly as everything else, and done for years, because doing less would be hurrying. While it hangs, the water memorizes it. That is where the ghosts come from.

And once per round, it calls.

It calls into the Seam, the layer that hears far and hears slow, and the note is so low that it is not a sound; it is a change in what the water is. The change travels. It takes a season to pass a region, and while it passes: the lawn bows all along its miles at once, every province agreeing for a moment about a single direction; the shell-towns open off-rhythm, the one time they do not breathe with the stone; the lantern-groves in the Upper Dim brighten, all together, as if a cloud had left off shadowing a sun that was never there. Even the fish rises from its ruin and holds still — holds still so long, that once in its life, for a decade or so, the water begins at last to remember it. Then the call moves on, and the fish moves on, and the memory lets go, unfinished, and neither of them mentions it.

If there were others of its kind, their calls would arrive on other schedules, and the schedule is empty. The Seam stays quiet between. Whether the Circler minds is not a question the Floor has a category for. It does not hurry. Its call is not a question — or it is a question that has learned to be satisfied by silence, which the Seam supplies, decades at a stretch, the way a long room returns nothing but the shape of the voice. Somewhere on the Floor, right now, the previous call is still traveling: the longest sentence ever spoken in this world, crossing above some province the speaker will not see again for an age, changing the water gently as it goes, arriving nowhere in particular, and not yet done.

⁂

Nothing rots here, exactly — and this should be said once more, because it shapes everyone. The dead sink slowly, growing pale as they go down, and the meadow takes them in root by root, and in a hundred years a body is a patch of brighter green. The Floor's graveyards are not walled off; they are distributed, legible, a census of brightness. To drift the Floor is to read it: here a swarm of threads rested out their one day and left a weather-shaped lightening in the lawn; here a shell-terrace went dark along its oldest edge; here, in the doorway of a remembered room, a small patch waits that will one day be fish-shaped. The dead are not remembered by anyone. They are remembered by *location* — and the locations are the meadow, which is also their food and their afterlife, and which keeps them the way soil keeps rain.

And somewhere, in a future the Floor is in no hurry to reach, the Circler will lie down. It will sink by the patience of its whole life, paling by degrees, and the meadow will take it in root by root for longer than the leaving took, and will make of it the brightest acre the world has ever grown. The last of the great appetites will be the garden's richest meal.

The leavers are a different case, and are kept in a different way.

They were not killed; they left. The drowning took so long that the going was courteous — room by room, generation by generation, each household walking away from the meadow as it arrived at the threshold. So the Floor does not count them among its dead. Nothing of them sank. They went up. The water kept their rooms, their sills, their stair; it did not keep them — whatever they finally were, they were not a shape, or they never stood still, or they had gone somewhere the Still Middle does not reach.

Once in a great while the Floor exhales one of their made things: a bead of sea-green glass worn to opal, a ring of shell too large for any finger, a hinge with nothing left in the world to join. The bead surfaced within sight of a seep once, and for a season the whole town leaned toward it as toward a small fire, and could not be persuaded it was not warm. The fish nosed the hinge and left it. The meadow grew over the ring, and then — out of a million years of parting around lines no one draws anymore — grew away from it again. The only tools left are ambiguous, and they are getting older: a hinge is a hinge while there are doors, and after doors it is a shape that remembers being useful, and after that it is a rib. The drowning is patient, and it is un-making even the memory of purposes, gently, the way it is un-making the Knees into bread.

Every ruin has agreed to lie down except one. The stair at the western Knee still climbs — fitted stone worn to nubs, ending, mid-water; ending is all it does now — and it aims at the Mirror: at the layer where up and down argue, past which nothing comes back, and past which, in the long keeping of the water, nothing appears to have ended. It is the only built thing that still points. It has been pointing for an age.

The Floor does not call that hope. The Floor does not call things. But the stair keeps its aim, and the aim has not yet been contradicted.

⁂

One resident remains, and it is the difficult one: the water itself.

It is not alive. It is also not not-alive, and nothing here is troubled by the gap. The Still Middle is a mind the size of the weather that has never had a single thought and has never once lost a shape — it keeps outlines, not purposes; it remembers that something stood, and was never told why; it holds noises too, briefly, and lets them go, so that the Floor's whole acoustics is this: *say something, and the world considers it.* It has kept the ghosts of houses, the print of the Circler's resting, the record of every graze and every grave. It kept the rooms and lost the reasons. If anything on the Floor could be said to know everyone, it is this one — and its knowing is all silhouette: it could draw you everyone's shape and nothing of anyone's why.

Everyone on the Floor is mostly water, being patient in a particular shape. That is the last thing the census turns up, and it may be the first thing after all.

So the residents, in full: one meadow, continent-wide, older than its reasons, never upright; some groves, standing like congregation, shining like courtesy; a few towns of shells, breathing with the stone; a weather of threads, one day long, planted in the high dark; one fish, unrecorded by the memory it lives inside; one Circler, perhaps the last, whose previous sentence is still crossing the world; the dead, kept as brightness; the departed, kept as a direction; and the water, which keeps the shape of nearly everyone and the purpose of no one.

Emptiness is not available here. It is not that the Floor is crowded — it could hold a thousand more kinds and not feel full — it is that everything is kept. Every volume of water carries something; every shape is held; every death is a greening; every leaving left a threshold. Nothing on the Floor ends so much as it changes address.

And over all of it the Circler swims its patient circle, above a lawn that will not stand straight again before it must lean; and under all of it the Knees breathe and the shells open and the Rivers carry the mountains' bread toward provinces they have fed for an age; and in the high dark the lamps answer one another, lamp by lamp, year by year; and somewhere, right now, an old low note goes on changing the water gently as it passes.

The Floor is warm, and layered, and green in the dark, and nearly silent. It is not finished. It has only, at the end of all its crises, chosen the one motion left worth making — the slow one, the leaning one, the one that looks, from inside the water, where no one is looking from, exactly like staying.
