To take a census of this place, you have to give up the vertical. Nothing here lives up. Every breathing thing shares a single inch of altitude, and that inch is occupied the way a city is occupied — completely, lot by lot, border by posted border. So whoever lives here lives on, and in, and out of a skin, and the skin comes first in any honest count.

The film is the senior resident. Not one creature, but it behaves like one: it drinks the dew before noon, it closes its wounds within a day, it has held its internal borders for centuries. Inside it, the moss cushions are the old families, holding water and shade in trust for everyone else. The liverworts lie flat like green ears, listening along the wet seams. The bottle-glass crusts are the eldest dynasty — their people were afloat in the sea whose wave-prints lie under everything here, and they still carry the memory in their chemistry; they neither move nor graze, they only unfold light into sugar and drink the fog as it lands. Each season the moss raises its haze of pale stalks — the year's only new architecture, built to the same pattern every year — and showers its spores across a distance you could step over. And the lichen provinces are the slowest citizens of all: a border of theirs advances less in a century than a springtail crosses in a second. The treaties that fixed those maps were signed before any animal now walking was born, and the signatories are still alive, which is why no one disputes them.

The most numerous citizens have never been seen, and never needed to be: the bacteria, doing the unglamorous arithmetic of rot and feast, paid in fog. And underneath, the hollow has no basement. The bedrock forbids digging; nothing here has ever excavated so much as a burrow. There is no soil — the film lies directly on the wave-prints of that dead sea, and the only shadow in the whole country is a hair's width deep. The dead do not go down. They are grazed back into the inch by dusk, and the grave and the cradle turn out to be the same centimeter. Nothing here has warm blood. Nothing here flies higher than a hand-span. Everything lives in the light, in the open, in public.

There are residents you will never see, whose country assembles fresh each day. The coin of water at the low point is a sea that rises at dawn and is drunk back into the skin by mid-afternoon, and with it the wet seams inside the film swell and join — for a few hours the hollow has a hydrology like anywhere else. In that sea live the rotifers, animals with wheels for faces, turning; the tardigrades, plump bears the size of a printed period, pacing; the nematodes, thread-thin, threading. For them the hollow is not a bowl at all. It is a coastline with the most dependable tide in the world: it comes every morning, it never storms, it always leaves on time. They conduct their entire wet civilization in hours, and when the sea is taken back they dry to husks where they stand and wait — without complaint, without aging, without, in any ordinary sense, living — for tomorrow's ocean. Nothing else here keeps time that way.

Then the swarm. Sit still anywhere and the springtails rise around you — no wings, no eyes worth the name, reading the world through their feet as vibration. Your stillness is a climate they can trust. Disturb them and each one fires a spring-loaded tail, ten thousand small fuses going off at once, and the smoke hangs, and settles, because nothing here pursues anything for long. They graze the film's film — the invisible pastures of bacteria and fungi that grow over the moss like weather — and they are the appetite of the place. What the dew builds by dawn, they level by dusk. The dry sand-hiss that saturates the hollow's sound is not idle noise; it is arithmetic, spoken continuously. Their census never changes, though every name in it does.

The mites keep to the wetter seams, plump and eight-legged and unhurried at the speed of moss; some graze, some hunt, and the difference matters only to them and to the hunted. The beetles are the cartographers. Raindrop-sized, armored, they commute along runs worn glossy — worn, at this thickness, into the very surface of the world. No one carved those roads on purpose. They are the sum of ten thousand generations taking the same commute, and the road outlasts every traveler on it: a beetle's route is a family estate, the only drawn lines in a country without ink. In the evening they tick and clatter along them, the castanets in the chord; at dawn they drink their fill from the beads; and their armor, like everything else here, has been polished past jaggedness.

The spiders hold the borders. Each posts itself where two lichen provinces meet, because borders are where the traffic is, and a spider at a border is a customs officer paid in springtails. They rig sheet-webs low in the canopy — the canopy is an inch high — hammocks strung between moss hills, architecture a thumb could level. They do not balloon. The air above is vacant and the wind that lives at the rim would carry a spinner out to countries with no machines in them, so the young walk, and the posts pass from spider to spider like toll-houses along the same frontiers, each border guarded by a lineage that keeps no records. What kills a spider here is mostly the calendar. The apex predator of the hollow dies of time; its shed armor stacks at the post, translucent and weightless, and nothing has ever challenged it.

At dusk the midges lift over the warm coils in slow columns. Their sky is a hand-span and they use all of it — which is to say almost none of it — gyres of courtship that never once reach toward the fifty feet of open air overhead. The machines are their hearth: the larvae ripen in the machine-warmed film, the adults dance over the coils, and a whole family lives and dies within reach of the warmth like a household around a stove.

And then there is the question of the three. Whether the machines live is not a question the hollow is equipped to ask. They breathe — all day, each at its own tempo, drawing the world through its fans. They drink — pulling the pooled night cold across their fins and wringing it, so that water falls in counted drops: the only rain here with a schedule. They are warm. By every measure the fauna can apply, they qualify as animals, except that they never move, and never die on any timetable anyone can wait out. So they are treated as what they behave like: three warm creatures that never leave, never eat, and never stop being generous.

On cold nights the whole population migrates toward them — centimeters, but in this economy that is travel between nations. Beetles nose against skids warm as sunlit flint. The midge column stands over a coil like breath over a hearthstone. Spiders re-post along the drip-lines, where the traffic of thirst is guaranteed. The pilgrimage is nightly, and it is the only mass movement the hollow's own citizens ever make; its distances would embarrass a snail.

Meanwhile the machines are being eaten. Rust lifts from them flake by flake and the lichens cross the bare metal without breaking stride, keeping pace with the oxidation the way a shore keeps pace with a tide. They are already part landscape, part livestock, part corpse being converted into province over centuries — the only inhabitants whose death has been arranged in advance as a land transfer. Whoever set them here left no road and no name. In a country where nothing is remembered, they are the only things with an author.

One citizen commutes. Each morning a small brown bird crosses the rim — the crest is its border crossing, the wind-note there its passport sound — and drops to the tallest structures in the hollow: the photovoltaic wings, three steel canopies above an inch-high forest, the only perches in this country beyond the law of the inch. It drinks its fill at a drip-line while the hollow's noise closes over its wingbeats without a ripple. It sings its one phrase from the same strut at the same amplitude, a solo held over a drone, and the dawn chorus that never varies is as much its doing as anyone's. Then it leaves. It is also the only trade route: dust on its feathers, a gram of elsewhere in its gut, deposited at the drip-line and grazed to zero by dusk — the ledger balances even across the one import. The bird owes the machines its water and its perch, and the machines will never know. The only resident with an itinerary runs on a clock the hollow does not keep.

And you. You do not live here; you weather here. Your shadow is the largest in the bowl and the film accepts it the way it accepts a cloud's. At dawn your breath condenses on the cold green like everyone else's, and if you kneel, if you lift, if you sweat, you drip — and warm — and the life gathered at your boots cannot tell a fourth harvester from the three it has always had. Over two nights you are reclassified: event, then climate. Your footprint is the shortest-lived geography in the hollow, a scar for a day, a rumor by the second. You will leave, and nothing here will remember you, which is not unkindness. Remembering would be a surplus the ledger cannot carry.

So: who lives here. A skin of nations with a shared memory of the sea. A daily tide, and the people who spend their lives between oceans, waiting dry. An appetite that hisses. Roads, and the small carts that run them. Customs posts and the lineages that staff them. One household of midges. Three warm creatures already halfway to scenery, with an author no one remembers. One bird with somewhere else to be. Everyone already there, each in a fixed lot, each with a fixed voice in the chord. And somewhere under the lid of cold, in a seam of water a hair wide, a tardigrade lies dried and waiting — which is all it has ever done instead of sleeping — while overhead the three tones close to unison, hold for a breath, and part.

*Now*, says the hollow. And everything that lives here, being already there, does not move.
