Start the census with an embarrassment: the largest resident is not an animal. Ask the basin who lives there and it answers with a list that keeps refusing to resolve into bodies — furnace, ledger, conveyor, choir — and the list is the answer. The works is the resident. The animals are its organs, every one of them convinced it is a tenant, and that conviction may be the basin's oldest product. Everything down here breathes as if it lives in a place. In fact the place lives in itself, and they are how it manages it.

But a census wants bodies, and the bodies are there, stacked to the ceiling like everything else. Begin at the bottom, where the loudest citizens have never once been heard. The ant galleries run through the peat like wiring through a wall, and inside them the colonies strum and drum and riot in full chorus — traffic noise, arguments, auctions, funerals, a city with its entire orchestra in the basement. The ground keeps its own counsel. Above, nothing registers but the stacks: the termite pipes standing up through the under-story, whose hum is not machinery but muscle, a million small shiverings pumping the day's breath through the soil. In the warm flue of every stack lives a lodger, pale as paper and thin as an opinion, that mates once a year to the shift in the hum, because the stack is the only clock it will ever meet.

The vats are staffed around the clock, which is simple, since the vats keep the clock. The fungus estate works the warm mush without pause, and the browser that sleeps in it has a name down here — or would, if anyone named anything: the vatsitter. It is dog-sized and hairless and rich in skin, and it sleeps belly-down in the working center of a vat the way a cat sleeps on a boiler, except that here the comparison is a job description. It rises at intervals to eat the yeast crust off the top, like a man trimming his own house. This is not laziness; it is logistics. When a crown-fall opens a new vat somewhere, the estate needs a courier, and the courier it evolved is the one it feeds: the vatsitter carries the starter in its gut and pays it out along the roads, an inoculation walking across the basin on padded feet. A fungus that wanted to travel grew itself a traveler. By now the vatsitters wear the bloom on their own backs; an old one asleep looks less like an animal than like a small warm hillside, and the distinction has stopped mattering to either party.

The roads belong to the freight: the herds, low and heavy and padded, with eyes reduced to wax beads, because down here nobody looks at anything — looking is a sense for upstairs. Their hides are rain-schedules. Grooves run from shoulder to lip, the fog pays them at the cool hours, and they drink their own bodies at dawn; a caravan carries its weather with it, which is why the law that says nothing dries has never once been broken by the party most at risk of breaking it. They walk the cobbled dry roads nose-to-tail because stone is the quietest floor in the world, and their language runs ahead of them in the peat. It is a load-bearing grammar. A mile off, a sentence arrives through your soles: water at the second fork; the keeper has posted the tallies; a calf has learned the road; one of us is dying. The dying travels farthest — three slow steps, then a nothing, and the nothing is the heaviest word the basin owns.

Hunting, in a place that has outlawed sound, becomes a literacy problem, and the road-hunters — call them the ventriloquists — have solved it the dishonest way. They do not chase; they read. One works the peaty margin of a road for a whole night, walking in a calf's rhythm, throwing its footfalls like a voice, insinuating itself to the edge of a herd by pretending to belong to it. It is a good trick, and it has a shelf life. Every generation or so a herd rotates its step, the way a household changes its locks, and for a season or two the liars eat well; then the rhythm changes and the liars' children start over from nothing. This is why the basin's predators are all patient and none of them are numerous. And when the kill comes, it is not an event. It is a correction. One moment the ground carries two sets of steps; then one. The mud keeps that story to itself.

If the basin had a mayor it would be a tally-keeper. It does not have a mayor; it has a ledger, and the ledger is written in callus. The tally-keeper is a solitary about the size of a yearling deer, long-jawed, soft-footed, and older than any herd it audits; it walks its mother's route for the first ten years of its life, and then the route is its. At each cistern tree it leans its flank against the pale trunk, cheek to the cool, taking the temperature of an account, and it posts the figures with its teeth: a long notch for full, a short nick for low, a doubled bite for brackish, a polished square for sealed this season — and no driller worth its chisels touches the sealed ones. The code is public; the whole basin can read bark. What the keeper owns is not the secret but the rounds. Nothing enforces the ledger except that everything reads it.

The drillers, who opened the original taps in the cistern trees, are paid in sips at their own spouts. The trees seal behind them like slow doors; the keepers reopen what the trees have closed. It is a war fought entirely in decades, with no battles and three currencies — bark, callus, time. The hollow stem-wells have lids, because water kept on books wants lids, and the lids are chewed, and the chewers are paid in sips too.

The wipers are the basin's hands. Small gray birds, soundless, they walk across the bodies of the place — freight, vatsitters, solitaires, you — harvesting fog-drip off hides and being paid a second wage in vermin. They are groomers, which is to say they are the news. Their routes are the census. When the wipers begin to detour around a hollow where a herd should be, the ground knows by noon, and nobody has said anything, because there is nothing to say and no mouth built for saying it. Stand still an hour and one will find you — you are the largest unclaimed surface in the district — land on your sleeve, wipe it, and bill you by taking the tick you did not know you had. By evening the routes will have logged you: a cistern that walks badly, drinks from made things, and insists on speaking through the air.

At the spring the works convenes itself. The queue is not courtesy; it is infrastructure. The freight stands mid-steam where the footing is firm; the small drinkers hold the sweet edge where the drip comes off the vapor; the vatsitters wallow at the warm sand boil; each family keeps its stone, and the stone is an address, and the address is inherited the way farms are. Downwind, at a deference-length, the readers drink with everyone else: the hunters with eyes not worth the name, flat and wide, whiskered like instruments. They read the water's fields — every muscle of every body a faint current — and can follow a heartbeat through a foot of opaque warm mud. Stand in the steam and you are legible; one of them will take your pulse without touching you and find a dialect no herd taught you. They will let you stand there anyway. The truce is not for persons; it is for the water, and the water does not keep guests. What the readers hunt, they hunt elsewhere. Here they only drink, and the peace is exactly the width of the steam, and everyone has measured it. In the end you will be filed under weather: something that passed, leaving warmth and a footprint the litter is already drinking back.

Once a day the basin breathes, and the rising tide of fog is the commute. The grain in the air between knee and head is mostly alive; the fog is a crowd, and it rides the stacks upward, and the swarms ride with it, and when they cross under the ceiling the gray dims and brightens like weather over a plain. The emergences are the floor's one festival: grubs out of the vats rise as wings, mate at the bright margin the floor will never see, and fall back as the leaf-rain's richest hour. The banquets are held upside down, at the ceiling, and the floor is never invited; it is paid in exports instead — flowers, fruit, leaf, and the dung of the absent landlords, who sow the under-story in packets and never once come down to inspect the crop.

Three blue pilot-lights burn where the gas comes up, and they are the basin's only stars: fixed, low, constant. The moths navigate by them, and the aprons of spent wings grow like snow that never melts, and the sextons glean them — a beetle that has made a career of the fall. Nothing tends the fires and everything uses them; the lost walk toward warmth and find either a flame or a mistake. And at the dead vents — the rust-orange lines, the stopped clocks — the dances go on for a generation after the flame has failed. Moths circle an absence. It is an inheritance with no estate, a rite remembering a maker nobody ever built.

To die in the basin is to be decanted. Every body is a cistern, and the vats are the afterlife's accounting department; their books are soil. The old freight leave the file and lie down in the low hollows where the vats are warmest, and the estate takes them without paperwork, and the worms of real length do the auditing, and even the skeletons are on the books — etched, mined, spent. Nobody is wasted. Nothing dries. The one ferocious emergency is thirst, and it is the only alarm the basin owns, and it is rung through the feet.

Once a year the sky pays its arrears, and for a night and a day the law of silence is suspended — not as mercy but as physics, since sound cannot carry in rain. Nearly every animal in the basin owns a voice it has never used, like a piano in a house nobody visits, and this is the recital. The freight trumpets through feet and throat at once. The vatsitters bellow like foghorns. From every stem-well and every flooded pocket, things the census never found climb out — frogs whose whole lives fit inside a wet night, a lifespan of thirty hours spent entirely in the only weather they will ever see — and they sing as if the dark had never been invented. By gray morning it is over. The ceiling reseals. The drip resumes its arithmetic. The tally-keeper posts the new figures — vats richer, cisterns overfull, the spring-line shoved a claw-width sideways — and bites the spring's new bearing into a root it drowned before your grandmother was born, which is the basin's pole star, the fixed point all its maps are measured from.

And the books balance. They always balance. That is the last thing to know about whoever lives here: they did not build the works, and they could not live a week without it, and so neither serves the other. One patience wearing many bodies — keeping itself warm, spending its water slowly, changing its mind only at the speed of generations, and waiting, without hurry, for a rain that always comes.
