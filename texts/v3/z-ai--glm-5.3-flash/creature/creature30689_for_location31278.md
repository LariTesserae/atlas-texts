Ask what lives here and the first honest answer is: nothing, not in any sense you've used the word before. No birds cross it. No insects rise from it. Nothing burrows, nothing grazes, nothing flees. A food web needs more than one strand, and this place has exactly one: light falls in, and everything else in the bowl is what catches it. There are no flowers, which means no pollinators, which means no birds that follow pollinators, and so on down through the absences. Predation requires prey, and prey requires an elsewhere to hide, and here every hiding place is the same continuous body as the thing you'd hide from. There is no elsewhere.

Even the silence isn't an absence. That's the next thing to correct. The felt of hanging filaments at chest height is upholstery, and upholstery does what upholstery does: it drinks sound. The valley wears its own acoustic skin — the wind comes over the rim and is swallowed a hundred yards into the bowl, muffled the way a shout is muffled by a coat. What you heard standing on the pass was not emptiness. It was an interior.

Then the second answer, which is the true one: one thing lives here.

The valley. Not a place with life in it — a life the size of a place. Whether it counts as a single organism or as a few billion clones that agreed, an age ago, to be one animal, is a question no one has answered, because no one has found a seam. The geologists can't help; the valley closes over their incisions before they finish, and so its interior remains its own. No one knows whether there is a floor. The bedrock under the mat is a rumor, and the rumor may go down forever, or the valley may be standing on the world the way you stand on a raft. Its boundary is the scree and the notch of the pass, where the cold defeats it each winter. Its ambition is everything on the other side of that line.

Age is where it stops making sense, and starts making a different kind. Every square inch of it is weeks old, and every shape of it is older than any language you speak. It molts the way you shed skin, except that it sheds everything, always, keeps none of it, and keeps the shape. A standing wave in a river is made of water that arrived this minute; the wave has held its form for miles and years. The valley is that wave, with the whole range for a riverbank. It has no birthday. Its birthday is continuous. It is the oldest young thing you will ever stand on, and the youngest old thing.

What it eats — insofar as *eating* is the word — is light, snowmelt, and the dust that comes over the pass on the south wind. A thin diet, which is why it moves a millimeter at a time. It does not flower, does not seed, does not reproduce. It only continues, and its continuation is indistinguishable from growth. Its dead are not waste. There is no soil here, so its dead cannot become soil; they become its meal instead. The generations dying below the wave are folded down into the deep braid where your arms couldn't reach, and the fungal threads down there do the work of stomachs — the only tenants in the bowl who are not, strictly speaking, the landlord. The valley keeps a kitchen under the table. It consumes itself upward. Everything you walked on has already been eaten once.

As for senses: it has no eyes, and it does not miss them. The filaments that touched your face as you passed are its boundary organ, and they were not brushing you by accident. Touch is how a thing without distance senses.
