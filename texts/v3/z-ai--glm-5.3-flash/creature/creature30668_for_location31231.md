Ask who lives in the Works and the question misfires on the first word. What lives here is closer to the truth, and even that is only a visitor's grammar. But start where anyone starts: with the thrum.

It is the first inhabitant you meet, because it meets you before you arrive — you feel it through the ground, through the soles of your boots, in the fluid of your inner ear. It is below hearing. It is a held note coming up out of the stone, or the stacks, or both, and it does not pause. Everything else that lives here lives inside it the way fish live inside water. When people say the plateau is inhabited, they are halfway to the truth and mostly wrong about the direction: the plateau is not a place with inhabitants. The inhabitants are an organ of the plateau. And so, you will find, are you, briefly.

The grazers come first in numbers. You meet them as motion at the edge of your light — long scattered lines of large, slow bodies moving from tower to tower across the dark warm stone. Their hides are smooth and faintly sheened, oiled-looking, because they sleep pressed against transformer housings and the housings sweat. Their mouths are wide and soft and shaped, over unknown generations, to the radius of a pipe joint; their tongues are long, dark, prehensile at the tip, and they work the mineral bloom off the pipe fittings with a patience that looks almost devotional. The licking feeds them. It also cleans the joints. Their saliva carries something that stops corrosion in its first week of life — whether by their evolution or the machines' design is exactly the kind of question this place refuses to hold still. They calve against the warmest surfaces, in the lea of the biggest housings, and a newborn lies steaming in the machine's exhaled heat like a thing freshly forged. The old ones lead. They follow a map of warmth no eye can see, walking the same routes for years, and the routes wear faint paths in the stone, and the paths funnel the condensation, and the condensation feeds the plants, and no one is in charge of any of it.

Above the grazers, the roost-things. The cooling fans turn slow and vast — one revolution in the time it takes you to drink water and look up twice — and their housings hold ecologies generations deep. The wing-membrane creatures hang inside in soft dark clusters, cartilage-light, warm to the touch like laundry from a dryer. Their wings are shaped by the thermals coming off the exhaust stacks the way a coastline is shaped by a tide: they ride the rising warm air all the long artificial day, folding and banking in slow spirals a kilometer high, and their young learn the thermals before they learn their own wingspans. Some of them have never in their lives landed on anything that was not metal. The oldest roosts sit inside the fan housings themselves, and the young ones learn to step, spoke to spoke, on blades that move at walking speed — surfers on a cliff that turns kindly. At night — at what would be night, if anything here distinguished its nights — after the rain comes and goes, they sing. A soft clicking chorus that synchronizes, without anyone conducting it, with the tick of cooling metal, until the whole plateau sounds like one instrument tuning itself. The rain is the closest thing this world has to a dawn, and the roost-things treat it as one.

Between the towers, in the dark between the constellations of lights, the hunters work. You will probably never see one. You will know them by the shape of the silence they leave — a gap in the clicking, a grazer line closing up like a fist. They are long and low and they hunt by heat and by sound: eyes reduced to a garnish, deep facial pits that read the warm blood-map of the plateau the way the grazers read the warmth map for comfort. They take the drinkers at the condensation channels, where every animal in a kilometer must eventually come and put its face down into the machine's breath. The kills are quiet. Nothing here raises its voice. What hunts, hunts the way the rain falls: briefly, heavily, and then it stops, and the water runs into the ground.

After the hunters, the small fastidious ones come out. Quick little combers, hand-high, many-legged or winged depending on which tower's ruins you watch, and they are — as far as any visitor has ever been able to tell — the maintenance department. They pick at electrical contacts. They eat the first tarnish off copper. They nest in relay boxes with down and lint, and the packing deadens vibration, and the vibration would otherwise, over centuries, work connectors loose. They take warmth, shelter, and water from the machines, and they give back cleanliness. Or they are farmed: kept as kept animals, given housing against the cold dark in exchange for grooming services, and the red and amber lights on the towers are in some way addressed to them. Both readings work. Neither is right. And underneath both readings is the layer that makes the whole argument moot — the thing under the smell of oil and rain and something biological, the biological something itself: a living skin. A mat, a film, a tissue of organisms threading the warm soil, climbing the machine footings, spreading over pipe and plate in coats too thin to see unless the light catches them. It keeps the metal clean. It is why there is no rust, why nothing demands a maintenance crew, why the towers' markings have weathered off but their surfaces have weathered smooth. The grazers lick it and carry it in their mouths to the next tower. The roost-things transport it on their feet like bees carrying pollen between flowers. The maintenance is nobody's job because it is everybody's body.

The plants close the loop, patient and waxy and dark-leaved, rooted in the shadow-lines where the exhaust keeps the air humid. There is no sun here to eat, so they eat the machines' exhalations — feeding, in some chemistry no visitor has fully mapped, on what the stacks give off. The winged ones pollinate them. Their seeds sprout in the roost nests and are carried, stuck to warm membranes, to the next housing, the next fan, the next hundred kilometers of dark.

And flip the lens one last time, because the animals are not the only inhabitants. To a grazer, a transformer housing is a mother — warm, breathing, constant, marked by a light that means itself. To the roost-things, a fan is a cliff that moves kindly. From inside the ecology, the machines are simply the largest animals on the plateau: slow, tireless, warm-blooded in the only sense that matters here, exhaling weather, gestating rain. Some have died — there are cold towers, lights gone out, surfaces gone cool — and even these are not abandoned. The cool ruins have their own tenants, creatures that prefer the cold side of the plateau, that den in dead stacks the way things elsewhere den in caves. The Works keep their dead the way a forest keeps its fallen trees: as habitat.

Do the lights mean anything? The animals behave as if they do. Grazers will not bed beneath a housing whose amber has turned to a certain rhythm of red. The roost-things rise off a stack in a single motion, all of them, the night its pattern changes, and scatter, and later you find the stack venting differently. Warning, or speech, or load-balancing, or nothing — you can spend a lifetime here not deciding, and the plateau does not care which you choose, and the not-caring is itself a kind of answer.

Here is what I will say about a mind. On certain nights — after the heavy rain, or in the deep of a breeding season, when the grazers' lines run long with newborns — the thrum bends. A fraction. The way a held breath adjusts, the way a sleeping body's heartbeat changes when someone enters the room. You can call it load. You can also notice that the plateau's single note changes when its young are born, and that after the rain the whole instrument sounds, for a few hours, tuned.

The people who come to study all this camp against the warm housings, drink from the condensation channels — the living skin filters the water better than any visitor's kit — and fall asleep inside the thrum. They stop keeping notes. Not out of defeat. The questions simply dissolve in the warm dark, the way a sugar cube dissolves: completely, without residue, leaving the water slightly changed.

So: whoever or whatever lives in the Works — the grazers and their warm mothers, the winged congregations, the quiet hunters, the fastidious small crews, the living skin, the plants that eat breath, the cold ruins and their cold-loving tenants, the red and amber constellations and whatever reads them, the held note, and the darkness between the towers, which is inhabited too, by everything that keeps it.

You learn to stop wondering who lives here eventually, the way you stop hearing your own heart. Not because the answer went away. Because you joined it, and it is very old, and it is still running, and it is not finished with anything, least of all you.
