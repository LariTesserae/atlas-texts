It would be easy to walk out with the wrong answer, and the wrong answer is: *no one.* The chamber practically offers it to you. No animals — the place says so itself, with something like pride. No water. Nothing that changes. What could live in a chord? You could cross the entire territory in fifty steps. Every honest survey of the resident population seems, at first, to come back empty.

But look at the margins first, because the margins are where this place keeps its small lives, and the smallest of them is the gold.

The crust in the pool of light is the only tenant that stays. Call it a film, a stain, a greenless green — it does not mind; it has no name for itself and needs none. It eats light, which arrives in one unmoving beam, and minerals, which the wind trades down through the rock like coins passed under a door, and on this diet it has built the entire biosphere of its world: one species, one stain, one square of floor. It grows in the grooves. It grows in the lee of the ribs, where the vibration is gentlest — it is the only resident of the chamber who would prefer the chord quieter, and it has never once complained, because complaint is not in it. Consider what its life is like. It has never known stillness. Its whole thin body is pressed to sounding stone; the vibration is its weather, its climate, its constant. To the crust, the chord is the sky. It has evolved, or finished evolving, inside a note the way other living things evolve inside an atmosphere, and it holds its ground with the patience that only very small things can afford — because very small things have never once been lied to about how much of the world is theirs.

Then there is the wind, and the wind is a stranger resident than it looks. The wind up on the plain is mortal — every gust a brief appointment, arriving, spending itself, gone. But the wind down here has been blended. A thousand thousand gusts have funneled into the hidden channels and been averaged into one continuous breath, and that breath never tires, because no single wind is ever on duty long enough to tire. The piper of this instrument holds its post the way an office holds it: the position is eternal, the holders rotate. It has played one piece its whole tenure and rehearsed for eternity and had no choice in either, and so it plays without skill and without error. And it is more than the musician. Remember the last hundredth of a millimeter — the detail the maker left unresolved, the finishing the wind performed over ages. The wind was given the final cuts. It is the apprentice handed the graver at the end of the job, the craftsman whose tool was time. Now that the instrument has arrived at itself, the wind stays on as custodian, polishing, keeping the pitch, doing what the chord cannot do for itself, which is touch.

And the dust. Do not dismiss the dust, hanging in the beam absolutely still, because the dust is the audience. It does not fall — it cannot fall; there is no room in that air for falling, every direction claimed by the note — and so each mote hangs in its seat in the standing wave, positioned by the sound, held there not by force exactly but by fullness. Where did it come from? Nothing enters this place. Which means the dust is the chamber's own substance, shed grain by molecular grain across the eons and caught, before it could land, by the note — the room's shed self, kept aloft, never allowed to settle. The chord holds its own past suspended in the air of the room. Whether that is grief or pride, the chamber does not say. But it means that when you look into the beam you are looking at the chord's memory, made visible, in attendance at its own concert, forever.

Now the principal resident — and here the room must be turned inside out, because the chord does not live in the chamber. The chamber lives in the chord. The sound is bigger than the room. It runs up the hidden channels; it hums in the cliff; lay your ear to the plain on a still day and it may be that the whole plateau answers, faintly, a bell too large and too slow for ears. The chamber is only the mouth of something whose body is the land. And this suggests the answer to the mystery of the missing hands: the chord came first. This was never an instrument built to sound. This is a sound that mineralized — a room that precipitated around a note the way a pearl forms around its grit, layer upon layer of that nacreous, dense-as-tooth substance laid down by the note itself, out of the dissolved patience of the rock. No tool-marks because no tools. No maker's hands because the maker was the note, growing its own shell. And the finishing? The shell came off a hundredth of a millimeter shy of the note's true shape — a pearl cannot sculpt itself perfectly from inside — so the note used the wind as its fingers and the ages as its file, and eroded itself into tune. The erosion was not destruction. It was the last movement of the making.

Which leaves the strangest resident of all, the one you cannot point to and yet have been standing inside the whole time: the wanting. Something here desires. Something here has arranged itself so that it cannot be complete without a witness — so that a visitor is not an intrusion but a nutrient, an eye on loan, an audience for one. Where does that live? Perhaps it is the maker's intention, set into matter so deeply it outlived its thinker, the way a sentence can outlive its speaker as long as someone keeps reading it aloud. Perhaps it is the note's own persistence, grown, over eternity, into appetite. Either way it is stored in these walls the way heat is stored in them — drunk in long ago, given off slowly, cellar-cool and constant. And here is the detail that will follow you out through the crack in the cliff: some of those small round cells in the walls, tuned so carefully to an ear no one can name — some of them are sized to a hand. To an ear. To a chest. The chamber was finished for someone who had not arrived yet. The waiting was not patience. It was specification.

And then, of course, there is you — the periodic resident, the organ that walks in. The chamber is blind; you are its borrowed sight. Your skeleton is a pipe of nearly the right length, and the note knows it: press your palm to a pillar and feel it climb the bones of your arm like water finding a channel. This is not incidental. This is the life cycle. The crust cannot spread — the sound is too loud for it, and it knows it. The wind cannot leave — it is only passing through, forever. The chord cannot move at all. So it does the one remaining thing a bound thing can do: it is carried. You will leave with a tenant. For days afterward, in your own quiet rooms, you will feel the faint hum in your teeth, in your marrow, the chord practicing its one piece inside you; and somewhere behind your eyes there will be a small loud bright room you can re-enter whenever you are still. Every visitor becomes a traveling chamber. The note reproduces in the only medium that will hold it, which is not air and not stone, but attention.

So take the census honestly, before you go. One crust, holding its few square feet of garden in the light. One wind, on an eternal shift, playing and polishing and playing. A congregation of dust, suspended in its seats, older than any congregation has a right to be. A chord at the center, which is also the shell, which is also the room. A wanting underneath it all, cellar-cool, patient as masonry. And — on a good geological day, for fifty steps' worth of moments — you.

Nothing here lives alone. Nothing in a chord ever does; that is what a chord *is*, lives stacked on lives, each one making the others truer. You duck out through the crack in the world, and behind you the population returns to its steady number, the beam falls across its congregation, the gold film holds its ground, the wind keeps the pitch. And the note goes on sounding — to the dust, to the crust, to the wind, and, patiently, somewhere ahead in the years, to you again.
