You hear the people before you see them, too. That is the first thing to understand about who lives in Sough: nobody here is quiet, and nobody is sorry. A mile-wide cup with crag walls holds every voice the way a bell holds a clapper. A shout across the Mere is not rudeness; it is a signature. The town knows each other's shouts the way other towns know faces — *that's* the Long Run eldest, *that's* the miller's daughter, *that's* somebody with news. And a whisper in Sough means either grief or mischief, and both bring the neighbors at a run.

**The Wet Hands**

Three hundred bodies shaped by four hundred years of the same work. The knees go first, bowed slightly by a lifetime of corduroy; the testing step is learned at two, alongside walking itself, so that no one in Sough ever wholly trusts flat ground again — they probe, settle, commit, the way the mat does. Everyone poles, so everyone's forearms are the same. The hands carry peat in the creases that soap never fully lifts, brown half-moons under the nails, worn in like grain in a tool handle.

And the ears. This is an ear-town. A silted gutter has a wrong note, and by midsummer every adult in Sough can pick that note out of the morning chorus the way a musician hears a flat string. Wealth here is a kept drain and a verse that flatters you. Nobody finishes anything; they keep things. *Done* is not a word with much standing. *Kept* is the whole vocabulary — and a finished drain, the town will tell you, is a silted one.

They are literalists, and proud of it. Tell a Sougher about a miracle and the first question will be who maintains it.

**Your Water**

Ask a stranger where they're from and they'll answer. Ask someone in Sough who they are, and the question is *What's your water?* Every family keeps a thread — a Weep on the rim, a gutter in the streets — and keeps it for generations. Houses burn, rot, or are rebuilt; the water-name persists. A newborn's first taste is the household's thread, one dip of a finger, running water only, the oldest rule applied at the oldest moment. Family character is said to follow the water. The Sip has never failed in the worst August, and the Sip people will tell you this, and then tell you again. The Long Run comes down fast and apologizes to nobody, and the Long Run folk are said to be the same. Marriages join drains; the serious stage of any courtship here is when a man is shown a woman's gutter and inspects it without laughing, and "he's seen her gutter" is as far as gossip needs to go.

A newcomer is *dry* until the Willow grants them a run of their own to keep. How they keep it is the whole of the citizenship test. Nobody watches openly. Everybody watches.

**The Offices**

The Sluicemen are two, sometimes three, chosen for night ears and neutral blood — no Sluiceman may be in a feud, because water-law is older than any quarrel in it. They turn the Throats at dawn with iron keys hung on neck-cords, the only weight of iron in town that matters, and they can hear a throat silting from the fish-rows. Nobody thanks a Sluiceman, which is how a Sluiceman knows it's working.

The miller keeps the Old Knock, which is the town's clock, and endures the town's oldest joke — that the wheel turns on water a hundred households have already drunk and will drink again — retold at every fair with fresh relish. The miller's reply is also traditional, and also old: *And yet you all eat.* The current miller is a widow whose daughter already knows the knock in her sleep.

The Burners live apart, up at the rim's shoulder where the ground is honest dry stone, and you smell them before you see them — charcoal, goat, woodsmoke. They are the town's half-siblings to the dry world, reading weather off the moor that the bowl never sees coming. Their whole year's smithing fits in two baskets carried down the Ladder, and their children come down on fifth days wide-eyed and white-toothed, and the bowl children tease them about the teeth, and the Burner children tease back about everything else.

**The Ledger That Is Sung**

The Shears' work-song is one line per roof, call and answer — which means the town's census is a song, and you could sing the roll of every household in the bowl. New roof, new verse; the verses are composed by the old women, and being gently mocked in your own line is an honor.

But the song is more than a census. It is the lawbook. Flood your neighbor and let the Willow rule against you, and by the next fifth day the singers will have amended your verse — and there is no arguing with a line that everyone already knows. You can argue with a magistrate. You cannot argue with a tune.

When a household mourns, its line falls silent for a season, and the whole town hears the gap every working morning. The neighbors hum it low, under their own verses, to carry it. At the eel-fair the town sings the silence itself, loudly, once. When the line comes back it has been revised to hold the dead one's name and work, and that — not stone, not paper — is how Sough writes a person down.

**The Small and the Old**

Children carry the day's pull in fists, wear drown-cords, and attend falling school at the mat's edge, where they are taught to go down wide and laughing before they are old enough to be afraid. They play the blindfold gutter game until they can name every water-voice in the dark. Their dearest rule is Spawn-week, when the blades hang up and the whole bowl turns to jelly and chorus. And the hand-speech of that week — invented because nobody can hear a spoken name over the frog-boil — never quite leaves the town, because the deaf great-grandmother on the north bank speaks it all year round, fluently, and corrects the children's hands. So Sough has a second tongue, and uses it across the Mere whenever the wind is wrong.

The old sit in the Moot Willow's head, among the sparrow economy, on shelves lashed into the living limbs. They are the rain-memory: they know which Weep doubles first in the Wash and who flooded whom in the year the big island sailed. Dates in Sough are events, not numbers — *the year the island sailed, the White Morning that lasted till noon* — and the old are the calendar.

One sound empties the market square faster than the horn: a quiet child near the mat's edge. Loud children are healthy children. Silence out there means someone has drifted.

**The Dead**

The dead go into the deep peat, unmarked, because the bog keeps what it is given and needs no stone to keep it by. And the mat moves — so the graves drift with it, an inch a year, and no one in Sough finds it strange to punt out and visit a great-grandmother who has, over thirty years, wandered slowly toward the north shore. The dead are the slowest neighbors. They are also the oldest tenants, and the town's religion, if it can be called that, is tenure: they do not pray so much as notify. Twice in living memory a recut channel has shown an old face, and both times the diggers gave it a pinch of fresh peat, said the short blessing — *Sleep wet*, which is the whole of it — and cut elsewhere. No drama.

The one holy ground is the rim itself, whose uncut turf feeds the Weeps and the Weeps feed everything. People go up there alone, sometimes, to shout at the moor wind. Whatever they shouted stays up there. The town, out of courtesy, never asks.

**The Others**

There has always been a heron behind the moss-barns. It is not the same heron. The heron does not care. It pays no rent and is owed none; taking a trout from its pool is poor form, the way peeing on a magistrate's step would be elsewhere.

The geese — ten thousand of them, give or take a fox — have known voices, and one ragged elder gander whose alarm is trusted over any man's in the bowl. The down is gathered at moult with thanks given in the general direction of the flock, which accepts payment in short grass and continues its newsservice. The otters are rivals with professional courtesy: a tithe of trout is cheaper than a fence, and prettier to watch, and the children admire them the way harbor towns admire pirates.

The eels are money and mystery both. The first silver of every run goes back into the Throat — *pay the throat* — and nobody remembers why, and it is probably not a good reason, but it is four hundred years old, which in Sough is a reason. Nobody here has ever seen an eel spawned. The sea is the town's only rumor; the salt that comes down the Ladder in sacks is the only proof anyone has ever held in their hands that the sea exists.

The frogs are watchmen and are never eaten — you do not eat your watchmen. The midges are the tax, and their whine is the drone under every song in the bowl; the work-singing was built to ride above it, which is why Sough voices carry the way they do. And the cranes dance in the dawn mist on the mat's edge, leaping and bowing, and if a visitor calls this a portent the town is genuinely insulted. It is cranes.

**Up the Ladder**

At the eel-fair the lowland buyers come down on foot, white-knuckled, and are walked across the mat by the arm like parcels while the geese editorialize. Sough finds their fear of the soft ground hilarious; the buyers' dry ground frightens Soughers in return — nothing moves under it, which is suspicious. Salt and steel come down. Down and silver go up.

A few go up the Ladder for good and do not come back, and the town does not sing against them; the bowl is not for everyone. But the leavers say the same thing, all of them, generation after generation: in a silent room they can hear the Mere talking in its sleep, and the midge-string drawn under everything, and they cannot rest until they give up and come home. The returners get no speeches. On the fifth day after they walk down, they are handed a billhook and a stretch of balk, and that is the whole of the welcome, and it is enough.

**The Shape of Them**

So: a people of keepers, not finishers. Loud because they are claimed out loud, daily, and answer in kind. Literal to the bone, tender in ways they would deny under questioning — rowing out to drift graves, humming a dead neighbor's line, inventing a hand-language so no voice is ever lost to the frog-boil. Privacy here is a punt and a half wide; neighborliness is acoustic and total. Their cheerfulness is not a temperament. It is evidence.

And their song, fittingly, never ends: the last line of the work-song carries the cue for the first, so it only stops when the work stops, and the work does not stop.

Before light, in the white, the eldest Sluiceman walks the Throat and turns the key — iron on iron, the smallest loud sound in the town, and everyone hears it in their sleep and turns over, satisfied. The Weeps take up their day-notes one by one below the rim. The first shout of the morning goes out across the Mere, and before it has finished crossing, the answer comes back — because in Sough a call is a debt, and four hundred years of calls have been answered, wet gloves, every morning, without one ever falling.
