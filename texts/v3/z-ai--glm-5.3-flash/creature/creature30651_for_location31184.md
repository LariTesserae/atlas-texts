Take the census properly and it takes a year, because some of the residents commute by tide and some by century.

Begin with the keeper, who is the easiest to miss, because there is only ever one, never for long, and always the same. The keeper is not strictly a person. It is one continuous attention, occupying whatever body is currently on the ladder. The body learns the place the way the rungs have learned hands: sleeps by the thud, wakes when the thud changes, eats at slack because that is the hall's noon. Somewhere past the second year, the keeper's pulse and the beam agree to keep time together, and after that the keeper is a resident and not a visitor. The kettle-leavings go to the samphire. The boots descend at the same hour daily, so that the mussels open at the sound of them before any light has reached the rungs. And the ledgers — the books that begin mid-story — are the hall's memory, and the keeper is how it writes. The first keeper wrote as though joining a conversation already in progress, because he was. The entries are instructions from the dead, delivered across centuries in handwriting that changes and a voice that does not: *do not latch the low hatch; feed her on Fridays; the crab again.*

The Warden is not a post passed from body to body. He is a person, the only true individual the hall contains. Older than the oldest ledger — he was already old when the first book began mid-story, and it is possible the first book began mid-story because its author was too busy watching him to know where a story starts. Thick as a forearm, pale-bellied, eyes like wet pebbles, moving with the patience of something that has never once needed to hurry. He comes and goes from a pipe that is sealed, and the keepers decided some centuries ago that they did not want to know how, and their not wanting to know has proved to be correct husbandry. He has never been seen to do anything threatening, and nothing in the hall has ever been seen to disobey him. This is the only kind of authority that lasts: the kind that has never needed testing. When the autumn work-party comes down and the hall is full of strangers' boots and the kettle screams in the dome, everything hides — the shoal goes deep, the crabs go under the slabs — and the Warden comes out. Slowly. To inspect the works.

The cod is an office, like the keeper. There has always been one; there always is. She is vast, and she takes mussel meat from the hand on Fridays, and when a cod dies the ledger gives her a line of genuine grief and a name, and the names are always some keeper's mother. Within a season a new cod arrives off the open sea, is offered mussel, considers it briefly, and accepts — as though shown the terms of a treaty and finding them fair. The current one is Edith. She is perhaps the fourth Edith. The Friday is uninterrupted.

The sprats are not residents exactly; they are the hall's bloodstream, or its weather given scales. In with the flood in a single silver rope, one wheel through the open water — the wheel is why the hall is a hall and not a pipe — then dissolved into the upper dark, and out again on the ebb the way they came. They know the surge before it comes. They always have. The first law of the place, *follow the fish*, is simply their spelling. The open sea keeps its own arrangements, but inside the grate a different set of rules applies, older and more courteous, and though nobody living signed them, nobody living has broken them either.

The crabs are residents in the fullest sense: local, opinionated, regular as bells. Each afternoon they cross the open slabs from the cold west end to the warm plume off the dynamo housing and bank themselves there like coals at a fire — the big channel crab nearest, claws flat on the warm iron; the small ones stacked on the big one; a count was attempted once and abandoned at thirty-one because they would not hold still. And among them, *the* crab: the one who gets into the valve. The ledger's oldest running joke and its most serious entry are the same entry. The beam's note changes by a hair, the keeper is already climbing down before the gauge knows anything, and that evening the book records, in a hand from another century and this one both, *the crab has done it again* — sometimes with the marginal note, *new crab; same news.* The crabs are the hall's proof that the alarm works.

In the silt-trays, the shrimps tick and click like sand on a drum. They are the housekeeping. They eat what the flood brings, keep the managed mud managed, and their sound is the sound of the place being kept when no one is keeping it. The mussels on the ladder are the tide-gauge with manners — open at the flood before the lamps have brightened, before the gauge itself has moved — and they are also the brewers. A thousand small filters ride those rungs; the clarity of the hall, and so downstream the sweetness of the ale, is mussel-work, and the town toasts nightly without knowing whom to toast.

And the marbled fur that took the place of rust is not dead skin. Let the lamp look closely: tube-worms in their chalk threads, a velvet of small feeding things, mats that turn out to be colonies. The rust stopped because life took over the surface. The plaques worn past their names wear gardens now. The hall did not merely erode into its final shape; it was colonized into it. Whatever it was going to become, it became a reef.

Late summer, on lamp-lit nights, come the pilgrims: jellyfish through the grate, slow lamps pulsing among the brass ones, and the shoal rises to the surface to watch them. Note this, because it is the hall's secret. Everything in it watches. Even the residents are an audience.

Which raises the question of the house herself. Resident or residence? The keepers, who are precise people, say both. The Old Lady is the only inhabitant who has never once left; her bearing is the only surface polished by motion instead of hands, and the first act of every descent is a palm laid flat on her housing — the greeting, older than any ledger line. Her stroke is the hall's heartbeat. Her water is its blood. The shoal is its breath, the dome its head, and the keeper the part of it that talks.

So the true resident, in the end, is the arrangement itself — an animal made by people and finished by the sea, whose mouth is the grate and whose throat is the shaft and whose heart walks its slow arc in the east. It took three centuries of careful emptiness to make so much room for life. The emptiness was the hospitality.

There is one more population, upstream. The water goes up to the cistern under the market, to the brewery, the baths, the fountain that runs ten minutes at noon, and every soul in town who drinks the ale or holds a hand under the fountain is, for a moment, holding the hall. The whole town lives here a little, twice a day, without knowing it. The fountain is the hall's only public voice. It says the same thing for ten minutes, and then it stops.

Count them at slack water at night, the only hour the census holds still: one keeper, asleep or nearly; one beam parked at the top of her arc, holding her breath until the tide turns; one Warden, exhaling his small steady cloud; one cod at rest; one shoal, dim, a suggestion of silver; some dozens of crabs banked at the warm plume; one ladder's worth of mussels closed like dark pearls; one drum of shrimps gone quiet; two lamps at their neap dimness; two rows of samphire, growing the way slow things grow.

One of everything that matters, and enough of everything else. The hall has never once stood empty. That is what all that emptiness was for.
