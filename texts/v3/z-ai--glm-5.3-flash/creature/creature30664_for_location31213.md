When the tollers go quiet, every smaller sound on the shelf is suddenly alone. A footstep loses its floor. The wind's long surf on the rim keeps on, but everything that lived underneath it — the tick of cooling stone, the rustle of felt-moss, the low talk of keepers — hangs there unsupported, and the keepers, wherever they are, stop what they are doing and look up, because a quiet this deliberate is not weather. The keepers say: *a silence is walking.*

The tollers go quiet for it first, because the tollers are what it drinks.

The keepers call it the Quiet, and it is the worst weather this world has. It comes up the last stairs low and long, furred in a nap exactly like the plush dry moss, wearing the grove's own softness as a coat, and around it sound thins the way the air does on the stairs. Where it sets its feet the pink gravel gives no report. It is not large. It carries its absence the way cold things carry cold. It has come for the drone — the deep round note the tollers hold rolled out beneath everything, the shelf's water-table — and it drinks that first, unhurried, head lowered toward the ground like a thing at a pool. The tollers' silence is not panic. It is the oldest trick they have: you cannot drink from a dry cistern. They pull the water-table down under themselves and hold it there, pouches slack, throats shut, until the Quiet finds nothing standing. Everything small uses the cover of the hush to fold itself into hollows and wait. And the keepers come out of their caves with the singing stones from the creche and ring the whole rim of the shelf — not to frighten it, because it has no word for fright, only for thirst — but to drown it. You cannot fight the Quiet with quiet. You drown it. A full grove's worth of sound is more than it can swallow, and by the time the blurred coal of the sun has moved its barely-perceptible inch, the Quiet is gorged, heavy, and — for the only hour of its life — audible. It waddles. It belches up stolen noise as it goes: a snatch of surf, half a toller's toll, someone's laughter from weeks ago that no one knew was gone. The children follow it, at a distance, to the head of the stairs, and listen to it make its thick embarrassed way down into the haze. For a day afterward the shelf rings like a struck bowl, and the trees, flooded, put out new ears.

To understand who else lives here, you have to stop looking and start following sound the way you would follow water anywhere else. The wind is the rain. It plays the rim and the black stairs, and the noise runs down the zigzags and spills over the lip and pools on the warm stone, and everything on the shelf arranges itself around where it pools.
