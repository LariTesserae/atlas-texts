Three names, then, for one heap of ground. Each one true in its own court.

The maps say the Rise because the country around it is flat as a griddle — old lakebed, tile-drained, plumb-line fields — and here is a long blue lip of limestone shouldering up out of it, the only thing for miles that could be called a hill without embarrassing anyone. The ledgers say Concession 1 because when the surveyors ran their baseline along its skirt, a hundred and fifty years ago, this was the first parcel the company ever wrote down in this country. Not the first good parcel. The first. The ledger's opening line, the file thick as a Bible now. And the people say the Weep, and they say it quietly, the way you'd speak of a person in the next room who has been crying a long time and has stopped trying to hide it.

To understand the third name you have to understand what the hill is for, which is holding water. The Rise is a jug of pale limestone, and the water has lived in it longer than anything else — longer than the graves, longer than the company, longer than the names. Springs came out of its skirt in the old days; every farm around it drew from a well that tasted of the hill, cold and sweet and faintly mineral, water that had spent twenty years coming down through the stone like a slow thought.

Then the company came back with drill steel. What they wanted was the stone itself — hard, pale, good for lime, good for ballast, good for whatever the century asked of it. They opened the north face in 1913 and cut it wider for forty years, and somewhere in there the teeth of the quarry reached the water table, and the hill was breached. That was the day the Weep began. Not a flood, not a gush — the water didn't leave, it just changed its address. It began coming out of the wall. A hundred seeps, a thousand, filaments of water threading down the pale rock wherever the stone is jointed, a face the size of a church that weeps everywhere at once, and never stops. Not in August. Not in the sleet. The water comes up out of depth at a temperature that has no season, and so the Weep is the one place on the rise that is never fully winter. In hard January the seeps build fluted towers of ice, weep-ice, columns like melted candles, and between the columns the rock stays wet and green with algae, and kids come out to lick the icicles and say it tastes like the inside of a church. In drought — the summer of '88, which the old people still use as a yardstick — everything browned to the horizon except the Weep, which slowed to a wrist-width and kept on. The hill was spending its savings. It did not stop.

The oldest resident is the water. Everyone else lives in its tenancy.

The quarry floor flooded years ago, a green lake the color of bottle glass, and the water leaves the cut by a notch in the lip and becomes a creek — a small, constant, unnervingly cold creek that is the only open water for nine miles in winter. Brook trout hold in it in January, over watercress, in water so clear the fish look hung in air. Deer come down to it in the cold snap, then the coyotes after the deer, then a great horned owl after everything; the mud of the weep-skirt gets stitched with tracks like a page of handwriting. A boy from the foot of the rise fishes it through the thin spot that never freezes shut and considers this his secret, though it is the town's worst-kept one. Turkey vultures roost on the high wall in summer and spread their wings to the morning like the wall's own gargoyles, come alive. Swallows work the pond at first light. Little brown bats slide out of the abandoned workings at dusk in a thread, one animal at a time, stitching the dark together. In the seep marsh below, skunk cabbage blooms in February inside little melted domes it makes for itself — a flower that generates its own heat, that flowers by un-freezing the world an inch at a time. Coltsfoot opens on the spoil heaps like small suns left behind from a smaller solar system. Maidenhair fern hangs out of the cracks in the wet face.

And on the north talus, under the coldest seep, lives a snail the width of a fingernail — a quarter-inch coil, translucent, a relict that came up the glacier's wake and never left. It survives only where the weeping rock breathes out cold, one small animal holding on to the Ice Age by the temperature of the hillside's grief. The textbooks give it a Latin name. The people here give it none. They just don't walk there.

The human residents sort themselves by altitude. On the crown of the Rise sit the Motts, two households that never sold, and their wells still run sweet because their wells tap the top of the jug, above the cut. The company's concession line comes up the fields and stops dead at the Mott fence in a whisker of rotted flagging tape, a number that ends in barbed wire. At the foot of the rise are the company rows — houses numbered once by the company, held on handshake and succession since the payroll thinned to a caretaker and a lawyer in the eighties. They lean toward the cut, those houses, the way people lean toward a radio. The men in them are mostly retired quarrymen who still wake at six because the whistle told their bodies to for thirty years, and the whistle is gone, and the bodies have not been told.

Cecil is the oldest of them. He was a blaster, and he walks the face on mild evenings the way other men read weather, and he can tell you which seep will throw ice tomorrow and which one is "getting thin." Ansel lives in the old scale house inside the gate, technically trespassing, and has for eleven years; he keeps a tally of the phoebes under the eave and feeds nothing but the birds, and the company's agent — the company's only other resident, two afternoons a year, a man with a camera and a clipboard — photographs the face and photographs Ansel, and neither man reports the other, and this is understood as governance.

On the crown, behind the Motts, is the burying ground: the first settlers, and then four quarrymen who share a single date in 1953 — the morning a shoot jumped — and then a rank of children's stones the size of bread loaves. They are the longest-standing residents, and the people know what the maps don't say: that the Rise is named twice, once for the ground and once for what's in it.

Nobody agrees anymore on what the Weep is named for. Some say the face itself, obviously. Some say the wells uphill that went dry the year after the breach, the farms that sold and left because their water moved into the company's pit. Some say it's for the widows of '53, four of them in one autumn. A few of the very old say the name was already there — that spring-skirt ground was always called weeping ground in this county, and the company didn't invent the name, only gave it cause. They agree on the name itself, which is more than can be said for most things.

What's left of the company fits in a locked steel shed: a gate, a sign, a stilling well. There is a gauge on the outflow, a data logger in a box bolted to the rock, and the weeping goes into the ledgers now as a number — Concession 1, discharge, monthly mean. Even the crying gets counted. The people find this funny in the way that isn't funny. And there is a saying, older than most of the residents: *when the hill stops weeping, you'll know why.* Cecil checks the gauge more often than he'd admit to.
