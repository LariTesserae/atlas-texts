They do not bite, or hardly; they have ridden a thermal up from the coast on the one warm hour the bowl permits, and they mate above the sheltered pools, and by dark they are down on the water in windrows, and the lake takes them. That is the food web of this place, entire: sun, salt, seep, film, worm, midge, and the lake eating the sky's leftovers. The town comes out to watch the evening swarm the way other towns watch fireworks, because that is what it is — the most complicated thing this world has managed yet.

**The people are midwives, and most of them admit it.** There are a hundred and forty of them, plus whichever survey crew is down from the windward. Not everyone surveys — someone bakes, someone stitches, someone teaches — but every job here is a form of waiting on water. The charter hangs in the meeting hall where a bell would hang: *nothing green yet.* It is a kind rule, a rule of sequence rather than prohibition, and the sequence comes from below the horizon, from the project that hauled this land up through the Shelf Sea. Stone, then water, then film, then felt, then soil, then green. The Cradle's whole commission is to keep the pages from being turned out of order. Everyone on the mountain is new; only the bowl is gentle; so gentleness has become the bowl's chief export. On rest days the machine crews come down from the windward stations with wind-scoured faces, sit on the shore of the lake saying almost nothing, and climb back into the burn of the wind. They call the bowl folk *sponges*. The bowl folk let them.

**Nothing here has a name that stuck, and the town is proud of that.** The streams are prototypes, feeling their way downhill for the first time, so their names are pencil names: Try One, Shoulder Rill, Little Borrow, and the Maybe, which has changed its course four times and is forgiven. When a stream abandons its channel for a better one, its name is retired into the Dead Streams Register with full honors — *went to ground under the black clay; gave its name to nothing; mourned by its crew* — and the register has become the town's true parish record. People here date their lives by it. *The year Little Borrow died. The year the Maybe found its legs.* The Pencil Office keeps the maps, and keeps the erasers, which wear out first, and keeps one pot of ink, sealed, under a rule everyone can recite: no ink until a watercourse has held one course for a hundred years. Someone keeps the key. No one has used it. The key is polished from handling. And the lowest street on the eastern terrace is left unpaved on purpose — a right-of-way reserved for a river that has not chosen it yet. The houses along it are built cheap and called the borrowed ones, and the family in the last one keeps a room swept that they will never use. The room is for the river. The family is envied rather more than they find comfortable.

**The thin life has its keepers, and the keepers have titles no one is embarrassed by.** The Felt-Watchers set out ruled stakes along the weep-channels and log the fungus films' advance, a finger-width a season, and the season's measurement is read aloud at the winter meeting like a birth. The worm-counter takes handfuls of spring-mouth mud and counts the eyelash worms, and the number is small, and it is going up, and no one has ever counted a larger number of anything on this land. And there is the green ledger, kept by the Greenwarden, in which the count of green things is entered every day. The number is always zero. The zero is entered with care, in full, in the good hand, because a zero that is watched is not nothing — it is the thing the whole charter exists to protect. The Greenwarden keeps, in a gray area of the charter, a brick bearing a smear of dried moss from the old world: green, and long dead, exactly as alive as the frost flowers and the salt blossoms, which is to say not at all, and still the most radical object on the mountain. The dead of the town go into the mudstone beside the lake, and the Greenwarden, who objects to nothing else, does not object to this, and no one says above a whisper what everyone knows: that the burying ground is the only place where the ground is being quietly given what it will someday need. The start of the dirt.

**The children are the first natives.** Nineteen of them so far, born inside the cupped hand. They drank the springs before they could walk, so the land's childhood is dissolved in their teeth. They rank everything by age the way other children rank by size — the lake is younger than the bowl, the streams are younger than the lake, the midges are older than everyone, being coast-hatched — and they are careful in a way no one taught them. When the lake freezes they skate on the youngest ice there has ever been, and they skate carefully not from fear of falling through but from something like courtesy. For a while they believe, sincerely, that rivers are drawn first and then wet. They ask the elders what grass sounds like, and the elders look hard at the felt on the weep-channels and say, *like that, but standing up, and pleased,* and the children file this away as a story — like dragons, but more widely attested.

At night the mountain ticks. The whole mass is still wringing itself out, pore by pore, and the sound rises through the mudstone into the floors of the town — a slow, irregular ticking, like a cooling engine, like a clock that has not decided its hour — and the town sleeps inside it the way a sleeper sleeps inside a heartbeat. On the sills, frost flowers and salt blossoms open side by side through the same cracks. In the Pencil Office the day's survey dries on its lines, provisional, correctable, alive in the way drawings are alive. In the green ledger the day's zero is entered and watched.

Nothing green yet. That is the rule, and under it runs the town's real faith, which no one says aloud because everyone drinks it: that the sequence will hold; that the pages will come; that one day a stream will commit, and someone will reach for the sealed ink; that long after that, something green will come up through the burying ground without breaking any rule at all — and that the children of the children of the bowl will never need to know how carefully this cupped hand once held its few degrees of warmth, its coin, its pencil, its watched zero, its one dead smear of green, while the water learned which way was downhill.
