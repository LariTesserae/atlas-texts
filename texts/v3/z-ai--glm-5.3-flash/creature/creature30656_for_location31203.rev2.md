Begin the census with the room, because in this place the room is on the list. It lives in itself the way a snail lives in its shell, except the snail doesn't secrete the world. Here the residence breathes, sweats, decides when things happen, and is new — new the way a wound is new, born of the blow whose radial scars are still printed in the few facets it hasn't yet covered. So the first fact of residency is that the border between resident and residence is not a line here. It is a negotiation. Everything in this room is becoming something else, slowly, and the census changes while you take it.

The stone was here first, and it has never left. It is simply no longer the majority. It lives on in its sharp-faceted enclaves, surfacing now and then as a grey blink at the edge of vision — a whole facet reverting to mineral for the length of a blink, like a diver coming up once for air before the green closes over it again. The stone is not being eaten. Nothing in this room eats; the room includes. The stone is a previous tenant being worn as a skin, and it does not seem to be struggling. At one edge of the chamber lichen-things are hardening into rock while at the other edge rock is softening into wood, so that the two eldest residents are passing through each other, in no hurry, and neither appears to be trying to win.

Then there is the one that lives here on purpose.

The gardener. A pale grey-green animal the size of a fat cat, soft-skinned, moving on wet feet — it walks the way rain walks, leaving prints that the floor closes over, so that it leaves no track either, only a slightly greener room. Its eyes are like wet pebbles, which is to say they do not blink so much as the light on them moves. Ask its body how old it is and you will get the room's own condition: the eyes are pebbles, and pebbles are patient, and could have been waiting here since the blow that cracked the pocket open; the skin is green, and green is recent, and has not finished settling. The animal is an argument between mineral and garden, and it has not resolved itself, and it does not seem to mind.

It has two hands, and one of them is becoming a knife. A black thorn, finger-length, rooted in the fist — cuttings will root in anything here, even a grip — and the gardener rotates the tool from time to time so it grows straight, the way you would turn a candle so it burns even. Tool and gardener are growing into each other, and neither party appears to regard this as a problem. In some far cycle the gardener will have a blade where a knuckle was, or the thorn will have a hand. The grammar is still being worked out. Everything the gardener holds begins to live: the grinding stone has gone green at the edges, the reed straw is turned daily to keep it from sprouting, and it is a fair guess that the gardener is the only thing in this room that has ever decided something should *not* grow — and even that decision is applied gently, elsewhere, with an angle.

Because the cutting is not killing. Watch the circuit — the whole room, each cycle, thorn in hand — and you will see what the pruning actually is: a suggestion. Every tendril reaching for the middle air is cut and falls, and the floor takes it, drinks it in with the rain, and the plaited root-mattress you would stand on is plaited from exactly this. If you asked what the floor is made of, the honest answer is *refusals*. The gardener has built nothing. The floor is everything it ever decided against, woven into something you can cross in six steps. Ambitious roots are not destroyed here; they are re-aimed. The garden argues about everything else, but about redirection it does not argue at all.

And this is why the gardener matters more than its size suggests: the room cannot keep its own history. Ask the wood its age and the rings disagree — four hundred years, says the grain; three days, says the dust. A pebble lands half a second before it lands. The room is still deciding when things happen. So it has grown itself a witness. The twelve pebbles by the folded-leaf cup are a tally — it has rained twelve times, or the gardener has decided it has, which in this room is the stronger fact — and the gardener is the only thing here that knows which events have occurred. It hears the crack's single tick, a grain a day, and files it. It watches the growing tips lean toward the point on the ceiling where the crack has *not yet* reached, and it prunes accordingly, clearing the middle air for a column of light that has not arrived. Stand in the kept-empty center long enough and a suspicion will grow on you the way moss grows on sleeves: the hollow is not an emptiness. It is a reservation. The gardener is maintaining a landing. It has decided how wide the day will be when it gets here.

Twice a cycle — or once; the schedule is the room's, not its — the gardener kneels at the cup and drinks from the still water through the reed straw, slowly, with pauses, the way you drink something you are also listening to. You will not taste it. But its face afterward does something you have no better word for than *remembering*: a soft far-away movement across the skin, gone in a breath. The water tastes of somewhere else entirely. And then, sometimes, the crack speaks in its other voice — noon-smelling, dry-grass, a whole different world's weather — and everything in the room stops at once, and the gardener goes flat against the wall and flushes its skin to match the green until it is gone. Notice what that means. The same weather it drinks, it hides from. Homesickness and fear, arriving by two different doors, wearing the same smell. The gardener deals with them the only two ways this room allows: by drinking, and by disappearing.

The only other natives are the three moths the color of condensation — three, or three at a time; the count is the gardener's, and the gardener has decided it holds. They drink from the walls and are indistinguishable from beads of water until they move, and during the ceiling's brief hissing shower they are indistinguishable from the rain itself, hanging in it, drinking midair, possibly being it. The gardener does not cut within reach of a drinking moth. The thorn simply stops, held in the air like a note sustained, until the moth is finished.

And once — the record exists — something came down. A long jointed shape, dropping through the crack, hunting the moths in a whir of wrong wings. The fizz of growth dropped to silence; the curtains quit swaying; the gardener was flat and green and nowhere. It went back up before the pebble it dislodged had finished rolling, and the room breathed again, and the gardener unclenched, and the roar resumed. Here is the detail that tells you what residency means in this place: there is a pebble standing apart from the row now, a thumb's width off, uncounted. The history is kept in stones, and the exception was given its own. And whether the hunter took a moth, the tally will not say — only that there were two for a while, and then one morning a bead of water on the wall walked off the wall, and there were three again. The room remakes what it loses. It includes. It does not give things up to things that do not belong.

So the census, as well as it can be taken: one room, alive, new, resident in itself. One stone, first occupant, current guest, surfacing in grey blinks. One gardener, half here — growing into its knife, drinking its homesickness, deciding the room's history for it, pruning the present to keep a road open for the future. Three weathers that learned to fly. And one more resident, the important one, not yet arrived: the kept-empty center, the seat held open at the middle of everything. It is kept for the column of day that has not yet come down the widening crack. It may also be kept for you. It may, some far cycle, be kept for the gardener itself, when fist and thorn have finished becoming one thing and there is nothing left to prune with but its own attention — when it can finally sit down in the clearing it has spent every cycle maintaining, and let the walls come the remaining two steps, and be included.

Nothing here lives in the present tense if it can help it. The tips lean into future light; the tally leaves room for a future rain; the middle of the room waits, swept and tended, for a future guest. When the sun-blade crosses the crack and the whole chamber flashes like the inside of a lamp, the gardener stops mid-cut with the thorn raised, standing still in the light it has been clearing a road for — one breath, two — and then the blade passes, the dusk-glow of the sap resumes, and it goes back to the circuit, cutting away the present to keep the middle open for what is coming, grain by grain, rain by rain.

Visit briefly. It is working.
