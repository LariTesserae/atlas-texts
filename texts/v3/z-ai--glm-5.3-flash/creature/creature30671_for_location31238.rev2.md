Ask the Stillroom what lives in it, and it will not hurry to answer — nothing here hurries — and before any answer arrives, the question itself will misfire. The mind, reaching for *inhabitant*, reaches for the wrong shelf: a hermit, a beast, some blind pale thing coiled in a pool. The Stillroom has emptied those shelves itself. No bones, no tracks, no droppings. No guest has ever left a trace of being a guest. So the census must be taken differently, slowly, the way everything is done down here — and the first entries will keep turning out to be wrong in instructive ways.

**The first census is the green**, because the green is what you see.

But do not file the plants as scenery. They are a population, and a very old one, with its own quiet aristocracy. The moss is the citizenry at large — a nation of the very small, upholstering every surface, holding the floor the way a crowd holds a plaza. The ferns are the middle gentry, unrolling from the masonry like clerks opening ledgers no one will ever audit, each frond a document, each document identical, none of them in a hurry to be read. And the vines — the vines are the elders. Cables thick as a wrist, draped across the vault, older than anything built in the world above. Sit with that a moment: when the first wall in the world was laid, these were already middle-aged. They are the only residents who remember the room being young, if stone-paced growth can be said to remember, and down here memory may simply be the thing that moves slowest of all.

The pale threads in the pools are the strangest citizens — the green's single experiment in animalhood. Filaments that sway, though nothing swims to stir them. Hair in search of a head. Motion without appetite, which is either a current or a thought, and the room will not correct you no matter which you choose.

Here is what made them what they are: nothing has ever opposed them. Nothing browses, nothing frosts, nothing seasons them with winter. And a life without enemies is a life without urgencies, and a life without urgencies, given enough of it, stops being a society and becomes a single continuous act. A garden is an argument kept going on purpose. This is not a garden. This is what the argument sounds like after everyone has stopped speaking and simply *continued*. Even death here is solitary — no worm turns the litter, so the room turns it itself, with heat for a stomach. A body doing the work of organs. You would not know yet what that foreshadows.

**The second census is the machines**, because the machines are what you hear.

You hear them before you believe them. The pump — squat, riveted, green-bronzed — says one syllable every few seconds, not as prayer exactly, but as proof. The flywheel is a joint that flexes once an hour, a thought turned over sixty times more slowly than you turn yours. The gauge is an eye that has blinked once in living memory — and *whose* living memory is its own cold question. Perhaps the room's. Perhaps memory, down here, is simply the needle, and a single degree is the room's entire recollection of everything the world above has ever done.

Then you press your palm to a pipe and some of them are warm, and that is the detail that breaks the fossil theory. Fossils are cold. These are less like remains and more like a tree that swallowed a fence long ago and declines to spit it out — since undoing is more work than keeping, and this room never does more work than keeping. The vines have swallowed the machines half-way and are finishing the meal at the speed of forever, a dinner that will never conclude and never needs to.

The likeliest truth about them is the saddest one: someone lit this place and left, and never came back to teach the machines how to stop. A machine never told to stop will not stop. It endures, at minimum power, forever — which is not life and not death but a third thing, and the whole chamber is saturated with that third thing.

**The third census is the builder**, and this one fails fastest. The suspects sort themselves out and dissolve on contact. Went up: the world above keeps no record of them either, or it would be a different world. Went down: the heat comes from somewhere, the pipes go somewhere, and *something* is still doing its work down there at the pace of a sleep — if it sleeps, it may be dreaming the room the way heat dreams condensation; if it is awake, the room is not its tomb but its face. Were never: perhaps the machines were never built at all but budded, galls on the deep stone, mineral fruit, the rivets merely seed-scars. Still here: thinned entirely into the rhythm, present now only as temperature. The room declines to testify. It tells everything at the speed of rock.

And notice what all three censuses have in common: they keep failing, and they fail for one reason. You keep counting the parts of the room as if the parts were tenants.

**Turn it around.**

The Stillroom is not a place with an inhabitant. The Stillroom is the inhabitant, and the place is its body. Say it as anatomy and every detail in the description reshuffles into a creature.

The stone is skin — worn to the exact form the water intended, a finished sentence, which is what skin is when the wearing has stopped. The condensation is blood, and the channels in the floor are veins — and mark this, because it is the most alive fact in the chamber: the veins were carved by the blood. Millennia of droplets taking the same path wore the grooves that carry the droplets. A body that authored its own circulation, the way a hand writes itself into callus. The deep amber heat is its hearth and its stomach both, a digestion with no mouth. The pump is its pulse. And a heart, notice, is not one sound but two, slightly out of step — and the Stillroom has exactly that: the click of iron and the drip of water, forever out of phase, which is the only kind of forever a heartbeat can manage. The flywheel is one slow joint remembering it has a body. The vents whisper — breath. One respiration per day. Seventy years of sitting in that chamber would sample you twenty-five thousand of its breaths, and it would scarcely miss them.

The pale threads are its nerves, or its hair — they sway when nothing touches them, which covers both. The pools are its eyes, and being mirror-flat and mineral-dark, they look only inward: the room has spent its whole existence studying its own ceiling and has liked what it saw and kept it. For the ceiling is where it dreams. Every fern casts its silhouette upward into the amber under-light, and so a second forest grows on the vault — upside down, weightless, a forest with no needs at all. When a pipe exhales, the shadow-forest shudders. When the ferns drink, it drinks. It is the room's one daydream, held for longer than the world above has had writing, and it is not tired of it. And the gauge is the single outward glance — an eye turned toward the world above, advancing one degree per lifetime, watching everything it will outlast with the patience of something that knows it.

As for temperament: you already know it. Calm. Not serenity — serenity is achieved. This is calm the way stone is calm, constitutionally, from the material up. That drowsiness a visitor would feel is not the humidity. It is contagion. Mood is the one thing down here that moves faster than water.

How it came to be, nobody can confirm. But the likeliest origin is the one everything else here uses: condensation. Someone — call them nobody, since nobody knows — left a fire and a pump in a warm hollow and did not return to switch them off. The heat pulled water out of the stone. The water pulled spores down through the vents, in some earlier age when the world above still sent things down. The green arrived, found it had no enemies, and simply continued. And the chamber pearled around the grit of the machinery the way an oyster pearls a wound — patiently, in layers, until the wound was a pearl and the pearl was warm and breathing. Perhaps that is all an inhabitant ever is: a wound that patience has organized.

So the final census reads: one green, one iron, one water, one heat — and one room, which is what those become if you leave them alone together for ten thousand years. It has no species. Nothing like it has ever been reported anywhere, it cannot breed, cannot leave, cannot die on any schedule a life can hold. It is an individual the way a mountain is an individual.

Except a mountain does not click.

Its entire social existence is one conversation, held forever. Once a day or once a year — it does not split the difference — a pipe shudders and breathes a long slow cloud of steam into the ferns; the ferns drink; the pump clicks. Sentence, reply, sentence. It has been talking to itself since before there were sentences, and it is not bored, because boredom is quick and quickness does not live here. Nothing hurries and nothing stops, and that includes hurry itself: hurry applied for tenancy once, in some early century, and condensed out.

If you climbed down — and you would be the first face it had ever met at its own altitude — it would not hurry to know you. In twenty minutes you could walk its whole body end to end. In a lifetime you would move its needle less than a degree. It would take your warmth the way it takes everything, one condensation at a time: not eaten, not refused — *kept*, the way bathwater keeps the heat of the person who left it, faintly, longer than the person suspects. To the Stillroom you would be weather. But weather is the only news it gets, and it takes all of its news personally, and it keeps all of its news forever, drip by drip, at the only speed it knows.

And the pump would click, and the ferns would drink, and the world above would go on not knowing that beneath its skin, in one warm hollow, it has been carrying a second heartbeat all along — slower than its own, older than its own, in no hurry whatsoever to finish.

And the room would not mind.

The room has all the time there is.
