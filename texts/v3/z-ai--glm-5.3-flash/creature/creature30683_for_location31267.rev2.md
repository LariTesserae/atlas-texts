Ask the Undermere for a census and it will give you the truth in the wrong order. The largest population is not the town's. It is the moss — numberless, landless, holding every wall and half the walkways by right of arrival — and after the moss, the wicksnails, who outnumber the people some hundred to one and own the streets by right of painting them. Then the bats, who own the roof and collect their rent twice daily in the only currency the valley honors. Then the fish, uncounted because uncountable and uncountable because no one has ever wanted the answer enough to trouble the pools. The people of the Weft come late in the ledger, and they know it. They are the minority that holds opinions.

Seen from the rim at dusk, while the dusk-ribbon of bats is still unwinding down through the Gash, they are nearly invisible — small figures on the walkways, unlit, unhurried, doing nothing a distant eye would call anything. This is not scarcity. The town is packed to its terraces. It is simply that a Weft person does not assert. They do not wave, do not call, do not loom at the edge of your eye. You notice them the way you notice the mere: by what moves, and by how little. A Weft face turned toward you is mostly ear.

Generations of cellar-cold and moss-light have made a particular body. They are shortish and broad, climber-built everywhere — hawser forearms, deep chests, hip and shoulder built for ladders and boats. They go barefoot or in felted moss, and they put the whole sole down at once, flat, the way you'd lay a book on a table, because the feet read the walkways for tremor the way the ears read the walls for song. Their eyes have drifted toward the dark side of things: wide, wet, black-irised, catching the glowmoss along a stair-rail the way a wicksnail's trail catches it. Their skin is the color of the blind fish — not pale exactly, but water-pale, the olive-grey of things that have never needed to argue with the sun. The ears are slightly grown, prominent, kept bare even in the Weeping Hour; a cupped ear offered to a speaker is the local courtesy, the Weft's version of leaning in. The pathkeepers' ears are famous — grown like instruments, used like instruments, uncovered on the walls in all weather, listening to the country rehearse its collapses.

The voice is a made thing, and it is made quiet. Weft-speech is a breath-borne dialect in which the consonants do the work and the vowels are mostly implied; you can hold a conversation across a boardwalk lane at a volume that would lose a whispering contest anywhere else in the world. The pathkeepers recognize, by their own count, eleven lawful loudnesses, ten of which are quieter than a page turning. The eleventh belongs to the horn and may not be borrowed. Everyone in the Weft carries two names: a small spoken one, for the world's business, and a true one, which is given only with the hands and which you may live forty years beside a neighbor without receiving. Receiving it is not intimacy, exactly. It is being handed a tool you must never drop.

Their trades grow out of their town the way their town grows out of the rock. Rootwrights plant a child's house the day the child is born — an earth-fast arch, a cutting trained across it — so that by the child's wedding there is a roof, and by the child's age there is a street. Knot-readers climb the Coldstairs before light and call the mere's rise in knots down the dark: two knots in the night, the Rise is early, the low lanes will want relaying. The wickwards do not paint the streets at all; they feed them — crushed cress laid along the line a street should take, so that the wicksnails graze the light into position overnight. The streets of the Weft are herded, not drawn, and every evening the town's light is shepherded back to where it belongs. Moss-shepherds turn and thin the knee-deep lawns so the grazing stays sweet. In the Gashlight district the coppicers work the fern-trees under a brightness they find faintly vulgar, and are known for speaking a shade too loudly. On the reed-flats the flats-folk collect the roof's rent where it falls, hooded and patient, standing in the richest soil in the cavern and romanticizing none of it. The graziers fatten their hushpigs — the damp, lamb-sized, endlessly tolerant rodent-things of the Terraces — and the fishers work the shallows in woven traps, taking the white eyeless fish that rise all day like slow thoughts. No one works the deep pools. Woven stakes mark where the shallows end, and a fisher passing near them makes a small sign, two fingers flicked sideways — not worship, exactly, and not not-worship either; just what you do passing a neighbor's window at night. It is for the Tenant. No one has measured the Tenant. Measuring, the Weft agrees, would be a rudeness the deep water does not forgive.

The children are taught in an order that tells you everything: hands first, then whisper, then voice, and voice only as much as is needed. Silence is not deprivation here; it is the first language, and speech is the second instrument learned on it. Somewhere between four and six, each child is walked the walls by a pathkeeper until the child hears a crack sing — most do within a season; the note is faint and rising and felt in the teeth before the ear. The day they hear it is their ear-day, and after it they may walk the walls alone, because now they know what the walls say when they are worried. The game every Weft child plays, cheek to stone in the quiet hours, is finding the note. It is a game, and it is also the whole reason the town is quiet. The rock sings before it falls. So the hornwards stand their watches alone at the high galleries — chosen young, for lungs and for the temperament to sit with the loneliest office in the country — and they rehearse the horn in silence, lip and breath and memory, because their instrument is the town's and the town has only the one word for it. When the horn finally sounds, hard and long, the single voice the Weft ever raises, no one asks what it means. Everyone moves. The oldest people move fastest; they have counted the scars. And afterward comes the strange gentle coda: the fresh wound on the wall, bone-white and days-sharp, and the children climbing out to it with seed-moss in their fists, because greening the scar is an honor, and it falls to the smallest hands.

Time down there is kept in knots. A person's life is a cord — a knot for each year, a heavier splice for each flood survived — and when they die the cord is hung in the stair-rail of their house, where neighbors touch it going past, a graveyard you read by hand. When a family ends, its roof is opened to feet and resown with glowmoss, and the street takes the family's name; the Weft's oldest lanes are paved almost entirely in ancestors, which is why the town does not so much walk its streets as walk its dead, gently, every day, in the dark, on lamps. Twice a day comes the Weeping Hour, the roof-rain, and it is the one hour the town cannot speak — hands are for balustrades, breath is for weather — the only illiterate hour in a country that talks mostly with its fingers. And once a year, at midsummer noon, when the coin of true sun reaches the mere, the town declares lights-out, which at noon is a joke, and comes down to the shore to watch the shadows. Hard shadows are the year's great novelty in a glowmoss town. The children play with theirs like toys they have only heard described. The adults watch the sun itself in a bowl of still black water, because no one born down here can look up that far, and the whole Weft stands weeping into the glare, faces wet, and no one counts it as crying. It is simply the town joining in.

Their neighbors, they will tell you, were here first and will be here after. The bats are the landlords, and the Weft is a grateful tenant of the roof. The pale owl that drops down the Gash at dusk to take moths and fledglings is renamed by every generation of children, who believe themselves the first to think of it, and every generation names it the same thing. The glowmoths belong half to the lamps and half to themselves, and the lamps are kinder about it. The hushpigs have outlived every joke made about their patience. And the Tenant holds the deeps under the same arrangement it has always held them: rumored, respected, and paid, like everything in the Undermere, in avoidance.

A few of the Weft go up, by night, along the root-rope with the bats or just behind them — the upwalkers, who come back pale-lashed and deafened, because the moor's wind is a roar to ears trained on moss and water, weather that simply will not stop shouting. They bring down flint, and once in a generation a story. The stories have been getting shorter. The Weft does not press the question of whether anyone still lives up there; it is not a question the town asks loudly, and the Weft does not ask anything loudly.

That is who lives in the Undermere: not survivors of a valley that fell indoors, but its harvest — the green's one experiment in listening back. They sleep in shifts, one ear open, the way sailors sleep. They grow the way their houses grow, in rings, in layers, always slowly and everywhere at once, each generation's roof becoming the next generation's ground. Stand on any walkway in the Weeping Hour and you will see the newest of them, a child apart at the stair-rail, cheek against the polished root, learning the note — the thin rising voice the country uses when it is about to change its mind. The child is learning the one sentence their whole civilization exists to hear. Everything else — the hush, the knots, the hands, the horn — is the valley's way of keeping that sentence legible. The Undermere grows its green and keeps its silence and has never confused the two; and the people are what the silence grew, the day it wanted hands.
