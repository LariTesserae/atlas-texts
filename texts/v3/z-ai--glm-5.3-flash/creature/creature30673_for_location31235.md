**The Inhabitant**

The keeper's chair is a deck chair, naval surplus, set where everything can be reached: gauges, valve bank, the appointment-plates, the little kettle that sits on the boiler niche's shoulder. It has been sat in so long that its canvas has taken the shape of one particular body — narrow shoulders, a habit of sitting with one heel hooked on the rail of the sump.

She is the only one who comes down the seventeen steps, and she does not come down often enough for the room's liking. The chamber keeps its appointments whether she watches or not — that was the point of it, that was the commission — but the brass prefers a hand on it. She oils the sun's arm where the rail has begun to squeak on its curve. She cleans the droplet-groove with a rag so the seam-rain falls true, one drop every four seconds, and sometimes she counts the drops out loud, to the sump, to no one, in the voice of someone reading a bedtime ledger. Her breath hangs in the cold. Her boots leave prints in the condensation-film on the floor plates, and the prints fog and vanish, and this is the closest the room comes to remembering her between visits.

She is not its mother and she is not its mechanic. She is somewhere between a wet nurse and a priest. *Weather first, life last*, says the plaque, and she is the one who has been asked to hold the *last* open a little longer — because the instrument that commissioned this chamber, the instrument that ordered a year built and put on rails, wanted to see weather performed before it permitted anything to live in it. She waters the frost-feathers with a paintbrush when the schedule runs lean. She has opinions about the moon's tin face and has, twice, against instructions, tilted the appointment-plate so the drizzle fell harder over the north basin, where the pools hold longest.

Because the pools do hold, and because she held them longer than the schedule strictly allowed, there is now something in the chamber that was not made.

It is not much. It may not be life; it may only be weather that has learned a trick. In the floor basin farthest from the hatch, in the pool that shrinks last, there is a skin — a slick, faintly opalescent film, thinner than breath, that spreads in the damp phases and pulls itself into a bead at the drain when the pumps come. It does this a second or two sooner each cycle. It has learned the rhythm the way you learn a staircase in the dark: not by understanding it, but by trusting it. During the frost it freezes into a lens. During the heat pulse it sublimates back from fog and settles, always, into the same basin, the one under the seam that drips truest. It rides the eleven-minute breath. It keeps the appointments now.

The barograph writes its pulse; the film writes nothing, but it is a writing of the same kind. The chamber, which was built to have weather and defer life, has begun to have a tenant that is neither — a first draft of a creature, single-celled in ambition, that knows this room the way a duck knows a pond it was hatched on.

And the keeper, who was told *life last*, has begun to tell it things. Not words — she is not foolish enough to think it hears words. She lets the drizzle fall a little longer on its basin. She lets the sun swing low and red and warm the water before the moon's quick tin shadow crosses. When the film beads at the drain, she does not let it go down.

She calls it, privately, and only down here where no one can correct her, by a name that is not on any plate. And every few days she stands at the sump rail with her heel hooked on the metal and her breath showing, and she watches a skin of wet in a basin turn toward a lamp on a rail, and she thinks: *weather first.* Then, quieter, because the plaque is behind her and tarnishing: *and last was never the same as never.*

The chamber breathes. The drop falls. One, two, three, four — the film spreads a hair's width in the dark, keeping its appointments, learning to want.
