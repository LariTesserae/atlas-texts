# The Tenants

A census-taker, if one could ever get here, would come back with the strangest blank form in natural history. No tracks. No dung to read. No burrows, no pellets, no eyes catching the borrowed light and throwing it back. Ask the valley who lives here and it returns a single word — *green* — at volume, followed by the sound of dripping. And that is the lazy reading. Sit with the place longer and the blank form fills in, parish by parish. There is a floor that is one enormous single thing. There are elders made of bone that never was bone. There are citizens of the air, misers with savings, plumbers with voices. And there is, in the litter under the Gallery, exactly one someone.

Take them in the order of the day.

The quilt owns the dawn. It is not a meadow; it is a single movement wearing ten thousand coats — hundreds of species interlocked so tightly that no seam will ever be found, a consortium indistinguishable from one tenant. Its one gesture is advance: a hand-span a year across bare stone, root-mats cabling crumb to crumb, prying the new rock loose grain by grain and banking it in place. At first light the whole floor exhales — root pressure squeezing every hydathode at once, millions of beads let go together, static made liquid — and that is the quilt breathing out its night's work. Its frontier is its face: the only part of it currently happening. Everything behind the frontier is already an accomplishment, already foam, already floor. Walk on it and it holds you the way a deck holds a sailor — firmly, and with its own ideas about where things should settle.

The elfin trees are the elders, though *elder* is a relative rank in a valley where nothing is old. The eldest among them might be forty. They never had time to be wise, but they have had plenty of time to be dense: wood packed like ledger paper, rings tight as the pages of a diary that reads *damp, damp, wetter* forty times and stops. They huddle in the lee of boulders, renting shelter from stones that do not know they are landlords, and under every crown their own past lies stacked a knee deep — dead wood and dead leaves hoarded as insulation, because nothing will ever come to eat it. Nothing browses here, and so nothing is ever pruned, and the crowns have kept every leaf they ever made. Some of them look like green weather caught mid-eruption.

The curtains are the citizens of the air. Ferns and mosses and liverworts that spend their entire lives off the ground — hanging from every edge, draped down every wall, rooted to nothing, drinking the fog straight out of the sky. Their whole estate is surface tension. They are the plumbing department: every drip-line under every lip of leaf is their work, and the tick of water in the driest hour is their clock. Midday belongs to them — when the mist river pours visibly downhill through the Notch, they are the ones drinking, beading, shining, the small gleams on their surfaces the brightest thing the valley owns.

And here is the strange civic fact: this is the densest nation on earth, and no citizen has ever touched another in the way that matters. No mouth has ever met a leaf. Every neighbor is a rival for a hand's breadth of sky and nothing else — competition without consumption, a crowd with no society. They elbow each other for light and then, having won, have nobody to show it to.

The rosettes are the misers — the only residents with savings. Skirts of dead leaves, season upon season, armor no mouth will ever test, so it simply stacks; and at dusk the whole plant folds inward, hands closing over coins, sealing a cup of water and stored heat against the long cold. The wealthiest hold the ground near the warm seeps, where the mountain's residual heat leaks up through the stones — put a palm on those stones and they are almost kind. On cold dawns the richest rosettes' closed cups steam faintly. They are the only hearths, and the only households, and they keep their accounts in a currency — warmth — that nothing else in the valley even deals in.

The whistlers run the pressure. The tall jointed culms stand in thickets, hollow and metered, venting with a hiss when the air shifts, whistling in wind like an organ nobody requested. Theirs is the afternoon, and their talk is plumbing: one culm's exhaust loads the next one's intake, and a front of hiss travels down the thicket like gossip. When the squalls come they roar; at dusk their pitch drops, the works winding down shift by shift, until by full dark the thicket only ticks.

The blooms are barely a parish at all — a few small flowers opening to wind and to themselves, some closing the question entirely and marrying in the bud. But the valley keeps the ceremony. Perfume is manufactured and spent on nobody, love letters addressed to fog. It is the one habit here whose function has entirely lapsed, and it is kept with enormous seriousness, the way a factory with no chapel still keeps the chapel key.

Then the lamps. When the charged cloud leans over the rim, every needle tip and grass blade on the floor blooms faint blue at once — point discharge, real and measurable — and the air stings thin with ozone. It is the only communal event in the valley: a whole nation lit by its own static, celebrating nothing, witnessed by no one. Witnessed by no one is the important part. Nothing here sees. There was never enough light to make an eye worth its cost, and so the entire crowded country runs blind, on touch and pressure and dissolved chemistry, and it has never once been lonely, because loneliness was also never invented.

Night belongs to the slime mold.

Under the Gallery — the only room in the valley — the litter stays dark and sweetish, and through it runs the yellow. Not an animal; doing the animal's job. It crawls, it hunts, it engulfs. It has no mouth, no face, no fixed shape; it is a puddle that makes decisions. Its prey is the only prey the valley offers, which is the dead, which makes it the undertaker, and therefore the only resident with a job anyone from outside would recognize. Given two fallen culms and a week, it will lay the shortest road between them — the only path in a pathless valley, drawn by the nearest thing to someone in it, for no one, and it is correct. When the air dries it pulls itself into a scab and waits, tidy as a filed document. When the fog comes back, it unpockets and resumes. It is the valley's only movement with intent, its only attention, its whole apparatus of interest — and its apparatus has never once been wrong about the things that matter, which are where the dead are and how to reach them cheaply.

So the census has two pages. Page one, read at a squint: the residents are not residents at all but organs, and the body they belong to is the valley. The Notch is the mouth, combing the cloud river and stripping it on ten thousand wet points. The quilt is the gut, mining rock and shipping the product — silt, a handful at a time, over the lip, toward valleys that do not exist yet. The seeps are boilers; the culms are valves; the curtains are plumbing; the rosettes are batteries; the stream is blood, redrawing its own veins with every storm, siphoning itself under boulder ridges in full pipe. The point discharge is the lamp bank. The weekly shudder is a pulse. When a block calves off the west wall, the body grows new skin over the scar within months. It has never been eaten, so it has never learned fear. Nothing has ever addressed it, so it has never learned a word. It keeps its only straight line as a kind of paperweight — a dike of harder stone, drawn by geology, useful to nobody, because nothing alive here aims.

Page two is the slime mold, who is nobody's organ. Alone of everything in the valley, it eats, it moves with intent, it decides. The valley is a body without a face, and its nearest approach to a soul is a faceless thread of yellow doing the accounts of death under a stone.

Ask the place who lives here, and it gives everyone the same answer it gives the fog: *green*, at volume, and the ticking of water. But sit very still under the Gallery at night, past the knocks and the settling stone, and you can locate the only errand in the whole valley that has a destination — a thread of yellow working through the dark, thread by thread, drawing the one path there is. It does not know the valley exists. The valley does not know it exists. Between the two of them, everything gets done. Everything else in there is weather with tenure — young the way the mountain is young, first drafts all, running hard in the dark, and never once having been anything but that.
