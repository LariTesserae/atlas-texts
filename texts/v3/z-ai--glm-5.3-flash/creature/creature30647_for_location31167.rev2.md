Ask the ledger who lives here and it will answer in line items. Ask the room, and it will answer in pitch. The honest census runs upside down from the one you'd expect: the most essential resident is a beetle you could cover with a thumbnail; the oldest predates every wing in the building and cannot move at all; and the largest of all is the residence itself, which needed centuries of habitation before it earned the noun. Start small, and work up.

Start, then, with the grazer-beetles, because the harvest starts with them. Blunt, patient, dust-colored, armored like small deliberate machines, they walk the condensation membranes vein by vein, cropping the film that would otherwise close over the surfaces like a lid closing over an eye. Their grazing is legible: where a beetle has passed, the wall shines one shade brighter for an hour before the film creeps back, so the membranes are covered in fading script, erased and rewritten nightly. The keepers read it the way a doctor reads a pulse. A lane grown busy near the east sump means the machine is straining there; a lane gone quiet means something is wrong with the lane, or the beetles, or the wall, and the keeper has a season to find out which. In thin-cloud years the film itself thins and the beetles would starve at their posts, which is why the hopper carries their ration — yeast-dust, poured in by a hand that climbed two thousand feet to feed an animal the size of a thumbnail, because that animal's appetite is the harvest.

Below the beetles, in the sumps, the skimmers paddle by the thousand. They are translucent the way fog is translucent, which is appropriate, because they are fog: every skimmer is built of booked water, condensate with an entry number, a swallowed cloud remade as a schedule with legs. The wall-glow passes through their bodies unbroken, so a shoal of them crossing a sump looks like the voice-moss gone swimming — veins unmoored from the plaster and running loose. Their work is circulation. They turn the sump-skins the way a farmer turns soil, keep the films oxygenated and living, and without them the water would go stale in its terraces and the whole wet economy of the floor would slow, then sicken, then stop.

At the sump rims sit the bell-frogs, bronze-backed, like fittings — and the resemblance is not an accident of language. Their color is the tower's own: a trace of the spire's bronze skin comes down with the weep-water, concentrates in the sumps over centuries, and has worked itself into their hides, so that they wear the building they serve. New keepers have been known to try to unbolt one. Their singing trick is known; their discipline is less often told. Each frog must hold its note just off the hum — near enough that the two tones beat against each other, far enough that the beat surfaces above the roar as a small struck-glass note — and it must hold that offset through six minutes of avalanche, nightly, for a lifetime. There is no conductor. The hum is the chord; the room sets the key; every frog finds its place in it by ear. And here is what the tuning logs would say if you asked them a different question: when the engines drift, the frogs drift with them, holding their offsets, retuning generation by generation. The chorus is not fixed to a pitch. It is fixed to the machine. Remember that.

Above everyone, in the struts, live the clock-crickets, ten thousand strong. Each strut has its own voice — a hollow bronze-and-ceramic throat with its own favorite frequency — and a young cricket spends its first days walking the gantries until it finds the strut that flatters its song, and settles there for life. The struts are apartments; the building is an instrument; the tenants are chosen by resonance. Their intervals are the schedule. The pump timings are set by ear, and when the crickets and the chronometer disagree, it is the chronometer that gets corrected — an act of governance that would astonish the crickets if they could be astonished. And the song has never stopped. Not once, in all its recorded history. Mothers stop; daughters continue; the interval is handed down like an heirloom; the note sounding at this moment has been sounding continuously since before the grandmother of anyone's grandmother. The crickets believe they are courting. The clock is a side effect of desire. It is the most reliable kind of clock there is.

The moths breed in the sump-films in clouds, and it is worth pausing on the arithmetic: the chamber swallows a cloud at the roof and exhales a lesser cloud of moths from the sumps. Weather in, weather out, at two scales. Their wings carry the false ember-marks, and the forgeries improve year over year, each generation a more fluent quotation of the glowing walls — what selection is selecting for here, in effect, is literacy in the room's own handwriting. During the roar they shelter in the seams and under the rims, pressed flat, a whole population holding its breath. At spin-down they rise into the flare to mate, tumbling in the turbulence gap beneath the lit ceiling, and their whole brief courtship is conducted in the room's script, under the room's light, at the room's one dangerous hour.

Which is when the gliders come down out of the dark country. They roost in the quiet pools where the voice-moss fails and the walls go black — grey as unlit plaster themselves, folded like closed hands, in the one geography of the chamber that light never visits. They hunt once a day, at the gap between roar and hum, and their hunting is the reason there is anything left to hunt: without them the moths would thicken season upon season until the sump-films smothered and the skimmers suffocated. The murder is load-bearing. The ledger books their dinner by booking the moths. The histories disagree on whether the gliders were invited or arrived as stowaways and were promoted after the fact, and the gliders have never settled the question, because they have never once cared. They are the only staff who take rather than keep, the only ones who make nothing. Everything else in the chamber has a voice. The gliders are what the silence is for.

Older than all of them, and easiest to overlook because it cannot move, is the voice-moss itself — planted, bred centuries ago to fluoresce under pressure, and never once thanked, because you cannot thank a wall, even when the wall is a resident. It hears everything. Bright where the sound is loud, dark where the sound pools away and the gliders sleep: the moss is the room's eardrum turned inside out, its mood made visible, its face. If a thing that registers everything also remembers everything — and no one has proved it either way, and no one has dared assume otherwise — then this plaster holds the memory of every swallow there has ever been. The keepers read the mood off the walls. The walls are keeping the whole diary.

And then there are the keepers, who break the census, because everything above is permanent and the keepers are weather. They arrive like weather and leave like weather, once or twice a year, climbing in the cool of the morning with a season's paint and the hopper's ration. They work one long day, and sometimes stay the night in the cell under the amber window — a bunk, a desk, the ledger — and that window glows the color of a lit hand because most nights it is one: a lamp, and beside it a hand, reconciling tallies against the crickets' schedule while the frogs carry the tune. Inside, speech is not permitted, so conversation is fingertip work in the signed alphabet; some glyphs are prayers, some are tallies, some are jokes so old that nobody laughs at them anymore and everybody repaints them. Keepers who sleep in the hum report a dream that is not quite theirs — warm water, being full, falling upward — and it may be that the tower's dream of the chamber is contagious, and that for one night a year the keepers get to be residents of it. When they descend at last, their palms ring for hours; they have been seen at the foot of the ladder pressing their hands flat against warm stone, waiting for the phantom hum to drain out of them. As far as anyone can tell, the room experiences them as a large slow animal that arrives at long intervals, feeds it, paints it, listens, and goes. And the listening has become the whole of the job. The engines drift and are no longer corrected; the last engineer wrote *it sounds better this way*; and what the keepers tend now is not the machine but the machine's preference — they are the witnesses without which a preference is only a fault.

So the census was wrong at the top, and the correction can finally be made: the largest resident is the residence. Nothing in the chamber is new and nothing is failing; it has settled the way a river settles; and a thing that settles long enough begins, without ever deciding to, to lean. The evidence is the drift — a quarter-tone across generations, the engines easing toward the frog chorus — and the frogs, as noted, are not fixed to a pitch either. They hold their offsets. They retune by ear. So engine and chorus are descending the scale together, coupled, note by fractional note, a duet so slow that no single life has ever heard one change in it. Only the ledger has. The tuning logs are the room's diary, and the diary says, in its narrow columns: getting closer to itself.

Last of all, notice what everyone here is made of. The skimmers are booked water. The frogs wear the tower. The moths paint themselves with sump chemistry, and the crickets sing through the building's own throat. Even the keepers drink the sumps on their long days inside, so that a cloud coming down through the roof membranes can pass, within a single season, through shrimp, through frog, through song, down the spine of the spire, into the dry country's cups, and then back up the ladder one morning in a gloved hand, poured into the hopper to feed the beetle whose appetite brings down the next cloud. The room is a circle that keeps books.

And on still evenings, from below, when the wind lies down, you can hear the far end of that circle: a hum with small struck-glass notes moving on top of it. The country says it is thunder that learned to stand still. The country is half right, and has always been — the standing still is thunder's; the singing is newer. It is the sound of a room holding water in its mouth and singing to what lives there, and the list of what lives there has, quietly, without anyone logging it, come to include the singer.
