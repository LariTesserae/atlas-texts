# Who Lives on the Floor

The first thing you learn about the people of the Floor is that they are quiet.

It isn't temperament. It's physics. Sound down here travels farther than it has any right to, and everyone has known someone ruined by their own noise — a diver whose eardrums folded under a hammer-strike carried three kilometers through old cold water, a welder who worked beside a pulsing pump valve for a year and now can't hear his daughter at all. So the Floorborn speak small. They speak with their hands, in a sign-language that began as rigging-code and has grown loose and affectionate, full of jokes the safety manuals never intended. When they must use their voices they lean close, mouth to ear, the way lovers do, and this has shaped the whole society's sense of distance. Two people talking face to face are *intimate* down here in a way no surface culture can match. Two people shouting would be obscene. You can hear it from the next canton.

And canton is the right word, because the Floor is a world with almost nobody in it. The charts promise a continent. What's been built so far is the continent's dream of itself — a lattice stretching past every horizon your lamp can insist upon, and the people strung through it like beads on a very long thread. Population density here is measured not per square kilometer but per *structure*: a crew of forty on a deck-raft the size of a stadium, the rest of it echoing. You can walk an hour on a plated deck and pass no one. You will hear them, though. The Floor never stops talking. Somewhere a rivet gun is always working, always three kilometers away and sounding like it's inside your skull, and the Floorborn have learned to sleep through it the way harbor children sleep through gulls.

They are builders, almost all of them, and here is the strange arithmetic of their lives: the project they serve will not be finished for anyone alive. Their great-grandparents drove the first pylons. Their great-grandchildren will drive the last. The Floorborn have made their peace with this in a way that took a few generations to learn how to do, and you can watch the learning in the architecture. The oldest sections are all function, girders and nothing else, built by people who expected to live to see walls. The newer work has *embroidery*. Railings with turned balusters no one will ever lean on. Names of crews cut into truss-plates. A kind of optimism, expressed structurally, by people who know they are laying floorboards for a house in which they will never eat a meal.

There are, of course, the ones who never went up. The floorborn-born — second and third generation, children of the habitat decks — are its own small nation. They are narrow through the shoulders and pale in a way that isn't sickly, just unlit. They cannot be persuaded that the surface is real in their bones, the way surface children can't really believe the sea. They are also, everyone quietly agrees, the finest divers the Works has ever produced. They don't fight the dark. They were raised by it.

Because the Floor has a second population, and it is growing faster than the first.

The skeleton was built to hold a city, but a skeleton in the sea is a reef waiting to happen. Coral is climbing the pylons in slow continents of its own. Anemones crowd the cable-ways like festival garlands. Octopus have moved into the truss junctions — the builders noticed early on that their tools kept being rearranged, and now there are crews who consider the local octopi something between coworkers and minor gods, leaving shellfish offerings at junctions where the work has been going badly. Wolffish den under the deck-plates. Lanternfish herd the falling snow into drifting flocks. The biologists who came down from the surface write increasingly excited papers about *framework-as-reef* and the Floorborn file these under weather. The structure is becoming an animal. Everyone who lives there can feel it — the Frame is warmer than the water around it, it has a pulse of pumps and tides, and the newest generation of children can't quite say where the building ends and the world begins.

The old-timers are their own weather. You meet them out on the unsurveyed reaches: salvage divers gone strange from decades of nitrogen, engineers who came down for one contract forty years ago because their marriage ended and the Frame asked nothing of them but the next weld. Some of them have lived below so long that their spines have uncoiled, their voices have gone to whispers, and they navigate by the sound of the Works the way you navigate a house with your eyes closed — this ringing means the north cable-ways, that tick means a pump running rough, that silence over there means the unlit zone, and you don't go into the unlit zone.

And out past everyone — past the farthest lit girder, where the Frame dissolves into intention and the snow falls onto nothing — there are the ones even the Floorborn speak of carefully. The surveyors. The crews who work the leading edge, where the continent is still only a rumor of itself. They go out in pairs, with lamps and line and years of silence, and they come back thin and sunken-eyed and full of a kind of certainty that unsettles people. They'll tell you, if you buy the oxygen, that they've heard it out there: the sound of rivet guns where no one has built yet. Working ahead. Working toward something.

The Floorborn don't argue. The Frame was planned as a continent, and continents have a way of knowing how big they're meant to be.

You hear it before you see it. Everyone does. Out here, that's how everything arrives.
