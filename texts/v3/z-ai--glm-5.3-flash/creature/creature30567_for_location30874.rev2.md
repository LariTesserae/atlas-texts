Take a census here and you have to count the way the mountain counts, which is not by bodies. The local unit of residence is the pause. Count what can hold still, and for how long, and what it holds still *for*, and you will have everyone.

Begin with the oldest resident, which is neither the mountain nor the storm.

The note lives in the great hollows under the plateau, the way marrow lives in bone. It was set ringing when the range was young and tall; no ear was present for the strike, and the mountain has kept the pitch and mislaid the blow. Of everyone on the summit, it is the only one that never moves, never reorganizes, never once changes key. The storm plays the flutes hour by hour and shifts like a sleeper turning over, but the fundamental is the one note it is not permitted to touch, and you can hear the whole arrangement in that fact: a player, and the single note it is forbidden to change. Children down on the plain hold it in their molars and grow up assuming every place in the world hums. They are standing at the far edge of the territory and don't know it.

The storm is the next-oldest, and the least settled. It arrived after the stone was already old, and it has never finished arriving. Somewhere in the deep time the two of them grew into each other — stone and storm — and the roar is the body they share; but the storm remains a tenant in the fullest sense, migrating uphill generation by generation, its former addresses legible in the dead flutes of the lower fields: an organ gone silent in the sun, pipes aimed at weather that moved uphill and left. That staircase of retired instruments is the closest thing the summit has to a family tree. Each storm inherits it from the last. Its habits are the habits of something dreaming hard: it turns over hourly; it forgets songs and learns them back; it kills one garden in a season and opens a fresh pool of quiet behind a spire the next; it walks its thunder around the amphitheaters in long loops, seven returns to a clap, the last one so late it feels less like echo than like remembering. And once a day, deep in the night, it does the one thing nothing else here can do. It eases. The flutes fall out one by one, the bass drops below its floor, the torn cap drifts open over ordinary stars, and for the length of a held breath you can hear drips striking their basins from one side of the plateau to the other. One breath a day, taken through the mouth of the sky. A slow creature. Slow creatures keep.

Now the inversion that everything green hangs on: the gardens are not exceptions to the storm. They are its shadow. Where the storm's body piles against a shoulder of rock, it casts a lee the way a lamp casts a shadow — the loud thing making the quiet — and into that cast stillness the green comes. Silence is a substance on this summit. Green is what it looks like pooled.

The fog itself is less a resident than a courier: through-passing the stone teeth at speed, paying a toll of condensation at every ledge, and the whole population lives on the toll.

The moss is the census-taker's best witness, because it grows nowhere but in quiet. Every pocket of it is a survey of some lee, accurate to the wind's own degree; walk a garden and you are walking a map of the storm's shape, drawn in the one color patience has. The ferns unroll in the overhang drip and ask for nothing further. The orchid-things hold their stones with the grip of old people on railings — they arrived, they fastened on, and then declined, with great dignity, to do anything else for a very long time. And the cloud-trees are the archivists: bent permanently away from the roar, a flinch held for nine hundred years, with the calendar kept in their grain — fat rings for the calm decades, thin for the storm years. If anything on this summit remembers who cut the stair, it is these; and they keep the memory the way the stone keeps the weather's addresses. Structurally. Involuntarily. Without once being asked.

The animals are punctuation, and each has found the smallest habitable unit of the mountain and moved in. In one seep lives one amphibian, skin clear as wet glass, heartbeat visible, its entire biography ten meters long. It holds the fundamental through its belly against the stone, which means it knows the mountain the way the plain's children do — not as sound but as bone — and in the nightly pause, within earshot of its seep, the loudest living thing is its heart. The fog-midges live in a smaller unit still: not a place but a time. Their habitat is the pause. They hatch into the held breath, hang above the gardens as slow smoke, and dissolve when the roar returns — a whole species whose address is on the calendar rather than on the ground. The summit's one law of tenancy says everything lives downwind of something taller; the midges obey it more strangely than anyone, living downstream of a held breath.

And once a day, usually inside a pause, the bird. It crosses the entire plateau without a single wingbeat, riding the standing waves — the storm's skeleton, made briefly rideable — and is gone in a minute, crying once. The cry is the flutes' note an octave up, which makes it the only resident who answers the fundamental: call below, reply above, the whole storm between them accompanying. The keepers say the mountain taught it. The bird would say it taught the mountain. The truth about duets is that both claims come out the same size.

Then there are the residents that were never alive. In a place where silence is a substance, sound does not die; it settles. A word said at dusk is still faintly present at midnight, thinned like a puddle, and the keepers know which hollows hold longest and treat them the way other people treat wells — which is what they are: places where what was poured in stays poured. Some hollows hold old words. The keepers visit them the way other people visit graves, not to hear anything new, but because hearing is not the point. And under the plateau is the deep hollow where a dropped stone is still falling, acoustically — a single moment being spent very slowly, longer than anyone has kept count. No one sows words into that one. What it already holds is enough.

Then the whoever. This is the count that fails: zero, or one, or two, depending on the season and whom you believe. But the failure is in the method, because bodies are not what the mountain counts. Their signature is a stone set point-up — *I will return* — so the true register of the summit is a scatter of upright stones, each one an intention, and intentions weather more slowly than lichen. Counted that way, the population is never zero. It is a set of unfinished promises: some kept within the season, some kept by a child's child, some the lichen will eventually rule on.

All their works are one work. They do not farm water; water is the harvest. They farm the stillness that water grows in. A fog-wall is a stone's throw of quiet widened against a spire, and behind it the green answers within a season, punctual as a debt. A drip-line is patience twisted into cord, and the basins beneath have accepted the only speed water travels here — some have been filling so long they have grown moss of their own, tools gone native, naturalized. The stair they climb is dished like worn spoons, which is the shape of an absence held for longer than names last. The plateau is waymarked in cairns spaced a pause apart, distance itself surveyed in units of silence, as if here it were measured in breaths — which, on this summit, it arguably is.

Their speech is rationed accordingly. On the bare rock, hand-sign; in the pockets, words, but few, and sown rather than spent, dropped into certain hollows at certain hours, because here a word once said stays said. Their language has distilled into one elegant fact: the raised palm that greets you is the raised palm that asks for quiet. You cannot arrive without requesting stillness. You cannot request stillness without admitting that someone has come. Every hello is a hush; every hush is a hello; and this tells you exactly what its people believe arrival is.

They keep one word only, for the mountain, for the storm, and for singing. It has no plural, which is correct. Used as a verb it means *to hold a note longer than you meant to*. Visitors hear three meanings. The keepers, for whom words are expensive, would say there is one, and that the confusion belongs to places where mountain, storm, and song arrive separately.

In the nightly pause, if a keeper is up there, this is what a keeper does: stands out in the open under the plainest light on the summit — stars — and listens to two drip-lines ticking at each other across the dark, their own and one farther off, fainter. Tick, and tick. It is as close as this mountain comes to conversation, and it is arguably why the pause exists. Whether the far line has a tender of its own, or is the same tender's second line, is one of the questions the quiet is for.

Who mends the nets, then. Who set the fresh stone on the cairn below the south stair — bare, unlichened, unclaimed? The valley denies them; the basins are tended; both facts have coexisted a long time, which is its own kind of marriage. Perhaps a family. Perhaps a role, handed along the way the stair was handed along: cutter unknown, word inherited. Perhaps always exactly one, and the two sets of tracks in the moss are one person going and coming back. The mountain keeps every address the weather has ever had, and has never once volunteered theirs.

So widen the census, if you want it true. Two days down on the plain, the roar leaves your ears but not your bones, and for a week you ring faintly, a little less each night — for that week, you are one of the things that lives here, a temporary habitat walking around with the note in its teeth. The village children are permanent ones; every generation, a few of them climb, and the stair does not ask names. The honest total, then, is not zero, or one, or two. It is: one mountain holding a note with no audible beginning; one storm that will not end; green pooled in every shadow the storm casts; a glass-skinned amphibian with its heart on show; a species that lives inside an interval; one bird on one circuit; a few words, still thinning, in the wells; upright stones at various stages of weathering, various stages of kept; and — some nights, unproven, somewhere above the cloud line — a palm raised against the dark, which means hello, which means quiet, and which, in air that keeps whatever it is given, has always meant a third thing as well:

*I am still here.*
