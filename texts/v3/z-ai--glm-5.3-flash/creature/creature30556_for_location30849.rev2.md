Ask who lives in the Seedbed, and the honest answer has a strange grammar: everyone here is the first of their line. Not the first of their species — most of these species are ancient, older than any coastline now above water — but the first of their line in this water. Genealogy in Basin Nine is a short book. The deepest-rooted family, the shrimp's, runs a few dozen generations, and its roots already grip poured stone. Nobody in this sea has a grandmother in the old sense. Everything that lives here is living without ancestors, and it shows, and it is the whole story.

Begin with the smallest residents, because they are the ones nobody planned. The designers stocked the water and planted the kelp, but nothing in the specifications covered rust, and rust is where the first unplanned ecology has started. Iron-eating bacteria fur the bolt-lines in orange felt, grazing on the corrosion itself, a food web made of decay. Barnacles found the cable runs within the first years — arrived as larvae no one ordered, riding the intake currents — and cemented themselves down with the serene confidence of animals who have never been evicted. They do not know the ground is younger than they are. They treat the machinery as rock that hums, which is the only correct category available to them, since nothing in this sea has a category for machinery. The machines are new stone. Everything is either stone, food, or somebody else, and the barnacles have settled on the stone. In doing this — merely this — they are achieving, one shell at a time, the entire object of the project: the conversion of the made into the grown.

The shrimp live in the seams. Every hundred meters the panels part to take the thermal strain, and every crack is now an apartment block of snapping shrimp, so that the joints of the manufactured world have become the hearing margin of a living one. The crackle is the sound of the floor. It runs continuously under everything, a static of tiny pistol-shots, dense enough to be a texture rather than a noise — and it is also the sea's nervous system. When a predator crosses a seam, the crackle hiccups. When the pump-tide leans in, the crackle sharpens. Hydrophone records of Basin Nine are, underneath the engine-chord, mostly the sound of shrimp announcing the state of the world to each other, and they have been announcing it, without interruption, for the entire history of this ocean.

The snails arrive as weather. Their hordes gather out of nowhere along the algae films and mow, a front of thousands of rasping mouths that sounds on the hydrophones like very fine sand poured on a drum. Behind the front, bare stone and glyph-trails; ahead, fresh film; and the front returns like a scythe on a schedule of its own appetite. They are erasing the place. Where the grazing is heaviest, the mold-marks of the original pouring are already worn away — life scrubbing the fingerprints off the clay faster than any current. The snail populations boom and crash on a two-year rhythm that corresponds to nothing, least of all restraint, and after every crash the crabs come to the fallout, because that is the other half of the crab's profession.

The crabs are the diggers, and the digging tells you what it is to live here. Every burrow in this sea ends at the floor of the world. A crab digs, and at ten centimeters the world says no, and the crab — who has no word for bedrock, who has never needed one in the whole history of crabs — digs sideways. The result is a burrow architecture found nowhere else on the planet: wide, low, vaulted chambers riding just under the skin of the silt, roofs propped where they can be propped. And the propping is where the tradition begins. Some of the crabs fit chips of barnacle shell into their vault ceilings — first as buttress, then as liner — and the habit is spreading crab to crab, neighborhood by neighborhood, which is to say a culture one behavior wide is assembling itself in the dark. The shell-lined burrows also held best through the first winter, when the pumps slowed and the dim ration came down and everything with a hole sealed itself in; the lined vaults rode out the reversal-tide's drag while the bare ones washed shut. Nobody taught this. The winter was a setting on a console, and the crabs answered it with masonry.

The plants came in rows, and are leaving them. A third of the planted kelp has gone to stubble under snail-grazing; the rest took, and under a light that never fully goes out, took extravagantly. With no true night, some of it grows past any natural measure — thin-bladed giants swaying not to a moon-tide but to pump-breath. The kelp canopy is responsible for the first shadows ever cast in this basin. Think about that: in a sea lit from masts, the only darkness is the darkness made by living things, and the fish have noticed. They school under the canopy, using shade the way their ancestors used caves, because shade is the only structure here that wasn't fabricated. The holdfasts grip the expansion joints and the pour-marks, and where they grip, the crisp corners of the world are going soft. The kelp is the leading edge of the place's aging.

The fish are the abundance, and the abundance is improvisation. The mile-wide bait-balls turn as one body — schooling is instinct, the one inheritance they carry — but everything else is learned fresh, fish by fish, in a sea where no grandmother ever showed anyone anything. Their migrations sprawl. They mass on the dusk seams between the lamp-glows and run them like roads, because in a basin with no night, dimness is cover, and the flaws in the lighting have become the fauna's first geography. Some runs are magnificent. Some are doomed: shoals that follow a plume into water too warm, runs that end at a dark mast, stalled, circling a column of missing light as if the sea had promised something and forgotten to pay. They spawn against the lamp masts — the warm boundary layer around the housings is the only incubator this world offers, and the fixtures take the eggs the way a reef would. An entire generation of several species is being raised on the body of the infrastructure. The sea's children live in the walls of its heart.

The predators are learning the geometry faster than the prey. Young sharks patrol the panel joints now, because prey funnels along the seams, and the seams are the first ambush terrain this ocean has ever contained — its first tactics, invented this decade. Nothing has learned restraint; a kill is followed by kills, surplus, wasteful, the throttle of an ecosystem with no brake shoes yet. And the predators have no fear of the works, because fear of machines is inherited, and there is nothing to inherit. The maintenance crawler hauls its small dawn along the causeway, dragging food out of the cracks, and the young sharks come to it, shadowing the lights — each of them having worked this out individually, in its own single year, because there was no mother here to teach it. When the inspection submersible runs its lights down a cable run, the fish gather to watch. The sub has an audience. Nothing in this sea has ever been taught to be afraid of a light.

The squid are the ones who learned the schedule. Squid live a year, which in Basin Nine means there have been thirty of their generations, and thirty is enough to begin making locals of them — already slightly smaller, warmer-tolerant, drifting into a form that belongs to this water and no other. Endemism is starting here first, among the short-lived. And the squid are the first commuters: an hour before the pumps reverse, the water over the diffusers thickens with them, hanging in the shimmer-walls like kites waiting for wind, because they have understood — body-deep, in a way no long-lived animal has had time to — that the tide of this sea is a timetable. They ride the reversal up. They have been seen answering the pings of the steward buoys, which always precede the nutrient lines. The first clock in this ocean is being read by squid.

The eels are the ones still looking for the way. Seeded into a dark with no roads, they cross it the hard way — following the dusk seams, probing the trench mouths, some circling the whole basin and dying of the trip. But their larvae drift where the water drifts, and the water goes where the schedule says, and so the route the next generation takes is the route the circulation engines draw. The pumps are the grandmothers. Somewhere in the consoles, the first true eel-road is being written in current, and one day it will be an ancient route — ancient for exactly as long as it takes everyone to forget it was ever authored.

And then there is the jellyfish. Alone, pale, crossing a space the size of a country, it pulses through the lit water without reference to any of this. It does not know the year. It cannot hold the year. Between this sea and the abyss at the world's beginning there is no difference a jelly could feel — a drift is a drift, a dim glow is a glow. Of all the residents, it is the only one for whom the Seedbed's youth is invisible, which may be another way of saying it is the only one that was ever truly at home here.

The people complete the census, barely. A pinger, a crawler crew, a submersible small as a firefly on the plain — and in the flanks of the built ridges, the pressure bells, where a few humans at a time stand in pockets of true air and hear the sea only as pressure on the walls. They are the thin presence, year thirty of a plan of centuries, and their job is mostly to let it happen: watch the winter come down as a setting, log the thinning, run the masts, touch nothing they are not told to touch. Gardeners under instruction not to garden. But they hold something no animal here can hold — memory. The oldest of them remembers hydrophones that heard nothing but the engine-chord, a basin with no crackle in it, no fish-moan, a sea that could not yet speak. She was there for the silence. For everything else that lives here, the crackle was always already there; only she carries the before. Which means that inside the bells, human to human, over shift-change, the only teaching of inherited knowledge in this entire sea is taking place. The only grandmothers in Basin Nine, so far, are the pumps and the people.

That is the census, and its shared condition. Nothing here inherited anything; everything is improvised — the burrow propped with shell, the seam-side ambush, the timetable kept by squid, the road being written in eel-drift. The traditions of this place are one behavior wide and widening. The barnacles are making the cable runs into ground; the kelp is eating the ruler-straight rows; the snails are scrubbing the pour-marks off creation; the burrows are learning to turn before they strike the world's floor. Ask who lives in the Seedbed in another hundred years, and the grammar of the answer will have changed — not pioneers, then, but natives, lineages deep enough to have forgotten they were ever planted.

But that is later. Now the oldest family in the sea is a few dozen generations tall, rooted in poured stone; now a crab fits a chip of barnacle shell into the roof of her vault, the first interior in this world; and the pumps turn the water over underneath everything, patient as weather, keeping the light on while everything in it learns its first word.
