The first inhabitant of the Underhalls is the drone, and the drone is not ambience — it is census. Every note in it is somebody. Ten thousand throats, ten million wingbeats, and beneath them all a bass made of footfall and chest-drum, all of it caught and held by hollowed rock that was shaped, ages ago, to hold sound the way a bowl holds water. To ask what lives in Verge is to ask what lives in that sound. The answer is a chord, and the chord has sections.

Begin with the highest voices. The flocks that wheel in the upper vaults — pale, eyeless, sized anywhere from a fist to a hawk — do not so much fly as displace, the way fish displace water. Their wings are membranes stretched on ribs of cartilage, translucent at the edges, veined with a faint cold light. They have no eyes to lose; deep-time took them early and repurposed the skull. A flock-bird's face is a resonance chamber, the bell of a living instrument, and when it calls you see the call: the whole throat-lantern flares dimly blue-green with each note. They drink fog. Their wings are shallow funnels, and at the pressure nulls of the great drone — the places where the standing waves cancel and the air goes strangely still — they tilt, hold, and drink the bead of condensate that gathers there. This is the whole drinking culture of the upper city: fly to the null, tilt, swallow.

The nulls are also where the danger lives, and the flocks know this the way sailors know reefs. Their circuits route around acoustic holes with a precision no living bird can account for, because no living bird needs to. A circuit is inherited the way a riverbed is inherited. A fledgling hatches into a loop that is already old, joins it, and never leaves; the loop outlives every member it has ever had. Some circuits complete in nine days. Some take ninety years. From the plaza floor, watching a single flock pass overhead and return, you are watching something with the patience of an orbit wearing the bodies of mayflies.

Below the flocks, the drone has registers, and the registers have jobs. This is the secret machinery of the dry miracle: the sound is the waterworks. Ten thousand voices, staged in the right vaults at the right heights, stir the rock's slow exhalation — the deep humid breath that seeps eternally from warm stone into cold — and hold it suspended at the nulls until it thickens into fog, beads on spore-dust, and falls. In certain halls it rains. Slowly, silently, once a generation, everything drinks at once, and the pools in the sunken plaza refill, and the moss ramps shine. The builders built for water once — the aqueduct ribbon overhead proves it — and the aqueduct has water again, though no river feeds it. The water is made of voices. Whatever people the builders were, their heirs inherited the hydraulics and became the pump. And when a flock dies — roost-chimney collapse, plague, an age of bad nulls — its register falls silent, and within a century the seeps beneath it falter, and the moss browns in a wedge below that part of the vault. In Verge, mourning is local drought.

The bass section walks. The grazers — the treaders — are big as cottages, moth-fuzzed, milk-pale, their backs so thoroughly colonized by moss and young fern that you may mistake one for a ruin until it exhales. They browse the velvet ramps at a pace that makes geology look restless; a treader may patrol the perimeter of a single district for four hundred years, drinking dew off its own coat, cropping fungus shelves, paying for the garden in the richest currency it has: its dung is the soil the plaza groves stand in. The velvet is pasture because the treaders pass. Their footfalls are part of the drone — a pedal note you feel in your sternum before you hear it — and their navigation is acoustic memory. When a wall comes down, once an age, the echo of the avenues changes, and the herds reroute as if reading the news. They are the city's most faithful residents: they keep the doorways open by wearing them, keep the streets floor-smooth by walking them. When a treader finally lies down, it does so in an avenue, and becomes a hill ahead of schedule. The city is settling into a hill. The treaders are the hill arriving one animal at a time.

Between the stones, the small ones work. The dust of Verge is not inert. Gleaners — shelled, jointed, glitter-pale — process the old mortar paste, the plaster dust, the calcite powder of falling walls, and their castings are the black sweet soil in which the parchment ferns root. Their trails follow the ancient street plan so faithfully, and their glow-moss borders burn so steadily, that the dead city's map is still legible — redrawn constantly, in faint blue-green light, by the traffic of things the size of your thumb. They carry the builders in their bodies. Wall-mineral becomes shell; shell becomes dust; dust becomes wall again. The walls are walking, a grain at a time.

Even the flora are residents rather than scenery. The great ferns spilling from the colonnade tops are parchment-colored because they are layered like parchment — generations of frond pressed on generation, a single clone on a broken colonnade possibly older than any treader alive. The shelf fungi are not just light sources; their grooved undersides are dew-chimneys, channeling condensate down to their own bases, self-irrigating. The garden is also plumbing. Everything here drinks the rock, and everything here, in its turn, helps the rock give.

And the predators. They do not chase; nothing in Verge chases. The hushes — that is the only honest name for them, because they register first as absence — live in the nulls, at the reef-edges of the circuits. One hangs in a colonnade two galleries from the plaza: a folded wet-sheet shape, breathing perhaps once an hour, acoustically invisible because its body simply swallows sound. A hole in a passing flock is not a gap. It is a mouth. The hush does not pursue its food; it chooses a coordinate where, in forty years, something will be, and hangs there until the schedule delivers. Nothing hurries, not even hunger.

Now the harder question — *whoever*. Whether anything remains in Verge with hands. The evidence is thin and grainy. There are dry-stacked stones at certain seeps, placed with a patience treaders don't have and gleaners don't need, redirecting water with no mortar and no waste. There is a doorway off the sunken plaza worn smooth only at hand-height. And somewhere in the deep galleries, faintly, there is sometimes the smell of cured hide, with no carcass to explain it. If anyone is left, they are not what they were. Perhaps the builders' descendants forgot language, and fire, and their own names — forgetting being survivable — but kept the one thing that was not survivable to lose: the low song that steadies the seeps. Ritual repeated ten thousand years becomes instinct, and instinct is memory that no longer needs a rememberer. A people become a register. Or perhaps there is no one, and the lowest note in the drone — the one below the flocks, below the treaders, felt in the molars, with no visible source — is only the rock breathing, or water moving in the rock, and Verge does not distinguish between those answers. It accepts both the same way: slowly, permanently, without surprise.

What lives here, in the end, is the holding of a chord. The city is not decaying; it is resolving, and its residents are the tempo of the resolution — the sections that keep the sound — and therefore the water — alive while the architecture relaxes into its final form. In ten thousand years, some registers will have dropped out. In fifty thousand, the roar will be a hum, and the hum will be mostly footfall, and the footfall will slow, and the last audible thing in the Underhalls will be that one deepest note, which is either the last throat or the stone itself, and by then even the stone may not know which.

But that is a long way off, and the plaza is cool, and the light is steady, and above you the ribbon of the aqueduct carries, at last and forever, its cargo of made rain. Stand there long enough and your own pulse will find the nearest empty seat in the register. This is not menace. It is only how Verge works, at the speed of stone: it makes inhabitants of everything it accepts, and it has accepted you. The chord holds. Everything that lives here lives in the holding.
