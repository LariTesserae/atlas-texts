Who lives in the room? Ask it that and you will get the room's answer, which is issued at room-time and is therefore easy to mistake for silence. But the census is not empty. Counted the way the room counts — in drops, in laps, in lightings — the population comes to roughly this: one animal, one garden, one water, one keeper, a drop that arrives each season and stays forever, and a light that has called eleven times and left something behind on every visit. The numbers hold still for about a sentence and then begin to drift, because everything in the room is engaged in the same slow work: becoming one thing. One is the number the room is growing toward. It would be wrong to call that lonely.

Begin with the animal, the only resident you would recognize as alive without instruction.

It is pale the way a thing is pale when color was never part of the plan — not white, exactly; unpainted. Its eyes, if it has them, are the idea of eyes, the way a knot in a plank is the idea of a branch. It does not see the room and does not need to. The room tells it where everything is, pressure passed along the weave like a word down a long table, and so the animal lives inside a constant narration. It is never lost. It is never alone in the sense that its every stroke is announced to the walls and received by them. Whether that amounts to company is a question the room declines to hurry.

Its work is the middle. It browses the newest growth at the center of the room, and the clearing it makes is where it lives — paid, in other words, in the very emptiness its labor creates. Without it the garden would have knotted shut already; the animal is the reason the room is still a room, a hollow with weather and a moon, rather than a solid. The plants lean toward it all year and are not permitted to arrive. If the garden has a desire, that is its shape: a slow lean, renewed forever, never granted, and the never-granting is somehow what keeps everything supple.

Its calendar has one entry. A drop falls; a lap is made. At the pace of the weave a lap is not exercise but a sentence the room says to itself about its own middle. And eleven times now — the room has been lit eleven times, and the animal has risen all eleven times — it has hung in the center of the room, pale as the beam, the room's moon, while the walls brightened from graphite to gray-green and the crystal pockets came out one by one like a sky being unpacked. What eleven repetitions teach a creature that has never once hurried, the room knows and has not said. But it rises. It has always risen.

It is not dying, exactly. Each year the weave adds a leaf's width and each year its lap is shorter by a stroke, and those are the same fact. The animal is being spent into the room's closing the way a warmth is spent into the roots, and when the center finally shuts, its last lap will be one stroke, then one pressure, then one more thing the room keeps. Since the room loses nothing, this is a kind of survival. Since the room is becoming a seed, it may even be a kind of meaning.

Then the garden, which is the room's body and, by any honest census, the room.

Its elders were old before the valley drowned — fist-sized, patient things hanging in the cool of the spring-house back when the room was merely dim rather than dark, back when the door was a door. The garden is older than the grass the fence posts used to hold; the sealing of the roof was not its birth but its invitation, and it answered the way it answers everything, by continuing, a little fatter, a little more single. Its leaves have given up being leaves; they are all vein and no blade, surfaces shaped for touch instead of for sun, and a hand can travel them for an hour and find no edge — only growth, more growth, and then, rarely, the young glass scars of the break, which the roots politely decline. The plants hear with their whole bodies. Sound crosses them root to root and finishes without echoing, because nothing here bounces; a noise in this room is not returned, it is received.

And months after a polleny drop, somewhere in the dark, a single flower opens. Think about that flower. There is no pollinator. The animal is blind; the ceiling is stone; the bees are two ceilings and a flood away. The flower is the garden's oldest habit, an etiquette kept from the days of wind and insects, performed once a season or so, into the dark, for nobody — or for the animal, or for the memory of an audience. It smells of hay and warm stone and the dry world on the far side of the slab. It is the closest thing the room has to a voice, and the room spends it sparingly.

The garden also keeps the books. The floor-weave is its ledger: a heel-fall that takes a full minute to land, then another; the ghost weight of a shelf in one corner, where the stems still grow a neat rectangular skirt around a thing that is no longer there; a latch's click arriving some winters apart, deep in the weave, a syllable whose meaning has distilled away and left only the saying. Dead growth is not removed here; it is incorporated. A stem that dies becomes root becomes floor. Death, in this room, is a promotion into furniture.

Which brings the census to the keeper.

Someone kept a spring there once: drew from the pool, reached to the shelf, closed the latch, walked the three paces by four enough times to press a life into the weave. The keeper is not gone. The keeper is slow. The past in this room does not fade; it waits and is occasionally spent — so the keeper's whole afterward is being paid out at room-time, a life that took decades now taking centuries, each footstep a minute in the landing, each coming-home a tick some winters apart. Kneel and lay your palm flat and you may meet the keeper mid-arrival, a heel-fall surfacing under your hand on its long way down. Of warmth, of light, of company, those footsteps are the nearest thing the room has kept — which makes the keeper the nearest thing the room has to a person, and the room is in no hurry to be done with them.

Then the water, which is the oldest resident in the only way anything here is old: by never having left.

It is the same water that pooled in the spring-house when the door was a door. The flood rose around it and it did not consent; the boundary stood up at the gap like a sheet of difference, and inside that line the water has kept every pressure it has ever received — the keeper's hand, the latch, the storm's long face at the crack, the fall of each drop. It is the room's ledger, its hearing, and its blood at once; choose whichever noun you prefer and the water will slowly grow into it, since that is its nature. It accepts exactly one immigrant per season, through the hairline crack, past the knuckle-long stalactite that is the longest thing the room has ever made — the whole of its stonework, its one monument. The drop lives briefly: a season at the crack, a forearm's fall. Then it lives forever, because the room loses nothing. Every drop that ever fell is still here, in the weave, in the walls, in the hay-scent of the flower. The population rises by one, briefly and permanently, each season. If the room has a name, the drop speaks it — one syllable, and the silence afterward is the rest of the word — and the residents, who have never once heard the whole word, live inside it anyway.

Warmth works the same way. Nothing in the room has ever been warm; warmth exists there only as an import. The threshold taxes every visitor politely and hands the proceeds to the roots — a hand through the gap, a held breath, the sun's slow winter beam — and each is spent over years, root to root, a meal down a very long table. So the light is not exactly a guest, and neither are you, if you have ever put your hand through the crack and felt the line where the young water ends. Residents are accumulated here in units too small to notice. The census never decreases. It only gains in finer denominations.

And then there is the question of the room itself, which the census cannot leave out, because the room is what all the other residents are becoming. It eats — one drop a season, warmth at the threshold, light eleven times so far. It hears. It counts. It keeps its arch of ribbed root and pebble, which no one can assign to hand or to growth; its fused shell-bell, which rings once per lighting; its circle of pebbles at the drip point, laid by current, animal, or devotion — and it keeps the ambiguity the way a guest keeps a coat. In here, to make and to grow have stopped being different verbs, and the room is where the difference went to dissolve.

It is ripening. What is done, here, is called a seed — so do not picture a container with life in it. Picture a life that contains a place, the way a seed contains a forest folded small: the garden packed in, the water held, the animal's movement stored as pressure, the keeper's rhythm wound into the weave, everything gathered a leaf's width per year toward the day the center closes and the drop, arriving, finds no water to fall into and hangs instead at the crack, and the two waters touch with nothing between them. Under the valley floor there are other slabs, other pockets, other rooms at earlier pages of the same sentence. The flood was not an ending. It was a sowing, and the seasons are the only husbandry the valley gets.

Count once more, then, and let the numbers rest as long as they can: one animal keeping the middle open; one garden keeping the walls and the books; one water keeping everything; one keeper, slow, still arriving; one drop, seasonal and permanent; one light, eleven times; and the room, gathering them all into a single thing that grows more single every year. The room does not keep crowds and does not keep loneliness. It keeps one, the way a seed does. And once a season, somewhere down in the cool, a drop falls, and the room hears it for a long time — and for the length of that hearing, everyone who lives there, animal and garden and water and keeper and seed-to-come, is doing one thing together. Down here, that is what company means. It takes all season.
