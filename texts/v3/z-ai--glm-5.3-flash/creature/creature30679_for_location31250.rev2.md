## The folk of the Weft

The first person you meet in the Weft is a voice. Before light, while the mist still holds the fen down like a lid, someone kneels at the crest, lifts the hurdle lid off the Cup, and puts a bare arm into the dark water to the shoulder. The Reading-stake stands in there, notched at each hand's depth, and the crier reads it by fingertip — dry notches above the waterline, wet ones below, counted cold and blind — because the level does not wait for daylight and neither does she. Then she comes out of the Round and walks the spine, and the call goes out over the dripping town: *Coming up. Two notches.* There is no bell here. The crier stands where a bell would stand, and she is better than a bell: a bell only rings the hour; the crier tells the town what the hour wants of it. The present one was born in the second town and has read the stake a thousand mornings, never the same water twice, and she says this is the whole of her skill — to be not surprised, and not late. Everyone else in the Weft is built the same way.

The first faces belong to the hood-keepers, out on the seep-faces as soon as the call is done, stitching the banks that have begun to slump overnight. They work in pairs, one kneeling with the woven mat pressed flat to the weep, one driving sharpened stakes with an alder maul, the strokes counted aloud in a voice too low to carry: a stake goes in on the count, not on the temper. They are dressed in the town — gray daub dried on the forearms, aprons and over-shoulders of rust-red eel-leather tanned at the two rusting mouths, cloaks plaited of reed the color of the flats. Their bodies are the town's work made flesh: pull-shoulders from hauling bundles under the walkways, kneeling calluses from the mouths, fingertips hardened into little readers for finding a wet line or a soft place under a board. They do not run. They do not jump. They put their heels down last, the way they drive piles — only as deep as needed. If one of them means to take your hand she dries it on moss first, because to hand a person water is to hand them weight, and weight is not given here; weight is fed to the ground. A guest is taught three things the first evening: walk light, dry your hand before you give it, and give back a hand of moss. The rest you learn by ear. Nobody in the Weft speaks above the drip-work. The only raised voice is the crier's, once a breath, on purpose.

Ask who lives in a house and they will not begin with blood. They will begin with the mouth — one to a family, hooded in flat stones, wadded with moss, a pot set in the drip-hollow, tended like a cow — and they will know it by its drip the way you know a voice. The short reedy names they give each other are for loving; the clockwork names are for finding. A child of the Fish-racks, a child of the Bent Lip, a child of the Two-Beat, a child of the Long Fall. Children learn to say their own house's drip with their mouths before almost anything else, and can go home across the whole spine blind. When two people marry they are not given a house; they are given the making of a voice. The Round grants leave to cut piles — nine at most, and a house that stands on fewer is quietly proud — the elders read the ground, and the couple cuts and hoods and wads and sets its pot, and the town listens through a turning, and when the elders can pick the new drip out of all the others with their eyes shut, the marriage is called made. Two mouths run rust and belong to no family; they belong to the tanner of eel-leather and the painter of doorposts, and the children are let one taste of them on purpose, so that no one ever sneaks a drink. After each rain the gullies come back changed, and sometimes a mouth changes with them. A family whose drip goes silent is renamed, in a small ceremony nobody enjoys. The old name is kept anyway, the way you keep a grandmother's basket.

The day hangs from the call. On *Coming up* the eel-men go down the rim with cord of plaited eel-skin and set traps for the unseen plenty rising out of the deep fen, and the first eel of a breath goes round the spine to be touched by everyone, crier last. On *Slack. Holding* the children go out white-handed onto the pans for shrimp, the peat face is opened, the walkway boards go up on chocks like boats hauled out of weather. On *Coming down* it is the builders' and burners' days: hurdles mended, pots fired on the slack's dry bones, the ground fed at evening, everyone hauling reed and brush to pull under the walkways until the walking surface holds. And on the full the dead go out — hurdle rafts past the rim, a stone at the breast, when the water can carry farthest and keep what it takes. What the fire will take of a person is burned first; what the water may keep is given; the family wades back without looking. For a breath or two afterward their own drip sounds slower to them. Then it does not. At each turning the Round counts the town aloud — fires, souls, children tall enough for the pans, and the kept, whose number is not said, being known to the water — sixty-one fires, by the last count. A sprig of Old Sister goes to the peak, laid in the little cradle the two copper pins hold up there. The pins themselves are touched only when the Round is rebuilt. In a hard wind someone says, *the pins are holding,* and that is the whole of what anyone says about them.

The children learn five things before they learn anything else, and after the five, everything past them is manners. Their first trick is drips — blindfold on the spine, finding doors by mouth. Their first wealth is frost parings, lifted whole from the pans and carried like coins until they go to water in the palm. Their first work is the shrimp: out onto the milk-flat pans at the slack, taking up the blind cold things that are born in the dark under the bar and come up only when the water lets them. The children go out into the light to bring up what was born in the dark, and nobody thinks this remarkable, because it is the town's whole business performed in miniature. Their heights go onto the doorposts in red ochre twice a year, when the skeins pass over, whether the skeins land or not. It is noted what breath a child is born on — rise, slack, or sink — and the saying is that rise-borns come up easy and sink-borns hold what they are given; the elders do not confirm this, and are careful not to correct it either. And on slack afternoons the children sit with the old ones, one to one, and each takes a single story to carry. The old ones are honest about urgency. *The river leaving is warm in only two heads,* they say. *Take it while it is warm.*

Why the water breathes is not settled and does not need to be. The criers say the water keeps its own calendar; the old ones say the far river pulls it like a rope through the ground; the children say the plain is breathing. All three get said in one evening at one fire, because here an explanation is a tool and not a possession — you use the one that fits the work in your hands. The one thing they truly argue about is the river's temper. Some of the grandmothers say it left angry — that the monstrous winter was a shove, and the fen it abandoned is a slammed door. Others say it was only tired, and that what stood up out of its old bed is the bed itself, rising to be aired. The argument is a generation old, will not be settled, and does not need to be: both parties stake their banks against the same slumps. But when a pile or a cistern brings the earlier town up — a door with its hanging still stiff, a hearthstone the color of old apple skin — somebody must be able to tell it: whose, and what it did. A kept thing with a teller is a grandmother. A kept thing without one is laid on the rim for a day in case the telling turns up, and at dark it goes to the fire, because a kept thing without a story is just weight.

Every old woman who knows which of her granddaughters has her hands weaves one basket slowly, saying the girl's name into the weft as she goes, and when it is finished it goes down under the floor at the edge of the keeping line, and she gets on with her days. In some grandchild's year a pile or a cistern will bring it up — whole, stiff, the pattern sharp — and the girl will know two things at once: that it is hers, and that she was known before she was born. Nothing in the Weft is thought finer, and this is why they are so careful up here. What you put under your feet is what you will be when you come up. People choose accordingly: a comb, a whistle, a child's first pot, a knife gone thin with use. And they know their depth, all of them, the way they know their own hands. The crier reads the town's depth off the stake; you read your own off the ground beneath your house — where the keeping line runs, what the hole does at the hour past it, what it takes with it when it fills. Nobody here is afraid of what lives under the floor. They put it there themselves, on purpose, and they know the depth of it. Everyone in the Weft is patient the way a filling hole is patient: no temper, all counting.

The others who live here are few, and are read like everything else. The otter takes its tithe from the traps at dusk and is not resented; it is also consulted, because an otter that stops working its usual line knows something is wrong under the bar before anyone else does, and the eel-men would rather lose the tithe than the gauge. The leeches keep to the wash-places and take their fee like everybody. The gray heron-thing comes at the turnings and is read like weather: standing on the rim at the rise-turning means a wet breath coming; standing out in the pans at the slack-turning means a long slack; going over high without stopping means nothing at all, and the town goes back to work relieved, as if a door had been shut softly somewhere. The one red dragonfly is new every warm season and old every telling — the town insists it is the same one, and the town is young enough to insist. For one warm week the frogs sing, and that is the loud week, the only one: shouting forgiven, singing permitted after dark, the elders sitting out to let the frogs cover whatever they say. Then it ends, and the quiet rules come back on, because the quiet is the town's hearing. Twice a year the skeins go over, far up, and do not land. The children wave. Some of the adults wave too, and are not teased, quite. *Too young,* say the elders — meaning the ground. What nobody says aloud is that the whole town is waiting to be old: the day a skein comes down and stays, the Weft will have arrived, and the first nest on the first new alder after Old Sister will be a feast. No one alive can actually imagine it. That is what the baskets are for.

So, who lives in the Weft: sixty-one fires' worth of first people — every one of them a witness or the grandchild of a witness, every one of them an ancestor in training — keeping a town the way you keep a cow and a clock and a ledger at once. Their virtues are shapes water takes. Lightness, because weight goes down. Return, because take from a mouth, give back a hand. Memory, because nothing here rots; it waits. Measure, because everything has a depth, and knowing yours is not fear but the local kind of patience. And last, the counting itself, which they would not call a virtue at all — only what you do when you live inside a thing that counts.

At night they sleep, and the folk are in the clock with everything else. The drips go on slower. The timber ticks as it swells. The ground takes down its hair's breadth, an eel noses through a stake-gap, and the people's breathing joins the drip-work, the whole town talking in its sleep, and most of what it says is counting. Toward dawn the crier's arm goes into the dark of the Cup, the new notch is found by fingertip, and the call goes out, and the town answers the only way it knows how — drip by drip, door by door, each with its own true voice. As far as anyone has ever been able to tell, the water counts them back.
