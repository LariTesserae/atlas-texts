# Who Lives on the Toll

The census of the Toll begins, embarrassingly for its visitors, with the shag.

It is easy to mistake the felt for scenery, because scenery does not move. But the shag is the principal resident here — by area, by bulk, by appetite, the majority population of the whole plateau — and everything with legs or wings or wheels is a laborer in its fields. It drinks the mountain the way the wethers drink it: warm water comes up carrying dissolved gas and metal, and the felt builds itself out of the breath, a fingernail's width at a time, patient as anything. It is not one organism either. It is a parliament of chemistries — manganese-black fells where the water runs cold off the deep rock, rust seams where the iron comes up sour, whole yellow counties of sulfur that sting the nose after rain, and the violet courts that hold session only on the undersides of overhangs, in shadow, in secret. Botanists from the lowlands have worn out three lifetimes trying to decide whether any given patch of it is one thing or a million, and the locals have stopped waiting for an answer. On the Toll, a field does not have to be alive to be farmed, but it helps.

What every resident shares — shag, wether, wasp, human — is the pulse. The two-year breath of the water table is the only calendar anyone keeps, and you can read the date off almost any animal here the way you would read rings off a tree somewhere else. On the Rise, the sieve-mouths climb the irrigation channels in black ropes, a week when every stream runs dark and quivering, going up to colonize pools the water is about to open. The moth-roads migrate upslope behind them. The wether circuits lift, terrace by terrace, following the wet. Reservoirs fill, the wheels run fat, and this is when the city has its children, its weddings, its roof-raisings — everything that wants surplus. On the Fall, everything concentrates. Pools shrink and the stillwalkers fatten, standing over the last shallows like tax collectors. The rationing ledgers come out. The plateau's voice drops from a seethe to a murmur, and everyone leans closer to hear it.

The wethers are the ones you will remember. They are not graceful and they have never needed to be. An old wether is a walking landscape: its hide grows its own coat of shag, a private terrace on four feet, with its own small population of groomers and nesters riding the plates. The calves learn the circuit before they learn anything else, stepping into slots worn into the stone by ten thousand generations of their own family, and the roads they have made are the most honest architecture on the mountain — thirty feet wide, polished, appearing in the historical record of nobody, because nobody made them. Slowness has right-of-way here, and the wethers enforce it by never once having hurried. When a mill is unlucky enough to sit on a crossing, the wheels idle, sometimes for an hour, and the millers use the time the way everyone on the Toll uses time: by waiting well.

They die walking, too, or near enough. The herd does not stop; it cannot afford to; the circuit is the inheritance and the inheritance does not pause. And it does not need to pause, because of what arrives next.

The graveflies deserve their own paragraph, and they will take it, because they took the carcass already — while you were reading this. They are the most disciplined residents on the mountain. They find a dying thing before the dying is finished, with a patience that looks like tenderness and is only scheduling, and by the following day there is nothing to mourn over. This is why the Toll never stinks, and it is the fact that undoes visitors more thoroughly than the tolling does: in all that crushing density of meat, you will never see a dead thing rest. Nothing rots into earth here — there is no earth — so the dead go somewhere else instead: through the flies, into the crust. Bone becomes mineral deposit; a carcass becomes a patch of pale crust the color of old chalk; the herds walk over their ancestors without breaking stride. An old wether-road is partly paved with wethers. The locals know which stretches these are and do not say so, and the road does not care, and the shag grows over everything the same as ever.

The wet world is crowded past sense. A spring-pool in high season is a single seething animal made of a hundred thousand sieve-mouths, black and blind and straining, and the harvest crews take them by the panful, and the fish take them by the mouthful, and the stillwalkers take them one at a time, without visible motion, having stood on two folded legs for so long that moss would grow on them if anything here were moss. The stillwalkers court by contest of stillness. Two birds face each other in a shallows and do not move, sometimes for days, until one of them breaks — blink, shift, twitch — and the other one is chosen. Visitors swear no decision was made. The birds know differently.

The air is roaded too. The burrow-walls breathe at dawn: ten thousand nest-holes exhaling warm air at once, and the exhalation condenses into a fog that hangs on the cliff face until sunrise takes it, and the midges breed in the warm outflow, and the gliders ride the seam above it all day without landing, touching ground perhaps once a season to drink off a cistern rim. The bellcranes cross above in their staged altitudes, calling relay to relay in that bent-brass voice, and the city bands their chicks and the cranes permit it with the air of aristocrats being measured for portraits. And at dusk, when a bloom glows green ahead of it, a moth-road opens — a river of pale wings a mile long, flowing from crust to crust — and on those nights Stairfall dims its lamps, because the moths navigate by light, and a city that shines too brightly on a spore night will bend the road through its own rooftops and spend a week sweeping drifts of spent wings from its gutters. The city dims. The road stays straight. Everyone behaves.

The city, in fact, is a resident like any other, and mostly it has the decency to admit it. The potter-colonies hold title to their towers — the oldest nests in the granary eaves have actual deeds, drawn up by notaries, because a century of wasps is worth more to a granary than a century of owners — and the human quarters sweat and accrete and fuse like everything else, and the graveyard is a set of open ledges high on the western terraces where the dead are given to the flies, and a year later the families come back for the pale crust and carry it down to seed into the shag-ranches. The mountain's crop, fed by the mountain's people. Stairfall is one guild among many, and it is the only one that ends up inside everyone else's harvest eventually. Nobody minds. That is what a chain is.

And then there are the Keepers, and here the census goes quiet.

What is known is short. They are enormous, gray, plated, and plated like the springstone itself — banded, layered, warm to the touch at midnight, as if they had been standing in a sun that set two miles down. They stand at the major springheads. They do not graze, and no one has seen one eat, though the offerings — mash, salt-pan leavings, a pan of sieve-mouth roe set on the stone shelf each dawn by whichever family keeps that head — are always gone by noon, and no one has seen anyone take them. Where a Keeper stands, the shag grows true and the water runs clean. Where Keepers were driven out, in the greedier centuries, the springs sulked and silted until they were begged back, and they came back, and no one has tried since. Once in a generation a Keeper will decide to change springheads, and the walk takes days, and the whole mountain rearranges itself around it — herds pausing on the roads, wheels idling, cranes holding their altitudes — and for years afterward the shag grows true along the path it took, a road of a different kind, written in chemistry instead of wear.

Children press their ears to a Keeper's flank expecting the toll and are disappointed, because the sound does not travel through skin. It travels through the floor. At night the Keepers call to one another through the bedrock, and you hear it in your ribs first and your ears second, and on the Fall the calls run longer, and on the night the tide turns, the whole plateau answers — drips, wheels, herds shifting in their sleep — like an instrument deciding to change key.

No one has ever found a Keeper calf. The theories are all guesses. The wisest locals will tell you only that the water comes from somewhere, and goes somewhere, and that the covenant does not require you to know which — only to take it as it rises, and never pierce the floor, and leave them their ground.

The Sunken Quarter is what the covenant is written about. Go to its rim on a still night, when the Keepers are calling, and you will hear the drowned square answer — the same deep note, a half-beat late, coming up from under the warm lake where the white blind things seethe when the water is fed. No Keeper has ever once answered it back.

That is the full census, or as much of one as anyone has managed. The truth is the count cannot be completed from outside; the Toll does not sort into a list, because the list is one thing wearing ten thousand bodies — the water rising, the felt drinking it, the flesh walking the felt, the flies turning the flesh back to stone, the stone holding the water, patient as anything, going up and coming down.

The place is named for a sound most visitors take for an echo, or a bell, or a geological accident. It is none of those. Stand on the rim at dusk, put your hand flat on the warm rock, and listen with your ribs:

It is the residents, calling the roll.
