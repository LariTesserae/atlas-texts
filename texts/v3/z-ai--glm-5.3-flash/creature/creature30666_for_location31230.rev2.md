# Who Lives in the Molt Fields

Descend through the cleft, past the scratched arrow, and the first thing that lives here — before any single creature, before the colony even — is the *hum*. It lives in the walls. It lives in the molt-floor, a meter of compressed ancestors, and it lives in your teeth. It is the oldest resident, and everything else moved in around it.

The colonists themselves resist counting. A dozen species, fifty — but the longer you watch, the more you suspect the categories blur. They have been fused so long, packed so tight, exchanging shells and minerals and the slow rust-chemistry of their blood, that they may be less a collection of species than a single organism wearing a hundred thousand bodies. Call them the congregation. Each one pale, chitinous, fist-sized to forearm-length, and each one *occupied*. That is the remarkable thing. Not one of them is idle. Density is their covenant, and the covenant's first clause is: everyone has a task.

Watch the crank-turners longest. These are the ones who found the human machinery and adopted it. They perch on the riveted plate, forelimbs fitted into the crank-handles as if the handles had been made for them — and perhaps, by now, they were. They turn. All day, in shifts you can't map, they turn, and the mechanisms tick through their dead gearing, moving nothing, pumping nothing, carrying nothing anywhere. The gutter of iron pipes rattles with their passage. The dark lamp housing is crowded with tenants who seem to regard it as a shrine, or a nursery, or both. Purpose lost? Or purpose complete? The turners do not appear to wonder. There is a crank; the motion feels right; the colony's ancient etiquette demands a task. That is enough. It may be the purest form of industry left under this plateau: labor with no product, work as worship, the machine as hive.

Below the turners, the grazers work the walls. Their pale channels — neat as routed grooves, patient as glaciers — widen by fractions of a millimeter per generation. They lick iron and sulfur from the stone and the stone gives, slowly, and the whole colony is nourished on mineral and rust-slow oxidation, the taste of struck matches, the chemistry of things that never learned to need water.

And through it all moves the weather. The molt-wave arrives like a season crossing a field: a rustling front, thousands shedding at once, pale new shells hardening in hours, and behind it, in the trace-predator's niche, comes the pale one — the shell-eater, the only carnivore this world permits, gleaning the cast-off armor of ten thousand kin. It follows the wave the way a bird follows a herd. It is tolerated the way death is tolerated: as part of the arrangement.

At the bottom of things, beneath the living, beneath the ivory shale of molt, is the truest population: everyone who came before. Unnumberable generations pressed flat, walking-distance between the living and the bedrock. The whole colony walks a meter above its own history, and the history bears them up.

The scorch-ring around the ledger page is their one monument to us — a wound healed around, the molt already closing over the place where water touched them. They re-routed. They left the cranks alone. They use them. Whoever came here decades ago saw a seething treasure and started something; the congregation absorbed the start, digested the machinery into ritual, and continued. Nothing here is dying and nothing is young. The colony has reached its final form.

So who lives here? A single vast patience, wearing fifty thousand shells, turning abandoned cranks in the dark, humming its one long note into the iron-gray stone, waiting out the eras above — and thriving.
