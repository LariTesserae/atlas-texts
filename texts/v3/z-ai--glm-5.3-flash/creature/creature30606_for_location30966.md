# Who Lives in the Stills

Begin with the mossers, because everything else is arranged around them the way a forest is arranged around its deer.

They are low and broad and unhurried in a way that reads as wisdom until you realize it is simply tempo — their bodies tuned to the region's master rhythm, their whole metabolism idling at the pace of the presses. A mosser is the size of a stove, capybara-shaped, upholstered in a nap of graying fur that has evolved to hold dew the way a wick holds oil. They drink the morning off their own backs. Watch a herd come up the terrace road at grid-rise and you will see them pause, every one of them, at the same moment, and shiver once from shoulders to rump — this shakes the accumulated condensate down toward the mouth, and it is the nearest thing the Stills have to a public ceremony. Then they graze.

Their roads are the region's true architecture. Not the terraces, not the gutters, not the Pressfields — the roads, worn into cast composite that was supposed to be immune to weather. Nothing else has marked the ground of the Stills. Rain failed. Wind failed. A thousand generations of hooves succeeded, and the result is a network of smooth, faintly concave channels connecting heatsink to heatsink, polished to a dull sheen, sometimes as much as three strides wide. The surveyors never planned them. The mossers did, following the thermal map the way their ancestors followed it, and their roads are the only geography in the whole works that was made by the living rather than the poured. Old herds follow roads their great-great-granddams cut; the roads outlive any mosser by a hundred lifetimes. They are infrastructure. They are, in a sense, the only indigenous monument in the region, and no one is counting them.

The mossers remember the Fresh Margins in their bodies. A herd will travel miles out of its way — days of travel, in their terms — rather than cross uncured ground, and a mosser that has merely stood near a fresh pour comes back with a new behavior: it stamps its forefeet before drinking, once, twice, a checking gesture. The stamp propagates. The herds carry the memory of the Margins as ritual, which is to say they carry it perfectly and understand none of it.

The lickers are the mossers' opposite: small, quick, precise, solvent to the mossers' cloth. A licker is salamander-dark, the length of a forearm, with toes that end in suction pads and a tongue that is the whole point of the animal — broad, flat-rough, worked in squeegee strokes that leave a stripe of clean pipe behind. They are territorial about gutter-lines in a way that has produced, over uncountable generations, an etiquette of astonishing refinement. Two lickers meeting on a shared condensate line will swap ends with a choreography so practiced it looks like a single animal folding. They own stretches of gutter the way elsewhere creatures own burrows, and the boundaries are visible: the polished sections versus the dewy ones. A gutter worked by a good licker gleams. An abandoned stretch grows a skin of moisture-film that clouds over and goes stale, and the local swifts complain about it, in their way.

The lickers obey the dew-tide. When the cloud-sea swells and floods the lower terraces, the whole licker population migrates down with the water and works the misted galleries, and for a season the region is full of small wet sounds that are almost music. When the tide withdraws they go with it. No licker has ever been seen to fight. Their contests are contests of grooming speed — two rivals will work the same pipe from opposite ends toward the middle, and the one who arrives at the junction second concedes the territory. It is a duel fought with housework, and it is settled in minutes, and no blood is ever in it.

The stack swifts nest in the flue-mouths, and they are the only thing in the Stills that could be called numerous. Pale, moth-heavy, silent — they contribute almost nothing to the sound budget even in a crowd, their wingbeats absorbed by the dead acoustic of the cast ground. They ride the warm columns rising off the working machinery the way seabirds ride sea-wind, mouths open, feeding on the airborne film that blooms in the lamp-heated air — an insect-adjacent ecology of condensate and drifting spores that exists nowhere else in the region and exists everywhere in it at once, invisible, the Stills' true plankton. The swifts are its net.

Their nesting is worth describing because nothing else in the region nests. A flue-mouth holds a shelf-comb of pressed dew-silk — saliva and condensate and the fibers they glean from cable-tray linings — and the combs are translucent, and when the grid comes up through its morning cycle, a flue full of them lights up from inside like a row of paper lanterns. The young are reared on regurgitated film-plankton and are fat and helpless and pale as grubs, and the whole colony goes quiet when the cooling lines vent. The apologetic rain, when it comes, is the swifts' festival. They fly into it. No one knows why. Old-timers say it is the only water in the Stills that falls rather than condenses, and something in the swifts has never gotten over that.

The lamp-hawks hang above everything and own nothing. They are bright-eyed, narrow, patient past the point of gluttony, and they hunt the swift-columns at the edges of the thermals where the rising air goes cool and the flyers stall. Their killing is done in stillness — a hawk does not stoop here so much as stop, letting the target rise to it, which is somehow worse — and after a strike the whole local swift population rises off the flues at once in a pale, silent exhalation, then settles again within the hour. The hawks are few. One per thermal, roughly, and the thermals are fixed to the machinery, so a hawk's territory is a pump-flank or an exhaust gallery, and hawks inherit them, and a hawk that has held a good thermal for years is known to the lickers below, who will not work its gutter-line when it is hanging. Not fear, exactly. Courtesy between still things.

At the Basins, the patient murderer.

The wading thing is heron-shaped and wrong in the shoulder — the joint sits too high, the neck works on too many angles, and when it finally does move, the movement is wrong in the way that a stop-motion film is wrong, each frame complete and none of them connected. It stands over a condensate pool for a full day without shifting its weight, and the pool's translucent larvae-strainers rise toward the surface film, and the thing takes one, and the taking is so fast that witnesses consistently describe it as the pool's decision rather than the bird's. There are only a handful of these creatures in the whole region. They do not seem to breed often. They do not seem to die either. The mossers give the Basins' stake-lines a wide berth, and the lickers refuse Basins water on days when the wader is standing, which they can apparently tell from a great distance by some cue nobody has catalogued.

The pools themselves are the region's smallest nations. Each one is a closed world — filterers that graze the mineral slick in grazing lanes you can see as faint brightness-marks, grazer-mites riding the filterers' backs, and at the bottom of the food web something that eats dissolved metal, which is why some pools run faintly rust-fringed and others chalk-white, and why no two pools have quite the same citizens. The pools do not connect. Their fauna do not travel. Each pool's little kingdom has diverged from its neighbors' over what must be, for them, deep time, and a biologist from anywhere else would give a career for the comparison charts. Two pools ten strides apart, in the same Basin, under the same lamps, and their filterers are as different as two islands' finches.

And then the strangest inhabitants, which are not animals and not quite the works either: the things that live at the seams.

Where two pours meet and their batches of reality disagree — the double-shadow alleys, the Long Stair's uncertain count — the fauna behave as though something lived there. Not much. A faint avoidance, the way you avoid the center of an empty room. The swifts route their dawn flights around the seam-alleys with a smoothness that looks like instinct and is hard to tell from refusal. Mossers will graze to the edge of a shadow that falls in two directions and then stand there, chewing, looking at nothing, for longer than grazing requires. Whatever occupies those places, the region's life has made an accommodation with it that is older than any animal's memory and expressed only as geometry.

Even the silent districts — the staked, muted squares where sound dies — are not empty. Things go in. Dew forms there like everywhere else, and it forms *faster*, and it falls in complete silence, drop after drop, and once a surveyor reported that inside a mute district the gutter-tick continued — but arrived before the drop, a tick with no water behind it, as if the quiet place had learned the sound and was performing it from memory.

This, in the end, is what lives in the Stills: a civilization of small fast damp things riding a world that is too slow to notice them, organized entirely around the machine's body heat, drinking its breath, wearing paths in its skin, nesting in its mouth. The fauna are the only clock that moves. The mossers' roads are the only hand-made thing. The swifts' lantern-combs are the only light that warms.

The presses make ground. The animals make everything else.
