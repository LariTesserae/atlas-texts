Ask who lives in the Fumarole Market and you will get two answers, and the seam between them is the most important line in the city: those who remember rain, and those who must take it on faith.

There were perhaps four hundred at the first digging. Their children now outnumber them, and the children are the strange ones — the first people ever native to this place. The founders came down already shaped by the surface: sun-scars, wind-cracked lips, a way of squinting that no gallery has ever given them a use for. The born-below have none of that. They are a small, smooth, stooped people, soft-skinned from the eternal humidity, wide-pupiled, amber-eyed under the orange drinking bulbs, with ears trained so fine that an elder can lay her cheek against a tunnel wall and tell you how many rail-cars are moving in the next district and whether one of them is overloaded. They stop growing tall early, or rather the tall ones stop lasting — so the tall are a visible caste, assigned to the fan-halls where the ceilings forgive them, and everyone else has a permanent apologetic bend by thirty-five, a bow they offer to no one, only to the stone.

Their calluses are legible like script. Rope-callus on the heel of the palm means fan-crew. A thumb polished smooth and slightly yellow means fin-scraper, someone who has spent twenty years drawing a bone blade down copper gills, collecting the run-off in a bowl held in the crook of the neck. Black forearms to the elbow mean oiler. Callus on the cheekbone and jaw means listener — the guild who press their faces to the rock before any dig, who insist, when visitors call them oracles, that they are merely plumbers of sound. The children's calluses are on their palms and their soles equally, because they run the rails, small and light where weight matters, and their feet know the way home through the dark better than their eyes do.

But the deepest thing about their bodies is the breath. A hundred and eighty times a day the master valve in the fan-hall comes around on its great cam, and the whole city exhales at once — the long hiss, felt in the floor, in the teeth, in the water. And everyone stops. This is not discipline anymore; it is anatomy. Children learn to freeze before they learn to walk. A ladle hangs mid-air over a pot. A hammer pauses a finger's width off a hot rail. A quarrel suspends itself mid-sentence, and resumes, on the release, at exactly the same temperature. An outsider standing in a gallery when the hiss comes will feel their skin crawl: hundreds of people going still together in the green-gold glow, like a city caught in amber, then moving again as one. Lovers time their kisses to the breath — a whole kiss fits inside one, if you're good. Oaths are sworn on it. Thieves despise it, because during the breath every eye is open and no foot is moving except theirs.

They speak quietly, all of them, all the time, because the stone carries voices too well and because — this is the folk belief, and folk beliefs down here harden into law within a season — the rock is young, and young things collect names. A name shouted in a gallery is a name the rock will remember. So names are chosen in whispers and spoken in whispers, and much of the city's talk has migrated into the hands: a finger-talk for the loud galleries near the fans, and a knock-code on the rails, which conduct it for miles. You can hear a courtship in the rails. You can hear gossip. You can hear, on bad nights, a district asking another district for water and pretending, in the patterning of the knocks, that it isn't.

The trades are the tribes. The fin-scrapers are the most numerous and the most tender, in the way that people who harvest something slow and living tend to be — they talk to the walls. The fan-oilers smell of machine oil and are said to dream in ticking. The water-assayers stand at the market gallery by the shaft-head, tasting phials in droplets rolled on the tongue, pronouncing them sweet, gray, or bitter — drinking water, wash water, the sulfur condensate that runs the vats — and their verdicts move more grief and joy than any wedding. The smiths live behind their orange pool of light, forearms bare and hairless from the sparks, and everyone is glad of them and no one lingers: fire in this city is a guest that must be watched, and the smiths pay a fire-tax in water for every hour their forges draw, which is the closest thing the Market has to a clergy paying for its mysteries.

The market itself runs the length of the bucket-chain, under the shaft, and it is the one place where the city's neat categories break down. Everything is priced in drops. A bolt of surface cloth, a lamp of imported oil, flour, needles — small fortunes, all of them. Dried apricots cost a month of fin-work and are eaten in increments the size of a fingernail, ceremonially, in front of children. The great wonder of the market, the thing the rail-children save their drops for, is fresh fruit from above — once in a great while a crate of oranges comes down the chain, and a child of the Market holding an orange for the first time will usually hold it a long time without eating it, turning it in the glow, because they have only ever seen that color in electric bulbs and they reasonably suspect the thing is a small lamp. Above, at the shaft-mouth, the surface traders conduct their half of the trade without ever descending — the cracked folk, the below-folk call them, hoarse and white-eyed in the wind, shouting contracts down the throat of the earth. Neither side entirely believes the other exists as described. Both are correct.

And then there are the second people, whom no one who lives here would leave off any census: the mats.

The biofilm farmers will tell you their cultures have lineages older than the city itself — carried down in the founders' pockets, in sealed jars, from vats on the surface that no longer exist. Each mat has a name and a temperament. Old Meridian glows stubborn and steady. The Widow sulks, dims for days if her terrace temperature drifts. The mats glow, and the glow is the city's light, and the farmers swear the glow is also a reading: the mats go gray-green before the rock grumbles, taste some change in the stone hours before the listeners hear it, so a farmer walking his terraces at shift-change is also a sentry. Every family keeps a home-tile — a palm-sized daughter of some district mat, glowing on its shelf like a tame piece of weather — and a wedding gift between families is always a seed-tile, which is why the farmers can tell you, mat by mat, how the whole city is related. The mats are fed mash of imported grain, which the farmers are the first to admit is an absurd economy: they sell their water to buy grain to feed the slime that feeds them. They shrug when you point this out. The city eats the mats as bread, drinks them as beer, and reads them the way surface people once read clouds. When a home-tile dies, it is a death in the house. There are mites in the mats, the only animals that live here, blind and patient, and the farmers consider them part of the mat the way a body has marrow, and no one troubles to strain them out.

The water makes the society, and the society knows it with an intimacy that surface people find unbearable when they think it through: every mouthful in this city has been someone's breath. The fans pull the whole population's exhalation through the cold galleries nightly; it beads on the fins; it runs down the channels; it sits in the brown-bellied cisterns; it is drunk. The people say the whole city is one body's sweat going around, and their idioms carry it — *may your seep run sweet* as a blessing, *don't dam me* as a plea not to be interrupted, *he's gone up* said low, for the dead and the mad alike. Wages are paid in sealed phials by finger-width. Debts, dowries, and fines are all the same ledger. And the gravest crime is the one the children recite before they can read: you do not dam another district's seep. The punishment is the one that fits: the Drying. You are given a hood and a hand of water and put up on the pan, and the wind does the rest, and no one says the name again, because the rock collects names.

The dead go up too — but gently, and paid. They are dried in the warm flue-niches behind the forge-hall until they are light as reed-bundles, and at season's end the oldest walk them up the shaft into the hurt daylight and lay them on the cracked clay for the wind to finish, and the moisture that left them — the death-water, the last sweetness — is poured back into the family's seep. Everyone is borrowed water. You pay it back at the end. This rite did not exist at the founding; the first burials fouled the Third Seep, and the Sorrow of the Third Seep is told to children as the reason three of their rules exist, and it is the closest thing the city has to ancient history, being twenty-some years old. Everything here is being invented while it is used. The old argue about ritual the way elders elsewhere argue about inheritance, because the rituals are the inheritance and they are being minted now, in front of everyone.

Birth fits the same clock. A child caught on a breath is ordinary; a child caught in the stillness between breaths is called a listener, and it's half joke and half not — several of the guild's best did arrive in the pause. Newborns are oiled and held up to the family tile, so the mat sees them first. At fifteen, every child makes the up-trip once, hooded, at dawn, because you are owed one sky before you choose your life. They come back down weeping, with dry eyes, which the city does not consider a contradiction. A few stay up — the evaporated — and are spoken of in whispers
