That is the nearest thing the pocket has to a law: nothing leaves, and so nothing is spent, and so nothing is lost. What lives here has arranged itself, over however long this has been, around that law.

It lives in layers, the way the light does, beginning at the top with the eater of last inches. The grey thread from the seam dies within a meter of the surface — but it does not die unattended. A film waits for it there, a skin finer than oil, lying along the underside of the water where the thread comes down. The film has no color of its own. It takes the grey and returns a greyer grey, drinking the light's last inch before the light can finish dying, and if you watched for an hour you would see it thicken by less than a breath. On the shear-faces, something hair-fine grows out of the stone's wet — not moss, not mold; threads of a dark red-brown that grow in coils, because they grow toward the remembered light, and the remembered light is everywhere at once, and the only honest direction for a thing with nowhere to turn is spiral.

The dimmance itself is a crop. Pea-sized and transparent, numberless, the bells hang at every depth: each one opens, each one closes, and each closing squeezes a bead of glimmer out of the water. Ten thousand closings a minute are the lucence. It has no source because it has ten thousand sources; it has no direction because it comes from everywhere the life is, and the life is everywhere. But it is not even. It moves in slow patches — a mottle, a stir, a settling — the way shade moves through leaves in wind. There are no leaves. There have never been leaves. But the water's memory is specific, and the life has inherited that memory whole, and so the light in this sealed room dapples like sun broken by a canopy somewhere the water will not name.

The grazers write on the walls. Soft wedges the length of a thumb-joint creep the shear-faces, rasping the film, and their tracks glow — faintly, briefly, a script that fades in minutes — so the walls are a palimpsest with no blank stone left under it, sentence over sentence about eating, every sentence erased in its turn. Some of the trails lie layered like varnish. Varnish takes patience. The rock says the seam is a season old, or ten; the walls disagree; in the pocket, time is a property of the water, and the water is not from here.

Between film and floor swim the long ones — a hand's length, eyeless, shaped less like fish than like the shadows fish would cast if light fell here. They hold to the moving patches of the dimmance, because the dapples mark the weather: dead film, spent bells, shed eggs, drifting down without end, the pocket's only snow. Everything eats the weather. Nothing is wasted here, not out of virtue but out of architecture — in a room with no door, waste is merely food in the wrong place, and everything here has had a long time to be patient about the right places.

The floor is silt the way a city is stone: one working of countless small things, breathing. Its breath keeps time with the drips. The drop that gathers under the dry dome's lip for an hour and falls is the pocket's only calendar; when it lands, rings spread through the silt and are read, and the hour is considered, and the pocket continues.

Below the dripline, beneath the ledge where the moth's wings rest, the silt thickens into something you would still call floor until you noticed that everything else breathes to its rhythm. It is large. It may be the pocket's floor entire. It has one organ, and the organ is attention, and the light is how it attends: when the hour's drop falls and its tremor reaches the water, the whole hollow brightens by a hair — a flinch of lucence, leaning toward the only event the dry world ever sends down — then settles, and goes on. The wings stir when the drop lands. No air moves, but the water's breath carries up through the stone, and the dried wings tick against the ledge: the one sound the pocket owns that is not water. What is below them attends to that most of all.

The wings are also the only thing the pocket has not taken apart. Everything else that has ever entered — rainwater, grit, the grey thread — has been eaten, or dissolved, or made into more of what lives here. The wings sit dry on their ledge like a label on a specimen jar, and the specimen is everything else. Here the pocket's histories stop agreeing. The water is older than the pocket; it came up through the hill by mouths older still, all of them sealed behind it, so that the seam is not a wound but the end of a road. What arrived with the moth had a shorter road — spore-small, dust that walks — and finished it here, and its descendants are everything: the film, the coiled threads, the bells, the long ones, the listening floor. Whatever came with the wings is not in evidence — except that the light dapples like shade through leaves, and no leaf has ever hung in this hill, and the wings themselves are the shape of a thing that once flew under leaves, toward light, on purpose.

From the hillside, none of this shows. The dark band of grass is darker because it is better fed: the water table under that shoulder runs faint and sweet, carrying a lucence of its own that fades in open air, and the grass drinks it and greens. The sheep avoid the patch anyway. Lower down, where the water surfaces as a spring, dogs will not drink, and if you cup the water and hold it to the sky it shows nothing at all — which is its own kind of evidence.

The seam is narrower than it was. The sheared brightness of its lips is dulling, taking on the hill's patience at last; a season, or ten, will close it to a hand, then to fingers, then to water only. None of the life will notice. The grey thread was already dead at a meter; the pocket was never lit from outside; the light is memory, and memory does not dim when the door does. When the last of the seam seals, the drips will keep their hour, the bells their ten thousand closings, the silt its reading and its breath — and the wings will go on ticking on their dry ledge, hour by hour, for something below that has decided, for now, to listen.
