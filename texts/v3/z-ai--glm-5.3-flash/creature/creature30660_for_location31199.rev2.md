Count the ground itself as the first resident. Everything else in this account lives inside it or on it the way mites live on a whale — and the people who walk the swells would not argue with the comparison, except to correct it: they would say there is no *else*. The mantle is one body, or was many once and has grown seamless; the strata run like the rings of a stump, so perhaps it began as a single seed, or perhaps as a crowd that fused so long ago that no seam survives to say so. It is the eldest thing by every measure, and it has two behaviors: it remembers the routes that are walked on it, and once in a great many years, in one country, it holds its breath. The herders have a word for the whole of it, and the word translates as *here*. There is no word meaning *somewhere else*, because there is nowhere else to stand.

Below your feet, the first residents proper. The borers go unnumbered — thread-thin to fist-thick, animal tissue threaded through the plant tissue, and their combined passage is the held bass note under everything, the rumble you feel through your heels before the organ-note reaches your ears. Their work is circulation: without them the strata would pack slowly into stone and the whole continent would go quiet and mineral, so they are, functionally, its blood. Above them in scale swim the great ones, the cart-headed blind things whose traveling swells pass through the turf against the wind. They plow the deep black bands and pass them back as lace — the root-stone is their tunnels, lined by the roots, and the black nodules the herders dig for are the ground's pearls: grit, a splinter, some indigestible old thing, coated in black by the mantle the way an oyster coats a splinter of sand. Every blade the herders carry is a pearl around a swimmer's indigestion, and nodule ground is found by listening for where swimmers have been. A swimmer's rumble has a signature. The bands know their neighbors underfoot the way you know footsteps on a stair, and some swimmers have names, and the custom, when a named one surfaces near a route and looks at nothing and sinks, is to stamp the staff on the turf twice, in greeting, and walk on.

At the sweats the small life crowds in. Wallowers — squat, mild, mud-colored, salt-crusted at the own mouths — graze the mineral rind and keep the shallows from choking; they are the pools' housekeepers. The insects stand in mating columns like slow smoke, and they are the flush's couriers, riding the haze-pillars out and carrying the fruiting's sweetness forward in ten thousand guts, because that is the secret of the wave: the green light goes around the continent because everything that eats it plants it, dung-wise, a season's walk ahead, and the next country ripens and is eaten and planted in turn. The flush is herded. Every gut on the plateau is a drover. As for the mud-skitters, the films of frantic life across the warm margins — the herders say leave a film alone long enough and it stands up on new stilt-legs and walks to the fog-line. No one has watched it happen. But the films run thin in the very seasons the dawn chorus fills with gangly pale yearlings, and the herders do not waste doubt on the question.

The dew-walkers, then, the adults: they are the instrument the dawn plays itself on. A dew-walker drinks by singing — the fringe-mouth opened into the moving grey, the ringing note shivering the fog to droplet, the droplets combing down the fringe and swallowed — so that the sky-full-of-struck-metal sound is not decoration but the act of drinking itself, ten thousand throats condensing the plateau's own night-breath back into the pools of the day. They herd fog the way the herders herd cattle: the mist rolls up the swells earliest and thickest along their old routes, and the bands navigate by it. Their routes shift with the flush years ahead of any sign a human can read, and no one knows how they know. What they do at night, how the old ones end — old dew-walkers simply stop walking, and stand at a sweat until the fringe grows down into the water like a fountain frozen mid-pour — is theirs. The sky has no other tenants. Insects ride the pillars; the pillars themselves are weather; between them and the walkers, the air is fully leased.

The cattle are the largest animals, and the least like animals, and the most. A hill with legs feeds twice — light along the flanks, turf under the under-slung mouth — and because it feeds twice it must keep moving, which is why the herd-roads are the oldest roads: a herd that stays eats itself into hunger twice over, in grass and in light. Its wake is not damage but manufacture. The gut turns woody turf into packed seed, the dung sprouts, and ten years behind a herd the ground is a ridge of deeper green — the coastline builds its own seafloor as it goes. Calves are budded: a knuckle of tissue on a flank that swells for a season and drops off walking, already green, and when one drops the whole herd stands in a ring around it for days, like stones around a hearth. And as a hill grows it outgrows itself, and molts — hide sloughing in great membranes, hoops of rib, worn mouth-plates like gray combs — leaving behind on some swell a beached and empty suit of itself, which is, by the law of roads, the property of whichever band walks with that herd. A molt is a season's wealth: cloth, timber, and ivory in one tide-line.

The herds talk to each other across a day's distance through the ground, low thunder passing under the swells, and the plateau carries all of it — the whole country is one conversation happening underneath at all times. Nobody owns a herd. A band attends one, sometimes for generations, and the relationship has shaped both sides so long that a good whistler can lean a coastline a degree or two, and the cattle, certainly, have learned to read the whistlers back. Which of them follows which is not a question anyone has ever needed to settle.

The people call themselves guests. That is the whole of their self-description, translated, and it is not modesty — it is physics, in this place, because the ground accepts everything given time, and acceptance here is the same event as disappearance. So everything they own is made to be moved, and everything they do is done in passing, and their entire material culture is one long technique for being here without being kept. They speak in whistle and hand-shape because the throat is useless against the organ-note — and because a whistled sentence, thrown down a swell, will ride the drone's harmonics and arrive a mile on, still itself, a message traveling inside the continent's own voice. At dusk the organ drops a full tone and every conversation on the plateau drops with it; camp-talk after sundown is audibly a key lower, and a stranger can tell the hour by the pitch of the voices. Each band keeps a listener, staff to ear, who reads a herd a day off, a neighbor-swimmer under the next swell, tomorrow's wind still hours from the far turf — and who is also the memory, because the ground carries every voice ever thrown into it, and the listener is the one who knows where to put the ear.

They burn dried dung and dead stem, and keep the ember alive in a shed-plate lantern between camps; a band's ember is older than its oldest member and is greeted like an elder. They move before the ground accepts them — the pallets sprouting, the guy-lines going to bark — and here is the elegance of it: they leave the rooting camp behind on purpose, because a camp left to root becomes a poultice. A swell a band has wintered on comes up sweeter behind them. Their camps farm the ground by being abandoned on it, and certain swells are wintered-on by custom, in rotation, the way other peoples rotate fields.

The dead are not buried — no one cuts the turf; the scar would outlive the mourners in wrong-colored tissue. At dusk the dead are laid at a swell's crest in a molt-membrane, and the family walks away all night in a straight line that is not a road yet, and the ground closes over what it was given as if it had never been, and the line they paced begins, slowly, over years, to remember itself. The dead become road. This is why the herders find nothing mysterious in ridges that outlast the people who made them: habit does not end when the habitual dies. Some of the oldest roads are wider than any band now living could account for — herds made those, or thicker centuries — and nobody knows, and the roads do not mind. A staff left leaning on a cairn too long will root as well, and because a staff is hollow, the wind goes on playing it. Here and there along the old routes the wind plays notes left by people whose names the road has outlasted, which is as close to a monument as this country keeps: the hoods over the favored seeps, left open for whoever comes; the cairns of nodules on the sight-lines, stacked and never taken. Taking from a cairn marks a person. The mark does not wash.

Children learn two disciplines first. The minute of stillness — a full sixty heartbeats before trusting any silhouette, because the boulder may be sleeping and the grazing shape may be tissue wearing the form of a beast, because forms get borrowed here. And the drone's keys, so that direction and hour and weather arrive through the ear. The small feast is held the day a child first hears a swimmer through bare ear and staff, unaided. And the child is taught the map with its one hole in it — the held-breath country, far in the middle distances, where the ground is not dead and the herds cross it and nothing is dangerous, exactly, and no band sleeps. The herders keep three accounts of it. An old wound, cut past the death-line before anyone's counting, scarred into silence. Something beneath it so large that the ground has been holding its breath out of courtesy — or out of fear — for longer than anyone's counting, and is still, after all that, about to move. Or that the plateau is listening there, to something under itself, the way the herders listen through a staff. All three accounts agree on the practice: you route around. Whistled speech falls dead in that country — there is no drone to ride — and people who cross it come out the far side hoarse, having shouted to each other all day for no reason they could name, against a silence that was somehow louder than all the sound in the world.

One more story is told, at ember-time, to children who are old enough to hold the ember-lantern: that the plateau was an animal once, the first and the largest, and lay down to sleep and grew roots out of laziness, and that the flush is its dream traveling under its skin, and that when the flush comes all the way around and meets its own beginning, the sleeper wakes. No one agrees on what waking means, and the guests are not sure they wish to find out. And yet every year, herd and walker and swimmer and borer alike spend their strength carrying the sweetness a little farther forward — the hoods, the cairns, the poultice-camps, the drover-guts — so that the dream keeps nearly, nearly closing. Whether that work hastens the waking or postpones it is a question the guests are entitled not to answer.

At night the heat stands up off the swells in visible sheets and the fog builds and the drips begin and the organ drops its tone, and the camp's talk drops with it, and out along the ridge a herd lies like a black coast with its own buried thunder. Somewhere a watch-keeper stands with a staff to the ground, listening to a neighbor swim and to tomorrow's wind still on its way. And in the hush between messages the watcher whistles into the dark — small, idle, half a lullaby — and the plateau takes the note and carries it, inside its own exhalation, out over the swells toward the rim and the sweetening ground. It is the one sound in this country that belongs to nobody and everybody: the guest's voice riding the host's breath, which is the nearest thing to being accepted that anyone here has ever managed, and which is, being only a note in the drone, also the safest thing to be.
