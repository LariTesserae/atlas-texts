...the deepest floors are where the sound changes character. Up under the roof, the seethe is quick and grainy, all small surfaces. Down there it thickens, slows, comes apart into its parts: each drip a separate event, each touch of water on stone given room to finish. The stone is faintly warm. The water is older than the stone it moves through, older than the hollowing, and it carries in solution the slow news of everything it has ever touched. But the deepest floors are not the end of the country, only the end of the map, and to understand who is holding the map you have to start again at the top — not with the plain, but with the noise.

Because the noise is the first inhabitant. Everything else is a neighbor of the noise.

The seethe is not weather. It is a census. Alone, no single tenant in the Hollow Country is audible. A mandible rasping a film of bacteria off a wet wall is nothing; the flex of a gill-fan is nothing; the footfall of a springtail is beneath nothing. But there are billions, and they have had a very long time to accumulate, and their small noisings arrive in the galleries overlapped and interfolded until they lose every individual edge and become what the dwellers are born inside: a continuous grain, a hiss with a texture the way daylight has a temperature. A dry-season seethe has a different grain than a flood-season seethe. An old gallery seethes differently than a young one. The inhabitants read all of this the way surface things read sky.

This is the first thing to understand about whoever lives here: they have taken the three apprenticeships of the dark, all of them, completely. They have given up eyes — not lost them, given them up, the way you give up a tool with no work for it, and the saving is spent on longer feelers, wider skin, ears grown outward until ear and body are the same organ. They have given up color, and so most of what lives here is white in the particular way of things that have never needed to be anything else, pale enough that the blood shows through as a faint blush at the joints. And they have given up hurry. Nothing in the Hollow Country is fast, because nothing here needs to be, and speed makes noise, and noise is address.

They live inside sound the way you live inside air. Each of them occupies, at any moment, a bubble of audible world — the reach of their own hearing plus the reach of everything that answers it — and that bubble is their horizon, the only horizon the country permits. In the wet seasons the seethe carries far, the bubbles overlap, the world is wide, and a dweller can feel the gallery full of neighbors the way you feel a crowded room by its warmth. In droughts the seethe thins. The bubbles shrink. There are droughts down here that last a generation, and in those years the country contracts until some of its tenants can hear nothing in any direction but themselves, and discover what the dwellers discover in such years: that their own heartbeat is loud, that stillness is the only privacy, that the smallest thing they can do — the smallest — is to slow the pulse and wait.

Under the roof of the plain is where the sky enters, and they all know it. Not as light. As taste. Rain goes into the karren through a thousand throats and comes down through the massif as drips, seeps, threads, and every drip is a letter from the surface, dissolved from what it touched on its way. So the dwellers beneath the roof know the surface's year intimately, though none of them has ever seen it. Spring drips taste green — of pollen, of thaws, of the first rot of young things. Autumn drips taste of leaf-sugar and leaf-death. A dry summer arrives below as silence in the drip-lines; a wet one as noise. They read the sky through the stone, and they are not sentimental about it; the drips are their climate, their post, and their clock.

That is the economy the upper galleries run on: the fall. Everything the surface loses — pollen, seeds, drowned insects, leaf-flesh carried down the sink throats, the bodies of creatures that went in and did not come out — sifts down through the sinks and swallets and is caught, floor by floor, by whoever is waiting. The grazers wait. These are the oldest trade in the country, soft pale things shaped like a need rather than an animal, ring-mouthed, flattened against the wet walls where the bacterial felt grows thickest, and they move slowly across their pasture leaving behind them meandering calligraphy of clean stone — feeding trails, three hundred million years of them, that hardened into the rock and now look, to anything that could look, like handwriting no one wrote.

Above the grazers, in the guano-warmed chambers near the sink mouths, live the herds: long-legged, hinged, silent-footed things that farm the fall, seeding fungus gardens along their own dung-trails and cropping them in rotation, patrolling their compost with the fussy competence of animals who have done one thing longer than mountains. They have never seen the green their whole economy depends on. It is not a problem for them. Green, to them, is a flavor that arrives from above in certain drips, and they know it the way you know a country you were born to by its cooking.

Some of the upper dwellers live in the drip-lines themselves. The chimes hang where the water falls — thin-skinned, bell-shaped, rooted to the tips of living stone — and they intercept the drip before it lands, drinking the sky one drop at a time, so that in the old galleries the percussion you would expect is full of gaps, and every gap is a meal. And where there are gaps in the country's percussion there is fraud, because the drip-sound is the most trustworthy sound here, the voice of the water-table, the landmark every animal navigates by — and so one of the dwellers learned to counterfeit it.

The liar is a pale, hanging thing, a fisher, and its trick is patient and almost perfect. It does not fake the sound. It manufactures a real one: it holds a bead of water in its mouthparts and releases it upward, and lets it fall back as an honest, unrepeatable, mineral-true drop, into a small cup it keeps beneath itself, and the sound of that drop is identical to the sound of the water-table speaking. Anything listening — anything thirsty, anything navigating — comes toward what it believes is a spring. The liar's drip is honest in every way but one: it gets tired. A real drip continues with the patience of geology. The false one must reload. There is a swallow between its drops, a hitch, a rhythm with appetite in it, and the old animals of the galleries test every spring against that — listen for the tiredness — and the young ones learn the test, or feed the liar. Its presence has shaped more bodies in this country than any weather: there are grazers now who will die of thirst a body-length from a dripping wall before they will drink from a drip they have not counted for a season.
