Three hundred and six souls stood in the streets at the last Hush, and the count was taken then, because the Hush is the one hour in the year when everyone in Fenharrow is outside and nothing is working, and the town likes to know exactly how many of itself there are. The number goes up and down by a handful between counts — a birth in spring, a planting in autumn, a hard season in the fungus tunnels — but it has not moved by a hundred in anyone's grandmother's memory. This is the whole of it: one extended family with machinery, related to one another within four handshakes, folded into a mile of raised brick and black water.

Everyone in Fenharrow farms something. That is the fastest way to know them. The reed-cutters farm ten thousand acres of standing hiss. The moss-men farm the north walls, stripping them like a field and waiting for the green to come back down the weep-lines. The slag-farmers farm heaps they will never harvest — seeding reeds into cooling glass at a season's pace so that their grandchildren, in a hundred years, can dig the ground up and pour it out again. The dredge crews farm the water itself, turning the ore-beds like a crop no one sows and everyone tends. And the oldest grandmothers farm time: the forty-year rotation of the beds is written nowhere it can be trusted, but is kept in four heads, recited at every Hush, and corrected aloud if a daughter stumbles on the order. Children are named for the bed planted in the year of their birth, so that everyone carries a harvest date in their name and turns forty when their bed comes ready. A birthday here is not a thing a person does. It is a thing the ground does.

They eat what they grow — reed-flour, moss-greens, the pale farmed fungus out of the slag tunnels — and what the barges bring in, grain and salt meat, since nothing here swims and nothing grazes and no one has tasted fresh flesh in living memory. The bargemen eat better. Everyone agrees on this without resentment; it is simply one of the differences between here and out there.

The engines have their keepers the way saints have their days, and the keeper families are old without being noble — no one remembers whether the families were named for the engines or the engines for them, and it no longer matters, because the keepers have grown into their machines the way handles grow into tools. The finest keepers go deaf by sixty and are not pitied but promoted, since deafness in a keeper is a qualification: a deaf woman with bare feet on the engine-house floor can tell you the temper of Patience's furnace through her soles, can lay a palm flat on the flywheel housing and say whether the week will be a good one. She reads the machines the way the dredge crews read the water — by sheen, by vibration, by the color of things. The rarest skill among them is parking: taking two hundred tons of turning iron and setting it down off its dead center, precisely, once a year, so that it can be started again. An engine parked wrong will tear its own heart out on the first stroke of the new year. So the town holds its breath for the ten minutes of the parking more than it ever does for the hour of quiet that follows, and the caulkers, going out to their sluices in that terrible stillness, touch the carved heron on the old boards as they pass — two hundred years of thumbs have worn the bird smooth — the way towns elsewhere touch stone lions.

The rest of life is the war on rust, fought to a draw and fought with rags, and it is not only war. It is devotion, and it is courtship. A child learns the figure-eight pass on a windowsill at a mother's knee, wool-fat worked into the grain of the cloth, the long slow strokes that leave a wall weeping clean instead of weeping red. A courtship becomes serious when a young woman quietly begins wiping a young man's stretch of wall — his father's seep-lines, the moss seam above his door — because in Fenharrow you do not give your labor to just anyone's rust. Wedding gifts are practical and tender in equal measure: a winter's worth of wool-grease, a rag-bag of good wool, a caulking mallet with the family mark. And when a house is raised a course — the raisers coming with their screw-jacks and their alder piles, lifting the family's whole life one generation's height — the family eats one last supper on the old ground floor, at the exact height their grandparents called a ceiling, and then the new timbers go in above and the old room becomes a cellar and the wet takes it, slowly, kindly, the way the marsh takes everything it is given.

Speech here is short shouts and a hand-talk with twelve dialects, one for each Level, and the dialects are close enough that a barge-crew from the Finger can bargain with a quench-keeper from the Thumb in signs, but far enough that everyone can hear — see — where a stranger learned to sign. The people tell their children apart by footsteps in the dark, and are themselves known by the particular weight of their tread on particular floors; a Fenharrow man can be embarrassed in front of his neighbors by having his furnace misread through the boards. Faces are lit from below, always, furnace-orange, and voices are kept low because the world is already so loud, and so the town has a quality of murmur under its din, like a church under its bells.

The children learn bird-words from books and from nothing else on earth, and so their birds are wrong and beautiful. They draw herons with too many joints and crows the size of chapels and marsh-birds of their own invention with names like the long-listener and the reed's-own-white, and nobody corrects them, because there is nothing to correct them against. The weathervane crow is the only bird that ever moves in Fenharrow, and it turns without living, and the children watch it the way children elsewhere feed ducks. When the grain barges come down the causeway canal with dogs aboard, the whole quay turns out to stare — dogs are the wild animals here, the exotic ones — and the dogs stand at the rail and tremble and will not set paw on the black stone, and the bargemen have long since stopped pretending they might. The barge-folk trade and go. No one has ever asked one to overwinter, and no one ever will, and the bargemen are glad of it.

And the dead. The water stands two feet down; a grave here would fill like a cup. So the dead are planted.
