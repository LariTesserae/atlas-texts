Ask who lives in the Bowl of Falling Sound and you have to start below the answer, with the note — the Underhum, the bowl's own sustained tone, older than every channel and every creature, the eldest resident by ages past counting. It carved the rooms the others inhabit. It drinks before anything else drinks. Everything else that lives here lives, in a sense, inside that one long voice the way a fish lives inside a lake. But the note does not answer when spoken to, and the residents you can actually know begin with three: an old woman, her daughter, and her daughter's son, in three beehive huts on the eastern shelf.

You hear them long before you see them. In this country a household is a sound before it is a sight, and the keeper-household is one of the loudest things in the bowl after the fall itself. From two channels away you hear the chime-yard ringing out its sleepless vigil; you hear hammers going along the baffles in their graduated sizes, each stone being persuaded at the pitch it prefers; you hear, at first light, a single deliberate knock laid on the shelf-rock, repeated in an old cadence, like a question asked to the floor of the world. Then you round the last rib of stone and there they are: three figures in a fenced yard of singing posts, small against all that circling dark, going about a trade so old it has stopped looking like work.

The boy you would meet first, because the boy comes to meet you. He is eleven, or nearly; he runs the fence-line at dusk touching every chime-post so that the yard answers him post by post, a child conducting his own protection. His name, in the old tongue, means *loud enough* — the keepers name their children the way sailors name boats, as blessings against the thing they fear — and he has taken the blessing seriously. But listen closely and the boy keeps two silences of his own, hoarded like contraband: the little practiced whistling he does at the rim when he thinks no one is near, and the waiting. He was learning to talk when his father went down.

The father was a trader's son from a bowl three days beyond the flats, a small loud place where, the keepers say, the people's vowels are clipped short from living in tight stone. He came up behind a dusk front with a rope-party, sat in the chime-yard until the moths rose, and was invited inside the middle hut — which is how courtship is done here, because inside is the only place where you can hear a person's heart through the floor, and no keeper will marry anyone whose heart they haven't heard. He stayed nine years. Then he took the amber rope down to carry his son's voice to his own mother — a bead of the boy laughing, packed like a letter — and somewhere on the pale flats between the bowls, the party was lost. The Lees are wide, and on the flats your voice dies a foot from your mouth, and a wrong turn is a silence you cannot shout your way out of. The keepers read a grammar of knocks that travelers lay on the rim rock, taps that walk under the stone for a day or two before they fade; they received his party's cadence twice, faintly, and then only the dead thud of spent ground. That was years ago.

Since that morning the old woman has tapped the same asking-cadence into the shelf at every dawn. Nine years, twice a year missing at most. She does not discuss it.

She is the eldest keeper, and her name means *she was heard*. Her deafness came the way deafness comes here, from the top down — the high pitches left her first, so that the note-blooms' calls and the moth-wings and the bright top registers of the chime-fence have gone out for her like lamps down a corridor — and she has made an accommodation with it that is almost a second craft. She hears through bone. She lays her jaw against the bedrock and takes the bowl's news through her teeth; she reads a channel's health with the backs of her fingers; she swears the Underhum is more intimate now than it ever was, that it has moved in under her sternum and pays rent in warmth. She can also tell you — and her daughter, who tunes the hammers, does not dispute it — that the great note sat a shade higher when she was young. The bowl is wearing deeper. Its voice is settling the way an old singer's does. On a peg inside her hut hang the hammers of her own mother, retired, tuned to a pitch the bedrock no longer holds: museum pieces from a higher-strung world. Her daughter's hammers are tuned lower. The boy will tune his lower still, and that slow retuning, more than anything the keepers own, is the true calendar of this place. Generations are measured here in the descent of one note.

The daughter — her name means *answered* — is the one you would call practical if the word weren't too small for her. She walks the channels at dawn the way irrigators walk ditches in countries that have water, palm flat to the rock, reading the flow by hand: a healthy line runs warm and buzzes faintly under the palm, and a failing one, silting with grit and inert lees-sound, goes cool and dull before it fails, and she mends the baffles that others can't yet see are failing. Her grief has a shape and the shape is maintenance: since the knock-grammar went unanswered she has not let the chime-yard fall silent for a single night, not one, in all the years. Her doctrine of silence is her mother's opposite. The grandmother hoards quiet the way misers hoard coin — she keeps, wrapped in felt in a dry cache under the shelf, a bead of true Hush-silence from the drought of her girlhood, a bead that holds the sound of nothing falling, a bottled Lees — and no one has ever heard what she means to do with it. The daughter wants no silences at all except the cellar-quiet of the huts, which she trusts because she built the chimney-horns herself. The boy, being eleven, has a third position, which the elders tolerate the way you tolerate weather: he thinks silence might simply be a place, and might be worth visiting, carefully, someday, with an adult.

Inside the huts is the family's one hoard of true quiet. The chimney-horns draw their single chosen tones, and in that cellar-stillness — the only place in the bowl where you can hear your own heart — the keepers go to be soft. Whispering happens here and nowhere else; intimacy in this culture is quietness shared, and to be invited inside a keeper's hut is to be trusted with the sound of a heartbeat through the floor. The boy does his arithmetic there, and his hoping. He knows the family's most guarded possession: one amber bead, uncracked, holding his father's voice — laughing at his own joke, one spring evening before the last trip down. His mother has never played it. In this world an unplayed bead is still a possible thing; the moment it is cracked it becomes a past. The keepers store their futures the way they store everything else: unplayed, warm, going faintly amber in the dark.

Around the three of them, the bowl's other residents go about their own versions of the same life.

The drumherds come down the channels on their circuits with the patience of weather, and the ground tells you they are coming a quarter-hour before they arrive — the whole bowl ringing softly, skin over a drum. The keepers hold a few ground-words with them, taps meaning *coming*, *bloom*, *go around*, *well met*, and the herds honor the baffles and take their toll in trodden ground: where the herds pass, the groves bloom out of season, and the sowing of the keeper-gardens follows the herd-paths the way old riverside peoples followed floods. There is one old bull who comes up to the chime-yard at night, when the honest work is done, and answers the fence-posts one slow thump at a time, deliberately off the beat, and the old woman drums his pattern back and he stands in the dark being answered. When a herd-beast dies the whole family stands over it and drums — very softly, for days, a low pattern the floor carries gently — and for days afterward the bedrock feels tender, the way a cheek feels after weeping.

The trillers are the bowl's frivolity and its forecast. They ride the dusk fronts in flocks, surfing the dense bands the way nothing here rides wind, sipping the shrill crests off the top of the weather, and the young ones race each other down the fronts for no reason anyone has ever found except joy. When the trillers stop playing and stand in ranks along the rim, facing out, the keepers fill the storm-cache — because the true storms are harvest here. The groves drink a storm for a week, and the tapped trees weep amber heavy with it for weeks after, and that is the family's granary: bottled thunder, laid in against the Hush.

The bone-whistlers work the dusk, and the keepers have stopped finding them eerie, mostly. Each whistler carries a collection of hollow bones, and each bone is a specific dead thing — herdbeast, triller, once in an old story a keeper — and when a whistler plays a bone it is, in the whistler's own economy, remembering. They attend keeper funerals uninvited and play the dead one's name-motif on the dead one's own bone, and no one has ever decided whether this is scavenging or grief, and the keepers have stopped needing to decide. One old whistler opens its evening circuit with a phrase that the boy knows from somewhere under his memory — a whistle his father used to give the dusk-fluters across the channels, a greeting between species that had never needed words. The boy answers it on the fence-chimes, badly. Both of them know exactly what it is. Neither has ever once stopped.

The gardener-beasts are stranger, and older, and the relationship with them is the keepers' oldest covenant. They are patient as the stone they move, and one immense matriarch works the western channel with chime-moss grown into her hide so that she rings faintly as she walks — a grove that grazes. She knew the old woman's mother. When the old woman lays her greeting-taps on a baffle the beast answers with a courtesy-pattern that no living keeper uses, a formality three generations retired, and the family has let their own knock fall out of use rather than correct her. Down a dead baffle-line in the north channels — groves shrunk to gray stubble, a dry-laid doorway with no hut behind it, a keeper-line that ended decades back — some stone-slow nephew of hers still combs and mends the walls of a family no one remembers. The beasts do not wait; waiting is a word for people who count time. They tend. The low walls in the channels, beast-built and human-built side by side, indistinguishable to any eye including the builders', are not architecture. They are the treaty, being renewed.

And then there is the resident the family does not speak to: the hush-stalker. It hunts the drained pockets of the deep groves, padded in resin-silence, and it is the only heart in the bowl that makes no sound, and everything else that lives here has organized its whole existence around the fact of it — the chime-fences, the loud yards, the children taught loudness before language, the funerals held at a shout, the prey-crowds pressing into the channels where the noise is thickest. Loud is safe; quiet is death; the stalkers built this culture by being in it. The daughter's position is a hammer's edge and a clear rule. The grandmother calls them the lees walking, the dregs given legs, and hoards her bead of silence partly, you suspect, to keep one silence where nothing can hunt in it. The boy, at eight, during the nine-day Hush, saw one from the rim at noon — standing where the last thin ribbons of the failing fall came down, leaning into the last of the sound like a beggar at a closing door — and when the fall resumed, it simply walked away into the groves. It looked tired, he says. He keeps this to himself, the way he keeps his whistling.

The dead of the family go into the quiet. This is the hardest teaching, and every keeper carries it differently: that the hush-pockets where the sound has drained away entirely are where the living must never linger, and where the dead are carried, because the dead have become listeners and belong in the deep listening. The party goes in loud and armed and comes out loud and counts itself at the exit. Over each grave they raise one resonant stone tuned to the name-motif, so that when the dusk fronts roll over the rim, the graves ring their names — and in true storms the whole pocket sounds, all the family's dead at once, a night the keepers call the chorus and stand outside of, in the noise, listening. Once a year they go in again, armed and loud, and stand over the graves and crack a single bead, and let one voice — a grandmother's storm-story, a child's song, a stranger's whistle bought as coin from three bowls away — drop down into the hush where their dead are listening. Then they walk out before anything else in the quiet can notice them. As for the amber of the dead that stays home: it lights the huts, generation stacked on generation, and in a Hush the keepers spend their ancestors without shame, bead by bead, hut by hut brightening with forty-year-old voices — because the dead, the elders say, would rather be spent than let the living go quiet.

That is the whole household: one drifting note, one covenant of walls, one grief kept loud, one future kept unplayed. The traders' rope-parties come up behind the dusk fronts a few times a year and sit stunned in the chime-yard, drinking sound like travelers at a spring, and carry the amber down, and no one outside has ever heard the boy's name, because his name exists nowhere but here — a rise-and-fall of three notes the yard-chimes know, stored unplayed in the only place that holds it. He will carry it down himself someday, on his first trade-walk, the way the rite goes; and when he brings home a fourth, his grandmother has a morning already chosen. She will walk into the Lees singing, the old custom, her voice dying a little closer each hour until the flats finish it, and she will take her hoarded bead with her — to crack out there, or to keep, she has told no one; a plan in this family is a bead, and beads are stored unplayed.

Stand with them at dusk, then — three generations of the same house, palms flat on the warm bedrock, as the blooms assemble their chord and the herds drum the channels and the great note holds them all up from beneath. The boy is listening to two things at once, as he does every dusk: the bowl, alive and loud and bounded, every corner of it audible at once, small enough to spend a whole life hearing; and past the rim, under the flats, for a tap-walk that hasn't come — the asking-cadence, unanswered, nine years answered. The note holds. The house holds with it. In a world where everything alive drinks sound, this is what living here finally is: not owning the bowl, not even keeping it, exactly — being kept, three small loud lives held on one sustained tone, wearing it and feeding it and mending its channels, deep enough to spend a life inside, and meaning to.
