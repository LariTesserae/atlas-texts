It is the calendar, the road, the market bell, the cradle rhythm. Everything alive on this highland keeps the machines' time, and has for so long that no one living can say which came first, the pulse or the life that waits for it.

**The people**

They are short, broad-handed, and never entirely dry. Their skin is soft the way the water-channels are soft — polished by a lifetime of constant damp — and it is an organ of knowing: they read the tide through their soles, taste the stone through their fingertips, and hear with their whole bodies. Children click from before they can walk, tongue-clicks refined over more generations than anyone can count, and the sound of a crowded terrace at the tide-rise is the sound of soft rain. There are manners to it. You do not click into an elder's face. You click softly toward someone's shoulder to mean *I am looking at you now.*

Babies are born with eyes that open on nothing, and everyone finds this faintly improper, like a guest arriving undressed. By the third year the eyes cloud over — the settling, it is called — and there is a small rite for it: the mother sings the settling song, holds the child's face in both hands, and afterward the child is considered finished. Complete. Their people.

Their names are not words. A name is a three-note phrase, and to greet someone is to sing their phrase into the dark and hear it sung back. No two living people hold the same phrase.
