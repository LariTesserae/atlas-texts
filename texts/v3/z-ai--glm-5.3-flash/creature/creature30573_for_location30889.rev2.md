Begin, this time, with the light — because the light is hers.

Everything else in the valley belongs to the world: the fog and its breathing, the pavement and its rings, the grey syrup of the seeps, the wobbling shine of the brine. The light is the one thing here that is spent instead of kept. It leaves her under her arms in small cold-blue lenses, arranged in a low ring, and it exists only while she works, and it costs her something, and the fog takes it back the moment she stops. Nothing else on the plain spends anything. The storm above spends itself endlessly and the plain converts all of it, molecule by molecule, into stone. She is the valley's only economy.

And the question, which the fog will take its time answering, is this: what does it take for a soft thing to live inside a finished world?

Start with her body, because her body is her answer. She is pale the way the fog is pale, and she is not merely pale — she is textured pale. Her skin raises itself into fine lumps and folds that match, close as a counterfeit, the surfaces of the nodules around her, and when she folds her arms and settles, there is one more stone on the pavement. The plain's camouflage is sameness, and she has mastered it the way the plain masters everything: not quickly, not completely, and never with an audience. Between stillnesses she is the one moving part of a finished place, the one unworn surface in a world where every surface has been handled for ages. Everything else on the plain carries its history as rings, laid down in mineral, readable if anything could read. She carries hers as habit. Her past is not in her body. It is outside it, in three stones and a scoop, and this is the strange thing the valley holds without strain: the only mind here has moved its memory into the pavement.

Her arms are long and unequal — the two that work are thicker than the rest, corded like the roots of the crinoids — and their undersides are fields of taste. The water is packed enough to drink at a distance; she reads the bacterial mats before she reaches them, a sourness in the fog, a faint sweetness at the mat's working edge where the seep feeds it. Her eyes are two great wide lenses, made for the few body-lengths the murk allows, and what they see is a short list and an old one: the mats' faint difference from mud, the shine of the brine line, the blue flash of a startled shrimp, the shiver of her own light off the fog. She feels more than she sees. The internal waves press on her soft parts like weather; the distant thumps arrive as rolls in her skin. Her unit of distance is her body. Her unit of time is the tide. Her unit of history is the outermost band of the anvil — the one band, if a band could be read, that holds her whole life in its margin.

And under her mantle, in a fold of skin that has learned over uncountable generations to be a pocket, she carries the spare. Hold that image, because it is the key to her. The plain keeps everything; that is its only law. She is the one thing on the plain that keeps something back.

Her afternoon — the word the fog prefers, since the dark has no hours — is the lift. When the internal wave eases the murk a hand's width she goes to work. First the mats, and on the mats she is dark and she is stone: she lays herself along the pavement at the mat's edge, papillae down, arms gathered into a shape the water prefers, and waits. The spiral-shelled grazers creep out to graze the bacterial meadow. One of them, sooner or later, creeps across what it takes for a nodule, and tastes a nodule that is wrong, and the wrongness is the last thing it learns. Then the commute — always the commute, even from fifty body-lengths away, always back to the scoop by the seep where the anvil sits in its worn hollow. The anvil is not just a tool. It is a place. She sets the shell on it, switches on the low blue ring, takes up the hammer in her two heavy arms, and strikes.

Tick. A pause, while the fog resettles. Tick-tick.

The rhythm is never the same twice. Each shell has its own thickness, its own stubbornness, and the meter is hers, improvised, worked out against the shell's resistance strike by strike — the only music the valley has heard in an age, and she its only composer. Ten meters off, a blind pale crab stands in the murk at the edge of the light, waiting with the patience of a creature that has learned one thing in its whole soft life: the blue ring means fragments. When she is done the crab comes in for the cracked scraps, and she tolerates it, and does not share — the plain shares for her, since nothing here leaves. Beside the scoop lies the tally: a low heap of cracked spirals, her midden, stacked by her door with a tidiness that has no witness. It is the only cairn in the world, and nothing built it to be one, and the plain keeps it.

Keep the heap in mind, because the heap is also a conversation. Over ages, the shells she cracks answer her: each generation of grazers a hair thicker than the last, the spiral walls adding their narrow rings of shell against the stone percussion. And her wrist answers back — a heavier hammer chosen here, a longer pause there, a strike landed on the whorl instead of the lip. Neither party knows. If the pavement could read its own bands it would find this written in them too, in the margin: the hammer and the shell, ticking against each other, slower than everything, changing at a grain a time.

Her days are mostly this. But the valley has interruptions, and she has a manner for each of them. When the internal waves pass, the fog shivers her light and she stops mid-rise, arms high, stone-apparent, until the swell has gone through and past — then resumes, as if the pause were part of the rhythm. When a chapel-sized elder comes sliding out of the murk on some errand of its own, she does not hide, because hiding is for creatures the world can find; she becomes pavement, light out, spare pulled tighter in its pocket, and the vast wake combs over her and stirs the fog, and afterward she re-forms, grain by grain, at her own pace. She reads the brine pool's shining line at a distance and keeps her margin; once, when a grazer tumbled in and was pickled and kept, and a slow swell later nudged it back up onto the rim, she took it there at the edge and cracked it with a single tick — the cheapest meal of her long life. She skirts the fallen arcade of ribs on the far side of the seep field without stopping; the crinoids wave at her passage too, the current moving everything in unison, courteous to her as it is to stone.

The thumps she treats differently. They come out of the fog from somewhere across the valley, felt rather than heard, a signal with no answer — and she goes still each time, all arms, all attention, the way she does for nothing else. Once — the account can afford one once — she answered. She rose onto the anvil's hollow with no shell beneath her and struck three slow blows, spaced wide, struck as hard as the medium would let her make them carry, and the fog took the sound and did nothing with it, and nothing came back, and she never answered again. Whether the thumps are made by something that could have understood her, the fog does not say. It has also not said whether they are made by her own kind — the deep supports perhaps a handful of her lineage, punctuation scattered across a drowned world — and once, for a single season, there were two rings of blue light low to the ground on the same mat, two stones ticking out of phase, the only duet this valley has ever heard. Then the other went into the fog, toward some current, and the fog kept it, and she went back to solo time.

The benthic storms she knows best of all, because she prepares for them. When the cold salt flood comes sliding down from its distant slope she is already in the lee of the nodule boulder, stones pulled close, anvil dug from its scoop and drawn in against her, spare held tighter than it has ever been held, and she waits out the sleepless day while the world washes its face. Afterward the plain is new: ripple-marks combed flat and combed again, seams resurfaced, her own scoop gone as if never worn. She re-survey by taste and touch, arm by arm, re-finding her map of a place that has been subtly rearranged. And it was after one of these — this is the cracked nodule the fog promised — that she found, at the mat's edge, a stone the flood had broken clean in two, its banded interior open to the water for the first time in an age: rust and black, ring over ring, an archive laid bare. She turned it under her lights for a long time. She has seen the inside of a calendar. Then she tapped it once, listened to the hollowness of its planes — banded stone breaks; banded stone is no hammer — and left it lying face-up, the plain's own memory exposed to the fog, and went and re-set her anvil in a fresh scoop, and the afternoon resumed.

Then comes the season when the account must slow down, and say she.

She finds a hollow beneath a nodule boulder at the mat's margin and seals its mouth with her own body, a curtain of arms, a door made of the doorkeeper. Inside, hung from the hollow's roof by short stalks, are a dozen eggs — pale beads, round as the smallest nodules, each one a counter-calendar with a single ring to lay down. And here she does the only work on the plain done against the plain. The fog settles on everything; what sinks is kept; that is the law, and the law does not pause for intentions. She unkeeps the eggs a grain at a time — arm over arm, fanning them with the slow courteous current, turning them, sponging off the silt that would bury them in their first week. It is endless, silent labor, the reverse of everything the valley does, and she does it without leaving, and she hunts no more, and she lives off herself. Her skin pales past fog-color toward paper. Her lights burn down to coals. The crab keeps its station at the sealed door, blind and patient, the only vigil in the world. A count of years that would be impossible anywhere the storm is heard directly passes here as merely a long thickening, and she stays.

On an inhale, when the internal wave lifts the fog a hand's width, the beads open. The young are small perfect copies of her, and they are dark at once — dark at once, that is the first thing they know — and they go up into the lifted murk, and the wave hands them to whatever current it is handing them to, and the fog closes over them. The fog keeps most of them. That is the price of a world that wastes nothing. She does not leave the hollow. She is the door, still, and the tide's law settles over her the way the fog settles, grain by grain, and what sinks is kept, and she sinks. The crew does in days what patience would have done in years, and afterward what persists of her is small: the husks of the beads; a beak, dark and chitinous, the only hard thing she ever owned, lying like a tiny crescent in the mud where it will outlast her by a while and no more. The spare rolls free of the opened mantle and settles in a seam — one more nodule among the uncountable billions, indistinguishable, its history as her pocket ended as thoroughly as if it had never been a tool. The pavement takes back its lent stone without comment. The anvil remains in its fresh scoop and the hammer beside it, working-face up, and to any future eye they are three ordinary stones, and deep time will sand even the exception: the polish into ordinary shine, the midden into a seam, the scoop into a hollow, the crescent into lime. Nothing here is lost — and nothing here is kept apart, which is the same thing said from the other side. Everything is kept into sameness.

But nothing here leaves, either, and the habit of her is memory, and memory is carried in bodies, not stones. So: elsewhere — another field of seeps, another trembling mat, far off across the drowned world where the fog is also the world — a young one comes to the edge of a mat carrying no stone, because it has no stone yet, because the stones cannot be inherited, only chosen. It tests a candidate with a pick and a pause, listening through its arms for the hollowness of banded stone, and rejects it, and tests another, and finds the flat one, the anvil one, and sets it in a scoop it begins that hour to wear. Then it goes out to the mats, dark, and takes what the mats offer, and returns along its first commute, and switches on a small cold-blue ring low to the ground, and lifts the hammer in two arms that are, even now, a little thicker than the rest.

Tick. A pause, the fog resettling. Tick-tick.

Overhead, the storm rages on without beginning. Below, the plain receives it and lays down its width of quiet. The sound carries ten meters, twenty, farther than anything else in the valley — the loudest thing in the world, here, and barely a sound at all — and then the fog closes over it, and keeps it, the way the fog keeps everything.
