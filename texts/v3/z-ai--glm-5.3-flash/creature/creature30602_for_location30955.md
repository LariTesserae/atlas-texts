Ask who lives in the Shelves and the honest answer is: the water does. The water holds the tenancy, keeps the walls, owns the weather. But nothing that thorough runs unstaffed. The water keeps hands. You can walk a week and never see one, and then see one and not know it — because your first tender will look exactly like a standing stone, grey-cloaked at the rim of a pan, until the standing stone bends and lifts a little silt with a gourd scoop and sets it back where the year can use it.

You will mistake them for stones more than once, and they will not correct you. Being unread is a courtesy here.

They are not tall. Ten thousand years of stairs and crouch-work at weirs has spent their height where it was needed — in the legs, in the back, in the terrible patience of the knees — and what remains is a close-built, dense-boned people, hard to push over and slow to fall. Their skin is the color of wet stone left out in fog, weathered at the knuckles, and on the readers the right knuckle is pickled pale and glassy from a lifetime of tasting seep-lines against it. Their eyes have been trained in the dark, in dry rooms behind drowned doors and in cistern galleries lit by one candle, and they open wider than yours; they see dimness the way you see noon, and squint, hooded in sedge-brim, at the torn blue middays when the cloud tears. Their faces stay plain on purpose. Feeling is not spent there. It goes where the work is — into the hands, which are broad-padded, fingertip-flattened from reading moss and stone, and which a tender will hold out to you, palm up and slightly wet, before ever offering a word.

They walk like something being poured. No heel-strike, no rock of the shoulders; the weight arrives at each stone already settled. The wall-shoes are hide soled with sedge-felt, made to be unheard, because the wall-tops are the roads and the roads are also ears. Everything they carry is gourd, bone, wood, stone — nothing here has ever known iron — and the gourd scoop rides the left hip with the handle worn to the exact shape of one grip, a shape it has held through several owners. The coats are oiled sedge-cloth, re-oiled every spring with sedge wax, and they darken with the years until an old tender walks in something nearly black that beads the fog and sheds it in drops. You can read a person's age in the sheen of the coat, and they let you.

Their ears are the true instrument. A child here is weaned on drip intervals the way other children are weaned on lullabies, and the lullaby is the drip — a cistern-house puts its infants to sleep below the one gallery whose fall is steadiest. By adulthood a tender can walk the black core by ear alone, naming each cistern as they pass it: this one every four breaths, this one nine, this one with the stutter that means the rim needs a stone before winter. Their voices have been worn down to a narrow, level band. A tender's voice is something you could pour into a cup. They do not raise it. The Loud Years are the only history the Shelves keep in terms of sound, and the memory of water behaving like an animal lives in the throat of every person on the plateau; a shout on a wall-top sends people checking their weirs within earshot of the water, and everything is within earshot of the water, if you know how to listen, and they do.

Their language shows the long apprenticeship. Hurry and leak are the same word. A lie is spoken of as a tilt — a false level — and to say *she speaks level* is the highest praise one person gives another. To forgive is *to re-notch*. To grieve is *to seep*: sorrow that comes out of a person slowly, over years, through everything they touch. To gossip is to overflow a notch, gently, and it is not disapproved of; some news must travel somehow, and theirs travels by hydrology.

You cannot arrive in the Shelves unannounced. Your footsteps go down through the walls into the water and move ahead of you, changed, a mile ahead, then five, and by the time you reach a stair-town the rounds have already been adjusted for your weight. Strangers are not surprises here. Strangers are seep-lines. No one will ask where you are going. They will know before you say it, and they will know whether you told the truth, because the water told them first. This is why nothing is locked and nothing needs to be — a thief would have to move water to hide anything, and water tells.

Follow one, if you are quiet enough to be allowed. Sel reads the weeping cliff at Kessel Cross. She wakes before the fog lifts, climbs to the moss-skinned face, and holds her knuckle under the seep for a hundred count. Today the line runs slower than yesterday by a finger's worth over a hundred breaths. A sluice closed upstream, one shelf up, one day ago. Now she reads *how* it slowed: a full stop at the warm outfall would be a death — deaths close a sluice for two days, and the cress goes cold and everyone knows. A wedding opens extra. This is a plain day-slow, a repair or a message, so Sel amends her round and thinks no more of it until noon, when she closes her own sluice a finger's breadth for six hours and lets the answer start walking back upstream. Ten days from now, a hundred miles away, someone will taste a reply against their knuckle and nod. A letter takes a season here and a quarrel takes a decade, and both are considered prompt.

The stair-towns hold most of them. A house is a drawer of dark, a held breath of air, and a drip: dry rooms behind pool-doors, the water standing outside like a black curtain that has agreed, over centuries of tuned masonry, to stay there. There are no chimneys and no windows to speak of. Fire is small and banked into stone, and the smoke goes up through the moss roof so slowly the fog never betrays it. At night a stair-town is visible only from below or above at an angle — small yellow rooms burning quietly under black pools, like lamps in the hull of something patient. A candle behind the water means someone is home. That is their whole word for hospitality, and it is enough.

Children are raised to the level the way other children are raised to speech. The first task is to carry a cupped handful of water the length of a wall-top without spilling, and the walk that takes an adult four hundred paces takes a child an afternoon, and the afternoon is the lesson.
