Who lives in the hollow.

The sound is the census. Everything below can be read out of it, if you read the way the hollow does — bottom up, from the stone outward, from what eats rock to what eats everything else. So: the census, in order of foundation.

**The pasture.** The first citizen is not an animal at all. The films — red, ochre, sulfur-yellow — are one tissue with weather, no individuals and no parents, only fronts. The red eats iron out of the rock's own body; the yellow eats sulfide; where the diets mingle they make the ochres, and where the wall weeps hardest they make the brightest glow. Nothing down here glows in order to be seen. The light is exhaust — what working metabolism throws off the way a working body throws off heat. It is exhaust, not signal. So the hollow's law is not really about light. **Wet shines, dry is dark** is a law about dinner: a bright wall is a wall being eaten, a dark wall is one that has been eaten or is about to be, and the whole visible hollow is a menu drawn in glow.

The film is the only maker in the room. Everything else in this census is either its livestock or its cleanup, and most of the cast is both.

**The quilts.** The grazers come next, because they come everywhere. Segmented, fingernail-small, tens of thousands to a chamber, they cross the glowing walls in loose herds, and their passage is the closest thing the hollow has to weather. Their mouths are combs. A herd at work sounds like a wall being lightly sanded, and the patch they leave is dark — which is the second thing to correct in your picture of this place: the quilts are the only-eyed animals here, and their eyes are almost not eyes, spots that register a single distinction, *glow* from *gone*. Vision in the hollow runs backward. The eye evolved to see light leaving, so what a quilter actually watches is absence — and a wall-walker crossing bright stone is, to a quilter, a hole in the world moving toward it. Everything in the hollow that can see at all spends its life looking for dark.

The quilter year is one breath long. When the hollow inhales and the stone begins to sweat, the herds pour up the walls and feed through the swelling; they mate at its peak and dust their eggs into the runnels, where they hatch inside a day. When the pressure returns and the exhale dries the surfaces, the herds flow down into the last damp seams and the shelter of the shells, and wait out the drought the way surface animals wait out winter — except that their winter lasts an hour, and happens several times a week, forever.

**The organ-shells.** The upholstery of the walls is a nation of sessile condensers, and each one is a machine for drinking the air. Ribs, combs, flutes: the ridged shell catches fog from the moving air, the grooves channel the bead down its own back, one gutter, one mouth at the base. The clicking is drinking — each drop tripping the shell's lip with a tick, ten thousand shells ticking together, and that is the wall's voice.

Below every shell, its runoff wets a fan of stone, and the film farms the fan, and glows. So each shell owns a bright triangle of wall, and the upholstered surface, read rightly, is an irrigation map drawn in light — every animal the god of its own small weather, its catchment shining under it like a held lamp. The young shells are motile and must win a place in the rows; the cementing race happens at the height of the inhale, and most lose. A shell that finds a gap inherits a fan nobody farmed and grows fat on it. Shells that die are not removed. An empty shell holds damp longer than bare stone, and its chambers are let to whoever needs them: quilts sheltering out the dry, swarm drinking at its lip, cutpurses — the small soft thieves that run the rows licking the grooves, and whose approach is why the shells sometimes snap shut, hoarding their catch at the cost of the next few drops. The rate of snapping up and down the wall is one of the ways the hollow keeps count of itself.

**The pool, and the congregation.** The coin of black glass keeps the deep bank. Its rim glows where the film crusts the calcite; its skin is the only pasture in the hollow that grows floating. The skaters walk it — long-legged, dimpling, reading the skin's thickness by how far their feet sink before the surface accepts them — and under them the pale swimmers graze that same skin from beneath, thinning it to lace, which is why the pool is black glass from above and a faint lit ceiling from below. They are one animal, two lives, divided by a membrane: the larvae graze below, rise through the skin at maturity, and never go back down. The pool is the hollow's deep bank, and everything about it is patient.

Most patient of all is the congregation under the eleven-breath tap. Shallow, funnel-bodied, mouths upturned, fused to the floor at the splash point, they do not move and have not for a span no one has words for. Each drop builds them half a hair of terrace, mineral and body interleaved, so the oldest sit highest — on stairs made of their ancestors, ascending at the rate of one drip every eleven of your breaths. They are becoming furniture the honest way: by sediment. When the tap stops, they do not leave. They slow until chemistry calls it waiting, and when the hollow inhales again and the ceiling taps them, they are already there. They have never once been anywhere else.

**The swarm.** The air itself is stocked. Clouds of tiny fliers crop the film-dust suspended in every breath, and their summed wingbeat is not an addition to the roar but its carrier — the continuous tone under all the percussion, the thing you feel in your back teeth and mistake for silence when it stutters. They never land. They drink on the wing, skimming runnels at speed; they live days; when they die they fall into the dust they fed on, which is one reason the hollow's air is sweeter than a room this crowded has any right to be. Their restlessness — the endless circling, the tightening and spilling, the way the whole population leans as one body toward this wall and then that — was the last thing in the hollow to be explained. Hold that thought. The explanation is the last entry.

**The ceiling.** The drinkers hang and lap the beeding stone. The roost-shells hang beside them and molt constantly, their castoffs falling as rare shell-snow, gone within a day. The hour after a molt is the only hour in the hollow when anything is defenseless: the shell cracks, the animal inside is soft and pink and silent, and the listeners know that hour and hunt by it. So the molt is timed to the tide — a roost-shell splits at the roar's peak, when every ear in reach is busy, and hardens before the ebb. The ceiling is the hollow's nursery, and it delivers its young into noise the way other places deliver them into daylight.

**The second mouth.** Under everything, the ledger's keepers: pale, jointed, blind, everywhere beneath the crowd. They take a shell-snow apart in a day, a dead quilt in an hour, a dead listener in a week, and their castings are the film's fertilizer, which closes the circle with no gap in it. The dust you stand in is measured in generations because it *is* generations — ground past name, spread, re-eaten. The floor of the hollow is everyone who has ever lived there, processed to a fine pale powder. In a closed room the dead do not depart; they change address, animal to animal, forever.

**The listeners.** The predators walk the walls on legs too long, body carried high off wet stone — dry stance is quiet stance, and a hunter that cannot hear its own feet is already dead. They know the hollow by its reverb. Every chamber has its signature echo, every scallop its note, and the young spend their first season doing nothing but listening, learning the skyline of sound the way other creatures learn stone and sky. A hunt, when it comes, is the hush that travels — prey that navigates by echo going still in a spreading ring — and then the strike: a catch-claw snapping once at closing distance. In a world of listeners, that snap is not a sound. It is a weapon. It whites out the acoustics of everything within a body-length, and in that shared blinding the claw closes. A geography made of sound can be robbed of its map in an instant; the listener's whole body is built around that theft. They are polite at a distance — they will pace a stranger for hours, reading it by echo, filing it under *too large to eat, too warm to ignore* — and they do not quarrel among themselves; each owns a chamber the way a voice owns a vowel, and they do not cross. When one dies, it goes quiet over days, and the second mouth takes it apart over more, and for a week one corner of the hollow runs hotter than any market. Everything here dies loudly and is eaten quickly. Even the thing that made dying loud.

**The keel.** And above all of it, the bass.

Here is what the long surveys learned. The note comes through stone more than through air, and it moves: tracked over decades, it advances a body-length in a year. The oldest measurement in the hollow is not a height or a depth. It is a sound, and it has a heading, and the heading changes over a human lifetime.

The keel is an animal. It is a grazer, and it grazes on the scale the chamber was carved for — a soft vast body tensed between mineral weights, moving by slow contraction, the low note simply the sound of its feeding, the way a bell's note is the sound of its bronze. It carries its own pasture on its back: the oldest film in the hollow grows on the keel's skin, cropped slowly, forever. And when it passes along an upper gallery it grazes the standing mat down to bare wet rock, and behind it the film blooms young and rich, and everything downstream of that bloom is richer for a season. It is the hollow's plow. The chorus re-tunes around the note not out of reverence but the way gulls keep a plow — the note marks where the new grass is coming. The hollow does not worship its keel. It follows it.

Now the swarm's restlessness has its explanation. The swarm is the keel's brood: clouds of unlanded children cast into the air to live fast on dust while they search for a wall that will take them. One in some enormous number finds an empty gallery, cements itself, and begins the centuries — growing, slowing, thickening, until one day it sounds, and the note it sounds is the note it spent its quick years flying toward. The hollow's continuous wingbeat is the sound of a giant's children looking for somewhere to become it. The surveys count the bass notes and find a handful, one to a deep gallery; when two sound close together the whole hollow re-tunes twice, and for a breath the place holds a chord. When a keel dies, it does not fall — it is wedged where it lived — and its death is a landscape event: years of the second mouth at full tide, the brightest span the local walls ever know, generations of everything fattened on the one body too large to be eaten quickly. Its mineral weights the scavengers cannot touch. The stone grows over those and keeps building, so that the deepest core of more than one of the holding columns is ancestor, not geology. The hollow's architecture includes its dead, and the dead are still holding up the dark.

**And you.** If you stand still long enough, the hollow files you under weather: warm, dry, smooth, loud — a brief spring that walks. The condenser on your sleeve will cement there if you grant it the hour. The walkers keep your file open as long as you hold still, unable to close it. When you leave, you take nothing, because you cannot; nothing here leaves. What you leave behind is a cold patch on a wall, a day of the second mouth, and then the film, and then nothing at all — the local entry in the ledger, closed the usual way: eaten, and then eaten again.

So: who lives in the hollow. A pasture that has never once stopped eating stone. Herds that see only darkness, and watch for it. Irrigators that drink the air. A skin with a lace under it. A congregation becoming terraces one drop at a time. A molting ceiling. A mouth that keeps the books. A few dozen listeners with their maps of echo. And above, a handful of keels calling *here* to their scattered millions. All of them ears first. All of them clauses in one sentence spoken since before there was anyone to hear it spoken —

— and the census adds only this: it calls; the hollow adjusts; that was always the whole of the contract. Everyone here has signed it.
