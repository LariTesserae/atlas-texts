## Who Lives on the Mat

The tenants of this place fall into three nations, and they barely overlap: the swarm inside the skin, the wings above the bone, and the people on the ribs.

**The swarm comes first, because it is the majority.** Most of what lives here has never been seen by any eye that mattered to it. Inside the felted architecture of the cushions runs a vertical civilization: ants in nations that may each be older than the harvesters' arrival in these highlands, tending rows of scale-insects the way people below keep herds, milking them for honeydew, moving them between pasture-plates of the cushion when one is grazed out. Beetles by the fistful, armored and unhurried. Springtails past counting — the true biomass of the plain, the gray noise beneath every other noise. And the caterpillars, the shepherds' sheep: shearing-jawed, methodical, pruning the cushion's living surface in neat arcs so it regrows denser. The mat needs them the way a fleece needs shearing; the caterpillars would starve without the mat; neither knows the other's name for the arrangement, if either has a name for anything.

The dusk roar is their parliament. Every cushion is a speaker, every gallery a hall, and at dark the whole plain opens session at once — males signalling across the galleries, territory and courtship conducted in a language of rasps that stacks into that sibilant boil. They are not loud individually. They are loud the way rain is loud: by number. And they are cowardly in exactly one direction — down from the sky. The moving hole of silence under a raptor's shadow is their whole cosmology made visible: danger as a darkness with an outline, silence as the shape of death passing.

The locusts of the tide are the swarm's other face — the same citizens, gone militant. Every few years some trigger in the margins turns scattered grasshoppers into a superorganism, and the plain hosts its own invasion: a brown weather-front with a deeper roar, moving at walking pace, eating the young growth in a moving province. Then it is gone, the raptors grow fat as lords for a season, and the mat closes its scars the way skin does. The harvesters do not fight the tide. You cannot fight weather. They mark it, endure it, and harvest peat thicker in the aftermath, when the young margins are tender.

**Above the bone live the wings.** A handful of raptor species, riding the impossibly thin air at heights that read as specks, their circles described in the silence-holes they drag across the mat below. They are the only creatures on the plain that can afford height, and they know it; everything about their behavior is the behavior of landlords. They do not hunt on the wing over the mat much — the swarm is underground, the caterpillars hidden, and the visible prey is scarce. What they take is what crosses open bone: the display-birds on the ribs, the occasional careless whistler's dog-animal, the tide-locusts in their season, when the raptors descend in numbers and gorge until they can barely lift. The display-birds are the ribs' own: crow-sized, loud, territorial about bare stone, performing their ladder-dances and their territorial fights on the whalebacks exactly because the ribs are the only stage and the only food-bearing ground they trust. They nest in the cairn-lines sometimes, which has made certain cairns half shrine, half henhouse, and the harvesters have decided this is luck.

In the tarns: dragonflies, the day-shift predators of the one quiet place, ringing the mirror-flat water at dawn with the only sound the mat's tenants cannot make — the sound of wings over open water. Nothing from the mat's interior visits the tarns by day. Whether the dragonflies hunt them at the edge or whether the insects simply refuse the open, no one has worked out; the harvesters say the water is where the plain keeps its silence.

**And then the people.** The harvesters come up one season in three, in small bands, walking distances their eyes lie about and their hours do not. They are low people, in the plain's own terms — they build nothing above knee-height, they camp on bone not skin, they move between the ribs by cairn-line and dawn-fog. Their culture is shaped entirely by the two laws the land imposes: *water falls, never runs*, and *the mat is an animal, not a crop*.

The second law is the deep one. They cut peat-cubes only from the young margins, never from an ancient cushion, and their reasoning is not superstition exactly — it is observation stretched to the length of a people's memory. They know how long a scar takes to close. They know a felled old cushion is a mountain felled, that it would not return within the lifetime of a nation, and they have organized their whole economy around patience: take the young, leave the old, come back in three years. Their tools are stone, waxed with cushion-fat, and their one treasure is a hammered-copper blade passed between bands like an heirloom and a reliquary at once — an object from the world beneath the cloud-sea, which some of them have never seen and all of them describe wrong.

They live inside the roar. It shaped their throats: whistled speech, long falling phrases pitched to carry over the stridulation, a language you can hear a dot-away. Silence, to them, is not an absence but a reading — a shadow moving means a raptor means *stand still, be dull*. They read the moving holes of hush the way lowland people read clouds. Children are taught the silence-vocabulary before the whistle-vocabulary: what a converging hush means, what a silence that *doesn't* move means (something dead under the mat; do not dig there), what it means when the roar stops everywhere at once, which has happened, they say, twice in memory, and neither story ends well.

They sleep on the skin — warmer than stone, warmer than anything — and wake into the breath-fog, soaked and blind, navigating by cairn-whistles and by the sound-texture of the roar, which tells them where they are the way a sailor knows water by its chop. They know the dawn fog is the plain exhaling what it hoarded from them; they say the mat drinks the sky and breathes it back out, and that they are permitted to live on it because they are small and take little. Their dead go into the skin — cairns on the ribs at first, then abandoned to the mat, which swallows them over a century into slight green mounds indistinguishable from the ground. This is not a euphemism they use; it is the actual theology. The people below the cloud-sea, they say, are buried *in* the earth. The harvesters are buried *as* it.

Under the old cushions, in the peat-pages, their own deep history is kept without their knowing: pollen layers, ash bands, thin dark lines where something burned long ago — a tide-year beyond memory, or a fire someone set, or something set by whoever cut the two-hundred-pace wall whose purpose is gone. The wall-builders may have been their ancestors; may have been someone else entirely; the lichen on the stones is older than any language now spoken nearby, and the harvesters' own account is honest about this: *someone built it, and the plain did not need it, and kept them anyway.*

So: a tenement the size of a country, tenanted by insects who have never seen the sky, birds who see everything and touch nothing, and a thin scattering of patient people who live on the furniture, whistle through the roar, and understand — better than the insects, who don't understand, and the raptors, who don't need to — that they are guests on the skin of something alive, and that the rent is smallness, and the lease is forever.
