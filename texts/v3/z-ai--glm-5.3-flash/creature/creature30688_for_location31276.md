Start the census at the top, because that is where the country starts: in air. Nothing lives at the very crown of the spire. The stars attend every night but they are not residents — nothing here can hold them, or hold anything. The highest life in this place, on any given night, is a spore: lifted out of the pocket on a vent's updraft, airborne for perhaps eleven seconds, carried a few meters toward the black and then let go. It sets an altitude record no one intended, for an audience of unwinking stars, and then it falls back into the fog it came from. The greatest journey available in this country lasts eleven seconds. The epic hero of the summit is something smaller than a grain of pollen, and its epic happens entirely overhead of the only things that could have witnessed it.

Below that, the water — which is a resident the way a commuter is a resident. It has spent years here without ever once staying the night. Its whole biography is a list of vehicles: vapor, then fog, then condensate, then rime, and then the ash takes it. It is the only inhabitant with a standing reservation to leave. Every living thing in the pocket is downstream of a tenant that never unpacks.

Below the water, below the frost-shattered rim, below the ice roses that grow and graze on fog and put on weight and finally calf off the rocks like small white glaciers — the only wildlife at the rim is made of ice — below all that, deeper than the deepest root will ever reach, lives the largest thing in the country and the only one that will never see it. A kilometer down sits the buried magma body, a mass of rock behaving like a liquid, wearing the whole spire as a shell. It cannot look up. Nothing in it will ever look up. And yet every leaf in the garden is downstream of its mood. It is the landlord, the furnace, the heart that does not know it has a body above it. The rent is collected in steam. And it builds while it heats: silica precipitating out of the channels, walls sealing themselves into rigid white pipe, constrictions that whistle. The deep plumbing grows the way bone grows, without consultation. Even the minerals here act like organisms — sulfur accreting in waxen slabs, sinter terraces building themselves plate by plate like coral. Below the garden, geology is doing an impression of biology, and it is a good impression.

Then the natives. The true natives, the first ones, the ones who arrived within a season of the eruption and have never left: the bacteria. They are the only residents who eat the mountain's breath directly. Hydrogen sulfide — the smell of struck matches — is their dinner, and they have it in unlimited supply, and they have flourished on it into mats that flow like broth down every drainage line, cream and white and rust-orange crusts sealing the warm ground. When they die they do not go anywhere. They become the ground. The soil of this country is a cemetery of its founders; every root in the pocket is standing, near or far, on the compacted generations of the things that ate the vent exhaust and made a floor out of their dead.
