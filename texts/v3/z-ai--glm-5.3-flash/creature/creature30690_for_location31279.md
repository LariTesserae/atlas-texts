The mountain has been in balance — and whatever lives inside a balance long enough becomes one of its weights. That is the first thing you understand about the people of the Stair, before you ever see one: the place is too tended to be empty. The stone lips of the pools are too even. The channels run too straight. The bloom grows in stripes, in rows, in boundaries — rust-pink here, bone-white there, olive-black where it has been left alone on purpose. Someone has been arranging this moisture for a very long time. The terraces are not geography. They are inheritance.

At first you mistake the inhabitants for part of the rock. Figures stand out in the shallows, bent low over the pads, so colored by their work that they seem to be outcrops that learned to move. Then one straightens, and you see the tide-line — a rust-pink stain across the belly where the body wades, like a watermark on paper — and you understand that what lives here is human, or close enough that it matters more what it has become than what it was.

They are broad-footed and short-striding, built for slick stone, toes splayed from a lifetime of gripping. Their hands never fully un-wrinkle; the water has claimed them at the knuckles and simply never given them back. Their hair, stiffened with lime-water, stands up in white crests like little lips of grown stone, so that many of them seem to wear the mountain on their heads even hatless. Their eyes are slits, permanently narrowed against a light that has never once been soft, and they look at you the way everyone looks at a stranger here — slightly downward, slightly squinting, as if you were a brightness that had wandered in. Their skin is soft everywhere, softened too long in warmth, and it smells faintly of iron and warm chalk. You will not meet anyone from the Stair who is dry. The word exists in their language. It is used mostly about the dead.

Shade is the whole of their wealth, because shade is the one thing the mountain will not give. Every roof is a promise. Their houses are squat stone structures with deep overhanging eaves and low doors, walls thick enough to sweat deliberately, floors of smooth warm rock where a family sleeps at night like lizards on a heated shelf. Inside, a tame sunbeam moves on a long copper arm — cold-hammered native metal, mirror-brightened with lime and snail-powder — folding the terrible light down onto a cooking plate, then away again, on a hinge of bone. Wood is unknown to them and they do not miss it, because they do not know what they are missing; bone does everything. Bone ladders, bone looms for weaving felt-strip into cord, bone doorframes, bone frames holding copper mirrors at every angle the sun can be caught from. A rich terrace is one with many mirrors and one deep, dry room. A very rich family is one whose children have slept a whole night without touching water.

Everything they eat is the stain. The bloom is their wheat, their pasture, their thatch, their felt, their paint. They husband it like a crop with many breeds: a mild sweet strain for the bread, pressed into pans and baked on mirror-plates and in spring-heated stone pots until it comes out as dense brown pan-bread with a mineral sweetness; a long-fibered strain for felting, cut, trodden, dried into the stiff cloth of their hats and sandals and saddle-cloths; a coarse strain grown as living roofing, grazed back weekly like a hedge; a slow acid strain they lay along raw rock in thin deliberate lines, letting it soften the stone over years until a new terrace can be opened with hand-tools — they do not quarry the mountain, they cultivate it soft. There are pearl snails in the warm channels grazing the bloom, and pale shrimp in the deeper pools, and a low wide beast the lowlanders call the stair-ox: a wallowing, short-legged grazer with a bony visor built over its eyes like a hat grown from the skull, a voice like a far-off drum. Its dung feeds the thin terraces; its milk, curdled with brine, becomes white slabs of cheese; its broad back carries the pack-trains down the long descent to the salt sinks, felt saddle-cloths steaming in the cold morning haze, and brings salt back up — the one thing the mountain, so wet, cannot give them.

They go down rarely, and they are remembered when they do. Lowland traders know them by the rust tide-lines and the white hair and the squint, and by their walk, which is the walk of people for whom every surface has always been either wet or worse. The lowlands frighten them more than they will politely say — not the people, the silence. Dry air, they feel, is air that has stopped breathing. A stair-ox fresh up from the sinks will drink for an hour.

Their customs are all made of water. To marry is to grow stone: a courting couple is given an unfinished lip on an unused terrace, and they build it together over the long years of the courtship, fitting and smoothing, and when the lip is done the first water is let over it, and the first blush of rust-pink bloom on their own stone is the wedding. To swear an oath is to swear on still water; a lie, the elders say, will make the surface shiver, and the whole court watches the pool rather than the liar. The year is read in the banding of the old lips — white rings for fat years, thin rust rings for the lean ones — and the ring-readers run their fingers along the stone like the blind reading a page, because on the Stair history is something you touch. Etiquette is a matter of shadow: you do not step into another person's shade, ever; a guest is someone you agree to shade; at noon, the politest thing two people can do is stand together in silence under one hat's width of dark.

They are not a loud people, but they are a musical one, and their instruments are the mountain. At dusk the herders sing against the mile-long seepage cliff and answer their own echoes in two-part songs made of one throat. Lithophones of graded stone bars hang in the school-shelters; the snail-shell horns low along the channels; and children play the pools, cupping and spilling rhythms, learning early the notes water keeps. They have a word for the sound a fire makes, used in songs and lullabies, though no one alive has ever heard it. The grandmothers say the springs gurgle it in their sleep. For the whole theology of the Stair rests on a buried burning: the water is warm, and no rain has ever fallen here, and so they believe the mountain remembers being on fire — that a great heat sleeps somewhere under the terraces, old and banked and out of breath, and everything warm here is warmed on its memory. The sun is the only fire they acknowledge in the open sky: one scar in the burnt lid of the world, kept, wisely, at a distance. Children who ask what would happen if the sky-fire ever fell are told, flatly: it would find us, and the mountain would hiss once, and go out.
