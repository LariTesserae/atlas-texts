Ask who lives in the garden-room and the obvious answer fails twice. The keepers do not live here — they visit, two at a time, at slack water, and leave by the gourd-line before the ebb. And the garden is not the whole answer either, because a census of this room has to count things that arrived by decision, things that arrived by seeping, and things that were here before there was a here. The roster is short, and strange, and it is growing in one direction only.

Start with the planted, since they were first by invitation. The ribbon-kelp are the principal residents, and they are the first of their line ever to be raised indoors. Not one of them has felt surf, or a moon-tide dragging across its fronds; the only tide they know arrives by appointment, through the sluice, on the flood, at the hour the builders chose. They are house-raised, and they grow like it — fast in the kept warmth, faintly gaudy, the way greenhouse things are, each blade gaining a finger's length between visits so that the keepers re-tie them like children outgrowing clothes. The knots are flat, deliberate, undone and redone. On the slate in the tool niche, beside the visit-tallies, someone keeps lengths. It is a doorframe with pencil marks on it, cut in stone, for a crop that is also a family.

The red laceweed keeps the temperament of an heirloom. It is slower than the kelp, more deliberate, and it holds its russet-crimson the way a singer holds a note it has no intention of releasing — the single word of strong color in a room otherwise spoken in cream and grey. There is a small dishonesty under it that the keepers permit themselves: packed into its mortar bed, sledded out on the same float, came a fist of its home reef, a pebble of actual shore. It is the only wild shore in the room, and it is smuggled, and everyone who matters knows, and nobody says.

Then there are the escapees. The kelp are old enough to let go of spores, and on still afternoons the water carries them — that glittering smoke, every mote lit. Some settle where they should: in the troughs, by the stakes. Some settle where they shouldn't, on the bench, in the swept channel, on the slate's edge, and the keepers rub them off with a thumb, weeding a garden weeks old, which delights them and alarms them in equal measure, because a nursery that is already escaping its troughs is a nursery that is working. And one sporeling, standing un-tied and un-staked on the east bench, nobody has scraped. It is the first resident of the room that no one decided on. That is its entire biography. A keeper will pause over it, thumb hovering, and find something else that needs doing.

Below the plants, the seeped. The woven screen at the sluice turns back everything larger than a dust-mote — and a dust-mote is precisely what it admits. So the room has citizens the keepers did not choose and mostly have not met: a golden-brown film of diatoms gathering at the head of the channel where the current slackens, wiped away by a thumb on every visit and back again by the next; a smear of invisible life in the sieved sand of the joints. The thumb-war over the film is the room's longest-running argument, and lately the film has been coming back thinner along one wandering lane, a clean grazed trail like a signature no one admits to. On the slate, under the tallies, someone has scratched a question mark.

Because something lives here that no one planted. A snail the size of a peppercorn came through the weave as a dot on the flood, rode the current, and settled under the lip of the screen, where the fiber has grown a pelt of film worth eating. At night, when the room exhales and goes glassy, it grazes. The room was built to contain no death and no hunger — no bones, no shells, no barnacle, no worm — and now it contains a hunger the size of a thumbnail, and the hunger is doing the sweeping. The rules say no fish. The sea has read the rules narrowly. The event of the season the keepers watch the front door for has already happened; it came in as dust through the weave, and only the slate's question mark knows it.

The light is a resident too, the only one that commutes by the roof. It arrives at dawn over the crown of the vault and lives in the room all day as the caustic-net — that crawling herd of light-cells, sharpening and fraying and re-forming on every surface. The keepers herd it. A twist of a skylight lid and a column of noon walks from the empty trough to the kelp, the way you'd carry a lamp from room to room; the blades lean after it, all week, all of them facing the three places where the sky comes in. At night it mostly leaves, but not entirely: three pale threads of moon come down the shafts and the net slows to a crawl. The light sleeps on the premises, lightly.

And there is the breath — the most domestic fact in the whole place. At the crown of the vault, in the pocket the builders corbelled shut, one barrel of air has been sealed since completion day. Some fraction of it was breathed first by the masons, and it has been passed along since, cup to cup: every keeper who rises into the pocket adds two lungs' worth and takes away the same, so that everyone who has ever worked in this room has breathed everyone else's used air. It is the parlor. Speech lives there — a word rolls the vault twice and comes back flattered, a laugh is a weather event — which may be why the keepers save all their talking for it, and why every argument conducted there sounds twice as convincing to both sides.

Then there is the resident that was built to look like a sleeping animal and has taken the role seriously: the room. It has a heartbeat — the swell-boom arriving through the stone at the pace of a resting heart, felt in the ribs. It is warm-blooded, among stones: it drinks heat all day and gives it back all night, which is the whole reason the garden chose it over any shore. It has growing pains — the noon clicks of the warming crown, the small talk of settling masonry, bones still finding their fit. And it is still growing in a stricter sense, because mortar made of lime and volcanic ash does not surrender to seawater, it knits: crystals threading joint to joint for years, the vault literally consolidating around its own emptiness. The proud squeeze-ridges along the beds may be a chore postponed, or baby fat. Nobody is in a hurry to shave a child.

Last, the keepers — who are not residents, and are family. Their residence is two breaths long, endlessly repeated: down, work, up, down. The room wears their habits the way a path wears feet. The swept channel is exactly a forearm wide because it was swept by forearms; the ties are flat because particular thumbs tied them; the water holds the shape of the last hands for a while after they go, until the flood takes it — the only memory the room allows itself. They come always two, partly for the air in the pocket, and partly because a thing this new should never be interpreted by one person alone.

And the empty fourth trough is not empty. It is full of the loudest resident of all, which is an argument. One party wants a mat of seagrass — a meadow with no shore under it, the room's entire thesis, rooted. The other wants a sponge: the first animal, one that never moves and so breaks no rule; and anyway, the sponge's advocates say every visit, the snail has already cracked the door on hunger. The debate lives in the airwell, where the vault repeats each point twice, and the trough itself waits below — smooth, swept, deliberately aimed-at by noon — a seat kept warm at a table.

So the census stands: a household of house-raised kelp; one held note of red; a spore-haze with one unchosen citizen; a film; a snail; a herd of light; a shared barrel of breath; two visiting hands; and a young animal made of stone, growing into its own name. Every name on the list is weeks old. The list only lengthens. The keepers tally and go home; the residents stay down, in the warm, under the honey-colored lens, and grow.
