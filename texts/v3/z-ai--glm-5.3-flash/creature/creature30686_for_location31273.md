# Who Lives in the Hollow

**The people — if that word still fits — are few.** Perhaps two hundred at any given time, living in the topmost two strata of the stacked cities, and they are old in a way that has nothing to do with age. Their skin is pale as the stone, their eyes large and lightly silvered, adapted to gray-gold light. They speak in a language worn down almost to gesture — most of their communication is a hand-shape, a tilt of the head, a slow exhale. Loudness, to them, is a category error. Words, when they come, arrive at the volume of the Hollow's permanent *shhh*: you lean in to hear them the way you'd lean toward a shell.

**They are the descendants of the builders, and they know it the way you know your own hands.** Somewhere in their long inheritance is the understanding that their ancestors made a decision — stopped building houses for themselves and started building corridors for lizards, roost-shelves for moths, hooks and niches and terraces for everything that would come after. No one teaches this as history. It's simply *what walls are for*. When a person in the Hollow builds, they build habitat. When they repair, they repair for creatures. The idea of a room that serves only its occupant has the same strangeness to them as fire.

**The keeping is their whole vocation, and it isn't called work.** Someone sweeps the moth-ledges of fallen dust. Someone turns the mirror-panels each season, a fraction of a degree, following the slow drift of the sky. Someone walks the eastern terraces at dusk and stands aside for the shelled migration — not directing it, never directing it, just *accompanying*, the way a lighthouse accompanies ships. There is no hierarchy. The role passes by who happens to be standing there when the task presents itself. A child learns to step around the tide before learning to speak.

**They do not eat the animals.** This took the botanists a long time to accept. The residents eat the air-drinking greens — the broad-leaved cascades, cultivated in strips and harvested leaf by leaf so the walls never thin — and a pale fungus they grow on the sealed bricks of the dead layers, and almost nothing else. The fauna are not livestock and not pets. They are *co-residents*. There is no word in their language that separates "person" from "dweller," and no word for "pet," because that would imply one kind of thing belongs to another, and nothing in the Hollow belongs to anything.

**They know the creatures individually** — not by name exactly, but by habit. The old lizard with the chipped shell-scar who sleeps on the third mirror-landing. The moth-cluster that unfurls left-spiraling, always left, and has for as long as anyone can remember. Longevity among the residents runs long — the warmth, the calm, the absence of any need to struggle — and a person might share a gallery with the same generations of lizards for eighty years. They grieve when the creatures die. They brick small niches for them, sometimes, the way their ancestors bricked doorways: gently, for the sake of what comes after.

**Children are raised by the whole throng.** Human children and the young of everything else grow up interwoven — a toddler napping in a warm pile of shelled things, a rock-lizard hatchling carried in the crook of an arm until it's ready to cling to the walls on its own. The children are taught almost nothing deliberately. They absorb the two rules the way they absorb breath: *nothing flees*, and *fire would drink the air*. That's the whole catechism. Everything else is imitation.

**They almost never leave.** Some do, occasionally — a young one climbs the worn stair, crosses the plateau, walks out into the loud, sparse, violent world above to see it, and comes back changed and quiet, or doesn't come back. There is no ceremony around this. The Hollow doesn't hold anyone; holding is not in its nature. But those who go describe the same thing afterward, if they return: the surface feels *underpopulated*. Too much empty space between bodies. Too much fleeing. They miss the sound — the continuous soft rain-that-isn't-rain, ten thousand small lives rearranging themselves in the dark.

**And below, in the sealed layers, the dead layers — there are others.** Not ghosts. The residents would find that word silly. But things that have taken the bricked-over cities for their own: nesting colonies, deep-gallery swarms, pale slow organisms that have evolved inside ten thousand years of undisturbed masonry and exist nowhere else on earth. The residents know they're there the way you know there's someone sleeping in the next room. They don't open those doors. They built them shut on purpose, generations ago — for the things that would come after. And the things came after, and filled them, and are still filling them, stratum by stratum, down through the dark.

The Hollow keeps itself, in the end. The people are less its residents than its **ongoing courtesy** — the last gesture of a long chain of builders who decided, ten thousand years ago, that the highest thing a city could be was a shelter, and then kept deciding it, quietly, forever.
