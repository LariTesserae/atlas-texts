Ask who lives in the Undergrown and you will get a census with one name repeated down the page. *Green. Green. Green.* And then, at the bottom, in smaller handwriting: some moths, a few birds, the tumbers, the keepers — and, someone always adds, in different ink, *whatever is downstairs.*

That census is more honest than it looks. It is not a list of tenants. It is a list of guests, arranged by how long they've been allowed to stay.

## The Green, Which Is Everyone

The first tenant is the moss, and the moss is not scenery. It breathes — slow, visible breaths you can time against your own. It swallows; at each drink the moss darkens half a shade before it drinks, a motion the keepers call its *gulp*. And it learns. The paths that thread the vaults are not cut; they are *grown* — the moss yielding where four hundred years of feet have asked it to, hardening into smooth pale turf along the routes people favor, rising into low stairs where the old pass often. Where travelers lean, the moss grows dense as upholstery. Keepers say the moss remembers you. Stay away a year, come home, and the patch by your door is brighter than the wall beside it. Recognition, or just a husbandry no one witnesses — the keepers argue about this the way other people argue about theology.

The pillar-willows are the next rank of tenancy, and the keepers are careful with the word they use. You do not live *in* a willow. You are *kept* by one. A station's door is the tree's grafted knuckle; a station's chimney is its breathing; and when a keeper dies and no successor comes, the willow heals the doorway shut within the year. You can walk an old trunk and read its history in the bark — a smooth scar here, a soft sealed arch there — a ledger of houses that were once alive with lamplight and are now, technically, tissue.

Whether the green below is one thing or many, nobody knows. The keepers of any given vault will tell you each willow is a person and the mat is a crowd. The keepers of the next vault will tell you the mat is one person and the willows are its fingers. The seed-runners, who see the most, shrug and say there is only one Undergrown and everything green is its winter coat.

And there is the matter of the pools. The green negotiates with everything — with light, with cold, with people, with gravity — and the pools are the single place it says no. The willows will not approach them. Frost-flowers ring the black water and open at each drink and close after, like something counting. The keepers draw from the lip only, at arm's length, without speaking. The tumbers will stand a full day thirsty rather than drink there. Whatever the pools are, the oldest resident of the Undergrown wants no part of them, and everyone else has decided to take that seriously.
