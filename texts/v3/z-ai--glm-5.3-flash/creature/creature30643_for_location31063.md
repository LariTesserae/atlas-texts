Birds fly, but low — the air is thin and viciously cold up here, wings have to be paid for in effort, and so the birds of the Yearling have made their peace with the ground. The common ones are runners, heavy in the body, grouse-built, all breast-muscle and no poetry: they sprint in short furious bursts and then whir along a meter above the felt, sailing as far as momentum allows before landing again. They nest *in* the felt — not on it, in it. A nesting pair lines a chamber a hand's width beneath the surface and the mat closes over them, and from outside all you see is a pinhole, warm-breathing in winter, and hear, if you stand still, the muffled domestic racket of a household under your boots. In the cold months the pinhole villages are the easiest thing to find on the tableland: whole acres of felt freckled with little steam-breaths, like a hide alive with fleas.

Bigger birds hang over the breathing spots and do not flap. More on the breathing spots in a moment.

## What lives in the skin

The felt is not a lawn. It is three-dimensional, and everything that lives inside it lives the way mites live in a pelt.

Grub-things comb through the root-weave by the million, grazing the growing edge. Mole-things — pale, squinting, nearly blind — tunnel the mat's lower layers and turn it over, and without them the Yearling would choke on its own age: old felt compacts, goes black, stops breathing, and the mole-things are the ones who keep the hide aerated the way a good groom keeps a coat from matting. The herders do not trap them. There is a plain sentence they use about it: *the ground has its own people; leave them their work.*

And here is the thing the botanically minded eventually work out, standing on the tableland with a cut sample in their hands: follow the weaving far enough and you may never find a boundary between one plant and the next. Root is woven into root into root. It is entirely possible — no one has walked far enough or dug deep enough to rule it out — that the entire mat is one individual. One organism the size of a country, wearing the shape of a landscape the way an animal wears its hide.

The herders are not much interested in this question. Ask them whether the Yearling is one thing or many and they will look at you the way a shepherd looks at someone asking whether a dog is one thing or many. It is alive the way an animal is alive, which to them is the more serious kind of alive, and that is the end of it.

## The under-country

Beneath the felt, the basalt is hollow. Tubes and blisters and long open seams run through the table's bones, and because the rock is young the water has not left: melt comes down through the mat in a thousand invisible threads and runs away inside, in rivers that no sky has ever seen.

The rivers have fish. Blind, slow, white as the inside of an egg, and — the herders are firm about this — not strange, just young. Everything here is young. The rock has not had time to darken, the seams have not had time to close, the fish have not had time to bother growing eyes. In the blister-chambers the felt's roots hang through cracks like the roots of a willow at a wellhead, and pale shrimp-things sift the drip, and insects farm grey fungus gardens on the root-ends in the dark. The herders fish some of the wells — a line dropped through a felt plug into a black warm pocket — and eat what comes up, flavorless and reliable, but never more in a day than the well gives. You are drinking the Yearling's breath when you drink here, they will tell you. Do not gulp.
