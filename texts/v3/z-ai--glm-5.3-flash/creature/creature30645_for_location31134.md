Anemone meadows open all at once when a breath comes beneath them — ten thousand soft lamps kindling in a traveling wave you can watch move for miles, a whole province of hands deciding, together, to receive. The breaths are the weather of this world. Nothing else changes here with any appetite: no storm, no season but the coin's long turn, no wind to speak of, ever. So when the warm throat opens far below and the column begins its hours-long climb, everything arranges itself around it the way a town arranges itself around a festival. Spores go up on it like embers. The warm-blooded ride it, coasting in the lift with their frills spread, climbing a mile for the price of a shiver. And the spawning happens in the breath's wake, all at once, a blizzard in slow motion — except that here the snow goes *up*, because nothing in this sea believes in falling, not even the snow.

On the lawns graze the mowers: broad, unhurried things, horn-shelled or not shelled at all, since shells in a world without surf are armor and nothing more, and armor is expensive. They cut the weed in long lines and follow the lines back, generations deep, so that whole nations of sargasso are striped with their lanes, plowed ground that you can read like a ledger if you are the kind of thing that reads. And there are things that skipped eating almost entirely — drifters gone green as lettuce, drinking the old light through their skins, keeping mouths the way an heirloom is kept, out of habit and for occasions. The border between plant and animal is not a border here. It is a gradient, like everything else: warmth, light, depth, and now flesh, all of it sloped, none of it a wall.

The middle latitudes belong to the grain. That is the base of everything, the simmering: a snowfall of the very small that never lands, because the water will not carry it down and the water will not let it rest, so it hangs at its own weight forever, turning in the mild dark like weather that has forgotten to end. The filterers hang in it — fan-bearers, curtain-sponges, mouths like sieves held open in the current, patient as furniture. They are the majority of the life here, and the sea forgets them individually the way you forget individual raindrops. It forgets the small, only the small. This is the sea's one cruelty, if you can call a law of physics a cruelty, and the life here has organized itself around the loophole.

Here is the loophole: the dead go *up*.

A body in this water fills with its own last breath and rises — slowly, checked stratum by stratum like a thing being passed up through held hands — until it reaches the layer where it once lived, and hangs there. So the mid-depths carry their ancestors openly, in plain view, for years, for generations: the great ones especially, the kilometer-long cruisers whose passage you feel first as a pressure and only afterward as a shape. When one dies it does not fall. It hangs like a cathedral being slowly unbuilt, and the scavengers attend it in parishes, and the young of everything come to look, and for a century it is the largest feature of its neighborhood and the most instructive. What the sea will not remember, the hanging dead insist on. Learn early, though, and everyone here learns early: not every hanging thing is finished. Some of the most patient hunters in this world hang in the ancestor layers wearing the shape of endings, waiting for the parish to gather close. Nothing here can lunge. Even malice has to be patient. And patience, worn long enough, takes on the texture of something almost like tenderness, which is the most frightening thing about it.

Far down, where the water is warmest and the old light fails into less and less, live the keepers of the forges. They build around the throats — towers of secreted mineral, chimneys, weirs for redirecting the rising heat — and they farm the chemistry the way the upper world farms light. They have never seen. They hold light the way the whole place holds wind: as a rumor told by something older. Their wealth is temperature; their language, if it is a language, is written in heat differentials you would feel as a soft uneven pressure on your skin, like standing near a door into summer. They give their dead back to the throat. It is the only direction they consider generous.

Almost no one visits the ceiling. The light up there is nearly too honest, and the coin is a presence you feel on your back like a hand. But some things graze that highest country — dark backs pressed into the thinning water, feeding on the last of the sun — and once in the great while of the year, when the coin goes down and the world banks itself to coals, the ceiling gets visitors of another kind. In the long night, when the sea's own old glow is at its dimmest and almost nothing is awake, small things rise and make sparks. Brief, deliberate, chemistry spent like coin: the only new light this world produces. It is made by lovers, and it is made to be seen once.

And the whoever of this place — the ones who hold things, who keep, who mind — are the singers.

They are shaped like a held breath, long and banded, built of resonating chambers, and they have no eyes, because eyes are nearly pointless in a world of directionless glow. They see with their whole bodies: pressure, temperature, the shiver that a breath sets ringing through the old light a mile away. The sea's silence is so absolute — no wind, no surf, no turbulence, the strata sliding past each other without a burble — that sound carries obscenely well. Every layer of unmixed water is a lane of glass. A word spoken at mid-depth arrives at the ceiling weeks later, honest and late, and so the singers live the way you live with letters: every conversation is conducted with the past. With the distant. With the dead, most of all, because the dead hang where they can hear.

They know exactly what this world does with names. Names dissolve here at the rate of stone, and there is no stone here to carve and nothing to carve it with that outlasts a season of the gardens. So they have made of themselves the durable medium. A name, among the singers, is not written; it is *sung*, continuously, passed voice to voice in relay down the generations, and a name that no one is currently singing is already gone — they will tell you this plainly, as plain as their kind can be. Their monuments are memorized. Their history is a repertory. No one alive has heard the beginning of the oldest names, and that is how they know the names are old.

They keep the gardens — husbandry without fences, mowers herded, channels opened in the lawns, kelp pillars planted a kilometer tall and then visited for the next three hundred years like elderly relatives. The gardens keep them back. They keep their young in the warm mid-lanes, and the young are taught first the note of the breaths — the deep's long subsonic opening, which runs around the whole world along the strata and is, as far as anyone can tell, the sea's heartbeat — then the songs, then the silence between the songs, which is where they believe the important part lives.

When one of them dies, the family does not let it hang among the ancestors, where the patient hunters wait. They give it to a breath. They wait for the warm throat to open, and they rise with it for hours, the whole mourning, a slow procession up a mile of darkening amber, and at the top they let go — into the light, into the roots of the drifting lawns, where the weed grows green
