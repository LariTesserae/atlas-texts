The neighbors settled the question before anyone thought to ask it. They give the hollow and its occupant a single name — *the Pith* — as if place and dweller shared one syllable, the way a body and its owner share one. It is a tidy answer, and tidy answers are roofs: they keep the rain off while sheltering more tenants than the deed admits. So take the census honestly, beginning where everything here begins, with the sound. A mile out, the sound is a simile — a choir humming through a heartbeat. At the center, it is an anatomy. The choir is the thousand. The two-note pulse is the hearts. What lives in the Pith is the chord.

(The herons nest on the rim and commute over a resident the size of a hill. They have no name for it, which is the accuracy of neighbors.)

Start with the ones who live in it the way your gut lives in you. Gleaners graze the felt off the walls, small warm-blooded mowers herded to the dirty corners on gradients of scent — the softest leash in the world, worn by employees who cannot perceive their employer and would not be interested. Loppers prune for wages of sugar. Lantern-moths drone through the nectar market in clouds so dense the market tunes itself to them: customers who are also the instrument, an economy you can hear. Wallfish work the weeping walls like lymph; courier birds carry what the plant's own air is too slow for; beetles ferry the dead to the root-mouths, undertakers on retainer since before anyone kept records. None of these are residents. They are organs that wandered in and were kept — necessary, unconsulted, and in no position to resign.

The thousand souls are another matter, because they are the only living things in the Pith that could leave. That is their distinction, and it is the mortar. What does constant renovation make of a people? Present-tense people. Fluent in impermanence, wealthy in route rather than room, on visiting terms with walls instead of owning them. A favorite corner is a loan. Privacy is a courtesy the plant extends by narrowing a corridor. Their children learn direction as a tense: take the third vessel until it becomes a wall. And they are, by every account, cheerful — which tells you the shape of the real bargain. The Pith cannot grow a decision. It has foresight to burn, but wanting is the one import it cannot cultivate, so the labor divides cleanly: the people supply the why, the plant supplies the how, and the result is a machine for turning want into wood by morning.

Between the two, all day, go the clerks. Grown at dawn with the errands already in them, the way you wake with a sentence half-said on your tongue; reabsorbed at dusk. They are the only part of the Pith that has a face — a thousand small green faces, used one day at a time, none of them ever surprised. When one sweeps a sill for you or gestures you along a corridor, it is the city being courteous in person. Nothing here is wasted, including politeness: yesterday's clerk is in tonight's wall, which may be why the walls are so civil.

Then there are the fitting rooms, which no one can explain, and which explain everything if you let them. Consider what knowing means to something without eyes. It has carpentry instead of vision. To know you, it grows a chamber to your proportions and refits it nightly, in private, the way you rehearse a name you are afraid to get wrong. It practices you. At that resolution, hospitality and prophecy are the same discipline — a bridge grown two days before need is only a host who sets the table before the knock.

As for the dweller itself: it lives down. At the Thinking Floor when it is working, where the fauna nap on its deliberations like cats on a warm engine. Its moods are municipal — a nine-day breath of surge and prune; some hot afternoons it over-charges and rains indoors, twenty warm minutes, everyone slick and laughing while the landlord drinks its own sky. Once a year it visits the shrine, where it renews, to the tolerance of a ritual, a wire in amber that nobody alive can read — possibly not the Pith either, in the way you cannot read yourself. It keeps the shrine the way you keep a heart beating: not because the keeper understands the contract, but because the keeper is the contract. The rest of the time it lives wherever its sap does, which is everywhere, which is the strange fact of this address: landlord and lodging are one body with two kinds of tenant inside it. And the tenancy binds both ways. Traffic is the streets' only tenure, and the clause holds the landlord too — abandoned, the passages thicken to wood; abandoned entirely, the Pith would throttle itself back into a mound, a hill up to its ears, finally finished. The people are its bloodstream. It grew the bloodstream a city, and gave it the one power the plant can never grow for itself: the power to walk out.

So the census, honestly taken, reads: one ancient attention; one thousand choosers; a shift's worth of green hands; a gutful of warm organs and a wing of couriers; one wire in amber. And one more entry, the one you only see on the way out. The dead go through the root-mouths unhurried and return as gutter and flume, but their shape stays laid down in the strata — floors stacked on floors, knots that were doorframes, every generation a ring in the civic timber. Nothing in the Pith is old, and no one in it is gone. The great fallow halls are not vacancies; they are savings. The emptiness is inventory, and inventory, at this address, has a roster: everyone the shape has ever fit, held in reserve the only way this place knows how to hold anything — as room.

That is who lives there. Not a thing in a place — a place that has made living its final form, an ancient attention and a thousand mortal choosers grown into one chord, humming a mile out over the flat. And somewhere behind you, when you leave, in the fallow dark, it will still be refitting a room to your proportions. You were a guest for a night. You are a model now. In a body that replaces everything but its shape, that is what memory looks like: it does not keep you.

It keeps you possible.
