Your weight arrives and the floor arrives to meet it: a soft yielding, the whole valley leaning a finger's width toward your foot and settling back. Then, a moment later, comes the pulse. It comes up through your soles like the sound of a struck bell heard from inside the bell — one beat, slow, enormous, patient, about as often as your own heart, and you stand there for a while like a man listening to two clocks that refuse to agree.

Because it is a body and not a ground, it has a skin, and the skin is not grass. Grass is ten thousand plants pretending to be one floor. This is one plant pretending to be everything. It has no soil to sit in and no stones to interrupt it — nothing, at least, except the stair, which is stone the way a shell is stone: not quarried but grown, and therefore kept. Its skin runs in whorls and seams and knuckles, like the lines of an enormous palm. The hills of the valley are its knuckles, risen. The shallow basins are its cups, dipped to hold pools of water that is warm as blood and faintly lit from within, and in the pools move small blind fish with lanterns of their own, so that light lives at every level here — above you, around you, beneath you, in the water — and you are the only dark thing in the valley.

And the floor writes. That is what you learn on your second day, walking a path you walked yesterday and finding it faintly ridged now, inked with fine raised whorls that follow your own footfalls. Everything that has ever crossed this valley is written in its skin and never wears away, because the floor re-inks its records. Every hundred pulses it goes over its own handwriting and sharpens it. This is why the chisel-marks of the stair are still bright: they are handwriting too, only larger — the biggest letters in a long letter the valley has been writing upward for who knows how long. The floor reads intention through your feet. Decide where you mean to go and begin, and by the time you arrive the path has been combed smooth ahead of you, gentle-graded, waiting. The people who live here do not say that the valley is kind. They say it is attentive, and they distinguish carefully between the two.

The hills have legs. That is the next thing. There are animals here the size of cottages, patient, grey-green, their bellies seamed with soft mouths that pare the valley's skin in long pale curls, and the valley lets them — no: the valley hires them, because it needs pruning the way an orchard needs pruning, and in payment it grows its sweetest new skin beneath the mouths of their young.
