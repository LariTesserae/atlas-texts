You will not see the first one. That is the first thing to understand. Eyes are useless here and always have been; the dark is not a veil over things but the native condition of them. So the sound you took for ambience is not ambience. It is them. It was always them.

The low sustained tone — the one you felt in your sternum before your ears would admit to it — is a note they keep together, each worker adding its hum from resonating chambers in the shell, and they listen to how it comes back. The plain is an instrument and they are its players and its audience at once. The note goes down through forty meters of ice and into the young crust and returns changed, and by *how* it has changed they know the state of everything: where the ground has stiffened as it cools, where a seam is ready to split, where the ocean has thinned the lid overnight. They are forever playing one long chord into the world and reading the world out of the echo.

The knocking is something else. Three, a pause, two — you heard it as quarry-work, and it is that, but underneath it is simpler and stranger. Three is a question: *who holds this seam, report.* Two is the answer: *held. Present. Still here.* Millions of these exchanges are braided across the plain at once, overlapping, so dense they fuse into a single weave, and what you heard from the edge of the dark was the braid — a city forever taking roll of itself.

As for the workers: you would take them for stones, and that is not a failure of your eyes. It is the design. They are broad and domed, most of them the size of a boulder you could sit on, ridged shells like stagnant ice, furred white with rime so that they vanish into the plain they are lying on. When one moves, it does not look like something crossing the ground. It looks like the ground reconsidering. Underneath, rows of short heavy legs; at the front, two limbs that tell you everything about the civilization — one a blunt hammer, one a fine pick. A fringe of bristles along the shell's rim reads vibration the way your skin reads warmth. There is no face to speak of. The soft parts are kept against the stone, where the warmth is. The mouth, when it is working, extrudes a gray mineral paste that smells of hot rock, and this paste is their mortar, their cement, their monument.

The sharp-edged basalt islets are their quarries. That is why nothing is weathered — *unweathered* here means *unfinished*. Every clean face is a scar of their chisels. They split stone and ice with sound rather than force: choose the seam, place three blows in exactly the right places, wait for the sustained note to find the flaw and widen it. Then the slow work — prying, hauling, fitting, cementing — and this is why nothing quick and nothing quiet can both be true of them. The finger-width of new stone added to the world each year is added by hands like these, one dab of paste at a time, and no worker living has seen a wing of the mound finished. Projects here outlast their founders. The roster of a project is kept by knock, handed down seam to seam, and the young inherit work their great-ancestors began and will pass on, incomplete, to the great-grandchildren of strangers. What looks from outside like effort without consequence is construction at the only pace the cold permits.

The meltline is their farm, and the mound exhales on it deliberately. They wall the warm outflow into runnels with cut slabs of ice — gates of ice, opened and closed like sluices — and steer the heat across the flats so that no field of the dark felt they grow ever overheats or starves. Where the water runs warm enough, mats of it thrive, black-brown, sulfurous, smelling of iron and eggs; they scrape the harvest, press it into bricks, and cache it in cold rooms inside the mound, where it keeps like peat. Frost flowers bloom along the healthy field edges, and the workers graze them thin, and their presence is how you know a field is doing well. Not every crack in the ice is an accident, either. Some are kept — filled with warm outflow, their lips thick with rime — veins of warmth the far-flung work parties travel by, oasis to oasis, resting against the heat like caravans at wells.

The young are born in the warmest pools at the mound's skirt, and here is the one exception to everything: the young are quick. Skimming, darting, jostling in the nursery brine — quickness is youth, and the cold takes it first. Growing up means slowing down. An elder is known not by size but by resemblance: rime and mineral crust accumulate on the oldest shells until they begin to look exactly like the rocks they quarry, and the very old can only be told from stone because the stone does not answer when the roll call passes over it.
