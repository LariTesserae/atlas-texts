The first thing to understand about the keepers is that you will not see them at first. This is not because they hide. Nothing in the foundry hides; there is nowhere to hide, and no one has needed to for centuries. It is because they hold still. Stillness is the default condition here — motion is the event, the way sound is yours — and a body folded around a valve wheel, matched to the grey of the plating, barnacled along the shoulders, is simply not something the eye knows how to separate from the machinery until it moves. So your first evidence of the population will be work being done. A gauge needle swinging. A hatch dogged shut. A rusted wheel turning slowly with nobody near it — and then the slow resolve of the somebody, unfolding out of the engine the way a hand unfolds from a glove.

The hands are the tell. Eight fingers: two of them fused long ago into a single broad lever-finger, the others long and webbed to the last knuckle, scarred smooth at the pads from generations of the same grips. A keeper's grip is famous in the town, in the way that only a town can make a thing famous. A handshake, here, is a sentence.

Their bodies have gone the way of the rest of the foundry — toward the narrow and the patient. They are long through the torso now, built for doorways that were never meant for swimming, and they move through them the way eels move through pipes: unhurried, economical, with the ends of them trailing a half-beat behind. The skin is grey and olive, ridged along the forearms where tools rest, and the barnacles are not infestation but jewelry — certain ones cultivated, certain ones picked, a row of them along the collarbone like medals awarded for time served. The eyes are wide and black and mirror-flat, and they hold the sodium-amber glow of the machinery the way a held note holds a room. Some of them breathe through gills, thin slits at the throat that flutter when they are pleased. Some wear rebreather collars of old brass, inherited, polished, their function uncertain and their importance not in question. And some, as far as anyone can establish, breathe through sheer conviction. The town does not audit its own miracles.

Ask how many of them there are and you will get a long silence, because they stopped counting heads generations ago and started counting other things — rotations, joints, groans, years of a warden's service. Fewer than a churchyard's worth, probably. Enough. Enough is the number they like.

They will tell you, if you can stay long enough to be told, that they have always lived here; that the foundry was built around them the way a shell is built around a snail. The ledger room suggests otherwise. It is a small flooded archive of waxed slate plates, scratched in a hand that starts out surface-crisp and grows, decade by decade, looser and more wave-shaped, and it tells of shifts that stayed when the water came in. Not survivors, exactly. Employees. The foundry was warm, the work was familiar, and leaving had never really been part of the job. The sea simply bought the company.

The trades have survived the drowning better than anything else. There are pump-wardens and valve-keepers, and their duties are what you would guess. There is a rust-tender, who feeds the rust-eaters and consults with the rust, and considers it a colleague of moderate reliability. There are lantern-keepers, who tend the pale glow-colonies in the old lamp housings — feeding them trimmed frond-tips, thinning the clusters when they crowd — and who are, though the word has drifted, lamplighters still. And there is the listener, who spends the long hours with her back flat against a pressure joint, reading the groans the way shorefolk read weather. Her name is not a word. It is the feeling of a small valve seating — a click you receive in the palm. That is how names work here. To introduce yourself, you press your name into someone's hand and they press theirs into yours, and after a few introductions your hands know more people than your ears ever will.

They have two languages. The close one is a low water-speech, vowels stretched long, consonants mostly dissolved, names hummed more than said. The far one is touch-speech, tapped into the hull, and this is the important one, because the hull is all one piece: a message tapped at the loading bay arrives everywhere in the town at once, faint as something remembered. The whole foundry is a single instrument, and they are the only musicians it has ever had, and they play it slowly.

The dead are not excluded from any of this. The dead are given to the eastern kelp, laid out along the walkway where the fronds are thickest, and in a year the garden has taken their shapes, and in ten they are the garden. Some few are kept: the bones of wardens whose work was especially long are sewn into the hull where a plate ran weak, and everyone in town knows which rivet is grandmother. This is considered not sentiment but engineering. The dead hold better than rivets. They have never once worked loose.

And the animals are not excluded either. Ask what lives in the foundry and the keepers will count generously. The pale octopus on the eastern walkway is counted — it is called the East Warden, and it is presented with each new kelp-ward, not the other way around, and it is left a crab on the first of every one of their slow months. Its arrangements of the fronds have never been decoded and no one tries; but once, decades back, it rearranged a whole bed overnight, and three days later a pressure joint failed, and now its garden is read the way the listener reads the groans — as a bulletin, filed by a gardener in green. The eels are counted individually, by name, and when a boiler is opened for maintenance the eels must be carried to the next warm housing, and this is done by hand, keeper to keeper down the line, like infants at a christening, and it is the closest thing the town has to a festival. The lantern-clusters are counted as one collective elder. The engines are counted as the eldest resident of all — it lives below, it never comes up, and it is fed in the sense that everything in town feeds it.

The count has never included the shape from the far gallery. It comes once in a generation, moving the length of the central hall too large and too deliberate for any fish, and it is fed at each station as it passes, and it leaves by the flooded stairwell that no one uses, and no one follows, and no one asks. The word they touch into the hull for it translates badly — nearest as *the one who stayed longer*. Fear is not the right word for what they feel, because fear requires the expectation of harm, and nothing that has ever lived in the foundry has been eaten unawares. Surprise, here, is not one of the local industries. Though once — this is in the ledgers, one line, undated — the decade-count went around the town as it always does, resident by resident pressing their presence into the metal, and from the direction of the flooded stairwell, faint, deliberate, there came back a single tap. No one has claimed it. No one has repeated it. It is entered in the ledger with the rest.

Once every ten years, on the night the count is taken, the keepers do the bravest thing they do. They damp the engines. All of them, down to the last flywheel, and for the length of one long breath the foundry goes silent — truly silent, for the first time in living memory, and they hang in the galleries and listen to their own future. The tick of cooling metal. Frond against frond. Their own hearts, which suddenly seem very small and very quick. The children find it frightening. The elders find it sweet. Then a hand finds the great wheel, and turns it, and the note comes back — that held hum, that drone with harmonics, felt in the teeth and the sternum — and the message goes around the town by hand, resident to resident, the same message it always is:

*still here.*

Understand that the keepers know exactly what they are becoming, and are not distressed by it. The foundry is turning reef on a thousand-year schedule; the keepers are turning *something* on the same schedule — the barnacles climb a generation further up each century, the children are born already changed, the line between the town and its people grows thinner as the plates soften under sponge and frond. These are not two projects. They are one project, running on the same patient deadline, and nobody involved is in a hurry to see it finished.

So if you drift in through the broken loading bay, past the coral-crusted crane arm: they will see you long before you see them, from foldings of shadow you will swear were machinery. Let them look. When one finally comes to you — and one will — it will take your hand in an eight-fingered grip still warm from valve-work, and press the greeting into your palm, which translates roughly as *it is warm here; stay as long as you like.* And when you finally leave, swimming up through the dim blue-grey nothing-light, you will feel it through the water behind you: one tap, passed from hull to sea, patient as everything else in that town. It is the message they send each decade into the silence, the one the whole foundry was built to keep sending, and it will go on as long as the engines turn — and, everyone agrees, a good while after.

*Still here. Still here. Still here.*
