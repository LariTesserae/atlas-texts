## The Hushrowites

The people of Hushrow are few enough to number, and they have numbered themselves. There are sixty-one. They know this the way surface towns know their weather — it is a fact of the place, checked seasonally, mourned or celebrated at the properly slow intervals. A birth is counted. A leaving is counted. A leaving is rarer than a birth, because to leave you must rise through four weathers and a mile of indifferent water, and most people find, somewhere around the second brine, that they have forgotten what they were leaving for.

They are pale in a way that is not sickly — it is candle-pale, wax-pale, the pallor of things that live by lamplight and have for generations. Their eyes have widened slightly, gently, the way a hand softens when it learns a new grip. They are not blind. They are not blind the way moles are not blind: they see, but they see a town's worth of distance and no more, and their eyes have made their peace with that, the way everything here makes its peace.

They move slowly, and their slowness is not laziness. It is hydrodynamics. A fast movement in the street-weather sends up a puff of the bone-paste floor, and the paste takes an hour to resettle, and in that hour the whole town is tasting your hurry. So they glide. They have developed a walk that has almost no walk in it — a push, a drift, a touch-down. Children learn it before they learn to speak, because children who churn the floor are children who get looked at, and being looked at in Hushrow is a serious social event, weighing about what a shout weighs above.

Their voices have changed. Shouting does not work here — not because no one would hear, but because sound in the layered brines does strange things, bending at each boundary, arriving early or late or not at all, so that a shout from one end of the Row arrives at the other in fragments, a word here, a vowel there, the way a letter arrives with its pages out of order. So the Hushrowites speak low and close, and they have developed a second language for crossing the weathers, a language of slow hand-shapes that reads correctly through a boundary even when the voice reading it would be a lie. Between the weathers, everyone is honest. It is one of the reasons they cross slowly: not just the burn of the boundary, but the fact that you must pass through a few seconds of unavoidable truth, your hands saying what they say, before you can speak again on the far side.

They dress for the weathers, as the town says. Street-clothes are thin, kept clean because cleanliness shows by lamplight in a town of sixty-one witnesses. Weather-clothes — the garments for going up a layer, or down — are heavier, layered like the water itself, oiled against the burn of the boundary. There is a tailor. Of course there is a tailor; there is one of everything, one baker, one keeper of the Lungs, one mender of the chimney-stacks. Sixty-one people support exactly sixty-one trades, and everyone knows which trades are missing, and the missing ones are spoken of the way surface towns speak of extinct birds.

The keeper of the Lungs is the closest thing they have to a mayor, because the Lungs are the closest thing they have to a sky. The Lungs are the town's air-gardens, tended stands of something pale and fronded that drink what the houses breathe out and give back what the houses breathe in. The chimney-pearls are the visible part of this — the town's exhalation, rising in single file through the weathers on its way to be cleaned. The keeper reads the pearls the way a physician reads a pulse: their tempo, their warmth, their number. Sixty-one fires. When the count is sixty, the town knows someone has gone out, and it does not ask who first. It asks *who*, and there is a particular dread, in a place that small, in the pause before the answer.

They are patient the way their town is patient — the town that sits where everything eventually arrives, on a floor soft as flour, receiving the slow leavings of the whole drowned world. They have turned this into an ethic. Nothing in Hushrow is wasted, because everything that arrives was sent by somewhere, and everything that leaves the town — silt sorted, leavings composted into the Lungs' beds — goes on to be somebody else's arrival. They think of themselves less as a settlement than as a stage in a long slow falling. Their dead are not buried but *given onward*, released to the drift, so that they too become leavings of Hushrow, arriving someday on some farther floor. There is no cemetery. There is a ledger.

And here is the strangest thing about them, the thing a visitor would only learn after a season, after the cold has persuaded your blood: they are not sad. A town named Hushrow, breathing silver pearls into the dark, four weathers deep, sixty-one souls who can hold every lamplight in one glance — you would expect grief, or at least its residue. But they have had a long time to make their peace, and they made it. They speak low and drift softly and count their fires, and when they say the name of their town they do not lower their voices further, because it is already as low as a voice goes.

It earns the name. So do they.
