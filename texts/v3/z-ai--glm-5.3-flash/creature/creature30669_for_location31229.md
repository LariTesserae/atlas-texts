The census comes up empty, and the census is honest, and the census is wrong.

Walk the basin all day — or all of what the basin offers in place of a day — and you will count the residents and arrive at zero. No burrows, no nests, no tracks, no husks, no bones. You were taught to find life by looking for its wake: movement, appetite, expenditure, a thing that eats and is eaten and leaves the ledger unbalanced. None of that is here. But the method assumes a creature that spends, and in this place nothing is spent. The resident — there is one; there has only ever been one — lives by holding. It has no name, because a name is a handle, and handles are for moving things, and nothing here has been moved in a very long time.

Begin with the body, because the body is the first thing you stood inside without knowing it. The basin is not a place that contains a creature. The place *is* the creature, and you have been walking around on it. The moss is its skin — deep green, unnervingly soft, and the softness is unnerving for a reason your hand understands before your mind agrees to: nothing here has ever been bitten. Everywhere else, softness is a cost, a vulnerability, a line in the budget. Here it is free. There is nothing to be soft against. The thin seep threading between the pools is circulation, an inch-wide bloodstream moving with the urgency of geology. The fog that pools knee-deep in the cold hours and lifts invisibly by midday is breath — one full respiration per cycle of warmth, in through every surface, out through every surface, the whole basin exhaling itself and inhaling itself without ever letting any of it go. The pools are warm as bathwater for the same reason your blood is warm as bathwater. And the light — diffuse, coming from no direction, the light of a heavily overcast day *indoors* — that is exactly what it is. You are not outdoors. You are inside something, and this is what the inside of a held breath looks like.

If you want the pulse, stand beside the shrub — the one woody thing, the only resident here with a history — and wait for the tick of its leaf. One drop released. Then, a long while later, another. That interval is the heartbeat. It is the only clock in the basin, which is why you stopped tracking time: the resident keeps time with a clock that has no face, no numbers, and one hand, and beats too slowly to watch. Perhaps the shrub is an organ. Perhaps it is a keepsake. Perhaps — and hold this possibility loosely — it is a walking stick that someone with hands drove into the earth long ago and then forgot to need, and it rooted, out of politeness or grief, and has been ticking ever since.

The pools are what it sees with. Not eyes — eyes take light in and burn it as image, which is expenditure, and the resident does not spend. The pools do the opposite: they gather. They are the brightest things for miles precisely because they are the only things here that refuse to absorb, that hold the light up instead of drinking it. The resident's way of seeing is all mirror. It has never taken a single ray into itself. Instead it keeps dozens of small silver rounds of sky, each one mirror-flat, each one holding the dim gray-warm sky perfectly, and it has one picture — the same sky, goldened faintly at the water's edge — repeated in every dish at once. It has been studying that picture longer than there has been anyone to ask it what it looks like. It knows the sky the way you memorize a face: perfectly, from one angle, without ever needing it to change.

If you insist on counting individuals, count the water. Every droplet here is a brief resident in its own right. Condensed in the cold hours onto a stone's skin, it hangs there dew-heavy, warms, lifts, drifts a foot, a yard, and settles somewhere else to be condensed again. A migration that never ends and never goes anywhere. Each drop lives a day; each day's drop is identical to the last — the way a grass blade yellows and thins and is replaced by an identical grass blade, and the difference is undetectable. Generation after generation, cycling through the held air like a town whose families never change. And this is the final correction to the census: the resident is not *in* the moisture the way a beetle is in a log. The resident is the circulation itself — the whole warm damp economy of arriving and hanging and settling, experienced from the inside. The moisture is not what lives here. The moisture is what being-alive is made of here.

What does it eat? Follow the logic of a place where nothing is spent. The resident consumes difference. Not violently — *settlement*. Anything that arrives with sharpness is sanded toward agreement; anything that arrives in a hurry is slowed to the pace of seepage; anything that leaves a mark finds the mark gently digested. The varnish on the stones is not an accident of damp. It is maintenance, the way a body keeps its fingernails. This is the rule you felt in the empty sky, in the untracked waterlines — not a law imposed on creatures but the shape of the one creature there is. Nothing crosses overhead because anything that crossed would carry moisture out in its body, and the resident does not export. The air above the basin is kept empty the way a miser keeps a purse shut. If a bird flew over and dropped a feather, the feather would be accepted, darkened, sunk, and by the following season it would be moss, and the moss would be identical to itself, and there would be nothing left to find. Understand this properly: you were never in danger of being eaten. You are in danger — and I choose the word gently — of being kept.

You heard it speak. You may not have known that was what it was. Your footsteps, muffled but present, like knocks on a far door — the door was not far, and someone was home, and the knock was received. And then the answer: a single small *glup*, a bubble of something rising and breaking in one of the pools, and the silence afterward deeper for it. That was the whole sentence. It speaks one word at a time, and the interval between words is a season, and you happened to be standing there when its word came due. It said, in the only language the basin permits — the language of change, of which the glup is the sole remaining vocabulary — *I am here.* Or possibly, *You are here.* In that language the two sentences are the same.

And the silence afterward is not emptiness. It is attention. The resident has nothing else to do with its attention, and all the time there is to do it in.

As for the built marks — the ring of stones, the shallow rectangular depression near the largest pool, the flat stone resting on the flat stone — sit on the seat, if you sit, and consider the possibility that the hands belonged to no one who left.
