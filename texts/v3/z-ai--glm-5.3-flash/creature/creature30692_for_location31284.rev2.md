People live here. That is the first answer, and the town would give it to you with a certain dry patience, the way you'd answer a stranger who asked if the sea was wet. Fenharrowers. A few hundred of them, folded into the dish of the basin, and before anything else can be said about them it should be said that they are never dry — not truly, not in the marrow. Their skin never quite finishes drying; it has the softness of things kept under cloth. The black water gets into the grain of them: fingernails faintly tea-colored, creases of the palms stained a shade the soap never argues with. Their hair smells of peat-smoke and hot iron. They cannot smell it. Visitors carry the smell home in their coats for years.

They know one another in installments, because the fog shows faces a piece at a time. You may know a man for a decade by the heat of his hand, the weight of his step, the shape of his shout — and then one evening the fog lifts an inch and you see his whole face at once, and feel you've been introduced late. Nobody minds. In a town where the noise eats long sentences, speech is short clauses finished with the fingers, and identity lives in the body: everyone here has the name they were given, and the name the floors know. The timber rafts carry footsteps the way water carries ripples, and a house learns its family's walks and reports them. A woman cooking with her back to the door will set down her knife four strokes before her husband reaches it, because the floor told her. Children play step-and-guess in the dark galleries, blindfolded, calling out who is coming by the fall of their feet, and they are rarely wrong, and a child who is wrong is teased for a season.

Their ears are strange to out-country ears. The beat of the engines is the first rhythm any of them ever knew — cradles are strung from the beams so the pulse rocks them — and so they hear the world partly through their soles. An old man will miss a word shouted at his back and then, three streets off, feel a sluice go wrong through the floor and be out the door before any bell. Half the town is a little deaf by fifty and it signifies nothing, because everyone has been fluent in hand-talk since before they could walk, and a deaf Fenharrower is only a quieter one. They read fire the way other people read faces — *straw*, *cherry*, *coming round* — and describe the sky not in colors but in weights, the sky being *heavy* or *thin*, grey being one thing and the sky never being one thing. Their oaths go by the engines: *Patience willing*, *as Temperance is my witness*. Their parting formula, said with a flat upward flick of the hand, is *on the beat*.

Wiping is the town's devotion. The first chore of the smallest child is the latch; you can read a household's luck in the brightness of its iron, and a rusted hinge is quiet crime, discussed for a week in hand-talk across the canal. A dry rag left folded on a rail overnight is courtesy, the way a lit lamp is courtesy elsewhere — left for whoever passes. Everything is wiped, greased, wrapped, and wiped again, and the town smells of lanolin and peat and hot metal, and under that, faintly, of the fungus houses. They eat out-country grain that comes off the barges already faintly smoky from the warm shelves; they eat the mushrooms from the slag-tunnels, which taste of iron and hazelnut; they eat reed-heart in spring, and moss-bread when the grain barges are late. At weddings they pour pipe-water, genuinely cold, which in Fenharrow is a flavor the way vanilla is a flavor elsewhere. Visitors sleep badly the first night, the pulse coming up through the cot like a giant's heart under the pillow, and by the third night cannot sleep without it, and the town counts this a kindness it does.

Wealth here is a schedule. A bed of ore planted at a child's birth is a dowry the grandchild harvests; people reckon time in *restings*, a resting being the forty years a bed lies fallow, or roughly a grandmother. A house gains its raising every second resting. The dead go into the terraces: the ground will not take anyone — dig two feet and you strike warm water — so the furnace does, and the ash goes out with the slag into the black glassy heaps where the reeds come up within the season. In a hundred years that ground is ore-ground again. Nobody in Fenharrow says their dead are under them. They say their dead are ahead of them, coming back as ore, and they say it without any show of comfort, as plain fact, the way they'd say a bed is resting.

The dying are moved back onto the beat. The well-off sleep in off-beat rooms, walled and packed against the pulse, and it is considered a luxury, and the old, moved into one, complain of the silence like a strapped-on muzzle and are carried back gladly among the pipes and the hum to finish. If the year is kind, they are carried out into the street at the Hush instead, so that their last true sound is the note. A child born inside the Hush — it happens, some years — is given a name out of the books. *Wren. Heron. Swift.* The bird-words are the town's strangest household goods: children learn them from pages, a heron being a grey bird that stands in the shallows on one leg, a bittern being a fen bird whose call is a low boom heard across the marsh at dusk. The children look up from that page and hear, all around them, the engines, and once a year, in the one hour of quiet, something very like a boom. *The bittern booms in the reeds*, says the book, and the children ask whether the bittern booms like the Hush, and the old ones look at them and say, mildly, that the book would know. The books describe a country no one has ever seen. Or else — the thought stays with the children — they describe this one.

The other lives are green. The reeds are kept the way skin is kept, cropped in rotations, planted by hand by people standing thigh-deep in the warm black water in the fog-dawn, ankle by ankle, like rice-farmers or like people dressing a wound. The moss is a crop with a north-wall address; moss-men comb it with wooden rakes and sell it by the basket to dress cuts, because the tannin water scabs a wound in minutes and the moss finishes the work. The rainbow films on the water are alive and they listen — the whole town has seen them shiver into rings at the Hush — and so Fenharrow children keep rainbow-jars, the nearest thing to an aquarium in a world without fish, feeding a nail to the film and watching the colors bloom and change. Nothing here rots in the loud way. There are no flies to blow, no worms to take, no wasps; an unwatched thing simply goes down soft, grows a green-black pelt, and becomes ground. Decay here is slow, quiet, and vegetarian, and the town has mostly hired it out: the pale brothers of the fungus houses tunnel the slag heaps and unmake whatever is fed to them, and nothing in Fenharrow is thrown away, only fed.

And there are no animals. This is the thing you notice on the second day, after the noise has stopped astounding you and the town has had time to show you its stillness. No rats in the granaries, no eels in the Levels, no frogs in the warm shallows where frogs should riot, and no birds — not one, ever, in the sky, which is otherwise the busiest thing in the basin. Eel-spears hang on the tavern walls, kept sharp and entirely ornamental. There is one story everyone's grandfather tells: the year a barge-dog set one paw on the quay. One. The dog looked at the town the way you look at deep water, took the paw back aboard, and would not eat until the barge was a mile down-canal. Old men still argue about what the dog knew. The young have theories — the water is wrong, the beat unsettles the egg and the nerve. The old have only the phrase they keep for it, said in the lowered voices this town uses for exactly three subjects, of which this is one: *the marsh keeps a clean house.* Kept from what, and for whom, is not said.

The wild pipes argue with nobody about any of it, and they are the deepest argument. A pipe grows a finger's length a decade, which means that any pipe an engineer finds and caps today was begun three, four, five generations before anyone thirsted for it — begun, and run level, or uphill, for no gradient any map shows, to arrive precisely where a street would be. The ore regrows fastest nearest the works. The pipes arrive before the thirst. A thing that lays cold clean water under a town a century ahead of the town's need is not doing what wild things do. It is doing what Fenharrowers do with their ore-beds: planting for grandchildren. The young, who cap the pipes and are supposed not to ask, have started asking at the caps, quietly, with their hands: *planted for whom?*

Here is what the Hush sounds like from inside, and it is the whole answer. The engines park off their dead centers, and for the first time in a year the basin is still enough to hear itself, and it does — one long low note, from nowhere and everywhere, and the people who have stood on the galleries with instruments against the rail will tell you what the old keepers will confirm and the priest in the crow-vane chapel will not deny: the note is the engines' own pitch. Held. The way you hold a string still under one finger. While the heart is off its beat, the body keeps the time. And by then nobody in the street needs the anatomy explained, because they have lived inside it all their lives — the pumps for a heart, the black iron-and-tannin water for blood, the pipes for vessels, the reeds for skin, the fungus for gut — and themselves for the part that listens. That is what they are for. Every year the basin sings once, into a full street.

Ask what the marsh gets out of the bargain, and the town will wipe its hands and tell you plainly: an audience. It is not a joke, quite. A fen that wanted to be heard would need keepers; it would warm its springs, sweeten its ore, lay its cold water where a street would thirst, and keep its house clean of everything that bites or steals or sings for itself — everything, that is, that is not listening. The ore, the pipes, the reeds, the long patience are all one gesture. The marsh is planting people. The marsh hums, the birds leave, the people stay, and no one has ever said aloud whether staying is courage or a collar — but the people, who are not stupid, also do not want the answer, because they have noticed that the question changes nothing about the work, and the work is good. They wipe their iron. They fix the date of the next Hush. They give their dead to the terraces ahead of them and their names to the note once a year.

And when the hour is up, the fires come back and the flywheels turn and the oldest sound in the basin takes up its beat again, unhurried — older than any building standing, everyone agrees on that. Though the Hush-children, who were born listening, and who carry the birds' names like small unsettleable doubts, will tell you, if you ask them in hand-talk when nobody else is watching: they have heard one older.
