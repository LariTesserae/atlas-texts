You will look for animals. Everyone does. You crouch at the pools and wait for something to skitter; you lift a fern frond and expect the pale flash of something fleeing the light. Nothing comes. No worm turns the litter, no bat claims the vault, no mite rides the moss. The Stillroom has no animals.

It has residents. You simply have to slow all the way down to take the census.

Begin with the moss, because the moss is everywhere and the moss noticed you first — though *noticed* is generous, and so is *first*. The moss is the ground-floor population, the upholstery-thick mats lining every horizontal and much of the vertical, and it lives on almost nothing: warmth, dark, and the condensation that beads on every stone like the room sweating politely. Its growth is measured in fingernails per century. A cushion of it by the third pool is older than the language you are thinking in, and it has spent all that time doing exactly one thing, which is continuing. To the moss, you are weather. A warm draft that arrived, lingered, and will be gone before it has finished deciding — it will never finish deciding — whether to grow toward you.

Above the moss, the ferns: tenants of the cracks. Their fronds unroll over years, and when one finally flattens open it is an announcement a decade in the making. Once in a long life the sori ripen to brown-gold dust, and then comes the room's one migration — the spore-fog. At any hour there are spores adrift in that thick sweet air, and they are the closest thing the Stillroom has to birds: a flock that never lands all at once, crossing the eighty paces of the chamber over the course of a day on the slow thermal breath. Most settle into the moss and begin, at moss speed, the long apprenticeship of becoming ferns. In this room, flying and rooting are the same life lived at two speeds.

The vines are the elders. Cables wrist-thick, stitched wall to wall, older than the names of countries. Their wood is nearly mineral; they stopped being furniture of the chamber long ago and became its rafters. They creak — once per expansion, a fraction of a millimeter — and that creak is their entire vocabulary, one word per season, and the word is *more* or possibly *yes*.
