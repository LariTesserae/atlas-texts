**The Levels**

Who lives in a finished country? Slow things. Small things. Things that have made their peace.

The first you notice are the stones' keepers — lichens the color of old coins and dried blood, mapped in rings across the flat rocks like the signatures of very patient signatories. They do not move, exactly. But over decades a patch of them will drift a hand's width across a boulder, the way a shadow drifts, and the old people here (if old people there are) say you can tell the age of a stone by reading its rings, the way you'd read a tree, except the lichen counts centuries instead of years.

Then the grasses, if grass is the word. What covers the ground is a mat of something gray-green and wire-fine that lies flat against the earth no matter which way the wind combs it. It doesn't grow up. Nothing here grows up; growing up was for the mountain days. It grows *out*, silently, in slow anonymous increments, so that the whole country could be one organism — one enormous grandmother-mat that has been holding the soil down since the rubble forgot.

The animals are mostly horizontal too. There is a hare — call it a hare — that has given up entirely on the escape-by-running strategy. Its legs are short, its body low, its ears reduced to soft flaps. When danger comes it does not bolt; it *stops*, and its coat, which matches the lichen precisely, ends the conversation. Generations of hares that ran were hares that got seen. What remains is the animal that became the ground.

There are birds, but they nest on the ground and walk more than they fly, and their calls are single notes, unornamented, dropped like pebbles into the silence — no songs, because songs are for advertising, and everyone here has already been found.

And in the hollows, where meltwater gathers into shallow metal-tasting pools, live the oldest residents: mats of microbial felt, layered like upholstery, each layer a thousand years of sedimented afternoons. Press a finger in — you wouldn't — and it gives like a mattress stuffed with time. They were here before the mountains. They will be here after everything else has finished too.

The one exception, the upright thing, is a tree. Exactly one, near the center of the flats, gnarled and no taller than a person, leaning at the angle of an old man listening. It should not survive here. It survives anyway, and the hares visit it, and the birds sing from it after all — one song a day, at dusk, the only extravagance in a country that has otherwise concluded its business and settled its accounts.

Whether anyone human lives here is harder to say. There are the worn slabs of the stair you came up, so someone made them. There are cairns out on the flats, low and old, arranged not as markers but as *sentences* — and the whole land, you realize, is the longest of those sentences, the one that ends: *this was high once, and it was good, and now it is level, and that is good too.*

Whoever lives here, they are not waiting for anything. That's the strangest part. Everywhere else, even the stones are waiting. Here everything has arrived.
