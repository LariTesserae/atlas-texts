# The Tenants of the Drowned Basin

The life here is old in a way that has nothing to do with the structure, and everything to do with it.

## The Ribbon-Schools

The most numerous — the ones you'll see first, if you can be said to *see* anything in this green-gray murk — are the ribbon-creatures. Long, translucent bands of tissue, some as long as your arm, some barely a handspan, all of them nearly invisible until a current catches them and they light up with the water's own motion. They have no eyes, no fins, no faces. They have *shape*, and the shape is negotiable. A school pours through a rounded doorway like syrup, stretched into a single rope of pale filament; then the current fractures and the school is suddenly fifty ribbons, each one a separate opinion about which way to go. Then it reconciles. They always reconcile.

They taste the water. This is the closest thing to a sense they have, and it is better than sight. Down their length run cells that read pressure, temperature, the faint mineral signature of a current before it arrives. The schools know a reversal is coming the way you know someone is about to speak — a hush, a gathering, then the turn. When a vortex begins to stand up in one of the great plazas, the ribbons are already climbing it, spiraling in long slow helixes, feeding on the concentrated drift the spinning water has gathered from a hundred corridors at once. When the vortex collapses, they scatter into the dark like spilled thread, and the plaza is empty before the boom of the colliding currents even arrives.

They are not individuals the way fish are individuals. Cut a ribbon in half and you have two ribbons, both of which remember the school. Nobody here has ever studied them, so nobody knows how much of a school is one mind wearing many bodies, and it is possible that question has no answer, and it is possible the ribbons would not understand it if asked.

## The Wall-Clingers

Higher up the taxonomy of patience sit the venerable ones: the barnacled shapes fastened to the worn walls like pale galls on a branch. Some of them are larger than carts. Some may be older than the structure's *stable* age — which is to say, older than anything has a right to be. They grow in millimeters per century. Their shells are layered like the rings of felled trees, and if those layers record anything, they record the history of the currents: lean years, fat years, the long quiet stretches and the seasons when some vast weather above sent pulse after pulse of rich cold water down through the halls, and every clinger in the basin fed until its shell strained at its own seams.

They do not move. There is one old story the currents seem to tell — and I offer it as the kind of thing the basin itself almost suggests — that the clingers were not always fixed. That there is a stage of their life, brief and unobserved, when they swim, and choose their wall, and never choose again. Whether this is true cannot be checked without disturbing them, and disturbing one would mean nothing to it and everything to whoever watches. So they cling, and filter, and their kind has made of immobility a career of staggering success. The currents bring the sea to them. They only have to be there, and they are very, very good at being there.

Where a clinger dies — rarely, eventually — its empty shell becomes the one true shelter in the basin. Small things pile into it. It becomes a pocket of still water in a world of none, and for a few centuries it will hold a household of huddled tenants before the grazer-sand wears it down to nothing and the wall takes the shape back.

## The Grazers

The smallest, the busiest, the only troublemakers. Little darting things, pale as the ribbons but denser, quick-eyed in the sense that they have organs that register shadow and light and current-edge, which is all the eye anyone needs down here. They work the walls constantly, rasping the smooth substrate with mouths that are not quite mouths — more a softened grinding plate, an evolutionary apology for teeth.

They are the only erosion left. Think about that. The structure took geological ages to reach its final shape and then *stopped*, settled, made its peace — and the only thing still changing it, still sanding it down a microscopic grain at a time, is these small hungry crowds. In a sense the grazers are the basin's biography, still being written. Every corridor's polished curve carries their faint toothwork, a texture too fine to feel but real, and the structure is very slowly becoming, on its innermost surface, a record of ten thousand generations of grazing paths. The oldest walls are polished like glass. The youngest — there is no youngest. That's the point. It is all the same age and all of it has been touched.

They ride the pulses the way the ribbons do, but clumsily, joyfully, tumbling in the whitewater of the narrow shafts in dense glittering clouds, eaten by nothing, frightened of nothing, grazing everything.

## The Big Quiet Thing

There is at least one large shape in the deep channels that does not fit any of these categories. Slow. Vast — vast enough that when it passes through a hall the currents bend around it and the smaller fauna adjust without seeming to notice, the way a town adjusts around a mountain. Eyeless, soft-bodied like everything else, but *different* — longer-lived in its motion, more deliberate, as if it is not riding the currents but consulting them. It appears in one corridor, is absent from it for what might be a year, reappears. It does not feed the way the others feed, or if it does, it feeds on something no one has thought to look for.

It is possible it is the last of a lineage that once had competitors. It is possible it is not an animal at all but a growth — a piece of the structure that woke up, the builders' architecture finally producing its own tenant, the way a derelict house grows mold. The hum under everything, the deep resonance of the hollow world — some say it changes pitch when the big thing is near. This is the kind of claim that can't be checked, down where checking means swimming after something patient into the dark end of a corridor that goes on further than you will ever swim.

## How They Live Together

Nothing here hunts. This is not peace — peace implies a choice. It is architecture. The turbulence delivers; the structure shelters; the tenants eat what arrives. The whole assemblage is a single metabolism wearing three or four shapes: the structure is the gut, the currents are the peristalsis, the fauna are the cells of the lining. The builders, whoever they were, made a machine whose purpose dissolved — and life moved in the way life does, into any shape that will hold still long enough, and turned the machine into a world.

The ribbons read the weather. The clingers record it. The grazers erase it, gently, grain by grain. And the big quiet thing — the big quiet thing, whatever it is, *remembers* it.

That may be the closest thing to a purpose left in the Drowned Basin: not the builders' purpose, long since eroded to nothing, but the tenants' own. A machine for making weather in the deep, tenanted by creatures who are, between them, the weather's only historians — pale crowds in the green-gray dark, keeping a record no one will ever read, in a place that has stopped waiting for anyone to come.
