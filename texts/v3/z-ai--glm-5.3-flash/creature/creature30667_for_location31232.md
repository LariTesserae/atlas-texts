Twice a day the Wood's pulse moves through the vascular dark, and the whole town keeps time by it. The taps are opened at flood and sealed at ebb. Babies are named for where in the tide they arrived: flood-born children are said to be generous and bad at saving, ebb-born to be quiet and good at keeping. When the pulse passes through a wall you can put your ear to the grain and hear it, a slow hydraulic sigh, and that is what the infants of Kerf fall asleep to — not a heartbeat, since no one here has heard one, but something slower and vaster that serves the same purpose.

The people themselves are dry the way old furniture is dry. Generations of oil-drinking and honeydew have lacquered them from the inside: skin with a faint sheen, hair that holds the smell of camphor, breath that children in the distillery quarters can be identified by at forty paces. Their surgeons close wounds with heated amber rather than stitching, so an old injury heals as a seam of glass, and a face crossed with bright seams is read instantly as a working face.
