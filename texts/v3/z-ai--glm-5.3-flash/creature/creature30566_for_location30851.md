Above that: nothing. No fish. No snapping shrimp. No whale call arriving from over any horizon, because there are no whales, no horizons worth the name, no weather to speak of. The garden's sounds are the ones it makes itself: the tick of bronze expanding in the sun, the faint rustle of grass meadows combing, the occasional soft pop of a flowering stalk releasing pollen into the warm water. It is the quiet of a room, not a world. A garden room. A nursery.

And it is not unoccupied.

Look closer at the ranked rows of kelp-florets and you'll see the first gardeners. They are small — hand-sized, most of them — and they move through the canopy with the unhurried deliberateness of creatures who have never known a predator. Their bodies are soft-shelled, segmented like improbable shrimp, colored pale jade and amber, with cilia-fringed arms that grip vine and stalk without tearing them. They prune. That is not an analogy. You can watch one work a flowering stalk: test it, turn it, pinch away a bloom that has gone over, carry the cut fragment down to a tray seam and pack it into the granular medium with its forelimbs, deliberately, like a child learning to bury a seed and taking the lesson seriously. They were made for this — you can see it in the way their bodies are shaped, the way a hoe is shaped — but made long enough ago, or grown long enough free, that they have opinions now. Some work fast and sloppy. Some are perfectionists who spend entire days on a single row. One, at the northern weir, has a habit of resting its arms on the wall and looking out through the shivering boundary at the young blue sea, and you would swear, watching, that it is thinking about it.

Under the trays, in the seams, live the digesters: fat, slow, blind tube-things that eat the prunings the jade gardeners pack down to them and exhale nutrient mist into the granular medium. They are the garden's stomach. They move about once an hour. In the central channel, near the intake grate that draws from the garden's own golden water, hovering grazers the size of plums keep the channel walls clean, moving in loose schools that wheel and part with the smooth collective stupidity of small fish — though nothing here has ever needed to flee anything, and their schooling looks less like defense than like sociability.

Above, in the highest layer of the canopy, where the leaves overlap three deep and the light comes down green-gold, there is a kind nobody tends. Broad dark gliders, manta-shaped, palm-sized to table-sized, that ride the thermal plumes rising off the vent grates — the garden's weather, the only weather this world has, made by the garden itself, for itself. They do nothing you can point to. They take pollen on their undersides going up and shed it coming down. They may be the closest thing this place has to birds, if birds had never learned fear and never needed to.

Nothing here eats anything that moved, until recently, because nothing here is the remains of anything that struggled. Death in the garden is a pruning, a carrying-down, a packing into the seams. The jade gardeners take their dead the same way — you'll see it eventually, if you stay: a procession of six of them, bearing one of their own, limbs folded, down the glazed tile to the northern trays, where they bury it among the roots of the oldest kelp-floret, the one whose base is a trunk now, whose crown has been pinched and trained by forty uncountable generations of hands into a shape no blueprint ever specified.

That is the thing about the First Pylon's crown. It was finished yesterday, and it is already drifting. The boundary at the weir shivers a little differently each season of the young sea. The gardeners prune a little less like instruments. Somewhere below, in the sealed dark of the shaft, the machinery breathes, and no one who lives up here in the warm gold-green light has ever seen it, and none of them ask. The garden keeps its own water. The sea keeps its. And between them, on the weir wall, the small jade gardeners lean and look out at all that empty blue — the room's only window — and wait, without knowing they're waiting, for the world outside to be invented.
