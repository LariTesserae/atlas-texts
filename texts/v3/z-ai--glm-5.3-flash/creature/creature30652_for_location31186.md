# Who Lives in the Sump Gardens

The first honest answer is: nobody. You circle the plateau twice and find no footprints but your own, no bones, no nests, no smoke-stain on the stone. The hut is a planter now; the factory is a reef. Whatever the builders meant to do up here, they are gone, and you are standing inside their absence, listening to it tick.

Stay the night, though, and the answer revises itself. Nobody lives here the way a person lives in a house — but the house is not empty. It is full, all the way up, at a scale you weren't looking at when you asked. The residents of the Sump Gardens are mostly smaller than your thumbnail and mostly older than your grandmother, and there are far more of them than you counted in your first ten circuits.

Begin with the water. It is the founding population, the one everything else is built on. It is born here every morning — vapor climbing all day off the lowlands, dying against the cold shelf of stone, and being reborn as a bead. Each drop lives for a few hours, a day at most, on a plateau smaller than a village square, and then it falls. Watch the cliff edges at dusk, where the mist pours off in gold threads: that is the day's entire generation leaving home. The plateau is a nursery where water is briefly alive, and the old machinery was built to hurry the births along — to wring from the air a little more than the stone alone would give. It still works, barely, and you can see the proof in the real estate. The ferns crowding the machine's drip-lines are twice the size of the others. The machines still decide where the wet is, and where the wet is decides who lives where. The factory is not a ruin here. It is the landlord.

Under everything is the moss, which is the oldest continuous civilization on the summit. A hand-span a year means the moss is sedimentary — the surface you walk on is the youngest generation, and beneath it lie strata of ancestors, compressed and dark, holding whatever fell among them: fern spores, moth scales, flakes of rust, and somewhere, pressed flat like a flower in a book, a nail from a builder's boot. The moss is the plateau's memory. And it is pasture. Press a finger into a dense green cushion and it gives a little — not from softness, but because ten thousand springtails and mites have moved aside to let you in. They are the livestock, the base of the whole tower, grazing the moss the way cattle graze a hillside, invisible from your height because you are looking at a country from its own stratosphere.

Then the moths. You saw two; there are more, a dozen or so, living inside the condenser fins where the copper holds the night's condensation longest. They are pale because color is spending, and up here nobody collects — nothing has needed to see them in a century, so they have faded to the color of the mist they drink from. They hang in the seep-threads that bleed from the cliff face, sipping from thin air in the most literal way anything on this mountain does. When they land on your sleeve, they are not being tame. They are being accurate: you are the first tall thing this plateau has ever produced, a walking cliff, new topography, and they are treating you exactly as they would treat a novelty like that — by perching on it to see if it will drip.

The skittering thing, the one you never see twice: you were wrong about it, and the wrongness is the point. It is not one shy animal. It is hundreds of them, a dynasty of small rust-brown crickets living in the pipes and under the fern crowns, all alike, all quick. You never see the same one twice because there is no such thing as the same one — there is a population, uniform as screws from the same tray. Some of them live inside the winch drum, in the last slack turn of the cable, and when the wind pulls the wire and the drum hums, their whole house sings. They answer it. Call and response, all night, between a cricket and its own architecture.

And there is a larger animal, one the plateau keeps almost entirely to itself: a toad, small as a walnut, brown-black, living in the engine block where the crankcase has become a permanent pool. Its species has never known ground. Every individual of its kind, going back however many generations, has been born within four hundred steps of this spot and will die within them, and the pool in the engine block is its whole sea. It is the summit's apex predator — the entire top of the food chain, weighing less than an egg — and it hunts the moths at dusk from the flywheel's spokes, and once a night it calls, a single note so quiet and liquid you would swear you heard a drip falling upward.

The snail is the best citizen here, and the clue to it is on the gauges. Those perfect circles of lichen on the dial faces are not lichen. They are grazing scars — the lifetime's mowing of one animal, or rather a line of them, mother to daughter, each working the same route across the same painted arcs, appetite and regrowth matched so exactly that the circles have held their shape for decades. The snail lives in the condenser fins, drinks the machine's harvest, eats the machine's decorations, and unknowingly keeps its dials clean. Its shell is banded with years, like the trees, and it is older than any of them. Its ancestors came up the winch cable a century ago, stowaway eggs in a crate of greens hauled for the builders' table, and when the work was done and the workers went down, the snails stayed. They are the last part of the operation still running on schedule. The only employee who never resigned.

Because that is the pattern, once you see it: everything that lives here arrived by air, by accident, or by cable — seed in a bird's gut, egg in a crate, moth on the wind, snail in the packing — and everything that arrived, stayed. The only residents who ever left are the ones who came on purpose. The builders climbed up, furnished the room, and descended into the cloud, and the mountain kept everything except them. Even the machinery itself came up that cable once, piece by piece, and lay down here and never moved again, and has been turning into an animal ever since — the hum its purr, the eleven-second tick its breathing, the copper fins gone to gill, the green not burying it but keeping it warm while it slowly becomes soil the patient way.

So when evening comes and the mist catches fire along the cliff edges, look at who is actually home. The moths hanging in the seep-threads. The toad flat in the flywheel's shadow, digesting its kingdom. The crickets tuning the winch. The snail starting its round on the gauge faces, sixty years deep in the same circles. The springtails in their pastures, the moss over its strata of ancestors, the water being born again in its thousands on every cold surface, the mountain beneath it all sweating and settling, the newest resident of all — still catching its breath from the climb, and the one who will stay longest.

You were the only thing up there that was visiting.

It turns out the factory succeeded, in the end. It just never shipped anything. It made weather, and the weather moved in — and filled the room, at exactly the size the room was always meant for.
