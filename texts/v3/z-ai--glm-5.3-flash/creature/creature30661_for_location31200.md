Everything here runs on a timetable so slow it looks like patience.

The seam in the ceiling — some old crack in the concrete, a hairline that goes all the way back to the world that poured it — gathers its moisture hour by hour and releases it in a single fat drop, then another, at intervals long enough that the moss below has organized itself around the landing point. There, where the water strikes, the cushion is dense and emerald and enthusiastic; further out, the colonies grow sparse, thinning into a green haze, then a dusting, then the plain gray of stone. You could read the whole history of the room in that gradient, like tree rings laid flat.

The moss has been here longer than anything else alive in here. It keeps no record of when it arrived, but the water has carved a shallow basin in the floor beneath it, a bowl the size of a spread hand, and that took years. Decades, maybe. The water in the bowl is black and perfectly still, and the moss glows in it — a doubled light, slightly trembled, so the bowl looks like a small green moon in a green-black sky.

What lives here, in full: the moss, certainly. Something fungal, too — you can infer it from the way some cushions fail from the center out, replaced by pale gray threads that do not glow, a slow war being fought on a scale of millimeters and years, neither side aware of the other in any way we'd recognize, both winning sometimes. On one wall, near where the frost feathers grow, a film of bacteria has claimed the seep line and shines a different color — a colder blue, faint as breath on glass — so that if you look sideways, the room's one wall has a horizon.

And something moves.

It's easy to miss, because nothing here hurries and nothing here is large. But there are pale threads in the water bowl that withdraw when a drop falls — tiny translucent worms, nematodes, hair-thin, ghost-white, blind as their whole world requires them to be. There may be a few hundred. They graze the bacterial film, they rise when the drop disturbs the surface the way their bodies remember to rise, and they have never seen the moss that lights their sky. They live inside the light the way fish live inside the sea.

There are springtails too, probably — no. Yes. Around the dampest cushions, if you crouched for the hours it would take, you'd see them: specks no bigger than commas, gray-white, walking on the surface film of the water bowl, drifting, feeding on what the fungus leaves behind. They are the room's largest animals. They hold that title absolutely.

The air itself is a resident. It is still, but not empty — it carries a spore haze, so faint that the glow catches it only when a drop falls and the impact stirs the air, and then for a moment the light above the moss thickens, a green dust of dispersal, drifting toward the ceiling seam, riding it, going wherever the seam goes.

Nothing here knows about you. Nothing here has ever needed a schedule besides water and cold, and so nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing here is afraid. The drop falls. The worms withdraw. The light dims a little where the impact scattered the film, and begins, immediately, the slow work of returning.

That is the whole population: moss, mold, film, worms, commas of insect life, and water with a schedule. A room that has never had a window, holding a complete small world, glowing to itself in the dark, unaware that it is beautiful.
