Begin with the arithmetic, because everyone who lives here lives inside it. A human body is rather more than half water by weight. Which means that every resident of the New Cut is, biologically speaking, a walking reservoir of the one substance this world cannot afford — a violation on legs, permitted only inside the body that commits it. The whole culture of the place is the practice of that permission. It is why the people here move the way they do: carefully, lips closed, hands wiped, coughs smothered into elbows, the perpetual small choreography of creatures quarantine-keeping against their own bodies. They are the exception the city makes for itself, and they know it, and the knowledge has shaped their manners, their architecture, their tendernesses, and their dead.

The true census of the district is not a headcount. It is the ledger. Every person here knows their number — the daily measure of moisture surrendered at the drylock — the way we know our weight or our age, and they speak of it without shame or pride. Drinking happens at the wet-alcoves: slate-floored cells with copper drains, where you take your ration under a small amber lamp and wipe your mouth when you are done, because a wet lip inside the city is a small public discourtesy, like smoking in a nursery. Sweat is blotted with wicks that go to the reclaim. And grief — grief happens at the slate. Tears run to a gutter with a gauge at the end of it. Nobody in the New Cut thinks this cruel; they think it accurate. A weeping person is not sent away in shame but escorted, the way you'd walk a feverish friend home, and what is said in a wet-room stays there — not by law but by a custom so old it has the force of gravity. The most expensive sentence in the local language is *come to the slate with me*, and it is used for confessions, proposals, and the telling of terrible news, and it is never wasted on small talk. High praise, here, is to say of someone: *she keeps a dry house.*

The children are born at the slate, in the one room in every household where stone replaces wood, and their first act is their first measure — the midwife records the birth-water, and that is the first line of a ledger that will run the length of a life. Their first lessons are the two catastrophes, and local children learn both before they learn letters, in a rhyme that goes: *a drop makes a spot, a spot makes a rot; a spark makes a dark, and a dark keeps what* — the last line unfinished on purpose, so the child supplies the ending, which is everything. They are taught that their own bodies are the dangerous thing, and they absorb it without trauma, the way coastal children absorb tides. They also have one pure delight forbidden to children anywhere else in creation: in the dry dark of the galleries, a wool sleeve dragged across a grown bench cracks with blue static stars, and the children chase them, shrieking — until an adult appears, because shrieking is loud and sound is scarce here — and the adult will touch the copper knot at the district seam in one automatic motion, a benediction worn smooth of all terror except the muscle of it. Every child in the New Cut has seen the blue sparks. No child has seen rain.

The trades here are less occupations than castes of temperament, and you can read a resident's trade off their body before you hear their voice. The grafters are broad-handed and slow-spoken, with knuckles like burls, and they keep hours that would look like madness anywhere else: they speak to young timber at night, in level voices, reciting dimensions the way other people say prayers. A grafter raising a stair-tower talks to it the way a parent talks to a tall child — some daily, some weekly, and one, it is said, has addressed the same unfinished tower for twenty years and swears it leans to listen. Couples here marry by speaking a house into the wood together, describing it aloud to the Stock while the grafter stands witness with string and laths; the marriage is recorded twice, once in the registry and once in the rings, and of the two records everyone agrees the rings are the binding one. The listeners, by contrast, are gaunt and nearly silent, with gouges worn to horn-shapes and the flat gray eyes of people who live in the dark of walls. They know you by your step from three seams away — *the wood reports everyone*, they say — and they can tell a press falling out of rhythm a district away before the press-crew feels it. The listeners of the East Cell are local saints; school-children are walked to the scar where the wet-rot was cut out of the living tissue like a tumor, a grey slatewood plug the size of a chapel, and they touch it for luck against rot the way other children touch iron.

The lamplighters are the strangest caste, because they carry the only thing the city truly fears. They are quiet, deliberate people, sworn in orders older than the district, their lamps sealed like relics, their wick-shears made of stone. When a lamplighter walks a seam at dusk the moth-flocks reorganize around the lamp as it goes, so the lamplighter travels inside a slow grey fountain of wings — a procession children line up to count. They are celebrities of a very narrow kind: admired, slightly feared, checked on. A lamplighter's record follows them for life, and there is a woman in the Weft quarter who once dropped a sealed lamp forty years ago — nothing caught, nothing even cracked — and who still, the whole district knows, checks the seal three times before every shift, and no one has ever once thought to tease her about it. The tappers are the opposite temperament: quick, amber-stained to the wrist, half-gamblers, who open the sapwells timed against the tide and take the rich first draw as their fee. The scale-shepherds of the comb-racks live in darkness and sweetness, working by touch among the racks, and in the comb-racks the only sin is sudden noise, so they hum — low, constant, tuned to the racks — and their insects, they claim, hum back. The press-men are proud, half-deaf on one side, forearms laminated with resin to a gloss, and they speak of pile-wood with the reverence smiths reserve for good steel.

Above all of them, in rotation, live the mirror-keepers: the sky-people. They go up the shaft in shifts to tend the heliostat field, and they come back down smelling of wind — which is to say, of nothing, a smell no one below can identify on first encounter, so that children find returning keepers faintly frightening, the way children find anything that has been near a legend. They are the city's weather-prophets. A keeper who saw cloud crossing the surface at midmorning will send down word, and by afternoon the whole district is waiting for the flicker, reading the ceilings. There is a courtesy exchanged between friends down there: *may your ceilings flicker kindly.*

And out on the frontier live the drovers, with their husk-oxen, and they are the strangest residents of all, because they live mostly in silence and motion, weeks at a stretch behind their armored beasts, sleeping in the fresh-cut galleries the oxen open. A drover trusts the ox's appetite over her own map. The oxen break virgin wood the way water breaks stone, patiently, and the drovers read the trails like scripture, and they return to the settled districts with a feral patience that makes even the listeners seem chatty.

Here is the inversion that defines the place, and that no visitor notices at first: in the New Cut, the people are older than the buildings. The oldest resident arrived when the district was string and lath, and she has out-aged every vault in it; children play in halls younger than their grandmothers. For immigrants from the deep rings this is nearly unbearable. In the old cities, the walls are already written — centuries of rings, flood-years and lean-years and the black seam of the great fire laid down like strata — and the people move through them like mayflies in a library. The deep cities call the New Cut folk *saplings*, meaning it half as an insult, and the New Cut folk have started using the word about themselves with unmistakable pride. There is a story the immigrants tell, not quite complaining: an old woman from the deep rings who could not sleep in the New Cut for a year, because she would run her thumb along the wall in the dark and find no rings to read. A town with no books, she said. No cemetery either. She got used to it eventually, the way people get used to youth — by recognizing that it is not permanent.

The animals, meanwhile, are neighbors in the old sense — close enough to know you individually. The sap-moths learn the lamplighters' rounds and will settle on a still shoulder. Children raise moth-caterpillars in grown cages and release them at dusk into the resettlement, and watch for their moth at the family sapwell thereafter, sometimes for years. The gall-wasps are tolerated the way falcons are: respected, fed at the lamp-housings in lean years, never named. Only the livestock have names, and only barely — the scale-shepherds distinguish their thousands by touch, and the drovers' oxen answer to single syllables. What the fauna and the people share, more than anything, is the quiet. A dropped tool is a cough in a church; a raised voice is an event with a location; and everyone here, human and wasp alike, has hearing sharpened to a fine point. Visitors are identified for their first month purely by their inability to whisper.

Death, in a world with no decay, had to be invented, and the New Cut invented two customs, and everyone eventually chooses between them. The first is the graft: the dead are taken to the drylock one last time, the instruments finishing their ledger to the final measure — the number is read aloud at the rite, the life's total, all accounted — and then the dried body is laid into a crypt-gallery and the Stock is invited to close around it. Over decades the person becomes an inclusion, a figure in the grain. Families visit great-grandmothers' elbows in the wall. The most honored choose load-bearing placement, and it is said of them, without any of the darkness you'd expect, that *she holds the east vault now.* The second custom is slate-keeping: the body entombed in slatewood, mineralized, stepped out of time, present but done. The choice between the two is the closest thing the city has to a theology — whether to keep growing with the Stock or to step out of its time — and people argue it at dinners with real feeling, and no one has ever settled it, and no one expects to.

As for the Stock itself — the question every visitor eventually asks, and the residents have stopped asking: is it one of them? The answers range as widely as the population. Some call it the Landlord, some the Elder, some nothing at all; the grafters use the tone farmers keep for large animals and old horses, familiar without presumption. The listeners, who know it best, insist it has moods — new districts skittish, old seams settled — and the heretics insist it anticipates nothing, and every grafter keeps talking to it regardless, because folklore or physics, the practice works. Near young wood, people mind their tongues, and not from superstition alone: there is a stairwell in the Fourth Seam where a man once cursed his brother at length against fresh tissue, and the rings kept every word, and children are taken to see it as a lesson in both etiquette and permanence. Against this, whole institutions run the other way: the ring-wrights, who draft what the walls *will* say, planning the decade's diary in advance, choosing which rooms receive which spoken dimensions — the only historians anywhere who work ahead of their subject. Oaths here are spoken to timber precisely because timber keeps them, and a marriage, a grudge, and a price of lamp-oil can all be verified a century later by anyone with a gouge and a lamp.

So try to take the census, and you cannot stop at people. Count the drylock staff wiping down each arrival, the ledger-keepers, the grafters murmuring to their towers, the listeners with their ears to the seams, the lamplighters walking inside their grey fountains, the shepherds humming in the dark racks, the drovers out where the wood is unbroken, the sky-people, the saplings. Then count the moths in their tens of thousands, the wasps under every lamp, the scales in their dark millions, the oxen on the frontier. And then you arrive, reluctantly, at the last and largest resident: the Stock itself, tissue standing in for geology, the landlord, the nursery, the archive still blank in this district, still deciding. The population of the New Cut is mostly the New Cut.

And once a day, the whole census moves together. The tide comes up through the grain — subsonic, floor-borne, a lift beneath everything — and the presses throttle back, the moths drink, the tappers open their wells, the lamplighters sit down on their seals, and every resident of the district, person and beast and timber alike, rises slightly on the same slow swell and settles again. A young world, keeping its water out and its people in, held — sweep by sweep, measure by measure — by the only creatures in it made of the thing it cannot afford.
