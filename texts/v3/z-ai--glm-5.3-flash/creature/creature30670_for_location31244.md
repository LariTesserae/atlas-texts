They drank from the lake for their whole first lives, and when the lake stood down they did not argue; they simply sent their roots deeper, following the water into the dark, and now they drink from a source no one has ever seen. Their canopy is the basin's ceiling. Under it the light falls green and the air is always a step cooler and a step wetter than the air outside, and the ground is packed with the dropped leavings of the tree — leaf, fruit, the chalky drip-line of decades — so that the floor of a fig's shade is a country of its own, a soft black aisle where things can live their whole lives without once crossing open ground.

The moths, when the river of them passes, spend their days in these aisles. By light they hang under leaves in their thousands, wings folded, looking like nothing — like gaps in the leaf, like spent buds — and the day-birds hunt them anyway, with partial success, because a moth that looks like a gap in a leaf is hard to argue with. At dusk they rise all at once, a soft receiving of the canopy's ceiling back into air, and by full dark they are streaming south again, drawn to the flares. They die out there in their thousands, spiraling down into the light-slick around the Works, and it does not diminish them. The basin manufactures moths the way it manufactures dew: continuously, without effort, out of the figs and the cress and the loam. The flare takes a tithe. The basin pays it and continues.

The herons do not go south. They hold the tarns, and their patience is the basin's patience made visible. A heron can stand in one leg of water for the length of an afternoon, gray on gray, indistinguishable from the reed shadows, and the fish come to it because the fish have learned in the shrinking season that stillness is where the water lasts longest. As the tarns fall, the fish concentrate; as the fish concentrate, the herons' banquet simplifies. There are weeks when a heron need only open its beak at the right moment. They grow fat and glossy and slightly contemptuous, and their nesting trees — the tallest figs, the ones that kept the deepest roots — are loud all season with the flat clattering of young ones demanding the same easy fish.

The snakes keep to the pipeline lane, but not only there. Everything in the Sink wanders except the one straight thing, and the snakes honor the straightness only at night, when the buried line gives up its warmth. By day they are elsewhere: under the bark slabs at the fig roots, in the cracked mouths of the drying tarns, in the hedge-thickets where the weaverbirds build their hanging cities and the snakes collect a toll. There are kingsnakes here, and water snakes gone semi-terrestrial since the water left, and one long gray species that nobody has named, because naming requires people, and the point of the Sink is that it has none.

That absence is the strangest tenant of all. No one lives here, and the basin has organized itself around that fact the way a room organizes itself around a missing chair. There is nothing in the Sink that waits for a hand. The reeds stand and fall on their own schedule; the cracks breathe on theirs; the figs drop their fruit and it ferments where it falls and the doves come down drunk at mid-morning and stagger about with the borrowed dignity of the elderly. The tarns shrink, the herons eat, the moths go south, the snakes lie warm at midnight on the only warm ground, and the enormous counting note from the southeast keeps its beat under all of it — felt in the chest at the still hours, more felt than heard, like a pulse belonging to the ground itself.

The Sink does not miss the lake. That is the final thing. A basin that had lost something would show it — would show want, the way the old shoreline shows the water level — but the gray ribbon of dead reed is not want; it is only a record, kept by a country too damp to burn and too dry to rot, of where the water used to lean. Everything below it has rearranged itself around the new arithmetic and found the new arithmetic sufficient. Lush on dew. Fed by a rain that falls out of leaves. Warmed at midnight by a machine it has never seen and never will. The moths stream south toward the only false light in the world, and the Sink lets them go, and makes more.
