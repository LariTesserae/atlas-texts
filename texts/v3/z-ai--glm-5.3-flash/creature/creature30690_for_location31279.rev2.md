Begin counting at the rim, because the rim is the one place in the Weep where sound still lives.

Up on the black hills the mist gives up its claim and the air goes thin and dry and — this shocks every visitor — *loud*. The flash-post keepers live two to a post, a season at a time, mostly old herders past the eyeshades and young ones not yet ready for the pans. They keep the mirrors clean and the census moving: herds counted in blinks, relayed post to post down the line of hills, the basin counting itself in light. Between flashes there is nothing to do but talk. So they talk. Out loud. At distances that would be unseemly anywhere below. After a season of it they come down the switchbacks shouting like freed prisoners, and there is a saying in Weepstead, always spoken with love and a steadying hand on the arm: *loud as a post-keeper*.

The Rim Yard stands at the head of the stair, the last station before the quiet takes hold. It is the town's one licensed loud-house. Caravans rest there; lowlanders recover there; Weepstead folk going up spend an evening there getting the shouting out of their systems, like a fever breaking. Grain comes up the switchbacks, and timber, and iron, and news. Salt goes down, and silk, and shell-plate, on molt-year rakers that have walked that grade since before the mains had names. Over the rim-cliffs the thermal-riders nest, out where there is real wind — the basin has none — and their fledglings learn the air in the one classroom that still makes noise. The post-keepers say the wind up there is chatty. Arriving Weepstead ears take a full day to stop flinching at it.

Descend into the steam and the loud country falls off you like a coat. At the head of the stair live the engineers, because the heat lives there and the engineers are its tenants, and their whole craft is tenancy done well. The oath is administered with a palm flat on a main — *draw no faster than the Weep weeps* — and the main is warm under the hand, which is the point: you swear it to something that can feel it. Their instruments are the pans themselves; a senior engineer reads a pan the way a doctor reads a face, color by color, rim by rim. Everything they build is built by press. The Weep has no hammers and no striking anything — the copper comes down under slow hydraulics, through a long second, the glass comes up out of the blue mouths' breath — and by now, everyone will tell you, this is not merely the law. It is the physiology. It is the temperament.

The mains are staffed. The brushers are small hot-water snail-things, mute and industrious, and each main's colony has its page in the works ledger and its keeper, who knows them by shell — the shell takes the mineral of the main it grazes, so a brushed shell is a passport, and an engineer can read a stranger's brusher like a letter from home. Brushers come to warmth, ride pockets, tolerate affection. In a cold snap the keepers walk the line with felt bags, moving colonies toward the blue mouths the way a man carries cats out of a flood, and when a snap takes a colony anyway there is a small silent funeral — everything here is silent — a dish of warm water poured back down the main it kept, and the year's scale runs late, and is forgiven.

Over the Seethe the vapor-bells do their slow work: born the size of a fist, leaving — if they leave — the size of a doorway. They drink the vapor, ride the columns, exhale a salt-dust so fine the caravaneers call it flour and price it like thread. At the season's turn the silks part and a bell settles onto whatever pan is beneath it, leaving a ring of salt on the water, the ghost of a chandelier. Bell-rings are swept and saved; children string the rims like coins. Nobody rings them. Nobody would know how, and the Weep has never once wanted to try.

And the bloom is the oldest resident, and the most reticent. It was laying down its rings of rust and saffron before the Stair had risers, and it keeps no calendar anyone can read, though the pan-tenders try. In a place with no flowers, Weepstead supports gardeners anyway, and the garden is color. On warm nights bubbles rise through the mats and burst in small cold sparks of light, and the pan-tenders call it the bloom talking in its sleep, and the engineers call it chemistry, and both parties keep tending — which tells you what both of them actually believe.

The town itself is graded like its ground: mains below, streets above, sky-juice over all, and the people arranged through the strata accordingly. They are recognizably people, at kissing distance. Nobody ever dries here, so nobody is ever rough: hands like river stones, skins that shine, the old looking fired — glazed, pearled at the knuckles from years of foot-pans, wearing faint rings of saffron and ink up the forearms from years of pans, the bloom claiming them the way it claims everything it touches. The ears are narrow-range: raised on whispers, they resolve everything inside a yard and blur the rest. The feet are wide, callused soft, permanently apologetic to the stone. And the posture is the tell. Everyone leans. A conversation is conducted mouth to ear, and a life of leaning tilts a body, so that old couples in Weepstead stand like two trees that grew into each other's weather.

A whisper is addressed. A shout is thrown away, and here it is not even thrown very far. So the town says what it means close, and to be leaned on is to be trusted, and the whole social order hangs on that: intimacy is proximity, and secrets die of closeness, which is the only death this town permits them. Court is conducted in mist-hand, on the theory — borne out, everyone insists — that the hands are illiterate liars. There is no word for *deaf* in Weepstead speech. The nearest is *far*. And the sign for quiet is the flat palm going down, which is also the sign for the mist, and for sleep, and for the Verge. One gesture carries all of it. The language has known for a long time that these are the same thing.

The Weep's one instrument is bitten, not blown. Tooth-harps — rails of copper tines clenched in the jaw — deliver the song straight into the skull, and at weddings the guests bite a common rail so the tune arrives in forty heads at once, which everyone agrees beats any lowland choir for togetherness. Lullabies are tapped through bedframes. The turbine's slow vowel is the town's drone note, the water-clock its timekeeper, and every child here falls asleep to the feeling of being spoken to through wood. The clock, heard by exactly whoever stands beneath it, turned out once the town noticed to be the finest meeting place in the world: you go to the clock to be alone with someone.

First work of the day belongs to the sky. The whole town is up on its roofs at first light with long scrapers, milking the night's condensation out of the sky-pans before the sun takes it back, and below them, in the same ten minutes, the sippers swarm the overhangs doing exactly the same job. For a few minutes each dawn, the entire population of the Weep — human and otherwise — is employed at one task: licking the night off the stone. A house with sippers in its lagging is called warm-hearted. They arrive like weather and leave with it, and twice a season the dawn-nets take enough of the run to flavor the month. Nobody feels badly about this. The sippers come back. Everything here comes back.

Wealth is cistern depth, and health is light-count. Every cistern keeps its lights — glass-fleas, smaller than grain, that graze the films the sky-juice brings down and shine coldly back when children lower a lamp-glass to count them. A cistern that goes dark is drained, scrubbed, and re-seeded like a hive, and its owner is pitied more deeply than words go, which in Weepstead is very deeply indeed.

The Clatter Ban is the oldest law on the books and makes one exception for everyone in the town: it does not apply to anyone under a year old. Babies are Weepstead's one licensed weather. A child's milestones run acoustic in reverse — the first sign-word, the first name learned as a rhythm on a rail, the first herd read by its silences — and the games are training with the training removed: *stiller-stiller*, the freeze game, where the loser is whoever moved, which is to say, whoever got seen. And once or twice a season, when the warm rain comes down through sunshine, the children are permitted to shout. Most do, once, and then stand stunned at their own volume, having never heard themselves at full length. Some cry. The town regards this as the true rite of passage, and stands at a distance, shining, so as not to embarrass them.

Courtship is a study in millimeters. The lean closes by degrees over months, and a first touch is often a temple laid against an ear, and a proposal is made with the mouth an inch away, so that a *yes* arrives as warm breath. You will see old couples leaning at the rail by the hour, saying nothing, the mains carrying the conversation they are not having. You will also see widows leaning at the same rail, alone, and understand that grief in Weepstead is a missing weight at your shoulder. It is considered a small kindness to stand next to such a person and hold still.

The Book of Threads is the town's true census. It holds every seep on every riser, each with its name, each name with its year, and the Namekeeper walks the rolls at every boil-tide because a tide can retire a thread or raise a new one and the record must keep up with the water. Names outlive their people here. The dead of Weepstead come back as water: a family petitions, a thread takes the name, and thereafter the town drinks their grandmother coming down the riser every morning, and no one finds it strange. It is agreed to be the best afterlife on offer — cold, constant, slightly mineral. The dead themselves are dried, the one thing the Weep does not do to its living: packed in raker salt, wrapped in felted sinew, laid in warm crypts cut into the risers, placed flush against the mains where the talk is. Run a knuckle down a crypt lintel and the name taps back. They lie within listening distance. It is the town's one extravagance with its dead.

Out on the treads, the herders live in felted tents pitched on ground the Stair heats for free, dark inside to spare the eyes, and they work the herds barefoot — because the rakers talk through the ground. A slow foot-pressure, repeated, travels a tread like a word down a rail, and a good herder, sole to herd, can tell you whether the animals are feeding, moving, or worried. Which is the craft inside the craft: you track a stiller by what the herd stops saying.

The stiller is the Weep's one predator and its best citizen. It is quiet in the air, which everyone knows, and quiet in the stone, which is the harder discipline — its feet read the crust before they commit their weight to it, and its eye betrays it only as a single glint at the right angle of sun, which is why herders watch eyes. It kills by press, the way the smiths forge. Nothing in the basin strikes anything. A young stiller's first year is a starvation curriculum: it goes hungry until it learns to be ignorable. Custom says let it hunt, and custom is arithmetic — moving herds do not graze the treads bare, and the stiller keeps the herds moving. The town would no sooner remove its stiller than its turbine.

Once a year the rakers' young balloon, and the morning air over the Stair fills with glinting threads, a blizzard that makes no sound at all. The children run the treads with oiled frames catching silk, the year's cloth is decided by lunch, and the afternoon is a holiday observed in the local fashion: nobody says anything, and everybody is understood. At the tides, the herds come down to the fresh-painted rims and the stiller comes down after them, and up on the rim the posts begin to flicker — the basin, counting itself again. Twice a season, if the tides are generous, a rider comes down to drink at the foot-pans, folding like a grey tent, standing taller than a shed, its wingbeat arriving not as sound but as a pressure, a door closing gently over a whole terrace. Seeds ride in its feathers from everywhere. Every seed dies politely. Nothing here has ever flowered, and the Weep, with all its practice, declines them without regret.

At the foot of the stair the pans go bath-warm, and this is where everyone goes to think. The old sit in them up to their chins by the hour, glazed and gleaming, and settle the town's problems at the rate of one per afternoon. Beyond them the Verge begins: grey, finished, retired. Husk-beetles live out there, light as coins, drinking the dawn off the dead stone and spending their days expensively doing nothing. And the rakers, when they are done, walk out onto the Verge, find their angle, and stop. There are standing shells out there older than the town, and the town leaves them standing. A shell-reader can count tide-years off a flank the way the lowlands count rings off an oak. The Verge is the only history the Weep keeps that wrote itself.

Ask anyone born here what lives in the Weep and they will not begin with people. They will begin with the water, and they will be right in the way that matters, because everything here holds to the same terms. Heat is borrowed politely. Water is grown drop by drop. Light is spent in flashes, sound is spent mouth to ear — and everything speaks through what it touches: the bloom by color, the rakers through the ground, the brushers through their keepers' feet, the people by lean and hand and tap, the dead by lintel, the bells by light and dust, the riders by shadow. Sound left the air a long time ago, and nobody here has been waiting up for it.

And somewhere below, on the night shift, a listener lies along a main with her cheek on the warm stone, feeling the day's talk go by — flumes, herds, the turbine's vowel, a herder's coded rattle three miles off, a name from a crypt — and at shift's end she taps up the one report the town wants: *all quiet*.

In the Weep, all quiet does not mean that nothing is being said. It means that everything is.
