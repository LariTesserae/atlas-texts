The people of Welling are made by the sound the way the stone is made by the water: slowly, in layers, under pressure.

You notice the hands first. Everyone's hands are different from hands elsewhere — wider in the web between thumb and forefinger, the signing fingers quick and independent as the fingers of musicians, the palms callused in strange places from rope and wet stone and the carrying of things up terraces. Children sign before they speak, and their first words arrive already as an afterthought to an established fluency. Watch a Welling family at table and you will see the conversation continue when someone turns away to tend a pot; watch two acquaintances meet on a causeway in hard rain and you will see an entire exchange conducted inside a minute, both sets of hands moving under an oilcloth like fish under ice. The signing dialects are local to the point of being diagnostic — lower-terrace hands are clipped and small, the accretion yards' hands broad and slow, and the pump-engineers have a private register, half-sign half-whistle, that outsiders mistake for a language of its own.

Their faces are quiet. This is not dourness; it is allocation. In a place where the voice cannot travel, the face has been returned to its older work, and Welling faces are expressive in the way that faces in loud cities are not — a raised brow carries a whole clause, a slow blink is a greeting or a farewell depending on its length. Strangers read them as unreadable until they learn to read them, and then find them more legible than speech.

Their work is patience, and it has made them patient in a way that can look like fatalism and is closer to horticulture. The accretion-farmers are the city's aristocracy of temperament: men and women who walk the same wooden frames every day for forty years, adjusting threads of warm water by widths a fingernail could block, tending stone that will be a lintel in their great-grandchildren's houses. They speak of their pillars the way farmers elsewhere speak of orchards — this one a good grower, that one stubborn, that one fouled by a bad decade you can see in its rings. They are, without quite saying so, historians: the whole city reads its past in grown stone, and the growers know which fault-line in which wall is the swallow of a grandfather's time, and will touch it with two fingers in passing, the way you'd touch a grave-marker.

The wheel-keepers and pump-men are a different caste — louder in their own bodies, heavier, given to stamping. They live against the pulse, one vast stroke every six breaths, and you can see it in them: a way of moving that arrives on the beat, work done in the long exhale between strokes, a habit of standing with the weight on one heel and letting the engine's rhythm pick them up like a current. Old pump-men can tell you the water table by the taste of the exhaust steam and the feel of the floor through their boots, and they are consulted like physicians. When the swallow comes — that single century-breath when the plateau inhales — it is the pump-men everyone's hands go to, the way a congregation looks at its priest.

Everyone keeps lamps. This is the closest thing Welling has to manners. Because the city cannot hear, it speaks in light, and a person's lamp is their voice: shuttered and relit in patterns, carried high on the causeways at the set pitches of the steam-whistles, hung in the hushes — those circles of dense silence — where a burning lamp means someone was here, means the silence is watched, means you may pass but not stay. The lamplighters are a quiet guild even by Welling's standards, and the children love them the way children elsewhere love bell-ringers.

Their relationship to the drowned lower city is the most telling thing about them. They do not romanticize it and do not fear it. The old avenues are simply streets that have finished being streets; boats move along them in the ordinary way; a family's history is often told as a sequence of altitudes — which floor of which house their line lived on before the water took its step. No one fishes in the deep avenues, and this is the thing that marks them as a people rather than merely a population: not law, tact. Something large moves there once a season, seen as a bow-wave where no boat is, and Welling's response is the courtesy owed to a neighbor who has chosen not to be spoken about. They extend the same tact to the hushes. They do not explain them, do not test them, do not dare each other at their rims. A people who live inside a sound they call weather have apparently decided that some silences are somebody's, and it is not theirs to ask whose.

They are, on the whole, gentle — the mildness of the air has gotten into them, the mineral warmth, the unhurried water. But their gentleness has a stone's quality: it is the patience of things that intend to outlast you. Ask a Wellingite how long until a quarter is finished and they will say, signing, with a small smile, *it is growing* — present tense, no end date, no anxiety. Distances are decades. Grief is measured the same way. There are no loud funerals because there is no loud anything; the dead are remembered in ring-lines, in a pillar commissioned by a grandmother delivered to a grandson, and the mourning is a matter of hands: the city's thousand conversations pausing for a moment, all that silent eloquence stilled at once above the roar, like weeds bending in a current that doesn't touch them — and then resuming.

And under everything, in every one of them, the pulse. They do not hear it anymore; visitors always do. But you can see that they carry it — a slight deliberateness of gait, a way of resting that looks rehearsed, the whole population moving through its fog-lit amber streets in loose sympathy with a stroke that comes every six breaths from under the soles of the world. When the chord stumbles, they stop; when it resumes, they exhale with the plateau, all of them at once, a city breathing in time with the thing that made it — and no one says a word, because there is no word that fits, and anyway their hands already said it.
