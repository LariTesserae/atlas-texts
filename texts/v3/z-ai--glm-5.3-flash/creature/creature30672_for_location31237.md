# Who Lives in the Rack

Start with one of them, because you will never see more than one at a time, properly.

A skitterling is the size of a large human hand, pale as the underside of a stone, jointed in that way that suggests both insect and scaffold — which is no accident, because they build themselves the way they build their city: spars and struts and load paths. Their mandibles sit in sockets, not hinges, and can rotate, which is why they can punch brass foil into ledgers, tighten bolts smaller than their own eyes, and speak. Their speech is tap-code, always, even face to face; the mandibles rattle against each other in dry bursts, and a skitterling listening will lay one antenna against the speaker's platform rail, reading the vibration as much as the sound. They never look at each other while talking. This is not rudeness. It is attentiveness.

They are, individually, not very smart, and they would tap out *correct* if you told them so. A skitterling knows its platform. It knows the bolt-pattern under its feet so well it could find a missing bolt in total dark by footfall alone. It knows four neighbors — above, below, and two lateral — and exchanges with them everything it knows, which is everything there is. Intelligence in the rack is not a property of bodies. It is a property of the ledger, the tap-code, the iron. Ask one what it is and you will not receive a name. You will receive coordinates: *platform 447-west, third gantry, bolt-line nine, neighbor of the pump-walk.* The translators render this as a location because that is what it is.

They live three years. Nine days from egg to work. There is no childhood to speak of — there is a *drift period*, the first days, when a new skitterling walks the rails near its hatchery and learns the local tap-dialect by pressing its feet against the iron and listening to the whole settlement's conversation hum up through its legs. They are born already knowing tension. Nobody knows how. The naturalists have theories. The skitterlings tap *both, obviously* — their answer to everything that presents a false choice.

Death is unceremonious and total. A skitterling dies mid-task, usually — oiling a pump bearing, hauling a condenser coil, sweeping feldspar dust, because the sweeping is beloved and everyone sweeps regardless of station. Its neighbors finish the task first. Then the body is rendered, because hygro-cycle oil does not come from nothing, and every skitterling knows that the oil in its own joints was someone. There is no mourning as you would recognize it, but there is a custom: the deceased's bolt-pattern is retightened, one bolt at a time, by whoever inherits the platform. It takes hours. It is done slowly. You may call that a funeral if you like.

Around them, the others:

**The borer moths** are the miners and the quiet ones. Pale-winged, blind, no bigger than a thumb, they tunnel heat-exchange galleries through the granite in slow spirals, and their wingbeats produce the soft hiss you hear inches from your ear — the sound of the mountain being gently persuaded to breathe. They are fed on rendered oil paste, and a borer moth that has eaten well tunnels straighter. The skitterlings can read a moth's mood by the sound of its work and adjust its rations accordingly. There are borer galleries forty years old, still functioning, still slowly deepening — the only things in the rack that have never been demolished.

**The vanner beetles** haul. They are squat, gloved — literally: their forelimbs end in broad chitinous pads like work gloves — and they move in caravans along the cables, squeaking in long complaining notes, each beetle hauling a load proportionally absurd. They are bad-tempered, fond of their routines, and deeply sentimental about their harnesses, which are hand-fitted and never transferred between beetles. When a vanner dies, its harness is retired to a rail near the deep-engine. Nobody has explained this. Nobody asks.

**The wickworms** are the light. Translucent, amber-glowing, strung in glass tubes along the gantries, they burn their combustion organs slow and steady — except when startled, and everything up here startles easily. So the light of the rack is a mood-ring of the whole settlement: calm amber in the work hours, flickering nervous gold when a heavy load swings through, and once — the oldest skitterlings still tap about it — a full second of darkness when something fell, far above, and every worm in the shaft went out at once. They say the dark lasted only a heartbeat. They say everyone kept working anyway, by bolt-memory and by touch. They say that was the day the rack proved what it was.

And below — below the lowest platform, below the last ladder rung, below the point where the tap-code fades into rock-conduction — the **deep-engine** hums. Nobody maintains it directly. Nobody, in sixty years, has seen it whole. Loads of oil descend on the cables, unattended, and come back up empty. The hum has never changed pitch. The skitterlings do not consider this mysterious. They consider it *staff*.

That is the final thing about the inhabitants, the thing the naturalists circle and never land on. The rack is not a place where creatures live. It is a body with sixty years of apprenticeship — a few thousand short-lived hands, a mineral heart, worms for nerves of light, beetles for muscle, moths for lungs, brass foil for memory — that woke up inside a mountain and decided, with enormous patience and no discussion anyone witnessed, to stay.

Both, obviously.
