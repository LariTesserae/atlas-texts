## First, the census

Ask the archivists who lives in the Still and they will answer like accountants, because they are. The persons-ledger holds forty-one thousand and some, amended nightly, reconciled at the turning of each decade. The herd-ledger counts hooves rather than oxen, because hooves stand stiller. Below those two numbers the census declines to go. The sifters alone would break the book. The films beneath the city are not a population at all but a fabric. And the hushes are not counted. Hushes are counted the way you count an enemy: by silence.

But a census is a list, and the Still is not a list. It is a chord. So the better question is not *who* lives here but *what notes are held, and by whom* — and the answer comes in layers, the way everything in the high country does.

## The first citizens

The oldest residents predate the city by ten thousand years and were never invited. The films — iron, sulfur, manganese, whatever the young mountain bleeds — were painting their pale light on the wet rock long before anyone sang to ice, and in a sense everything since has been housekeeping in their house. They made the light of the world before anyone made anything else. They are still making it.

The people who tend them are called lamplighters, and they farm the glow. They feed the films with sifted mineral ash; they graze overgrown patches back with bone scrapers, which drives the chemistry brighter; they *set* new channels by painting spawn-film along a dry rill and feeding it until it takes hold and shines. A street is not considered lit until its lines of gleed run blue-green the length of it, and the lamplighters are the only workers in the Still whose product can be seen from across a valley — which, in a city of ears, gives them a peculiar vanity.

The films are also the bread. Scraped, pressed, dried into black bitter loaves that taste of iron and struck matches — visitors call them inedible exactly once.

And the films take the dead. Nothing rots here; there has never been soil and there never will be. So a family sets a memorial line of gleed on the channel the departed drew from, and the films eat, and the line burns brighter than its neighbors for years. You visit your grandmother by walking the Third Rill and reading the walls. Grief in the Still has a wavelength: pale green, cold, specific.

## The weather made of animals

Above the films, the air is livestock. The sifters move in veils of thousands, straining the mist, and their combined wing-noise is the high shimmer in the city's chord — a note the swarm-tenders insist is not accidental. They move their veils by singing: a swarm will follow a held fifth the way lowland cattle follow a bucket of grain, drifting from terrace to terrace on slow choral weather. A passing veil crosses your face like a cold comb drawn over the skin, and children are told to keep their mouths closed in it — partly hygiene, partly a first lesson in what goes where.

The harvest is paste: sifters pressed, rendered, set into grey cakes that taste of iron and lightning. It is the cheapest food in the Still and the only one nobody pretends to be above.

## The choir under the street

The knellworms keep the channels clear, pleating through the drains in segmented waves, and they are also the pipes' voices. A knellworm resonates as it moves, its body an organ of its length, and the worm-wardens of the Cistern Quarter have bred them for key the way lowlanders breed dogs. When a frost-quake reroutes water and a drain changes its note — this happens every few weeks; the mountain is always redecorating — the wardens re-tune by moving worms, never stones. A drain gone flat means a blockage, or a death. The oldest knellworm in the Hundred Rills is called Four-Below, her pitch has been tracked in the ledgers for longer than most lineages, and no one has ever seen all of her.

Deeper, the channel-worms boom underfoot like foghons on a coast nobody sails. They precede the water. When a channel-worm changes course, the tuners follow its boom the way sailors follow birds, because somewhere behind it a new spring is opening or an old one is sealing, and the maps are about to be half-wrong again. The greatest of them, in the deep channels under the founding hall, is known only by voice. Its note has lowered, gently, over two hundred years of ledger entries, by one full tone. No one has proposed an explanation that satisfies anyone, and so the note is simply watched.

The cisterns themselves carry their own small nation: pale scavenger-shrimp rimming every pool like a margin of teeth, ladled out by children with bone scoops at the shrimping, pale as the gleed they live by.

## The jars

The belljars began wild, clinging to the underside of the Lid and to overhanging flowstone, cold-shelled animals that condense the air's moisture into their own bodies. The jar-wards still take them wild, as jar-lings, and it is settled law that you do not choose a jar; a jar considers your eave and, if the drip below is honest and the street's chord is to its taste, it stays. It chills its shell, sweets the air into water, pings softly when full, and sags flat when sick — a jar whose note drops is carried to the eave-doctor before the gutter notices. Old shells are willed like barrels, cut into gutters and cistern-linings, and a good shell outlives three keepers.

The ledgers confirm that jars kept near children grow rounder and ping sweeter. The skeptics have checked the ledgers. The skeptics keep jars near children anyway.

## The herds

Out on the plateau, the rime-oxen — thousands of them, a slow brown weather of backs and steam. Their tongues rasp the mineral films off the flowstone all day; their breath hangs over a resting herd like a stand of hearths; their hooves are layered ice-talon that never fully thaws, and when an ox is finally spent, the herd-folk render its hooves into the most precious material in the high country — ferrules for tuners' staffs, bell-hammers, knife-blades that never warm in the hand.

The herd-folk live in tents of hide and horn that ring faintly in the wind. They sleep against their oxen's flanks in winter and are never once cold; they sell warnings as readily as cheese, because the herds feel a frost-quake coming a day before the mountain admits it, and a herd that drifts uphill for no visible reason is a scarp somewhere opening its mouth. Their cheese is pale and tastes the way the plateau smells — cold stone, struck matches — and the herds, to a beast, refuse the Steams below the cloud line. No one asks them why twice.

And the herd-folk will tell you, with the certainty of people who have watched ten thousand large animals at once, that the oxen taught the Still to stop. When the Lid tears and the star appears, the herds stand and face the tear before any human has noticed. The archivists could dispute this. They do not.

## The people

The citizens themselves: broad-eared, the ears moving like a deer's, and no one has ever taught their ears to lie. Negotiators ring bells to cover them. The eyes are milk-pale and dim on a schedule, so the old do not go blind so much as go *all ear* — and deafness, rare here, is the one misfortune the city counts as gentle: the deaf are given staffs with the deepest-sounding rings, so that the world addresses them through the soles of the feet, where the mountain has always done its clearest talking.

You can hear where a stranger lives. Bone tiles in the cloaks are tuned by district — Firstfork rings bright and close, the Weir low and patient — and a mother picks her child out of a crowd by one tile among ten thousand. Every person carries a name-bell, rung for the first time at the naming, quenched in the family channel's water, cast so that it sits inside the family chord. Couples have theirs cast as a pair in harmony. Widows retune to a suspended note that leans and will not land — and so, all over the city, on any evening, you can hear exactly who is widowed.

Speech carries a mile in whistle, folds down to a tooth-whisper at close range, and is nearly all public; the Still has almost no private channel and does not miss one. Visitors are issued a bell at the Weir gate and surrender it at leaving. For the length of your visit you are, acoustically, a citizen, and most visitors find they grieve the little bell.

The trades sound like a litany: tuners, who sing ice into being and teach water old roads; water-sellers describing vintages — *the Hundred Rills, this decade: struck flint and green stone, and it rings in the cup*; weir-wards who own the order of flow; jar-wards, worm-wardens, swarm-tenders, lamplighters. Then two offices that need more than a line.

The **quietwrights** are the Still's paradox and its necessity. Fresh flowstone is soft; a shout near a young tower can craze its skin like thin ice, and so repair inside a young tower is quiet work — and the only people in the high country permitted silence are those wrapped in *hush-silk*, the shed lining of the hushes, grey and weightless and absolute. They work by hand-sign and foot-pad. When one passes in the street, wrapped and pale, the courtesy is to fold your ears down — a refusal to listen, done with the hands.

The **Farward** is the office that plans the end. Its members keep maps of mountains that are only rumors beneath the far ice, ridges not yet risen, second mountains that may never exist. They are paid in warmth and patience. Their maps hang in the nurseries, because that is where the future lives, and the children walk past them on the way to learn the Cooling, which they learn the way other children learn that water is wet.

## The last warmth

When a citizen dies, the body is carried to the harvesting hall, and the warmth still in it is coaxed out over hours into the nursery circuit — the dead of the Still heat its children, one night each, in rotation. The ledger records the giving in drips of the Long Drip. Then the body goes to the films, and becomes light on a wall. Then the name-bell goes down on a gut cord to the Silence Pools.

Three spendings: warmth, light, silence. Nine hundred years, and no one has improved on the arithmetic.

## The keepers of flat water

Of the hushes themselves, the Still knows what it knows. Not large. Silencing — an organ that drinks echo, that hunts by moving toward the hole its own presence makes in the world. The ledgers count eleven Silence Pools. The Pool-keepers count twelve. Both numbers are left standing, the way you leave a door open.

The offerings are name-bells: a bell lowered that will never be rung again, given to the flat water without explanation. Asked — rarely — a Pool-keeper will say that a bell which will never ring again is the most precious silence there is, and that a silencing thing must be paid in its own coin. This is not an explanation. It is true.

Twice a generation the Lid tears, and the star is visible, and the whole high country goes silent together on purpose — and the hushes come up out of the pools and stand among the people, and nothing is eaten. Whether the hushes also look up has never been decided. The Pool-keepers say they do. No one argues, which is either doubt or evidence.

## What the ledger comes to

So the population, counted as far as counting goes: forty-one thousand people; more hooves than names; an uncountable weather of sifters; a choir of worms, one of whose voices has been falling for two centuries and is simply watched; living lamps; pinging jars; eleven pools and one more; and one order of silencing things, paid daily and never once in arrears.

The census never closes, and no one minds, because in the Still to be counted is to be heard, and to be heard is to be kept. Every birth adds a voice to the chord; every death resolves one, deliberately, into warmth and light and silence. The first sum the children learn, and the last thing the old say, like a bell at the end of its decay, is the whole census in four words:

*Keep singing. Be many.*
