Begin with the quiet, because the quiet is not what it looks like. The plain is not silent the way an empty room is silent. It is silent the way a library is silent: crowded, deliberate, everyone deep in something at close range and minding their voice. Stand still at dusk and the density files its small credentials — shell ticking against shell, the ring of drip, a stir in the basins — and none of it raised. Ten million years of being found out have edited loudness off this plateau, and what the editing left behind is a multitude that has moved its voice into materials that do not carry. Into water. Into bedrock. Into silk. Into the placement of one pebble against another. Into pigment, into rings of growth, into the slow lean of a tree. The air is the one medium nobody speaks in. You will hear your own breathing and be embarrassed by it; you are the loudest animal for a hundred miles, and the plain is too well-mannered to mention it.

Start small, because most of the citizens are small. A single pothole is a world with a population problem. A teaspoon of its water is a metropolis: water-fleas schooling in the space between your fingerprints, ciliates by the billion, a felt of algae at the bottom photosynthesizing like a green lawn the size of a saucer. And that eyelash of a shrimp, spiraling in its cup — watch longer and the spiral stops being wandering and becomes husbandry. It swims a fixed circuit that pulls a feeding current down through the felt, grazing the garden without grazing it out, a farmer plowing a field the width of a coin. Its eggs go down as cysts that can wait out decades of drought in a spoonful of dust. Its universe is the bowl entire. Fog passing over the lip is its weather system; the shadow of a comb-plant crossing at noon is its eclipse. It will live and die a full citizen of one cup of water and never learn that a plain exists, and nothing about its life will have been small.

The thumbnail frog keeps the same borders and writes on water. In a bowl-world, sound is useless — it hits the stone rim and stops — but the surface carries. Two frogs in one basin conduct long, slow conversations in capillary waves, sentences a hand's width across, and when the spill-chains run, water joins basin to basin and a ripple entered at the top of a chain arrives at the bottom. That is the plain's telegraph, open only in wet weather. Frogs downstream gather at the spillways to read what comes through. Some of them — the travelers — wait all year for the six-day river, climb out of their natal bowl, and cross the wet stone to seed basins that stand empty. They come home, if they come home, having seen the plain: more world than their language was ever built to hold. They try to say it in ripples. The homebodies gather at the spill to listen, and do not understand, and stay anyway.

In the joints between boulders, ferns the height of a finger hold their own dark weather, and each crack is a tenement. And in the soil pockets the burrow-towns tunnel, and the towns' great technology is silk. At dawn the low air is strung with it — line after line, comb to boulder to basin rim — each thread a gin for fog and a drift-net for seed-fluff. Breakfast is read off the web. A plucked line is a knock at a door; a slack line means the wind has turned; the whole town goes out each morning and reads its silk like a page. They farm the drifting fluff, drink their own lines dry, tunnel down at noon to where the night's warmth is banked in the stone. Their economy runs on dew and lint, and it is, by the standard of economies, a gentle one.

Scarce as the trees are, they are less like plants than like places. On a floor without features, a canopy spread like an open hand is the only address there is. Beneath the hand: the deepest soil, the capital of every burrow-town for a quarter mile, the shade-plaza where the herds plot their turns. The bark has been polished to the grain by ten thousand years of itchy flanks. The tree adds its millimeter and is done. You are not looking at an old tree. You are looking at a place that finished being a tree before your species had a word for one, and has been keeping the shape ever since. The stackers tend them — a ring of fitted pebbles at the foot of every trunk, a drip-channel here, a windbreak there — tending a thing that grows more slowly than their dynasties, which is saying something.

The stackers are the plain's hands. A cairn, it turns out, is a well: a stacked tower sweats better than flat ground, the night's fog beading in its crevices and running down the quiet courses to a cup-stone at the foot. Every cairn on the plateau is a private spring, and the bird that keeps it drinks from its own architecture each dawn before going to work the combs. They select their stones by beak-tap, ringing them like a mason, keeping only the notes that fit. A wall is a sentence laid down a syllable a year. A pebble set face-up says one thing; the same pebble on edge says another; a gap held open in the ninth course is a word the whole lineage has agreed to keep saying, and no one living remembers what it means. A young stacker does not inherit a nest. It inherits a wall, and keeping the wall is how it learns everything else.

Some chains of basins are the plain's own plumbing. Some were plumbed. And some walls run to basins that have stood dry for three hundred years — built toward anyway, generation after generation, the way you keep paying into something you were told would matter. Now, in the high country, water has begun arriving at exactly those basins. Draw no conclusions about what the birds know. Only notice that those walls are not memories. They are appointments, and the plain has begun keeping them.

Then the herds, whose enormity defeats arithmetic. From the rim you can watch a single herd own a horizon and be unable to say where it ends. At midday they lie down among the boulders and the plain grows a lake made of sky — ten thousand polished domes, each holding its copy of the clouds, a pavement that breathes. And they are not slow so much as deep. Their voices go down through their feet into bedrock that has had ten million years to learn to listen, and every animal is mouth and ear at once. Greeting is beak-work: tap a neighbor's shell and the dome answers in its own note, and that note is the name. Calves are named before they hatch; the egg sits in its warm pocket between stones and the herd's sentence passes through it all day long. An elder's memory is the map — which chains hold into the dry season, which combs drip latest, where the rock runs thin over water — because the circuit takes eleven years and the herd is the only thing that remembers it whole. When an old animal lies down mid-step and does not get up, the sentence closes around it the way water closes around a stone, and the herd walks on, the map carried minus one mapmaker. In a century the shell is a boulder with a view. Some of the rocks you rest against have names. This troubles no one here. It is how the plain makes its citizens permanent.

Above them, the sky is a second pasture, grazed just as gently. The gliders live on the air's plankton — hatching midges, drifting silk, fluff shaken loose at dawn — riding the thermals that peel off the warm rock, wings open and still. Their shadows are the plain's clocks; the small lives below tell the hour by them. A glider lands perhaps once a week. Landing is the only expensive thing it ever does, so when one folds down, others fold down after it, just to look. They nest below the rim, out on the faces of the dry ramps, under the mile of warming air — some of this place's citizens live beneath its floor. Courtship is two of them finding the same circle and holding it all afternoon, their shadows braided on the stone. You could stand there a year and hear nothing. After the annual rain, when the potholes hatch all at once, the gliders descend in their thousands, and even then the sound the sky makes is mostly landing: braking, feather by feather, the sky being careful.

The oldest citizens do not move at all. The lichen are the plain's scribes — a gold hairline for every rain, a black one for every hard year, the mats readable like ledgers by anyone patient enough to count. Some circles were old when the first forest anywhere was an experiment. They were here before there was a word for green, and they have written down everything since: the wet centuries, the dry ones, the comings and goings of the herds. And when a stacker wall reaches its thousandth year, the lichen signs it too, black along every seam, until architecture and geology wear the same signature and only the fit of the stones can tell them apart.

And the stone is a citizen, the eldest. It speaks once a day, at dusk, one word — tick — in cooling syllables, all night long, and everything on the plateau beds down inside the saying. It remembers being mountains. It has said everything it had to say, and now it listens, warm through the night under everything that lives on it, the way a floor listens under a full house.

Once a year, the plain holds its assembly. The rain comes in low for a day and a night, the chains fill and link, and for six days the top of the world has a river. Every layer touches every other. The herds converge and drink a year in an afternoon. The gliders fold down like a storm of dropped leaves. The weavers string their bridges across the flow and run traffic in both directions. The frogs make their journeys; the shrimp ride as cyst-dust on the legs of everyone; the stackers gather at a common ground and build the annual cairn, one fitted stone per bird — the only monument on the plain that grows, six days at a time, one year at a time. Then the rain ends, the chains close basin by basin, and each cup locks its door on whoever has arrived. New citizens unpack. Somewhere a returning frog is trying to explain the plain, in ripples, to frogs who will never see it.

So correct the first impression, because it is wrong in the way that matters. The plain is not what remained after the mountains died. It is what the mountains said. The herds are the range's weight, walking; the shells are its peaks, turned over and filled with sky; the cairns are the last new mountains — knee-high, built out of the old ones, the only things here that still move stone uphill after ten million years of everything going down. Nothing is ending and nothing is beginning. Everything is keeping.

Lie on the stone at dusk and the herd will settle around you like a cobbled beach, and the ground beneath your ribs will already be mid-sentence — a word begun before your kind existed and due to run for years after you stand up. You sleep inside it. You wake to surfaces that have wept, combs already working, the fog making its patient climb, the whole crowded calm of the place about its tasks, and no one anywhere speaking. It is the fullest quiet you will ever stand in. It is not the absence of voice.

It is voice, said at the speed of stone.
