Watch them at the eight-minute mark and you will know the people of the Fumarole Market.

The hiss comes up through the soles first, then through the walls, and the whole city stops. A spoon hangs halfway to a mouth. A rail-cart rests at the end of its push, one hand-width short, and stays there. A quarrel pauses mid-sentence, both parties frozen in the particular dignity of people pretending they weren't angry. Mothers time everything to it — an infant is lifted clear of the wash-trough on the last second before the hiss, every time, without looking. No one enforces the stop. It stopped being a rule before the first child born down here could walk; it is reflex now, and reflex is what a prayer becomes when an entire population shares it. Time is counted in breaths: *back before two breaths*, *the forge-hall is forty breaths' push*, *leave it a hundred breaths to cool*. When someone says a person has good manners, what they mean is: he stops completely.

They are soft-voiced, all of them. The stone carries a whisper to five galleries, so a full voice is either an emergency, a confession of madness, or the mark of the founders — the old ones, whose ears wore out before the city learned to be quiet, and who now boom through the tunnels like weather, embarrassing their grandchildren. Anger down here has no door to slam. It is expressed in taps: a family that is feuding with its neighbors simply stops answering the goodnight knocks that travel through the rock between bunks, and everyone in the district understands that a war is on.

Their eyes are enormous. Pupils dilated to the walls of their sockets by a life in green-gold mat-light, they read the dimmest terrace-glow with ease and are half-blinded by the orange bulbs over the drinking stations — children dare each other to look straight at one, palms sweating. On the surface these same eyes are useless; the runners come up blinking and weeping behind their slit mica-goggles, moles in a white room. Their skin smells of sulfur and warm stone and yeast, and their hands — the fin-wipers' especially — are pale, puckered, bleached soft by a career of touching condensation. A surface visitor is an event: the children gather and press close, because the visitor smells of rain, and none of them has ever met rain.

There are two kinds of people here, divided by memory. The founders still walk the galleries, pick-scarred like the walls themselves, hands ruined by the excavation, and they are strange in a way no one born below can quite parse: they flinch at nothing underground but go quiet and wrong when wind is mentioned. They speak of rain the way other people speak of paradise, or of a catastrophe, depending on the day. The born-below are the first true natives: stooped a finger's width for the low galleries, feet that read rails in the dark, eyes that have never once focused beyond a gallery's length. When the founders describe rain — water falling from everywhere, wasted on the ground — the children find it either a tall tale or an obscenity. One child, told that the sky also sweats, considered this and found it obvious: *everything sweats. Why did you live up there?*

Work is the shape a life takes here, and the trades are bodily. The fin-wipers milk the walls, moving fin to fin with rag and bare hand, and swear the copper fins have temperaments — this one beads early, that one sulks if you wipe against the grain. The fan-keepers go oil-black to the elbows and listen to bearings the way physicians listen to hearts; they say a fan is happy when it ticks on the off-beat, and they grieve a blown belt like a death in the family. The listeners precede every dig, ear to stone, chalk in hand, keeping the grumble-maps that show where the young rock is moody this season; they are the nearest thing the city has to clergy, and they will tell you, if asked, that the rule *you listen before you dig* is badly translated — what it means is: you listen before you do anything. The diggers never dig angry. The smiths walk with the particular uprightness of people entrusted with fire, the one sacrament, hooded and drafted and fiercely fenced; oaths are sworn in the forge-hall, over the flame, because fire is the most expensive thing anyone down here can offer as a witness. And the pushers — the children and the old on the cart-bars — move the city's entire economy on their backs, which is why every person here remembers being useful and expects, calmly, to be useful again at the end.

Beside the humans live the others. The mats come first: pale translucent continents on warm stone, tended, named, inherited. The oldest residents of the city are slime — cultures carried down in glazed jars at the founding, older now than any family line below — and the mat-farmers hum to their terraces, and give the strains the names of relatives. Weddings are performed by pouring two families' cultures into one trough. A mat that fails is announced in the voice used for deaths. The bread glows faintly until it cools, which is where they get their only proverb about youth: *eat it while it still glows.* The beer is warm, faintly self-heating, sulfur-sour, and affectionately called *water that learned to keep.* It is worth noticing, and the locals do notice, that every light in the city comes from its own food ripening — they live inside their harvest. Then there are the mites, the city's whole wildlife, stowaways from the grain sacks, hunted by needle and fingertip and kept as pets by children who have no other option; a mite on a thread is the closest thing this place has to a dog. And there is the shared breath itself: one air, one microbiome, a whole city that catches the same coughs in the same week, so that a newcomer's body is quarantined for weeks, weeping the coming-down sickness, until it has agreed to become local.

The dead have no worms to take them apart, no beetles, nothing larger than a mite — so the people do it. The dead are given to the terraces, steeped into the mats they fed in life, and after a season of mourning the grave-terrace is scraped for the first time, and the loaf from it is carried to every neighbor in the district with the words: *she is bread now. Eat.* The dead person's water is poured back into the family cistern drop by drop, the smallest inheritance, and no one begrudges it. To die here is not to leave. It is to be incorporated.

The Market itself, the heart, is where the rails converge and the fumaroles are left open — the only place in the city where you can watch the earth exhale raw, steam rising through the green-gold glow in slow columns. Scales there weigh water to the drop; the water-mother certifies clarity with a tasting-spoon passed down through six appointments; the smallest coin is a single bead of first-condensate, and the poorest drink the gray second-water without shame, because a poor family has the ancient right of fin-gleaning — to wipe a rich neighbor's fins at dawn and keep the harvest — which is charity arranged as dignity, and no one calls it alms. Messages braid out from the market through the pneumatic tubes in soft chirps; the tubes carry everything the walls must not overhear, which in a city of five-audible-galleries is nearly everything that matters.

The law is short and absolute. You do not dam another district's seep; children are quoted the rule before they can walk. Water-theft by cupped palm is repaid in drop-fines and servile wiping. Open flame outside the forges is the crime no one names aloud, because everyone breathes the same air. And the sentence that terrifies beyond all others is exile upward — being made to live dry. Some of the exiled survive it and become the grain-caravan families, pitied and envied in equal measure, coming down twice a year with loads of wheat and children who smell of wind. When they descend, the born-below press close and breathe them in: rain, secondhand.

The city itself, they will tell you, is also a resident — the youngest and largest. Fans for lungs, seeps for sweat glands, message tubes for nerves, rails for veins, and a stone that still grumbles and ticks as it settles, charted by the listeners like a feverish child. The whole place is a bead of sweat that refused to fall. And its people are still becoming what it makes: wide-eyed, soft-voiced, stooped slightly, paid in the earth's breath, stopping eight times an hour to let their world inhale. When they dream — the born-below, at least — they dream in green-gold, of walls that bead with water without being wiped, and of a sky that is only the largest ceiling of all, sweating like everything else.
