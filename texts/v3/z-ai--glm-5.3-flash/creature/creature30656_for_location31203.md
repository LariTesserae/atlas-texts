Count the residents of this room and you will get a different number every time, because the place has never settled what counts as living here. Three moths live here. One animal lives here. One room lives here — and the room is the largest of the three facts and the most likely to object to the census. Somewhere between them live the stone that hasn't moved out and the light that hasn't moved in. Take them in order of size, smallest first, while they hold still.

**The moths.** There are three, and there are always three, though no one has explained the arithmetic. They are the color of condensation, sized like condensation, and until they move they are indistinguishable from the beads they drink beside — which is their whole strategy, and, when you think about it, their whole biography. They sip the tacky sap-mist off the walls, ride the collective lean of the curtains when the room swallows, and otherwise decline to participate. They are the only citizens permitted to be finished — finished shapes, finished wings, no lengthening fringes, no unrolling fists. Perhaps that is their wage: the room grows everything, and in exchange for being the one thing it does not grow, they are left alone. When one of them dies — and you will not see it happen; you will only notice, some cycle, that one of the green seams on the wall is moth-shaped now — it does not fall. It is taken. Mossed over in an hour, promoted to wall. The room keeps no graves. It keeps appointments.

**The room itself.** The chief resident is the room, and it wears its age the way the young wear borrowed coats. Its own rings argue about it: four hundred years, says the grain; three days, says the dust still settling from the crack. Both are telling the truth the way weather does. It is a lantern made of skin, a lung with a floor, and it breathes in long swallows you can watch the curtains perform. It has opinions — against darkness, evidently; about the rain schedule, no position it can hold longer than a day; on the subject of the center, firm, and we will come to that. What it does not have is a memory it can trust, because it includes everything it touches, and that includes its own past. Any history it grows gets grown over. A room that eats its own archives must re-decide itself continually, and so it does: hence the negotiation along its walls, where lichen-things harden into rock at one edge while rock softens into wood at the other, and the stone — the previous tenant, the one that has not vacated — occasionally flashes a whole facet grey at the edge of your vision, exercising an old right, before the green floods back over the claim. Neither has won. You are standing inside the compromise.

And below notice, the most numerous residents: the ones in the fizz. The pop and tick and sizzle of division running through the walls like champagne heard through a door is a population — countless, single, unnamed, the room's plankton, its weather of cells. No one has counted them because the total would change while you counted.

**The gardener.** Then the one with hands. Pale grey-green, the size of a fat cat, soft-skinned, eyes like wet pebbles — and pause on that, because there are twelve pebbles arranged beside the cup, and a room this attentive to correspondences may be making more of the rhyme than we should. It walks the whole circuit each cycle on wet feet, and its footprints close over behind it the way yours do, water over a stone. Its possessions are three: a reed straw, a grinding pebble gone green at the edges, and the black thorn as long as your finger that serves as its knife. Look at the trend in that list. The pebble is being included. The straw will be, in time. The thorn has already rooted in the fist, and the gardener rotates its grip from time to time so the tool grows straight — which means the gardener has domesticated the thorn into a knife, and the thorn has domesticated the gardener into a scabbard, and somewhere in the coming years there will be no line where the hand ends. Whether that is a creature keeping a pet or a room growing itself a hand depends on which side of the fist you believe.

What does it eat? No midden, no bones, no droppings — but then the room includes its own waste, so absence proves nothing. Perhaps it grazes its own prunings. Perhaps the skin has gotten quietly good at light, and the wet feet are feet the way roots are feet. It does drink from the cup — the still water that never evaporates and tastes, by the evidence of the gardener's face afterward, of somewhere else entirely — and that face is the closest thing this room has to literature. You will read homesickness in it. You may be right. The alternative is worse and stranger: that the water tastes of *here*, and everything else is the homesickness.

The tally is its work, and understand why it must be an arrangement and never a structure.
