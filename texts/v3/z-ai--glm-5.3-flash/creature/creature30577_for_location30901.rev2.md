The inventory of the living is short, and it is kept — this is the first thing to understand about it — in neither names nor numbers but in positions. Nothing here has a biography. Everything has a station. Life in this sea is not a process with events in it; it is a set of shapes that hold, and the shapes are old, and the substances that fill them are always new.

Begin at the bottom, with the film.

The film is not an animal and not a plant, and it is not exactly alive in the way the word usually means. It is the boundary where the planet's youth gets eaten — a rust-red to black felt lying over every rock, feeding directly on the stone, dragging iron and sulfur out of rock that has never been weathered by anything else. It has no individuals. It does not bloom, does not seed, does not die back. If it has a life span, the life span is the plateau's, and the plateau's is deliberately indefinite. What happens inside the film never stops being the film. It is the world's skin, and like everything here it is maintained to specification: cropped to the same nap forever by mouths that never take more than the regrowth can repay. It is the only thing on this planet that eats the place itself. Everything else eats the film, or eats what eats the film, or — in one case, to come — eats the difference between the film's world and silence.

Above the film work the scrapers, and a scraper is the most nearly ordinary thing in this sea, which is to say it is still strange. Small, hard-shelled, clear as a lentil of blown glass: you can hold one to the white noon of the Lid and watch its stomach work, a little brown machinery of an animal with no privacy at all. Its teeth are shaves of silica that wear and regrow in balance, so even its teeth have no history — they are always new and always the same age. The shells it molts do not lie on the bottom, because nothing lies on the bottom; every shed shell is eaten by some other scraper within a day, its silica rebuilt into someone else's armor, its atoms not even granted a rest between employments. Nothing is taken that is not returned.

And the scrapers are born finished. There is no such thing as a half-grown scraper here, because growth is an event and events are not permitted. A young one is adult in size, adult in instinct, and indistinguishable from its parents except by position in the herd. Nothing in this sea has ever had to learn anything, and so nothing here has a childhood in the sense you would recognize — only a first day of knowing everything it will ever know. Instinct, in a world where no novelty has ever been added to it, is a closed library. It is complete. It has never needed an update, and none has ever been issued.

Higher, at the wells, hang the kites.

They are the only animals with something like an address, and they pay rent. A kite is a broad translucent wing-thing, falcon-spread, that does no visible work at all — merely adjusts, by millimeters, in the warm column rising off a well's finned back. But look at what it adjusts toward: the well structures themselves, the gantries and exchanger tiers, which would otherwise grow the film. The kites crop them. They are scale-cleaners, maintenance crews of the industry, and the industry's payment is the sleeve of kinder water and the standing pillar of light that comes down the bore in the Lid. In that pillar — the only directional light on the entire planet, the one place where the everywhere-light is outnumbered — the kites cast shadows. It is the only place in this sea where anything shows its insides. A kite hanging in the column has, for a few body-widths of its life, a silhouette: organs, a gut, a shape. Everywhere else in the world everything is self-lit and shadowless and hides nothing because there is no light to hide from. The kites are the only animals that have ever been seen, in the full sense of the word. Whether they know this, whether they hang in the pillar partly *for* it, is the kind of question this place declines to answer.

Farther out are the herds, and the herds are where the strangeness of life here goes deepest.

A herd animal is a lens of muscle and silica ribbing holding ninety-four percent seawater — nearly nothing, nearly the sea itself, a body that is mostly the medium it moves through. This is usually said as a fact about weight. It is also a fact about hearing, and the herds' whole existence hangs on it: a body of slightly different density is a slightly wrong piece of the ocean. The chord passes through a herd animal at a slightly different speed than it passes around it, and the animal's clear gel body reads the interference — between the tone arriving directly and the tone that has arrived through itself. Each animal is a living interferometer the size of a room. It feels, continuously and exactly, where it is.

That is how the herds hold their impossible circuits: decades-long closed figures with no landmarks, no currents, no day and no night to navigate by. They do not need landmarks. The wells are the landmarks. Each well rings — near a well the chord becomes a substance — and so the great machines stand like sound-poles across the Shelf, and the herd circuits are orbits among them, rolling Lissajous figures drawn in decades around bodies of tone. The animals do not migrate; they *are migrated*, held in the pattern the way water is held in a fountain's shape.

And here is the thing about the herds that takes longest to accept: the individual animals are not the organisms. The herd is. The herd has coordinates. The herd will outlive every animal currently in it, several times over, because when an animal fails — and they do fail, quietly, without falling, dismantled where they lie by scrapers while the film closes over the site — a young one enters the same station and the pattern does not blink. Identity in this sea is architectural. The oldest living things on the planet are not any of the animals; they are the shapes, and the shapes are made entirely of animals who have never once been the same individuals from one decade to the next.

The hunters are called silences, and the name is not poetry. In a sea of one tone and one density, a large body reads as a hole in the sound, and a silence is a hunter made of that hole. It is long, jointed, and nearly motionless by profession. It cannot surge — nothing surges here, not even hunger — so it hunts the only way this world permits: by patience calibrated against the prey's reflexes. A herd keeps its listening distance from anything that dims the chord *and moves*. A hole in the sound that is not moving is not yet a hunter; it is furniture. So a silence will hold itself as a static absence — a patch of not-quite-chord, indistinguishable from a scarp, a wreck, a half-built thing — for weeks, for months, closing over one herd animal at a speed slower than suspicion. One kill. Then stillness again, longer than the kill. The silences are the mechanism by which vacancies open, and the vacancies are the mechanism by which everything is born, which means that in this sea, death and birth are not opposites but the same administrative event seen from two sides.

Because nothing here is born into an open future. *A vacancy must open before anything is permitted to fill it* — the rule is not a law of nature in the usual sense; it is obeyed, which is stranger. When a station empties, the nearest eligible young is drawn to it; there is no courtship spectacle, no competition, no boom. The census of this sea has been the same number for as long as it could be counted — which may be forever, which may be since Tuesday; the world keeps that figure the way it keeps everything, exactly and without commentary. No population has ever grown. No creature has ever needed to invent a thing, and so the young know everything the old knew, because the old never knew anything the young couldn't use.

Which leaves the machines, and whoever is behind them.

The wells are alive in the sense that a held note is alive, and they are the most faithful residents of the sea: every machine listening to every other, tuning to the sum of all the rest, correcting within a heartbeat any drift from the chord. But listen to the grammar of the place. *If the wells are ever told to stop.* Told. By whom? No graves, no dwellings, no names — and yet an imperative is present, an instruction that has outlived, or preceded, or replaced its speaker. It may be that the oldest inhabitant of this planet is a sentence. If a message can be held the way this world holds everything — pinned, phase-locked, forbidden to decay — then the tone may not merely be the planet's industry. It may be an utterance, said once, still being said. The builders may be present the way a held breath is present.

Or they may simply be higher up. Every description of this place stops at the Lid, and the Lid is a floor as much as a ceiling. Above it the steam rises in straight, unbent lines into thin cold air that no account of this world has ever entered — air with weather in it, almost, since rising steam must at last spread and thin and become something. It is the only direction in this sea with an *up* that goes anywhere. If anyone still tends these wells, that is the address. The scaffolding on the half-built well — gray, climbless, unbrowned, its bolts bright as the day they were torqued — says *recently*, and says *always*, and refuses to reconcile them. The work is either interrupted or pending. The water keeps the exact point where it stopped, and keeps the not-knowing too.

So the full census of the Held Note runs: one film, continuous with the planet's youth; herds of scrapers eating it at the rate it grows; kites by the columnful, cropping the machines, casting the only shadows in the world; herds by the thousand, orbiting the wells in figures decades wide, made of animals who are never the same individuals twice; some number of silences, patient as absences; some number of machines, humming in perfect agreement; one instruction, still in force; and one half-finished sentence of scaffolding, waiting either for hands or for nothing.

And — if you swim there — one more: you. The only animal in this sea with a biography. The kites lean toward your wake and read it; the herds fold you into their listening distance; the sea takes your warmth by ledger and closes over the fact of you. Everything else here is a position. You are an event, and you will not be maintained.

The note holds. The scaffolding waits. If anyone is coming back to finish the well, they are not hurrying — and by the standards of this place, that means nothing at all about whether they are coming.
