Almost no animal life worth mentioning — that's what the description says, and it's true, by the standards of places that have finished becoming places. But *worth mentioning* is the wrong ledger for this shelf. In an old landscape you inventory the animals and call the plants scenery. Here the roles haven't been handed out yet; nobody has sorted into lead parts. So the census reads strangely, and it has to start lower than you'd expect.

**The ground is the first resident.** Walk anywhere and you're walking on it — not over it, on it, the way you'd stand on a body. The living layer is only a hand-span thick: moss, liverwort, fern, the woven root-mat, all of it drinking straight from the weep. Underneath is the rest of the resident — generations of dead selves, compressed, brown-black, half-turned to something that will eventually be permitted to call itself soil. The mat grows by standing on its ancestors. And it eats. Out at the cliff edges, lichens are working the raw stone with slow acids, prying minerals loose grain by grain, doing the rounding-off that three hundred years of weather hasn't gotten to. The moss is finishing the rock's sentence for it.

Some of those cushions are older than the cliffs around them. The first films of green arrived while the shelf was still tidal mud, still rising, and generation by generation the mat kept its surface at the waterline as the land heaved it up into the air. The oldest residents of the Weeping Shelf were born at sea level and now live at altitude, and never moved an inch to manage it.

The trees are the newcomers. Tall, narrow, beaded with water, standing in their loose constellations — they aren't starved, they're *green*. Nothing on this shelf is old enough to be starved. A "tall" conifer here may be younger than the pipeline. They stand apart not because the place is harsh but because nothing has forced them together yet: no shade to compete for, seeds still drifting into gaps, root-pillows still separate islands they each built themselves out of their own shed needles. Walk out to one and you can put your arm around its pillow and feel that it's hollow-ish, spongy, a tree standing on its own discarded centuries. They are residents who arrived recently and are waiting — for the gaps to close, for the light to change, for the chapter after this one.

Then the water, which is either the oldest resident or the first one to leave, depending on how you count. It rose with the rock — trapped in the sediment, carrying the chemistry of the floor it used to be — and it has been departing ever since, drop by drop, seep by seep, downhill toward the lowlands and eventually back to the ocean it came from. A farewell three centuries into its first century. The annual rerouting of the streams isn't chaos; it's undecidedness. The water hasn't learned which doors to leave by. It has no loyalty to last spring's channel because last spring's channel was nobody's idea, just a crack that happened to be convenient. The water is the only thing on the shelf that remembers the old address, and it is leaving as fast as it can, and the mountain is in no hurry to let it.

Too small to see, and therefore easy to leave off the census: the painters and the glasswrights. Some of the pink in the pink-gray stone is their work — salt-loving microbial films, russet and rose, lining the seep-threads where the old sea chemistry touches air. And some of the famous glitter is manufactured too: the standing pools in the rock hollows are threaded with diatoms, each one carrying a shell of pure glass, so that when the white sun doubles off the water, part of what flashes back at you is the accumulated architecture of organisms you'd need a lens to meet. Nearly everything else here is too big to notice. The smallest residents are the shiniest.

The birds work the streams, straining larvae out of the mineral flow, and they live in a deaf world and have adapted to it. Nothing carries, sonically. A song is pointless past a few meters. So their songs have been trimmed down to short chips — close-range sounds, conversational sounds, and in fact they carry almost exactly as far as a human shout: three meters. The entire social life of the shelf's birds is conducted at the distance at which people can speak. What needs to travel, they've made visual instead — a white flash of wing across the glare, a posture held on a stone, a display aimed at the eye because the ear is useless. Their nests are sewn into the moss-pillows, a hidden chamber with a neat round entrance, invisible from a step away. They are tame in the way of animals from a place where nothing has ever yet eaten their grandmothers. And they are commuters, most of them — immigrants from older valleys downstream — holding territories that may not exist next spring, when the stream they fed on cuts west. Their maps are as temporary as the water's.

And the tracks. Small, five-toed, appearing at dawn along the seep-lines, pausing where the water evaporates and leaves its mineral crust behind. Whatever makes them is doing something no other animal on the shelf does: it is eating the mountain — licking the salt and stone off the rock, drinking the newest water straight from the weep. Nobody has seen it. The crews have names for it, several, all affectionate, all invented by people who have never once gotten a look at the thing it names. It is a rumor with a gait. But the tracks say something larger: where there is one, there will eventually be more, and after that, things that hunt them, and after that, the slow complicated machinery of a real ecology. The dawn tracks are the first line of a chapter the shelf hasn't written yet.

Then the people — a few dozen, scattered wide across the outposts, rotated through on schedules, no one born here and no one likely to be for a long while. The noise has made them a particular kind of people. You cannot speak to someone across a room, so attention arrives by touch first — a hand on the shoulder, a turn, then the words, close, face to face, breath to breath. They have a gestural shorthand and a habit of leaning. The deep thrum of the pumps lives in their teeth and their sleep, and it has made a specific ruin of them: when they go home on leave, ordinary quiet keeps them awake. They run fans. They say it's the silence that's deafening, and they are not entirely joking.

You can tell the veterans by what they've stopped doing. New crews scrub the green off the walls; veterans let it fur. The moss closes over initials scratched into furred metal within weeks — there's a standing joke that you can't scar this place, it heals in green — and the crews have stopped minding that the land is gently digesting their works, because it is digesting them too, gently, in the same way: the roads half-swallowed, the mornings smelling only of mist because the sheds' steam and the mountain's breath became the same substance years ago. They drink the product daily, quality control, and the long-timers can taste which shelf a sample came from — the deep water salt-heavy and mineral, the young seeps faintly green. They have become fluent in the flavor of the rock's farewell, and they ship that flavor downslope by the tankful.

They name the pumps and not the streams. Nobody planned this; it's just how it's gone. You name what stays where you put it, and the water won't. There is a board in the pump station where someone keeps redrawing the drainage map and striking out the dead lines — a document that is more palimpsest than map — and the one who keeps it has stayed past his rotation twice now, and nobody asks why out loud, because everyone already knows: he's watching the water decide.

So the count, in full: one enormous plant-body playing the part of ground and doing the work of weather; one ocean in the middle of leaving; painters and glasswrights past numbering; a fistful of damp, tame, close-talking birds; one five-toed rumor; and a few dozen humans growing slowly into their walls. Beneath all of it, the host — the youngest resident and the landlord at once, still rising its few millimeters a year, still rerouting, still mid-sentence, carrying everything aboard it up with it whether they mean to go or not.

Nothing that lives here is old. Nothing that lives here is finished. Everyone on the Weeping Shelf arrived recently — including the shelf.
