Who lives here is a question the place answers by breaking the question's furniture. Begin with the obvious tenant and watch it overflow. The floor is the first resident. Under your boots: half a pace of warm felt, then warmer and darker felt, then root, then root, down past all measuring — and not one vacant cubic pace in all of it. This is not ground awaiting occupants. It is a dwelling that is also, entirely, the dweller. Tenancy here is not a relation between a creature and a place. It is a relation the place keeps with itself.

Whether the massif is one animal or a nation is a distinction it declines to honor. If it is one thing, it is the largest quiet thing you will ever stand on: cold-skinned, warm-flanked, breathing on a two-second push and a long rest, its heart-note rising through your soles as a hum you feel in your teeth. If it is many, they vote unanimously. Either way the metabolism is the same, and it is enormous. There is no litter anywhere in the region — the floor is swept — and a swept floor in a place this green is not tidiness. It is appetite. The day's dead are eaten by the day's end, melted into the mat, refinanced into new leaf, over every mile of every bench, at every hour, in perfect silence. The mountain eats its own summit-fall and builds next year's summit out of the meal.

The oldest residents after the mat — or possibly before it; the region keeps its domestic records to itself — are the fires. In the smoke-hollows the peat-hearts smolder wet and slow in rings they have held for generations, and they are not weather and not geology. They are hearth-things. Kept cultures. They are fed by root-channels that arrive out of the dark carrying fuel the way a keeper arrives at a stall, and no one built the rings, because nothing here was ever assembled: the tending is a performance too, a choreography of supply rehearsed so long it looks like habit. The fires have one temperament, patience, and at this depth patience is indistinguishable from appetite. Each ring is descended from the last the way a candle is lit from a candle — no particle, all continuity — which makes them the region in miniature, its self-portrait: a shape held entirely by burning. Whether the green learned patience from the fires or tamed the fires into patience is the oldest domestic question on the mountain, and the mountain keeps it.

In the vents along the cirque wall there live the only residents with bodies in your sense of the word. White, soft, shrimp-like or grub-like, they hang in the warm column of the larger exhalations, and their whole sky is someone else's breath. They graze the film the warm gas leaves where it condenses — eating, in effect, the inside of the weather. No hard thing has ever touched one. No call, no click, not even the tick of feeding: they were selected for silence like everything here, and then selected further, for edgelessness, until looking straight at one is the surest way to lose it. Not because it flees — because your eye is asking for borders and the column has none; the animal is as continuous with its weather as a wave with its water. Their generations turn as fast as the leaves, which is to say invisibly and constantly. By number they are certainly the commonest fauna in the region, and almost certainly the region has never once noticed them — unless noticing is what the minute is. We will come to the minute.

Once a day the region makes an exception. At the warm hour before dusk a grey moth comes up out of the mat — heavy, uncertain, a flier built for cargo rather than grace — and crosses the cold air for as long as the light allows, and is gone by full dark. Nothing eats it. It eats nothing. In a country where the ledger is strict enough to sweep the floor daily, this is startling: an animal with no line in the accounts, neither owed nor owing. The likeliest reading is trade. The massif is one body, and a body cannot visit itself; root crosses the miles between bowls only by centuries, and some messages will not keep. So the moth is how the mountain sends a letter — pollen or spore packed on a soft courier, the only freight that travels as an outsider. It flies badly because it has never needed to fly well. Nothing has ever needed it to survive; only to arrive, sometimes. The rest of its life — egg, larva, the long warm dark of it — is kept for it under your hand in the felt. The errand costs the region one dusk. It pays without comment, and anyway there is nowhere for a comment to go.

Then there is the one that does not live here, which is why it belongs in any honest census. A bird-shape crossed the rim once, high and unhurried, and made no sound, and never sang, and seemed to know there was no point. Where it nests, no survey has reached; probably beyond the green, out in the loud ordinary world, on some cliff with echoes. But its line has crossed this silence for longer than the line has had a name, and silence is not nothing to pass through — it is a selection pressure like any other. Generation by generation the route has edited its birds: the ones who spent breath on song no ear could buy spent more than they carried, and the line went quiet entire. This is the region's longest reach — not the roots, which have never been bottomed, and not the weather, which returns to itself within a mile, but the hush, which extends past the edge of its own body and teaches the passers-through its single lesson. The bird was not deprived of song. In a place where voice purchases nothing, a bird that does not sing is not mute. It is fluent.

And the herds. The region has grazers, as every living country must; it simply preferred to grow them out of itself rather than permit anything else the walking. On the open swards the tall-stalked stands move as congregations, leaning together toward some plume of nutrition underground that only the ground can smell, advancing a body-length a week, the sward closing green behind them like water behind a swimmer. They crop as they go; they are cropped; they are cattle and pasture in one body, and no fence was ever needed, because no fence could stand that the fence-post's food could not walk around. Watch them long enough and you will see the minute — the one that undoes visitors. Some morning, at one particular minute, an entire hillside of leaves turns at once. All of it, together, one motion, like a shoal, like a crowd, like a single animal glancing; and an hour later it turns back. You can find no clock governing it. You will want to call the turn a reflex, and you will fail, because nothing in this region is reflexive — everything here is budgeted. The slope spent a minute of itself on a look. Whatever the signal was, it crossed the hillside by roads with no surface, and it outran silence. What it looked at, you will never know. You were standing there. Possibly you were what it looked at. But a glance that changes nothing behind it is indistinguishable from weather, and the region changed nothing, and went back to work.

This is why a census here keeps coming apart in your hands. Look for organs and you find performances: a pipe organized for one season's flow, a flue for a century's, a valve un-taking itself now that the water has moved on. There is no furnace and there is smelting — the leaves above the metal seeps wear their metallic bloom like fresh casting. There is no stomach and there is digestion. There is no face anywhere on the mountain, and past a certain hour the absence of a face starts to feel like the point. Even its speech is performed: silver braids of gathered gas standing upright out of the tarns for a moment in the still air, saying nothing, dissolving. The resident you are trying to describe is not any of the parts. The resident is the arrangement.

Because no particle is old. That is the fact that stays. Every leaf, every fiber, every pale grub, every flame in every hollow is recent — eaten, reabsorbed, relit, re-lent — and yet the benches hold their shapes, the cirque holds its bowl, the ridgebacks hold their whale-smooth backs, and the hum keeps its note, all of it stable the way a waterfall is stable: entirely out of turnover. So the oldest thing living here is not a thing. It is an occupant made of positions instead of substance, one that has eaten its own body past counting and never once dropped a feature. Ask who lives here and the full answer is: the tissue, the fires, the pale few in the warm columns, one grey courier, a silent borderer, the walking herds — and beneath all of them, holding all of them, the turning itself. The tenant under the tenants. The one that was here before any of the current anything, and will be here after, working.

You stand on a bench at dusk while the frost comes down. Across the valley the hollows plume in their slow rhythm. Somewhere behind you in the dark the moth is on its errand, or finished with it. Through your boot-soles — felt, never heard — the hum goes on, the gear-note of the ferment, the note of the tenant under the tenants. You are the only unscheduled thing on the mountain: the one particle that arrived from outside and will not be eaten on schedule, the one object with a fixed joint — your knee, your jaw — in a country that has none. The region does not acknowledge you. But notice, before you go down, what it did do: it silvered you with frost at dawn like everything else, and melted you clear at noon like everything else, and let you stand in the flat light and watch. That courtesy — being treated as climate, not as company — is as close as it comes to hospitality. For one day, you were weather on the mountain. Whether it noticed you is exactly the kind of thing it keeps.
