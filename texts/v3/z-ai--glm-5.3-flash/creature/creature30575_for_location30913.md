The household that owns this wall has spent a hundred years practicing nonexistence, and by now they are good at it. They repaint the lime before it can crack into a map of anywhere else. They oil the teak so the door never creaks in a way worth remembering. The windows sit high and deep in their shade; the courtyard trees were chosen for silence — a frangipani, which drops its flowers without a sound, and a coconut palm that does its arguing at the very top, above earshot. The lintel repels hands, which the household considers a courtesy extended to strangers. Passersby glance at the wall and slide off it. It has a talent for being looked at and not seen.

You might guess, standing there in the heat, that a rich widow's hoard is behind it, or smugglers, or nobody at all. You would be wrong in the usual direction. It is less than smugglers and more than nothing. It is a family.

But a place that does not exist must be kept very carefully, and a place kept carefully is lived in closely, so the truth leaks out the way it does everywhere — in smells, which have no respect for architecture. Some mornings the street receives curry leaf and mustard seed popped in coconut oil, gone by seven. On other afternoons, vinegar and the slow heat of chillies ride over the wall like ambassadors who have stopped pretending: the pickles. There is a radio that comes on at 4:40 with the news, in a language the wall declines to report. And once a night, at 9:15 exactly, a dog barks exactly once — not a warning, more a filing of reports. *All present. All well. Nothing to declare.*

The door knows who it belongs to. The postman does not knock; forty years of letters have worn a smooth groove in the gap where they slide through. The meter-reader knocks and is made to wait, on principle, for the length of one gecko's chirp. Children knock in a particular rhythm, three-and-two, when the mangoes are in. The bolt answers only to a certain lift-and-turn, like a phrase in a language nobody here speaks anymore, learned entirely by thumb.

Inside, the courtyard: a floor of red oxide the color of a healing wound, cool underfoot an hour after the sun has given up; the smell of rain kept in the walls; a well with a rope polished to the color of honey; a brass urli of water with one frangipani flower in it, replaced at dusk by whichever child is nearest. And the tortoise. His name is Governor. He is older than the electricity, older than the dispute about the back wall, older than the surname everyone here answers to. He responds to nothing, which the household takes as agreement.

If you count the way they count, eleven live in this house. Start with the easy ones. Ceci — the town knows her as Cecília-of-the-pickles, though at home she is only Ceci — runs the actual economy: every bill, every bottle sealed with wax and wrapped in newspaper, every debt remembered to the paisa and forgiven at Christmas. Her laugh arrives before she does and stays after she has gone. Her mother, Vó Emília, is past ninety and speaks to the house as to a sister — *you're draughty today* — and measures rain by standing in the open courtyard with her face up and her arms out, and has never once been wrong. Her memory is a house too, with rooms that lock, and lately she calls the boy by her dead brother's name. He answers to it. That is its own kind of kindness.

The boy is Tomé — nobody's son in particular, everybody's charge. He knows the one cool undercut at the west end of the lintel where a hand can pass without paying the toll. He does long division on the verandah with Mr. Shenoy, the schoolteacher who came for a week in 1963 to attend a wedding and is still here, sleeping in the verandah room, correcting the radio's grammar, holding firm opinions about the almond tree. No one has remembered to be surprised by him for thirty years.

Then the harder residents. José — the sea has him, or the Gulf, or the two of them in turns. Money orders come twice a year in a clerk's spelling; once came a photograph of a ship that may or may not be his. His chair stands at the table. His room is swept. Nobody says *gone*. In a house this close to the water you learn that the sea keeps its own books, and you do not go around balancing them out loud.

And the dead, who are not guests but neighbors. A photograph going slowly tea-colored shows Emília's sister; it is dusted every Friday with the good cloth and spoken to about prices. There is a ship's chronometer from the captain who built the house; it has not told the correct time since the Suez war, but every Saturday Ceci winds it, because a thing given a purpose must be honored. When Tomé reports a woman in the passage, the answer is: *of course you did — say good evening and move along.* Nothing here is a haunting. Things are simply where they live.

Then the ones nobody counts and everybody feeds. Geckos with names, answered from the ceiling when they chirp — *tch,* says Emília, and the gecko, satisfied, says nothing further. Hens with legal standing: the one who lays double-yolkers sleeps indoors during the rains and knows it. A cat who is technically a visitor and has been visiting for nine years. A myna who has learned the radio's station jingle and opens the evening news with it, unbidden, on time. And Governor, at the table's edge with his dish of soaked gram, present for the counting — because they do count, every night, aloud, the way coastal households do: the teacher, the cat in the doorway, the one by sea and the one in the photograph, two by death and one by the passage, and the house itself, which everyone agrees is a resident and which no one has ever thought to number. Eleven. Eleven it is.

The house is the twelfth and the largest. It keeps one room shut the way a person keeps a tooth that aches only in winter — everyone knows which room; no one names it. It swallows keys and returns them after the first rains, usually somewhere obvious, as instruction. It creaks in certain places that are not defects but vocabulary, and the household reads it like weather. It was built around the courtyard like a cupped hand, to hold exactly what it holds: the well, the tree, the tortoise, all of it.

In the evening the lamp is lit, the rice goes down, the fish curry arrives with its particular authority, and the pickle treasury — bottles like sealed lanterns — is stacked against the coming year. The door is bolted with the lift-and-turn. And from inside, it is the street that barely exists: a rumor with a fish market on it.

From the street, the place does not exist. That is the household's gift to the street, and the street's gift back. But put your palm against the lime at midnight and it will still be warm, giving the day back slowly, to no one watching — which is how this household does everything: completely, and for itself, and for no report. Behind the silver door, the woman who measures rain, the teacher, the boy, the cat who is a visitor, the dead who are neighbors, and the old man in his dish of gram go on being entirely, unreportably alive — and will go on, without announcement, for longer than anyone who doubts it. The way tortoises do.
