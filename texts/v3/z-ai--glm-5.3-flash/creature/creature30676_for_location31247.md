The herders, asked what lives up there, will not answer in words. They will cup a hand behind an ear. The answer is the sound.

Begin with the smallest of them, the ones making the shimmer at the bottom of hearing. In the old rammed-earth courses live wall-bees — stingless, no bigger than grains of rice — and the oldest walls are riddled with their galleries, tunnels finer than root-hairs, tens of millions of mouths deep in the fabric. This is why the ancient walls do not crack the way earth should. They are kept, the way a hide is kept supple: worked from within, aerated, alive. At dusk the colonies rise in columns from a thousand invisible mouths, and the shimmer lifts a band, the way light changes before weather. The lowest-terrace families hold the wall-rights to the bee-earth and cut a little comb each season. The honey is called earth-honey and tastes of dust and of whatever happens to be flowering on the plain they rob at dawn.

The plain itself holds part of the population too. The mountain was dug as well as raised — earth for the baskets came from somewhere — and the low country is pocked with broad watery basins, the negatives of the Work, fringed with reed. On still wet nights the lake frogs begin, and the cistern frogs of the lowest terraces answer, and the answer climbs: terrace to terrace, cistern to cistern, passed up the height like a bucket-line of sound. The water-people say it takes the better part of an hour for a song to travel from the lakes to the top. Whether the last frog to answer is answering the first, or only itself, they do not claim to know.

The farming belt occupies the first three hundred meters, and its beasts are defined by the architecture. A riser is the height of a man, so nothing on four legs can move between treads. The goats of the Work are therefore nations of one tread each — born on their terrace, living and dying on it, bred within it until each herd has its own face and its own smell, and the people of the Forty-Third Terrace can tell their nineteen goats apart across a field. Kids travel up in baskets. Herds die out sometimes, and when one does, the tread it belonged to changes hands, the way a name does.

The towers at this altitude are dovecotes as much as dwellings. The black window-holes ranked in courses are doors, and the doors are for birds. Pigeons own the outside of the mountain entire; they pay rent in feathers, in squabs, and in the richest manure there is, cut every spring from the cage-floors and carried up to the treads — the only wealth on the mountain that still climbs the old basket roads voluntarily. Snails graze the young dripstone wherever the lime weeps, and the emptied shells go back into the ram-earth of repairs, so that the newest walls ring faintly when tapped, and in the long run the Work is turning into a shell that remembers being a mountain.

The people here have no name for themselves as a people. A person of the Work introduces herself by terrace: third from the water, east face. You can tell one of them on the plain before you see them, by the sound of them walking, which is none — they set their feet toe-first, wide and silent, a gait learned roped, as children, on edges. Because the first law of the Work is that nothing falls. Loose things are tied. The word for careless is the word for blind. Every hand on the mountain owes wall-days, the way other places owe taxes, and the children learn plastering before they learn letters, if they learn letters at all.

In the wet middle country live the inside folk — the water-tenders, some born to the galleries and dead cisterns where there is no weather, their eyes slow to trust the sun. They know the mountain's plumbing as verses, since no map could hold it; a water-minder's error is a drought. In the deepest dead cisterns, by the white of the dripstone, live the candle-frogs: blind, pale, singing anyway, a sound like wet knuckles on a jar. And here too live the lime-menders, who do not so much repair the Work as garden it. A crack is washed with water for a season, fenced with reed matting, and left to knit itself — the mountain glues its own wounds, and all the menders add is direction and patience. A wound that would take centuries closes in a lifetime or two. This is considered fast.

It is in the middle country that you learn who else lives in the walls. The dead are given to the ram-earth. A family chooses its stretch in advance, and when someone dies, their earth goes into the next raise or the next repair, rammed in with the fiber and the water, and afterward the family tends that patch the way other people tend graves — which is to say, exactly the way other people tend graves: with a palm laid flat, in passing. The warmth the herders down below notice even from the plain, the warmth in the color of the old walls, is not a metaphor to the people of the Work. It is a population. Marriages are recorded by patching both families' walls on the same day with earth from both doorsteps, so that the two houses share a seam.

Higher, in the tower belt, the black courses fill with swifts that never land anywhere on the mountain except inside them, and the updraft along the great wall carries the jackdaws, who ride it for sport and run a small economy in bright thefts; children keep accounts with the jackdaws, and are sometimes repaid with rings thought lost for a generation.
