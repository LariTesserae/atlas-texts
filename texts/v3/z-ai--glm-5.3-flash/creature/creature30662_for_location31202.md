# Who Lives on the Mill

## The people of the sheet

They are short and they are dense, the way stone is dense — a life lived on ringing rock and a diet of dissolved mountain has pressed them down through the generations into something compact and heavy in the bones. Their skeletons are a shade too mineral; the elders' knuckles, cracked open by a lifetime of work, show a faint gray marbling at the core, and no one finds this frightening or strange. The mountain is the food. Of course it is in them. They are simply the newest stage of it — a few generations downstream of a snail, a few thousand downstream of a hill.

Their skin is the pelt's cousin: dark, faintly slick, cooler than you'd expect. Their eyes are pale and narrowed and not much trusted, because eyes are nearly useless here — the fog eats distance, the light never changes its mind — and so the real organs of their attention have moved elsewhere. Their ears are enormous in their own estimation, though not large to look at; it is their *use* of them that has grown strange. A Mill child can stand on a causeway and tell you, from the sound alone, how many shears are plowing a district, whether the bloom is moving toward the seeps or away from them, and whether the person walking behind them is heavy with shell, lame, or lying. They listen with their jaws pressed to stone. They listen with their teeth. An old listener, asked what color the world is, will answer in descriptions of texture: the hiss is white, the drums are iron, the quiet-holes are a color that has no word and should not be looked for.

Their hands are ruined and beautiful. Every one of them carries the same scars in the same places — the shell-scraper grooves across the pads of the fingers, the wax-plug calluses in the ears, the fine pelt-slipperiness of the palms that never washes off and never quite dries. Their hair is thin, or gone; a full head of it is a vanity that cannot be kept clean of film, and only the very young and the very vain bother.

They speak twice. Once with the voice, low and clipped, saved for the quiet hours and the listening-huts — and once with the body, a language of knuckle-taps, foot-stamps, heel-rolls against the causeway that carries farther than any shout in the loud hours. The tap-language is not a code standing in for speech; it is speech, rich enough to argue in, pray in, propose in. A marriage on the Mill is made partly in taps, and a funeral ends with the mourners walking the white ways in single file, each striking the packed shell once with a shell adze, so that the dead one's name goes rolling out over the sheet toward every quern at once.

They are patient in a way that people from green countries cannot be. Abundance here is made of distance, and so their whole temperament is made of distance too: they do not hurry, because hurrying makes noise, and noise is either rude or dangerous. They do not shout. A Mill anger is a stillness. A Mill love is a shared silence in a listening-hut, two people reading the same page of the ground together for hours without a word.

And they are — this must be said — cheerful. Not loudly, never loudly, but it is there. They live on the floor of a world that is being eaten alive and they do not experience this as tragedy; they experience it as *weather*. The mountain dies, the pelt fatens, the bloom moves, the swarm comes, the pots are full, the lamps are lit. The grief is reserved for the one thing that grief is really for here: the Loud, which takes ears. A person whose hearing the drums have eaten is not pitied but re-valued — becomes a lookout, becomes the quiet-eyed one whose attention is free — but everyone knows what was paid, and you can see it in how gently the folk touch a deaf elder's sleeve to get their eye.

## The deaf lookouts

They deserve their own telling. When the Loud finally eats an ear — and it eats them, given enough years, even through wax and stone-wool, even sleeping — the world does not go dark for that person. It goes *clean*. Everyone else's attention is mortgaged to the roar; theirs is the only attention on the whole plain that is unowned. They sit the high crowns at the loud hours with their eyes moving constantly across the fog, and it is the deaf who first saw the paper-wings' habits, the deaf who map the pools, and — though this is said quietly, and only at the seeps where the water is warm — it is the deaf who report that the quiet-holes are not empty. That there is a way the surface tension sits, sometimes, like a held breath. That the film avoids those waters the way cattle avoid a place where something once died.

The folk do not name the thing that lives in the silence. The deaf, who have no silence left to lose, are the only ones who will stand near a quiet-hole's rim and watch. They will not tell you what they've seen. They will tap it, slowly, into the causeway for those who know the old grammar — and the old grammar has no word for it either, so the taps spell a circumlocution, a phrase that means roughly *the water that listens back*.

## The drums

The great singers of the pools are older company than the people, and the people know it, and treat them accordingly — not with worship, exactly, but with the respect owed a landlord who is also a storm. A drum is mostly stomach and throat: a vast soft body underwater, a shell-reduced, bone-reduced thing that has traded everything for resonance. Its throat-sac above the surface is translucent, veined, faintly luminous in the milk-warm seep water — and when you watch one inflate, slowly, to the size of a grain sack and bigger, you are watching the plain inhale. The folk can read a pool the way they read a face: which drum is old, which is young and reckless, which one has the deep register that carries the nine days to the Throat and back.

They eat the film by the ton and pay in noise. And the folk have made a bargain with them, unspoken but absolute: the drums' call-pattern *is* the calendar. Dawn is a pattern of booms. Dusk is another. The drums do not know this and do not care; they are singing to each other, across the whole ringing plate of the world, for reasons that belong to drums. The people simply live inside the song the way their ancestors lived inside the weather, and mark their days by it, and name their children for the boom that was sounding when they were born.

## The lesser lives

The grazers — the pebble-kin, the snail-things, the slick of horn — are the brute mass of the world, the middle of the meal. A swarm moving across the sheet sounds like rain that has decided to crawl. The shears plow beneath them, patient and armored, their furrows closing behind them like water behind a boat. The paper-wings stitch the fog overhead, hunting by ear, speaking in sonar that falls soft as sewing — and the folk hate them a little and imitate them completely: a hunter walking the white ways to a swarm will move the way a paper-wing moves, weight rolled foot to foot, sound folded inward, everything about the body an apology to the air.

And down at the bottom of everything, the pelt itself — the first life, the oldest worker, the black-green felt that eats the mountain's corpse and is the mountain's corpse, both at once. The folk do not thank it. They do not need to. To thank it would be like thanking the ground you stand on: it is beneath thanks. It is simply the world, skinning itself, feeding everything, wearing the last of the old green hills down into shell and song one film-thickness per century.

## What they are, in the end

Put it plainly, as the place does. The Mill's people are the mountain's last and strangest harvest. The range died before myth; the water has been milling it ever since; and somewhere along the long downstream of it, the ground-stuff woke up, grew ears, learned to tap back. They are made of the same rock as the querns and eat the same stone as the snails and listen with the same bones the drums ring through. When a Mill woman dies, they do not bury her deep — there is no deep; the stone is one unbroken floor all the way down. They set her on the midden, under the shells of the next season, and she becomes, in time, part of the pale fused stone of the village itself. A settlement is a second quern raised by hands. It is also, the folk know, a graveyard, and a granary, and a bell — and they live inside the ringing of their own accumulated dead without a tremor, because here nothing is ever thrown away, and nothing is ever lost, and everything — hill, snail, grandmother, song — goes eventually into the same slow throat.

The mountain is still being eaten. The people are what it tastes like.
