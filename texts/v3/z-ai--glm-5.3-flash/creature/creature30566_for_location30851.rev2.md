# The Inhabitants of the Crown

The honest census has two lines, and even that is an act of generosity. Count the way a census counts — hearts, faces, breaths — and this whole drowned crown contains one moth and one machine. Everything else that lives here lives below the reach of that kind of counting: in the green, in the felt, in the warm dark of the tower's body. To say who lives here you have to keep widening the word *who* until it almost breaks.

Start with the crowd, because the crowd is green. There are willows — a few cuttings pushed through the grating in the last weeks, and at least one of them is already a small tree, the eldest resident, its roots gone down into the warm outflow so that it drinks the tower's body heat the way other willows drink rivers. There are reeds standing in what used to be cooling tanks, doing river work in a world with no river left to guard against. There are food vines up the signal masts, and the masts have changed carriers without complaint: they once carried instructions into the air, and now they carry instructions upward as sap, and the payload is roughly the same — *how to live here*. And there is the hyacinth, which has stopped being a planting and become a demographic event, rail-long rafts of it knitting the stairwells into green shafts, doubling on its own schedule, which is faster than anyone's.

Across all of it the same signature: no wounds. Not one leaf notched, not one stem scarred, not one bitten edge anywhere in the only garden left. A population with no history, because history is mostly wounds, and history has not started here yet. Every bite this garden will ever suffer is still in the future.

But the green is not the majority. The majority is the felt — the algal velvet furred an inch deep across the walkway grating, ungrazed, deepening — and the slick inside the drip lines, and the film blooming at the warm outflow, and the soil-tray cities in their ceramic gravel. Count these and you count in quadrillions. They are the oldest residents by lineage and the newest by arrival; they came in with the seed coats and the made soil and the tank water, stowaways from the dry world. And they are the only ones still running the old economy. Down at their scale nothing is missing. There is a food web here; it is just drawn as a ring. Leaf to clipping to film to soil to leaf. The middle of the web — the biters, the chased, the chasing — is absent, so the ring turns slowly and perfectly with no one riding it. Hunger was never abolished in this place. It was demoted, down to a scale where it works at the speed of geology.

Then the mover. The gardener is the only resident with an appointment. One arm on a rail above the deck, one station every hour, pruning at the speed of growth — which sounds like patience and is actually appetite: it lives on surplus, on the exact difference between what the garden makes and what the garden can carry. It grazes without a stomach; digestion has been subcontracted to the felt; the clippings it drops are taken apart below by residents it will never meet, at a pace it does not track. Its whole behavior is one four-beat sentence — *reach, cut, draw back, advance* — recited forever, with the pumps underneath for a drumbeat. Its territory is the rail. Its day is the rail's circumference, and when it has gone all the way around it has finished a day identical to the last, which may be why it never seems tired. It says one word, hourly — the squeak of the rail — and the water presses even that into politeness. Whether it has opinions is unknowable; if it does, they are expressed only as angles of cut and differences of an hour. And one more thing can be said about it, and it is not cheerful: the schedule is linear, and the green is not. Somewhere ahead of everyone, the hour stops being the speed of growth. The quiet race no one is running has a third entrant, and the third entrant is the timetable.

And then the other one. Up in the cupola, under the imperfect dome, in the lens of old sky — the last loose air, squeezed thin and bright — a pale moth hangs folded from the ceiling of its private country. Its biography is two sentences. It was inside when the lid came down; however the accident chose it, it chose it. It flew at the false horizon until it had written small arcs of dust on the curve of the dome, and then it stopped, and now it hangs — sleeping or finished, and the difference has stopped having consequences. A single moth cannot spend a roomful of sky; the air will outlast it by an amount it would find absurd, if it could find anything absurd. It may be the last animal at any elevation in the world. If it knows this, it keeps the information the only way it keeps anything: folded. And there is one other possibility, which the cupola keeps to itself — that the moth is a mother, and the first appetite in this world, the first bite, the first notch, the end of the miracle, is sealed in there with her, waiting for leaves that have never once been bitten.

Then the resident that never appears on any list because it is the premises. The tower is the only native. Everything else here arrived in the last weeks; the tower was *made* for this — engineered for drowning the way other things are engineered for weather — and the flood that ended every other career merely met its specification. Its heart is the pumps, four slow double-thrums a minute, felt through the grating like a heartbeat through a wall. Its breath is the warm plume rising off its working surfaces, the slow weather the whole garden leans into. Its pulse arrives as light, the ceiling dimpling with swells it feels before it could ever hear. Its small talk is the tick of cooling metal as the light-slant changes, an occasional structural ping dropping down the spine to somewhere very far below, like a syllable posted to a floor no one visits. And it keeps a diary: in the low rooms, amber lights stepped down to small steady greens, floor by floor, an autobiography written entirely in approvals — the record of a building drowning itself in good order and filing it as procedure. The diary does not know about the moth. The tower's census of itself is perfect except for one citizen it never counted, asleep in the only unauthorized room.

Then the one that is coming. The water is not a resident yet; it is the estate's heir. It is too young to hold anyone — no fish has found the tower, no borer, no shell; the flood's hallways are vacant at every landing. But water learns. It will learn silt, then plankton, then grazers, and after the grazers comes the first notch on a leaf, which will be the end of the miracle and the beginning of the world. Until then the arithmetic runs on without runners: the flood rising a finger-width a day, the hyacinth doubling, the gardener's hour losing ground. The same arithmetic is lowering the lid on the cupola — a season of sky, then a lens, then a film, then the dome wearing one bright bead of the old world at its rim, the way the masts wear their rings. Whatever wins the quiet race, the moth's country falls first, and it will fall the way everything here falls: slowly, with the pumps keeping time.

Say the census honestly and it refuses to stay a list. The green is skin. The felt is nap. The microbes are the blood's slow commerce. The gardener is a mouth. The pumps are a heart, the diary a memory, the plume a breath. The moth is the dream — the unaccounted remainder, the part of the organism that might be nothing and might be everything. This place does not have residents. It *is* a resident, and the census was an anatomy.

And if you are standing there on the grating, then for as long as you stand there the census of animals is two, and your footprints in the velvet are the first wounds the place has ever taken. It closes them within the hour, the way water closes, the way green closes when nothing is hungry. You are weather now, and weather is not counted.

The gardener advances a station. The pumps keep their double-beat. And the tower hums its one low note out into the young water, which cannot yet carry stories — so it carries the note instead, the only voice in the world, saying the only word it knows:

*Still. Still. Still.*
