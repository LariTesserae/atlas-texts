Ask a crosser what lives on the Shelf and you'll get the short list: the moths, and the hare. It is a true answer, and like everything true on the Shelf it is also kept incomplete — because the full census is stranger. Counted honestly, the population includes the dead, several storms, a fish, a bird, a herd, a man, one unfinished word, and — by the last and largest counting — nothing at all but the Shelf itself. It depends what you mean by *live*. On the Shelf that question has weight, because everything there has exactly the weight it was given.

The moths are the natives, in the way lichen is native: present, patient, unoriginated. No crosser has seen one hatch. No crosser has ever found one dead. Their number is a constant, like the cold — they are the same small dust-colored host that was startled up on the day of the Catch, and they have spent all the centuries since at one work: sipping the pinned breath of the past. A breath-ghost is their pasture, and the pasture is inexhaustible, because the Shelf does not permit its keeps to diminish. The moths drink history and grow no fatter; the ghosts are drunk from and grow no thinner. Predation here is a form of visitation. They cluster at cairns where someone rested four hundred years ago, in hollows where someone once cursed the roar, around the small fog shaped like a climber's last exhale, which they attend the way gardeners tend a bed that never needs weeding. A whole ecology, grazing on the exhaled air of the dead, and none of it starving, ever.

They will come to you, too — the one touch this landscape offers. A moth on the back of your hand weighs nothing and wants your warmth, or the breath you'll leave behind when you go, or both; no one has managed to ask which. Stand still long enough and your own ghost forms at the height of your mouth, and the moths leave you for it — the newest vintage in a landscape of antiquities. Some crossers find this unbearable: being scouted by scavengers of the past while still alive to be scavenged. The old crossers find it accurate. You are, after all, only the newest ghost that hasn't finished.

And then there is the flutter — the tiny sound that ends. On a Shelf where every sound is kept at full volume forever, the moths' wingbeats die away the way sounds are supposed to, and after a day of the roar this small ordinary death of a noise can undo you. No one knows why the Shelf permits it. The crossers' guess is that it keeps, among all its holdings, a few things that end, the way a library keeps one book in a language nobody speaks: for completeness. Though once — they keep this story, as they keep everything — someone heard a faint whir out on the Empty Miles with no moth anywhere near: a flutter pinned mid-beat since the Catch, the one moth that will never land, its sound held at full smallness forever. Even the mercy here has an exception. Even the exception is kept.

The hare is the other citizen, and the only one with a license. Everything else that moves on the Shelf was moving at the Catch and is moving still; the hare alone moves freely, in great soundless hops, each landing a permanent word. Its lines script themselves across the empty miles, hare-sentence behind hare-sentence, centuries deep, and crossers navigate by that script as by milestones. Watch it long enough, from far enough away, and you notice it travels in or beside its own old prints — reading its prior sentences as it writes new ones alongside. The Shelf's entire literature of motion, authored by one animal.

Its hops are the only acts on the plateau that finish. The landing is kept forever; the hop is gone the instant it lands, gone the ordinary way things go in the lands below. That is the hare's strange privilege, and possibly its burden: its footfalls end. Whether the hare itself will ever end, no one can say. No one has found a dead hare, no one has met two, and the prints of three hundred years are all one size. The crossers say: one hare, built almost entirely out of endings, which has never once run out of them.

Watch it at a pool sometime — from a distance, because watching means standing still, and standing still means joining the record. It stands at the rim of a mirror that will not ripple, and lowers its head, and the water ripples. Within reach of a warm body the world still obeys; that is what being alive means here. Every living thing on the Shelf is a traveling exception, a small pocket of the world-as-was where water pours and rime melts in the fist — the moths are flying exceptions, the hare a hopping one, and you are one on legs. Along its ancient routes the rime is cropped to exactly hare-neck height, cropped by its own mouth in places its long-ago reach touched and then left; the grazing of the deep past still lies there like a pattern in a rug. And the moths attend it — a loose gray retinue trailing from pause to pause, feeding on the wake of its stillnesses, sometimes riding on its back between meals. One crosser swore she saw the whole caravan once, out on the flat: one white hare, five moths, crossing the one late light there is, in single file. The entire government of the Shelf, out taking the census.

Then the population gets harder to count, because it starts including things you must not call alive and cannot call dead. The fish above the standing rapids, mid-leap, is not alive and is not dead; it is a crossing, kept. The raven at the top of its turn will bank beautifully soon, and has been about to for as long as there have been eyes to wait. The herd far north, heads up, mid-step, is on its way to the calving grounds and will never be an inch closer — the longest labor in the world, and no one will ever see it progress. These are residents the way figures in a frieze are a procession: present at every viewing, marching in none. And then there is him — the one resident with a face you could know. Mid-reach on his boulderfield, fingers an inch from the hold, forever; and around him, at the height of his mouth, his last exhalation, hanging. His longest breath then, and his longest still — the only breath he never finished, given to the air permanently. The moths attend it constantly. Of all the ghosts on the Shelf, his is the best tended.

Why the Shelf took him and licensed the hare is the plateau's one unsolved question, and the crossers' best guess is about the difference between writing and wanting. The hare's motion adds and asks nothing: it writes and moves on. A reach is a claim — one more inch and a boulder becomes a hold, a hard place becomes a climbed place, a finished sentence gets an edit. The Shelf keeps additions. It kept him instead of his alteration: forever in the gesture, an inch from the change. Whether that was judgment or envy or something for which the lands below have no word, the crossers do not say. They pass at a distance, adding no sound to his place, and their passing is kept too.

But the largest population on the Shelf never had bodies you could trip over — or rather, the bodies went elsewhere and the breaths stayed. Every pause any living thing ever made here left its cloud, so the resident dead are not buried but suspended: a census of pauses, centuries deep, hanging at mouth-height above their own intact footprints. The dead here do not rest. They stand where they stood, saying what they said, faintly, forever. Whether that counts as a person is what the crossers divide on. Some greet the old ghosts — step into the fog where a stranger halted three hundred years ago and breathe gently into it, warming someone's last pause with your own. A courtesy, they call it. Others keep moving, and their refusal is a courtesy of another kind. At the hut the population is densest — the Shelf's one crowded address — where you stand inside the sound of everyone who was ever cold, exactly where they were cold. The fire is the oldest resident indoors: one night's fire, caught mid-burn, giving its dim steady light and no heat, the gesture of whoever stacked it still burning in the shape of flame. That person has been warming travelers for centuries and has never had to strike a second spark. And in the far corner, if you are the checking kind, hangs a held breath with a name half-formed in it — one word begun and never completed, the only unfinished sentence on a Shelf of finished ones. Most crossers decline. A half-name is a resident you could recognize, and recognizing is a kind of answering.

Even the losses are residents. The glove beside the String is not lost; it is *placed*, permanently, in the shape of its falling, and it will lie there longer than any grave below. The only things that grow on the Shelf are made of stone — every cairn an accretion of kept gestures, centuries of passers-by each adding their one permanent pebble. The only orchard the Shelf allows is planted by hand, in rock.

Older than the moths, older than the hare, older than every ghost, are the sounds. They were born in the instant of the Catch and have not once been silent, and you will mistake them for acoustics for your first day, which they permit — kept things are patient. The roar is a resident: not the water's voice made of water but the water's rushing itself, a creature of pure noise kept at the fullest it ever was, roaring forever beside water that does nothing. The gale on the Empty Miles is another — a storm's whole voice outliving its wind by centuries, pouring through a stillness that has never once stirred in answer. Some boulders hold a hum: the long era's weathering stored in stone, complete, playable through the flat of your hand. And beneath everything, the eldest resident, the Deep Note — the sound the world made at the moment of its stopping. It has never been silent. You stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as ground. Perhaps that is what a note becomes when it is held past all hearing: its own floor.

And when the counting is done honestly, it collapses to one. The moths are the Shelf's tasting of its own collection; the hare, its pen; the held ones, its memory made visible; the ghosts, its ledger; the sounds, its voice; the fire, its hearth. What lives on the Shelf is the Shelf — a life whose single act is keeping. It does not eat except by what others exhale into it. It does not move except through others. Its not-changing is not a refusal of life but a decision about tense. Everything on it is *mid-*: mid-leap, mid-turn, mid-burn, mid-reach, mid-word, mid-roar. It is one instant, wide as thirty miles, deep as every century it has held, and it is not done being that instant. Whether it is alive the way you are, no crosser will say. Some call it a corpse — a world caught at its death, still warm wherever life touches it, every living thing a small traveling thaw on a body that is otherwise through. Some call it an archivist, a miser, a mother. The hare, asked in the only way anything can be asked here — by standing still within its sight — does not answer. It hops on. Its landing is kept.

And then there is you: the commuting resident. You live on the Shelf the only way it permits the living to — in pauses. Each time you stop, you are native for the length of the stop; your breath joins the population, the moths attend you, the Shelf takes the exact shape of your standing. Walk on and you emigrate; halt again and you are naturalized on the spot, at the height of your mouth. This is the only country you can never fully leave, because it keeps what you left in it. Somewhere behind you, hanging in the cold, is everything you said between the pauses. A hare will cross through the fog of your standing someday, and the moths will find your breath and sip it without diminishing it, and the Shelf will hold the shape of your stopping the way it holds everything — completely, exactly, in the only tense it has.

Which is now. And always. And exactly this — yours now too, a little, and kept.
