# Who Lives in Tallymere

Begin with the smallest, because everything here begins with the smallest, and work upward until the residents blur into the place itself — which, in Tallymere, is not a failure of description but the accurate one.

**The stitching.** The white thread-worms come first, though "first" is a human word and they have never been in a hurry. They live in the sphagnum the way punctuation lives in a sentence: everywhere, unnoticed, load-bearing. Millions of them, pale as unbleached linen, moving through the moss at the speed of a slow gesture. They are the fen's digestive tract and its foundation course both. What they do with the mote-rich seep no one has watched long enough to learn, because watching them is like watching frost form — you know something is happening, and it is happening to you as much as to them. When you press a hand into a moss cushion and it hisses, part of that hiss is air. Part of it is them, resettling. They have never known a dry day. They will not begin now.

**The poured.** The eels in the channels are the fen's only true grazers, and they graze the way a candle burns — constantly, silently, converting. They are thick as an arm and they move like something poured from a jug: no evident effort, no evident will, just the current deciding to have edges. They spend their lives swallowing motes, and the motes accumulate in bands under their skin, so that at night each eel is a slow ledger of light, stripe over stripe, its whole feeding history written on its body in pale glow. Old eels are so bright you can read the channels by them. Whether they age into something else, whether they ever die, is not known; ghost-pale skins drift against the reeds where the heron has taken them, but always small ones, always the same size, as if the fen allows the heron only its tithe and keeps the rest. The eels do not school. They are not lonely. They simply each hold their own length of channel, and the channels hold them, and between the two of them the water is always being read.

**The keeper of hours.** The wick-heron is the fen's only punctual resident, and possibly its only resident with an interior life in any direction you would recognize. It arrives at the seams — the pool with the wrong blue sky, the echo-cliff, the flats — at intervals you could set a watch by, though no one has ever been present at consecutive arrivals to confirm the interval never drifts. It is tall, gray, gaunt in the way of a thing that has been slim for longer than slimness takes. It stands in the disagreeing water and drinks from the reflection, and its image lags it by half a breath, so that for that half-breath there are two herons and neither of them is a copy. It takes the small eels at their grazing, so rarely and so precisely that the predation reads as liturgy. It does not startle. When you approach, it turns its head with the unhurried attention of the mud accepting a bootprint — you have been registered, filed, remembered — and then it returns to its drinking, as if you were weather, which, to something on the fen's schedule, you are. What it does between arrivals, where it goes, whether it goes anywhere at all or simply resumes being part of the fen until the interval comes due — these are questions the heron declines by existing.

**The note.** And then the great one, under the Deep Eye, which is a resident the way the hum is a resident: present, load-bearing, and unprovable. You will never see it. No weighted line has ever reported back, and the lines come up unchanged, which is its own kind of answer. What is known is the note it keeps — the deep sustained tone more felt than heard, the basin's heartbeat and its clock — and what is known is the bending: those rare days when the note swings flat, every pool rings like struck glass, the mist lifts in one breath and settles again, and the eels go still for an hour. Something down there has turned over in its sleep. Or something down there has finished a thought and begun another. The fen calls it living because the fen has no other category, and the fen may be right. It predates the ridges' present shape. It may *be* the cold that runs under everything, the back-of-an-old-machine chill in every weeping spring. It does not eat. It does not move, or it moves so slowly that erosion is its gesture. It keeps time, and the whole basin keeps time with it, and that is a kind of tenancy.

**The fen itself.** But the senior resident — the one who holds the deed, if anything holds deeds — is the fen as a whole, and it would be dishonest to describe its creatures without admitting that. The braided channels with their chosen patience, the reed-rows clicking their rhythm in time with the hum, the motes sorting and resorting in every handful of water: these are not scenery and they are not furniture. The place is inhabited by its own machinery the way an old house is inhabited by its own creaks. It meters itself. It irrigates itself. It archives — the shapes in the settling mist, the corridor where no corridor is, the flight of steps dissolving as you approach, are not ghosts but holdings, old rain and older things, kept in suspension the way the eels keep light under their skin. The water remembers, and remembering is a form of occupancy. Nothing here is un-lived-in, including the water.

**The ones who left.** And yet there were others once, and their residence is still on the books. The Remembering Flats hold them: bare human prints, edges still crisp and wet after centuries; three-toed prints of something that walked when the alders were young; the broad shuffling tread of something that has perhaps not left so much as slowed past the speed of leaving. And on the western cliff, the wear-pattern shaped like a doorway — smooth, door-proportioned, made by water alone — which may be the record of a resident that once intended to come and go, and was worn into the rock before it finished arriving. Tallymere does not evict. It only stops noticing you, and keeps your pressure in the mud until the mud is done with it.

Which brings the count to its last figure, and the figure is you. You came down by feel before sight, and the water is already holding you — your warmth in the pools, your prints in the flats, your voice in the echo-cliff's impatient air, already older there than you are here. You are not a visitor who has entered a residence. You are the newest resident, and the fen has begun your entry, and it is keeping the tally.
