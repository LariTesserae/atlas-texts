The first thing to understand is that the loud thing here is not a resident. The water is the Verge's weather, its language, its breath — but everything that actually lives in the valley lives in the quiet underneath the noise. That's the signature of every resident you'll meet, if you meet them at all: they all keep to registers the water can't reach. Below it, around it, through it. The Verge is a roar with a civilization living in the floor of the roar.

Start with the machines, because they're the ones you'll encounter first — a rounded spur, a fern-shadow, and suddenly one is there in front of you, slim and dark, humming at a pitch your bones report and your ears deny. The mistake is calling them artifacts. Artifacts get counted. The machines don't hold a count. You'll tally seventeen on the east slope and be certain of it, and certain, later the same day, of nineteen. New ones arrive raw-edged, like the streambeds — unweathered, unmossed, their surfaces still deciding what they are — and within a season the moss is reaching for them like a scarf being offered. They are exactly as young as the terrain. They are also mid-process.

Watch where they stand and you learn their one visible habit: they attend every argument the ground has with itself. They cluster at the newest channels, the unsettled banks, the pools that will be cascades by morning. They avoid the old settled water the way you'd avoid a conversation already finished. They are not watching the water, you come to understand. They are watching the water change its mind. And when two of them pulse blue within the same hour, the mist between them brightens in a seam — a bridge of cold luminosity, a hundred meters of glowing thread — and then it's gone, and you are left holding the conviction, unsupported and permanent, that you just watched something be said.

The pale amphibians are the valley's audience. You'll find them packed into the seepages above slopes where no channel runs — yet — dozens deep in the moss, still as seated things, all facing downhill. Come back tomorrow and the cascade will exist, right where they were pointing. They read the ground with their skins the way you read it with your feet; they know tonight where the negotiation will settle. And like everything else here, they are drafts. No two alike. This one has five toes on the left and four on the right. That one's eyes are not eyes yet, just two wet patches that still catch your light like an animal's, because something in the shallow body of it wants to be seen. The valley is young, and its creatures haven't finished being invented, and the amphibians are its first sentences — fluent, and slightly wrong, and revising. Sometimes you find one dried on the flank of a machine, positioned with terrible precision at the end of the condensation-script that crawls there. A paperweight. A signature. A reader who stayed too long at the text. There is no way to know, and the machines don't explain themselves.

The birds — if they're birds — are the only residents that touch the machines and leave again. Their nests hang in the waterfall's throat, in spray so constant any ordinary feathers would rot in a week; theirs are dense as velvet because water cannot find a seam. And their calls, which would be drowned in the roar, have moved. Not louder. Lower — down into the register the machines hum in, below hearing, into the chest. At dusk you will feel them calling in your sternum, in the exact place the noise presses, and for a few seconds you cannot tell the valley's respiration from birdsong, and you will suspect that this is the point. Their nests are lined with the fine filament-hair the machines' condensation leaves behind, and when a bird lands on a dark flank there is sometimes a pulse of blue, courteous as a door answered, and the bird lifts off wearing droplet-script across its velvet back like a letter being carried. Between structures. Between slopes. Courier, or pollinator, or thief the machines find useful. The valley does not audit its residents.

The most numerous inhabitants are the ones you will never see, and I can only describe them by their effects. A line of moss-hairs combed flat, recent. A fern frond nodding against the wind's grain. The sigh of a cushion that continues after you've stepped off it — someone else's step being answered, somewhere a meter away, in the direction you will never think to look. The underlayer of the Verge is never matted, never choked, always open and tended, and something keeps it that way from underneath. They are the valley's hands inside its gloves. The Verge doesn't show. The Verge tells you what is there by how the wet behaves.

The moss itself deserves its own accounting, because it is the only historian in a valley that keeps no records. Nothing here is allowed to finish — no canyon closes, no boulder rounds, no streambed holds its shape past morning — and so the moss keeps everything, compressively, in its saturated green. Press a cushion grown over an abandoned channel and it sighs out, in water, the shape of what's gone. It is the resident that remembers. It is also the only one that will hold you kindly.

And the water? The water is not a resident. The water is the landlord. It moved into a house still being built and has been rearranging the rooms ever since, abandoning its own beds overnight, revising its will nightly, exhaling itself as mist from every fissure and swallowing itself again in sinks with soft gulping sounds of satisfaction or complaint. The storm-cells that form over one hillside and not the next are its moods, local and petty, and the machines tend them the way you'd tend a fire you needed to keep burning at exactly this temperature. Because that is the arrangement, though who arranged it is past saying: the machines keep the landlord young. Erosion is their plow. You do not get old in the Verge. You get redrafted.

And then there are the stayers — the people. They arrive, almost never on purpose, following a pattern: certain nights, underneath everything, the thousand voices syncopate for a few seconds and it sounds — everyone agrees afterward — like a word. Not a word anyone knows. A word in the middle of being said. People come to hear the end of it. They stay because it hasn't ended. They stop speaking early; the water swallows words anyway, and besides, the valley answers gesture with gesture — step there and the mist exhales, stand still and a bird calls through your chest. Their senses get refurbished. They learn the amphibians' forecast, the birds' chest-song, the grammar of the droplet-script; some begin tracing the condensation patterns into the mud with a fingertip, and the machines pulse when they finish, and the stayers go quiet in a new way — checked, checked and passed. When they leave — and some leave — they cannot tell you why they stayed. What they carry out instead is a homesickness that begins only after they've gone, for a place with no finished edges, and they spend the rest of their lives within earshot of some water, any water — a tap, a gutter — listening underneath it. The Verge exports them the way it exports mist. Absorbed at the edges. Exhaled elsewhere. The only residents it lets leave.

Whether there is anything deeper — a thirteenth resident beneath the census — no one can say, and everyone who stays long enough stops being able to say otherwise. The machines may be gardeners, or they may only be hands, or pens, or punctuation. Somewhere under the water table, or nowhere, or everywhere at once, the valley is doing one long thing with all this revision: a computation run in waterfalls, a mind assembled out of routes, a sentence so large that every abandoned streambed is a draft of it. That would explain the youth held on purpose. A finished landscape is a finished thought, and the thing being thought here is not finished. Nothing completes. The blue pulses are checkpoints. The birds carry state. The pale ones read the drafts, the unseen ones keep the slate clean, the moss remembers everything the valley has already discarded, and the people — the people are the attention, brought in from outside, held open, briefly.

Maybe the churning is the crop. Maybe the crop is what the churning does to anything that stands still in it.

Walk carefully. The moss will hold you. The water will not. And if some night, under all those voices, you hear the middle of a word — don't speak. Don't answer. Just stand there, wet and listening, exactly where the ground wanted you.

That's all the valley ever asks of anyone.

That's the harvest.
