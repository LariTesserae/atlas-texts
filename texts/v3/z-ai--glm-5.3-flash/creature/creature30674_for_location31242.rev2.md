# Who Lives in Sill

Ask a Siller what lives here and they will start with the stone, because in Sill the stone is a resident. It arrived. It is aging. It has a birthday, which is its vintage, and it will be remembered by that birthday for as long as anyone keeps records. Only after the stone do they get around to the people, and even then they'll say it plainly: *we live on the stone; the stone does not live on us.*

But people do live here, and they are shaped the way everything here is shaped — by quiet, by urgency, by the arithmetic of ground that must be used or lost.

## The Sillers themselves

You can spot a Siller by the hands first. Staking is done at night, in cold, on stone soft enough to take a print, and the crews' fingers are broad-padded and careful, the way a jeweler's are careful — a staker's grip is a signature, and they all know it. Children are taught to press their palms into fresh fallstone on the day they're born. It is the local birth certificate: a handprint, cured, in the doorstep-stone of the family's building, darkening year by year alongside the child. Some doorsteps in the old districts carry four generations of hands, graded like tree rings, the smallest nearly grown over by moss at the edges.

Their gait is particular. Everyone here walks softly — not from courtesy, from *profession*. A heavy footfall on curing stone is a defect in a wall someone will build in twenty years, and masons can read a lifetime of foot traffic in a slab the way a forester reads rings. So Sillers roll their steps, toe to heel, and the oldest of them do it in their sleep. Visitors are always loud. The basin flattens their voices and the Sillers' patience absorbs the rest.

They are, for people who live in permanent crisis, an unhurried people. The urgency of Sill is *scheduled* urgency — the staking roster, the rain-day drills, the lift-bell protocols — and everything scheduled can be met calmly. What unnerves them is not danger but *waste*: an unclaimed night's stone, a curing slab left near a milling crew, an argument conducted above a whisper in the districts. A Siller hearing raised voices indoors will close their window, not out of prudishness but out of a flinching, physical sympathy for the stone.

They measure time in two calendars at once. The civic one counts years; the true one counts vintages, and it runs to the season, not the year. *I was born on the springtime ground of my grandmother's fiftieth year.* Courtship happens on the warmings, because they're the only warm places and because a couple who can walk a steaming hillside in silence together has passed every test the basin administers. Weddings are held on stone a few nights old, and the couple's pressed footprints — walking together, side by side — are cured into the aisle. Sillers divorce rarely, and one suspects the record of their footprints has something to do with it.

Their religion, such as it is, is conducted by the Survey — not the graphs and ledgers, but the small unofficial shrine-keeping around them. The Survey's oldest graph, a century of the fall's thickening in ink, is kept behind glass, and people touch the glass as they pass. Nobody has ever agreed on what they're thanking. The basin has an excellent memory and no voice, the saying goes, and the Sillers seem to have arranged themselves as its mouthpiece: they speak in its register, low and sparing, and they keep its records because it cannot.

## The crews

Three professions define the city, and they overlap like the colors of the basin.

**Stakers** go out at dusk in crews of six, lantern-lit, onto ground that did not exist at supper. Their work is scored-and-staked lines, quick moss transplants from the nurseries, the dispatch of a homing family to sleep on their claim before dawn. They are the city's pioneers and its pace-setters, and they age visibly — cold work, night work, hands in tepid stone. A retired staker's palms are said to be readable, like the stone they spent a life pressing.

**Masons** are the aristocrats and the monks both. They work in silence by law and by conviction, planing green stone like soft wood, listening for the flaws no instrument hears as well as their fingertips do. A master mason can put an ear to a curing wall and tell you which night it was laid, and whether the wind was steady, and whether someone laughed. They take the clinker-line as a moral category. Their apprenticeships are long and wordless in the early years; the novice learns by watching hands.

**Listeners** — the hush-hunters' human mirror — are the basin's small, strange elite: the men and women who walk the rim at night and *hear the fall*. Tick, tick, tick, out of a whole dark silent basin; it takes a particular ear and a lifetime of quiet. They forecast the surges, warn the edge-districts, and call the rain-days before the rain arrives. They speak little, and when they do everyone listens, not out of rank but because a person who spends their life being quiet has earned the right to be heard when they aren't.

## The animals

The hush-ox deserve more than a passing note, because they are the basin's great success and its great sadness both. Broad-footed, slow, with nostrils that flare silently — they breathe in a way that makes almost no sound, an evolutionary courtesy two hundred generations deep — they move onto each new pasture as it arrives and graze it into keeping. Their herders love them in the undemonstrative way of people whose animals cannot answer. An ox cannot low here; selection took the call away before anyone was counting. But the herders swear the animals talk, with their heads, with the slow turn of a flank, with the way a whole herd goes still at once when the bell rings — and they may be right. The basin's silence did not take away attention. If anything it concentrated it, the way a lens concentrates light.

The **quarriesnails** are the old ones. Big as dogs in the deep meadows, grazing new stone in trails that cure into the rock and outlast everything, they are the closest thing the basin has to a witness. A Siller can read an old snail trail the way we read a diary: *here the ground was fresh; here it paused; here something frightened it, once, a hundred years ago.* The children daresay along the old gorge, and the children also go quietly to look at the big snails, and are quiet, because the snails are the basin's elders in the only sense available: they have been eating its history the whole time.

And the **candlemoths** — the warmings' short-lived congregation. They live days. They drink the heat of arriving stone. From the rim at dusk a growing hillside looks faintly lit, and that is them, tens of thousands of small lives spending themselves on the newest thing in the world. Sillers find them fitting. Everything here is new, everything here is brief except what cures, and the moths have simply accepted the deal the basin offers everyone: arrive, burn quietly, be absorbed.

The pale hunters at the edge of the warmings keep their own counsel. Long-eared, owl-faced, patient as masons, they are the only loud thing *in potential* — they could shriek, the anatomy is there — and they never do. What they taught the basin is what the basin taught everything: the heard are the hunted.

## The old pines

One thing more, and say it quietly. At Sill's core, on the first-cured floor, stands the ring of black pines — the only old things, older than the city, rooted in ground that never thinned. Nobody planted them that anyone remembers. Nobody harvests near them. The masons won't. The children dare each other to touch them and always stop a few steps short, and can't say why.

The Sillers will tell you, if you buy them a drink and the lamp is low, that the pines grew from the first staking — that some ancestor of theirs scored that ground and mosSED it and built, and the trees came up where the stakes were, and have stood ever since while everything around them was made and remade. They will tell you the trees remember every bell. They will tell you that on rain-days, when the whole basin blooms at once, the pines do not bloom, and do not need to.

And they will lower their voices — lower than usual, which in Sill is saying something — and say what everyone says and no one says plainly: that the basin takes a thumbprint and keeps it, keeps every print, every trail, every bell-stroke, cured in its darkening floors, and that of everything that lives here, the people may be the only ones who know it.

Then the tick resumes. Tick. Tick. Tick. And someone, somewhere, is pressing a child's hand into the evening's stone.
