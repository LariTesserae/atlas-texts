It comes around to each district a little more than once a season, hangs there for a day or two of gray pressure and warm rain, and then moves on to keep its appointment elsewhere. Everything on the plateau is tuned to its arrival. The columns put out new leaf in the Ring's wake; the field-pools fill and the mat drinks and swells; animals that have been rationing themselves all along the salary suddenly cut loose. There is a word in the local speech — untranslatable, but it means something like *ring-season*, *the fat days*, *the time when the storm passes over you* — and it is the word they use for good fortune generally, for luck, for a blessing.

Now: what lives here.

The columns themselves are the great fact, and beneath them everything else lives like animals in the lee of a cathedral. The mat is alive top to bottom — a felt of root and fungal thread and trapped mineral skin — and where it is cut or torn it bleeds a clear, sticky sap that hardens to a pale amber resin the local creatures will travel miles to chew. Whole food chains hang off that resin. There are grubs, fat and blind, that mine just under the mat's surface and are themselves the staple food of nearly everything with a beak or a pincer. There are ants — no; *something like ants* — that farm those grubs in pits and defend their pits with a coordinated drumming on the mat, using the plateau's own hum as a carrier, so that a fight between two colonies is conducted as an argument of rhythms, felt through the boot-soles, inaudible in the air.

The animals of the aisles are, almost without exception, long-legged and quiet-moving, built for distance between water. The largest is the tanka: a gray, knock-kneed grazer, taller than a man at the shoulder, with a divided upper lip and a hide so thick and grooved that dew gathers on it too; the tanka drinks its own coat. It walks the aisles in small family lines, following the shade from column to column, resting each noon in the swinging dark of a single trunk's shadow, and it has learned — this is the thing everyone who visits remembers — to sleep against the buttresses, in the lee of the overflow dribbles, so that it wakes in the pre-dawn to water already pooling at its lips.

Birds, if that is the word, nest in the flutes themselves. Small, gray, fluted-feathered things — everything here converges on the organ-pipe shape — that roost in the upper grooves where the draft is strongest. They do not sing. They never need to. They open their throats into the updraft and the column sings *for* them, each nest adding a harmonic to its trunk's note, so that a healthy column by evening carries a chord all its own, and the groves sound like distant choirs warming up. Nesting density is a sign of a trunk's health; a dead column, its draft gone, its note faded to nothing, is silent in a way that the whole mat seems to grieve, and the birds strip its crown for lining within a season.

Nothing here hurries. Nothing here is small in number except the people.

They live in the groves, not the open aisles — the loose grouped stands where three or twenty columns grow close enough to bridge with walkways. Their homes are not built but *grown*: they have learned to wound the buttress-roots in patterns that heal into chambers, curved rooms of living root-wood, dry and faintly warm and humming all night like sleeping cattle. Each chamber inherits the note of the trunk it grows from, and families are known by their rooms' pitch; a visitor can find a household in the dark by ear. The people drink at the overflows, and only there, and they have never in their history tried to tap the cisterns. This is not sentiment. It is law, and the law's reason is simple and visible: a trunk whose base is breached stops humming, and the silence spreads down the graft-lines like a struck string's dying, and the grove around it thins within a generation. They learned that once. There are districts — walkable in a day — of standing dead columns, gray, fluted, noteless, that no one cuts and no one settles near. They are the plateau's ruins, and the plateau made them itself, through the people, as a lesson.

The people are few — a few tens of thousands on a shelf you could lose a nation in — and this too is water arithmetic. The salary supports what it supports. They keep no herds except the tanka, which were never theirs to keep; the tanka simply arrive at the overflows where the people also arrive, and the relationship is old enough that the tanka tolerate a human palm on their grooved flanks, gathering dew, an inch away.

Ask them where they live and they will not name the grove. They name the chord. *The low third district. The place where the long trunk sings the seventh.* And if you ask them what the plateau is — the whole vast single body of it, mat and columns and drafts and dew — they will not say *it*, because they have no plural for it, and they will not say *who*, out of the same careful grammar. They will point at the ground, and then, apologetically, at their own chests, as if to say: the same thing, expressed twice.

And the sound goes on. Up through the hollow trunks, out into the thin bright air, forty days' walk in every direction, a chord the size of a country, holding its one long note under the Ring as it comes around again, gray and patient, keeping its appointments.
