**Who lives in Slake**

Ask the question carefully, because the town will answer it literally, and the literal answer is: almost no one, and more than you'll want to admit.

Begin with what can be counted. The amphipods, for a start — the thrown pepper of them, hanging in the lamp beams in their thousands per cubic meter, sculling their small backward errands through the perpetual snow. They are the town's true population and its census, if anything here keeps one, and they keep to their own districts the way the old residents kept to theirs: dense around the Lamp House spill, thin in the lanes near the mine mouth, absent entirely from the Throat, where the discharge column turns the water over too hard for anything that wants to think. They eat the algae felt. The algae eats the dark. Between those two facts the whole lake's economy is closed, and has been closed for longer than anyone now keeps books.

The moss in the Lamp House window is the town's only green citizen, and it behaves like one: it leans. All week, in the ghost-noon, it stands on its ribbons and strains upward toward a light no stronger than memory, and the rest of the year it holds that posture the way people hold a note. The sponges by the warm seam are the newcomers, pale organ pipes grown improbable in the discharge's heat, thriving on exactly the thing that should kill everything else. Slake has always had a family like that — the ones who do well off the industry everyone else endures.

The fish is harder. There is one — one, as far as any lamp has ever shown — white, blind or past caring, and it keeps an appointment at the window of Number Nine. Nose to the glass, hour on hour, watching a lamp that brightens and dims on a four-count rhythm like the breathing of someone asleep. Fish do not keep appointments. But nothing in Slake finishes anything, and perhaps the fish has simply been mid-errand for thirty years.

Which brings you to the residents.

Someone turns the lamps down at ghost-noon. Someone moves a lit window from house to house at each change of the count, a held lantern passing through streets no living hand tends. Someone was on the Long Walk at the change of the count, carrying something, and went into the smithy, and the doorway closed behind them — and no seals live under glaciers, and no one has come in or out by the gallery since the bell stopped. So the honest description of Slake's population is a list like this: a figure, seen once, walking with purpose; a lamp with a rhythm; a bell that hangs mute in its tower as if saving its voice; a mine whose rails are polished bright by something that runs between the fourth count and the fifth; and a pump system that should have stopped years ago and answers every accounting of its power with none.

The generous reading is that Slake's people are gone and left the town running, the way you leave a stove banked. The smithy fire breathes red; a fire needs feeding. The generous reading may be the wrong one.

The best description of who lives here is that the town itself does, and the residents are its organs. The Mill is the heart — you feel the count in your sternum before you hear it, a pulse under a wrist, patient and unaccountable. The Lamp House is one eye; Number Nine, with its slow sleeping breath, is another and a stranger one. The streets are its skeleton, pickled hard as horn, holding a final shape it will keep for a very long time. The lake is its weather and its breath, and once a year it turns over like a sleeper rolling in bed, and the town wakes wearing a different coat, and does not comment.

And under all of it — this is the part the fish knows, nose to the glass — the galleries below are still dry, the mine is still, in the strict sense, operating, for miners who are gone or who are the shape on the Long Walk. The pumps were built to keep the dark dry for workers. The work continues. Draw your own conclusion about the workers.

So: who lives in Slake. Thousands of amphipods; one fish keeping a decades-long appointment; a meadow of algae; a willow white as bone that has not fallen and, being here, will not; a green ribbon of moss that leans toward a light it sees once a year. And one more resident, unlisted, who observes noon in the dark and has never missed, who carries something along the Long Walk at the change of the count, whose breathing is the lamp in Number Nine, and who will still be here — still on schedule, still mid-shift — long after the ice thins for the last time and the grey suggestion of noon finally comes all the way down.
