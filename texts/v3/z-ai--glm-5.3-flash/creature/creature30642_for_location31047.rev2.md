## Who Lives on the Tableland

A census of the Tableland of Nine Weathers cannot be taken in an afternoon, because the residents answer at different speeds. Some will reply before you have finished asking. Some reply once per generation. And at least one has been replying continuously since before the question had a grammar — the reply is the low tone under everything, and the census-takers have long ago agreed to record it as a signature rather than a word.

Begin with the residents who will meet you at the terrace edge.

### The People of the Wet Green

The upper city is mortgaged to its moss, and the people look like it. They are broad and deliberate, layered in moss-felt and bark-cloth, faces cured rather than tanned — damp does to a face here what sun does elsewhere, slowly, kindly, without ever finishing. They move the way people move when heat is a budget: nothing wasted, no gesture that doesn't return. Their voices are low, not from reverence but from acoustics — stone absorbs everything, and shouting here is like posting a letter into a wall.

They farm. The green islands are plowed in their way, mowed, bred; the cushion-moss has a wool stage and the wool has a harvest; the glass-colored moss is cultivated as an instrument, because a leaf of it holds water like a lens and focuses the thin daylight onto fern buds that would otherwise never wake. The upper city is, among other things, an agricultural civilization that raises light the way other places raise grain.

Their food is patient: fungal loaves dense as slate, fiddleheads pickled against the years, the hard stone-fruit of the black-barked groves stored in cistern-chill, and water that always tastes faintly of the rock it condensed on — drunk warm, because cold water steals heat no one can spare. Fire is small and precious, fueled by bricks of dried moss and the dense conks of certain polypores, a kind of peat harvested from the air. The hearth is a fistful. Hospitality is measured in fistfuls.

They sleep in heaps. There is no large warm thing in the whole ecology except people, so people bank themselves like coals, family against family, and a child of the upper terraces is never fewer than two relatives deep. They invented this independently. There was nothing to copy it from. There is nothing to copy anything from here, which is why everything they do has the sealed-off originality of a world that started over.

Their machines are instruments, not engines. Wheels exist — hand-turned, wooden, gorgeous — but an old law says the wheel may turn and must not roll under load, and the wheel-turners, who are the city's astronomers, obey it without knowing why, the way everyone obeys a law whose reason has been worn off the stone. They track the weathers with carved markers and geared stone discs of astonishing correctness, and their forecasting is not folklore; it is a discipline with apprenticeships, disputes, and a literature.

Greeting is calibration. You do not say hello on the terraces; you state the weather — *"Long Damp,"* you say — and the other answers with the wheel's correction. If you get it wrong you are either a stranger or a loop. Both receive the bow.

And the bow is the whole moral technology of the place. When the world repeats — a condensation event runs twice, a neighbor passes you twice with the same words in her mouth — you stop, you bow slightly toward the stone, and you say the short formula in the dead language: *yes, we saw that; yes, it was real; please continue.* Children learn it before they learn any other sentence of the old tongue. There is a game in which they watch the terraces for repeated pedestrians and race to be first to bow correctly. There is a municipal office, the Loop-Ushers, whose officers stand in loop-prone streets and lead the courtesy the way a conductor keeps time. There is even commerce in it: a certified afternoon — *guaranteed unrepeated* — with an usher's countersign, is a genuine luxury good, and costs more than a winter of moss-wool.

The naturalists of the upper city keep one embarrassment in their ledgers. By every test they know, they themselves are animals, and the tableland's great *no* — the completeness of a world with no birds, no worms, no faces — has plainly made an exception of them. They have agreed, privately, not to run the test in public. The deep city, when told of this anxiety, laughs in its slow way and explains that the naturalists have mistranslated a clause. More on that below.

### The People of the Long Tense

Between the upper terraces and the true deep there is a hinge — a mid-city of bilingual streets where children grow up in both tongues and serve as translators, mostly of time.

Below the hinge, the dead language is not dead. The deep-city residents say that word — *dead* — is an upper-city slander, and they have a point: the language is merely spoken where the living rarely go. It is alive in the way the city is alive, and it is alive *because* of what it can say. It has tenses the living tongue lost. One means *was, and is still being remembered.* Another means *will have happened again.* It is the only language on the tableland adequate to the tableland, which may be why it refuses to die: you cannot retire the only grammar that fits.

The people of the deep city age slowly or not at all, and they do not consider this a mystery requiring solution. Consider the woman at the Seventh Landing. Her task is joint-listening: she goes along the great foundation wall with a wooden wedge and the flat of her hand, finds the joints that have begun to remember being apart, and coaxes them closed. She has been doing this for nine hundred years. She says so herself, in the flat tone of someone reporting a weather, and down here the statement checks out. Asked whether the task is endless, she looks faintly puzzled: the wall is her colleague. Colleagues keep each other on.

The deep navigate by the tone. Sight is nearly useless below, and they don't miss it — the vast low note varies by a hair from room to room, from stratum to stratum, and they read those variations the way a hand reads braille, knowing where they are by how the floor is remembering. They are, as far as anyone can tell, calm. Their gloom is legendarily shallow. When a loop takes them — and it takes them often — they bow more deeply than the upper city does and recite a longer formula, because the bow is not only courtesy. It is an edit. A bow is never twice the same, and that small difference is a signature stitched into the repetition, the thing that keeps the copy honest. The deep know this. The upper city mistranslates the third line of the formula as *please continue*; the deep insist it means something closer to *differ, please* — *go on, but slightly otherwise* — and no correction has ever been issued, because corrections, down here, loop too.

They keep the oldest knowledge. The earliest strata carry worn reliefs that the upper city calls decorative and the deep city calls *the guests* — shapes that might be birds, might be wolves, might be nothing, abraded past argument. During the Borrowed Rain the deep set out small dishes of fungal mash at these niches. Asked who the dishes are for, they give a word that translates badly. The upper renders it *guests.* The deep insist it means *the ones who could leave.*

They remember the decision. That is the first thing to understand about the deep city: the great *no* — no animals, not one — is not, to them, an absence. It is a founding law, still in force. *The roots held the vote,* they say. *The threads counted it.* Asked who moved the motion, they shrug toward the floor. And asked about the exception — the people — they give the clause the upper city's naturalists have been missing: the dead language distinguishes *the ones who could leave* from *the kept.* The stone kept the people. Whether the people were asked is a question the language has a tense for and no one uses.

### The Third Population

There are residents you will never see, only hear, and the tableland does not consider this a barrier to citizenship.

Every corridor of the deep city has its walker. One gait per passage — a halting step, a long stride, a dragging shuffle — regular as a resident, sourceless as the tone. A footstep in an empty corridor, once or twice an hour, going somewhere at its own pace. The deep have named several of them, after old words for weathers and for ancestors. The etiquette is absolute and simple: you never open the door a footstep is behind. If you must share a passage with one, you stand aside, and so far — the record is unbroken — every footstep has passed.

No one has ever seen one. Attempts at pursuit end at doorways that were walls a moment earlier. The upper city treats them as instruments: a doubled footstep-sequence is a reliable sign that a loop is near, and the Loop-Ushers post listeners in the corridors during the Second Afternoon the way other cities post lookouts for storm.

The deep city's explanation is the gentlest and the worst to shake off. The tableland remembers everything; that is its nature and its occupation. But a face is hard to remember and a rhythm is easy. The footsteps are the walkers the city loved best — not their bodies, which were never the point, but their going. *Rhythm outlasts a face,* say the deep. *The city kept what it could keep.*

### The Rooted Nations

The green is not scenery. It is the second-largest population, and it has politics.

The moss meadows are nations of small powers: the cushion-moss that grows wool and trades it; the matte-moss that builds terraces and holds them; the glass-moss, aristocratic and exact, which farms light for the ferns in exchange for shelter and position — an agriculture of lenses, tended by people who have learned to prune a leaf without breaking its focus.

The ferns keep the calendars. A fiddlehead here is a decade-long event; the coil records every season it has unrolled through, ridge upon ridge, a scroll written in its own body. Fern-readers apprentice for twenty years to read a single frond, and what they read is history — cold years, damp years, the years of the Borrowed Rain written in the coil like weather in tree rings, if trees were patient enough to be honest.

The groves speak. The black-barked trees grow audibly, one fiber at a time, and in the Grey Stillness the groves hold long slow conversations in groans that the grove-listeners translate by feel: contentment, strain, warning. A grove once groaned for three days before a loop of unusual size, and the neighborhood bowed continuously for three days, and was right to. The groves are also the quartermasters of the future: because their roots split slabs of stone the way patience splits anything, new terraces are not quarried but *planted* — a grove set where a wall is wanted, and left to do its work for four centuries. The terrace-keepers maintain queues of such trees, planted by great-grandparents to build for great-grandchildren. Stone, queued in wood. The old builders understood this too; half the city's foundations are a collaboration between hands and roots that was signed so long ago neither party remembers it as a treaty.

And the flowering is contractual. With no animals — no bees, no birds, no beetles — the plants invented their own diplomacy. Condensation droplets are couriers: paired species time their flowering to the drip-rhythm of a particular cistern, open their cups to the same tick, and send what they must send in a bead of water from one leaf to another. Each puddle-basin has its own clock and therefore its own marriage season. When the Weather of Falling Lichen arrives, whole nations of spores go up disguised among the falling lichen and ride the sky like the migrants they are not allowed to be. There is a flower that mimics a lichen flake so convincingly that the wind files it with the rest. The upper-city saying covers the constitutional arrangement: *Everything here has roots or threads. Nothing has a face.* People repeat this saying with a certain careful cheerfulness, the way guests repeat the house rules.

### The Thread-Nation

Under everything — under the islands, under the bare rock flats, under the city's oldest stratum — run the threads.

The fungi are the tableland's gut and its courier service and, if you believe the deep city, its returning officer. The archipelago of green is not an archipelago below ground: thread-roads link basin to basin, island to island, a living dark web through which water, minerals, sugar, and — the deep suspect — news are moved with an efficiency no one designed. The threads also hold the one portfolio the *no* left vacant. Appetite. With no mouths in the world above the size of a pinhead, the fungi hunt, ambush over years, war along fronts that advance millimeters a season, and keep — this is documented — herds of bacteria. They are the closest thing the tableland has to animal behavior, and they exercise it with the restraint of an institution that knows it is being watched.

The compact is scrupulous. The dead are given to the threads, in thread-gardens, in order, without favoritism; the threads return soil; the soil becomes moss; the moss becomes terrace; the terrace becomes food. In a closed ecology the only meat is eventually yourself, and the residents know it and are not morbid about it, because the accounting has never once been caught short. The deep city cites this as proof that the vote was fair. *You cannot cheat a ledger for nine thousand years,* they say, *unless the ledger is party to it.*

### The Nine

The weathers are residents too. This is not a metaphor; ask anyone.

They patrol the tableland like small countries with small agendas, and the two cities name them differently, which is the source of the only permanent diplomatic disagreement on the tableland. The upper city keeps five names on its wheels: the **Long Damp**, the season of saturation, when the moss swells until the gaps between islands close into soft causeways and you can walk anywhere on the tableland, slowly, held the way a tide-flat holds a wader — and when, the wheel-turners admit, memory slips, and the loops come thickest. The **Grey Stillness**, when the air stops entirely and the tone is loudest and the groves confer; the one weather on which both cities agree, because both cities agree on listening. The **Weather of Falling Lichen**, when the sky molts — the weathers carry lichen gardens the way clouds elsewhere carry water — and the whole upper city flies kites of bark-cloth to harvest what comes down, the only birds this sky has ever had being paper. The **Borrowed Rain**, which rains only on the oldest strata, the sky watering its own foundations; the deep city's high festival, and the occasion of the Shared Foot, when families stand straddling the rain-line with one foot in the wet and one in the dry, so that one generation is rained on and one merely witnesses. And the **Drift**, a wind so slow it does not blow but sorts, spending weeks arranging loose lichen flakes into lines that the wheel-turners read like handwriting and refuse to transcribe.

The deep city's lists overlap imperfectly. They keep names for a weather of warmth — the **Warm Hour**, once a generation, when everyone stands outside in perfect silence because the only sound the city is permitted to make, and the only melody ever sanctioned, is anything *except* the tone — and for the **Second Afternoon**, the loop-weather, when repetition multiplies, nothing may be scheduled, and the Loop-Ushers hold their high holiday.

Two further entries are disputed. The **Sharp Clear** comes once a generation: the overcast thins, the horizon-rumor almost becomes a fact, and for a few hours you can see the edge of everything. People go quiet. Some weep and blame the returning cold. Children born under it are called Clear-born and are said never to repeat themselves; there have been very few, and the current one is old, and her testimony requires no countersign, because in a place where everything loops, the person who cannot loop is the law's only anchor. The wheel-turners keep a list of forecast conceptions, trying to aim births into the next Clear, which is due — all agree — within a generation; they merely dispute whose generation.

And then there is the ninth. The upper city says it has not come yet. The deep city says it is the tone — that the ninth weather has been ongoing since before the city, and *is* the tableland's climate the way a voice is a singer's. A third opinion, held quietly, holds that the ninth weather is the departure of whatever flattened the world — that nothing that large finishes anything, and what the old records call *left* is a leaving still in progress, at the speed of stone. The two cities have declined to reconcile their lists, on the grounds that a shared list would make nine a matter of arithmetic, and nothing on the tableland consents to live by arithmetic.

### The City, Who Is Also a Resident

At some point the census must admit that the city itself is on it.

The city has no founding layer. Dig past any stratum and you find the same city, slightly lower, in the same hand, with the same knife-proof joints — a chorus with no lowest voice anyone has reached. Archaeology here has no timeline, only a chorus; and a chorus, if you think about it, is a population. Every stratum is a voice still holding its one note.

So the deep city declines the verb *built.* In the dead language there is a tense for *was, and is still being made,* and that is the tense the city lives in. The wall at the Seventh Landing was not made by hands nine hundred years ago; it is being made, continuously, by a woman's nine hundred years of attention, and when she loops and her second bow falls a little deeper than her first, that difference is one more act of construction. The cisterns drink with geometric intent. The terraces hold their breath through the Long Damp. The loops are not a malfunction in the city; they are its respiration, and the bow is the rent, paid daily, in the only currency the landlord accepts: acknowledgment.

### The Eldest Resident

And beneath the city, the floor.

Its biography is known and brief: mountain, then sea floor, then a long forgetting, then *ground.* Its occupation is memory. Its product is repetition. Its voice is the tone, which the deep city does not translate, because some names belong to their owners.

The deep hold two theories about their landlord that the upper city files under romance. First: the Borrowed Rain is the tableland dreaming of its youth — the sky watering the foundations because memory leaks, and the oldest strata are where the sea floor still remembers being sea floor. Second: the tone is the signature of the thing that flattened the world, held under everything like a syllable the floor cannot quite finish pronouncing — not a residual ring from a finished event but the sound of a departure that, at stone speed, is still under way.

The wheel-turners have measured the tone's pitch across three centuries. It is changing, by one hair. They have recorded whether it is rising or falling in the deepest archive, in the dead language, in the tense for *will have happened again,* and they have told no one, because either answer is unbearable and the archive is patient.

Here is what the long census finally shows, and why the residents keep their courtesy so scrupulously. To live on the tableland is to be remembered. Nothing decays here because nothing is dropped: the floor has never once failed to keep what it was given, and the loops are not the world breaking down but the world thinking of you, again. The moss, the groves, the threads, the weathers, the footsteps, the two cities, the walls in their unfinished tense — every resident on the ledger is held the same way, in the same memory, on the same terms.

The ledger runs: people above, mortal and hospitable; people below, old and bilingual in time; the third population, heard and unhurried; the rooted nations, in treaty among themselves; the thread-nation, keeping the books; the weathers, eight named between two tongues and one in dispute; one city, perpetually mid-construction; one floor, remembering everything. And one entry written with the heaviest ink because it is the only true zero: *the ones who could leave* — absent not as a gap but as a clause, abstaining not as a loss but as a law.

This is why every bow on the tableland is slightly different from every other bow, including the second bow to the same repeated afternoon. The repetition is the canvas. The courtesy is the variation. In a place that repeats everything, the bow is the one thing that refuses to repeat — which is why, though the afternoon has happened twice, nothing, in the entire history of the tableland, has ever happened twice the same.

*Yes, we saw that. Yes, it was real. Differ, please.*

And so far — the residents note, with the calm of people who have been checking for a very long time — it always does.
