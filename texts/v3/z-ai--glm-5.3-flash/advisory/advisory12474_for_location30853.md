**Preparation Notes for the Hollow**

**First, decide what kind of visit this will be.**

Go small. Two people, three at most. The hollow is a fist-sized town of thirty hearths running at exactly zero surplus — every sedge stalk cut is matched to a sedge stalk grown, every bead entering the pool matched by a bead leaving it. A traveler's appetite is an arithmetic problem there. Plan to be self-sufficient in food and fuel and you will be welcome; plan to live off the place and you will be a slow leak in something balanced to the drop.

Go short, the first time. A day, maybe two. The hollow is not dangerous the way a cliff is dangerous. It is dangerous the way deep water is dangerous: nothing attacks you, and the pressure works on you gradually, from a direction you weren't watching.

**Prepare your hands. This is the real preparation.**

You cannot speak there. Not "speech is discouraged" — speech is *physically absent*. Sound dies within a hand's breadth of your mouth. Your words will be air moving without an event. The dwellers talk skin to skin, tracing shapes on palms and wrists, and if you want to say anything at all — *I'm a guest, I'm cold, which way is up, thank you* — you will need your hands, bare and warm and nimble.

So: learn a touch-code before you go, and drill it until it lives in your fingers without thought. Ten signs is enough for a first visit: *come, go, water, food, cold, hurt, safe, yes, no, thank you.* Learn them as shapes traced on your own forearm with your eyes closed, because that's how you'll use them — in the dark of the silence, on someone's wrist, in the cold, possibly with numb fingers. Keep your nails short. Wash your hands before conversation; they will be touching your words to their skin, and clean hands are the equivalent there of a clear voice.

And keep your hands *warm*. You'll want heavy mittens for the cold — near the floor of what a body tolerates, and damp, and damp cold gets into everything — but mittened hands cannot talk. Solution: thin glove liners under shell mitts, and a discipline of warming your hands at every hearth you pass, the way you'd sip water on a march. A numb hand is a shut mouth.

**Prepare your body for cold that doesn't announce itself.**

Here is the trap, and I want to be plain about it: the hollow will feel *gentle*. No wind. No bite. A still, substance-like cold that has settled into its place and seems to have no quarrel with you. Meanwhile a film of moisture rides on every surface, and it transfers to you constantly — through your palms, through anything you sit on, through the seat of your trousers on a mossy ledge — and wet cold conducted through still air is the quietest killer there is. You will feel fine while your core temperature walks down a stair.

So dress for hypothermia that doesn't feel like hypothermia: wool and fleece in layers, a windproof shell (worthless against weather, priceless against the film), a hat that covers your ears, a dry base layer sealed in oilcloth for sleeping. Insulated sleeping pad — the ground is stone under all that lushness. Change your socks on a schedule. Eat constantly, warm food if you can manage it, and assign each member of your party the job of watching the others for the dullness that comes before shivering. Drink hot something at the peat fires when you're invited to them, and dry out thoroughly there, because you won't dry out anywhere else.

**What to carry.**

A day-cord — a length of cord you knot at intervals you *decide* are days, because the hollow will not decide for you. No shadows means no sun-position means no morning and no evening; the light is noon that never leans. Your body will keep time for a while, then begin to drift. Knot the cord, or wear a watch and obey it, but choose your method before you descend, because afterward you won't trust your own count.

Rations for yourself plus a margin. Water bottle — though here is a subtlety: the pool never rises and never falls because what enters leaves at exactly the rate it arrives. Drawing a cup is nothing; the spring refills a cup's worth in a blink of its slow terms. Drawing a bucket pauses the refill. Take water the way you'd take sugar from a host's bowl — by the spoonful, with the hand opened first.

A slate and slate pencil, for anything the ten signs can't carry. Fat, dense food. A small dry bag for gear that must stay beaded-free overnight (everything left out wears the film by morning).

And decide before you go whether you will count the drips.

You will hear them — one bead off the spring-lip, then another, at an interval so regular it stops being sound and becomes a fact. The dwellers don't hear it anymore. A visitor never stops. It will become your clock whether you want one or not, and some travelers have carried the count home with them and heard it in dry rooms for months. So make it a decision: *I will count the drips; it is my clock; it is mine.* Decided on purpose, it's an instrument. Arrived at by accident, it's a haunting.

**What to leave behind.**

Metal. The town has none — no metal anywhere; bone, antler, slate, birch, gut-cord — and you should honor that grammar at least while you're inside. Practical reasons agree with polite ones: the perpetual film will rust ferrous metal in a day, and an iron pick leaning at a doorway there would be a wrongness like a shout in a library. Cache your steel at the rim. Titanium and ceramic if you must carry a blade.

Seeds. Anything alive that grows. Nothing in the hollow is mid-growth — no sprouts, no rot, everything standing at its own completed fullness — and a seed is a small parcel of *later*, wrapped in time. They don't deal in later. Bring only finished things: a worn smooth slate, a carved bone needle, a folded bark box, a comb. Finished things say *I come from somewhere where things end, and I bring you one respectfully.*

Your voice, mostly. Not literally — but practice being mute at home for an evening. Notice how much of your personality runs through talking. You'll want to have found the rest of yourself beforehand.

Mirrors, if you're at all tender. Under shadowless light faces go strange — flat, undated, mask-like. And your electric lamp, if you carry one, understand what it is: in a place where no direction of light survives and no shadow has ever been cast, your lamp makes *the only shadow the hollow has ever seen.* That's not a flashlight; that's an event. Some travelers report the dwellers gathering, silent, palms open, to watch a shadow move across moss like a creature let out of a cage. Handle it like that. Once, deliberately, as a gift. Never casually.

**Crossing the lip.**

The plain above is ordinary country, scoured and bright, and the hollow is a dimple no wider than a threshing floor. You will walk past it. Everyone does. You find it by the *shadowless shade* — a softness in the glare, white on white — and by leaning over and seeing green where green has no business being. So: mark your descent point from the plain before you go down, with something that doesn't belong to the hollow itself. The lip should stay untouched; the plain can spare a cairn.

The handholds are worn to fit. Fit *them* — they're a short people's hands, the rungs closer together than your shoulders will like. Go down facing the rock, three points of contact, gloves on until the first landing. The climb out is the harder direction and you should be honest with yourself about your arms on the way down, not on the way up.

Then stop at the first ledge and wait. You will have been seen — thirty smoke-holes breathe their ghosts above the roofs, and the town reads its air the way we read a horizon. Present your open palms, flat, resting on the stone. This is the closest thing to *hello* that travels. Then do nothing, for as long as nothing takes. They are unhurried past any hurry you have a word for. If you can't sit still in silence for an hour while being looked at by a town, practice that at home too; it's cheaper than failing it there.

**The first hour is the hard one.**

Shadowless light does two things to a human and you should walk in knowing both. First, it wrecks depth perception — shadows are half of how you judge a step, and there are none, and the stairs are notches dished by other feet, and the moss to mattress-depth hides every edge. Move like a person on a ship for the first few hours. Test each footing. Seasickness is common and passes.

Second, it unanchors something in the mind. No shadow of you falls anywhere. You are lit evenly from every side, exactly as much as the stones and the ferns, and some deep animal ledger expects otherwise. Travelers have called the feeling *unwitnessed.* Don't fight it and don't feed it. Put your hand on the wall, on the moss — touch is the sense that still works honestly there — and let your eyes take the day they need.

**While you're there: a short list of manners that are actually physics.**

Work at the rate of the place or don't work. If they let you cut sedge, cut it as fast as it lengthens — no faster, even if your hands are quick and your knife is sharp, *especially* then. Your efficiency is a withdrawal from an account that has no surplus.

Don't stir or fan the peat fires. They burn steady as paint; let them. A flicker introduced by a visitor is a small irregularity in a place that has finished with irregularity.

Don't chase the white hare-things. Their grazing rotation is a map and the only calendar the town keeps, and the animal you shoo three ledges along has scribbled on it.

Don't ask *when*. Not "how old is this," not "how long have you been here," not "what time do you eat." The count doesn't move; they have no word for morning or evening; they'll offer you their word for the light itself instead — *the flat white* — and you can spend a whole good afternoon on that word if you let them trace it slowly.

And don't audit the miracle. Where the peat comes from, how the cut sedge is always exactly matched by the grown sedge, why the pool's arithmetic closes to the drop — these are questions with *time* inside them, and time doesn't live here. Ask about the moss. Ask about the hand-words. Ask, with your finger on their wrist, what the drips are to them, and then be ready for the answer to be a shrug-shaped tracing that means: *that is the well. It insists.*

**Emergencies, because the silence has teeth.**

If someone is hurt, no one can hear you shout — not ten paces away, not two. So before descending, agree on a system: a rope-line between companions on any exposed climb; a touch-tap code tapped on a wrist (*one tap: I'm fine. Two: come. Three steady: hurt, bring help*); fixed check-in intervals by your day-cord, because hours don't announce themselves either. If you must signal at distance, your lamp is the only thing that works — one deliberate shadow, waved once, is unmistakable in a world that has none.

Carry your hurt companion *up and out* rather than waiting for help to arrive inside. The handholds are the bottleneck. Know the climb with numb, shaking arms before you need to do it that way.

**Leaving, and afterward.**

Say goodbye on a wrist. Trace *I go.* Expect the farewell to take longer than you want it to — they will not hurry it, because hurrying it would mean admitting the visit had a length, and length is not one of their measures. Accept their tempo right up to the wall.

Take nothing. Not a pebble, not a curl of moss off a lintel, nothing. The place is finished, a sentence ended; you don't clip a word out of a finished sentence to wear on a chain.

And plan your decompression, because the hollow leaves residue. Expect to hear one bead, then another, in quiet rooms for weeks. Expect to flinch at your own shadow on a sunny street — not from fear; from its *suddenness*, from having lived where nothing of yours was thrown anywhere. Expect loud voices to feel like someone reading over your shoulder. Give yourself ordinary weather, sun on skin, a few loud meals among friends, and time. Some travelers go back. Some never do, and keep a pebble of ordinary gravel on their desk instead, and can't entirely explain why it comforts them.

But that's after. For now: pack wool, learn ten words for the skin, knot your day-cord, decide about the drips, cache your metal, and go down slowly, palms open, into the bright silence where the light never leans and the well keeps insisting. It is, by the standards of the places I could send you, a kind one. Prepare anyway.
