# Preparing for the Proof Garden: A Field Guide for First-Time Visitors

## What you are actually visiting

Understand this before you pack anything: you are not going to see a garden. You are going to see a garden's first years. This distinction will organize everything else.

Somewhere in the multiverse you have walked old places — forests that predate your species, rivers that remember mountains that no longer exist. This is the opposite of that. Every stone in this garden was cast while someone alive poured it. The water has not yet learned to wander. The most extraordinary thing about the Proof Garden is not that it is artificial; it is that it is *young*, and youth at this scale — a whole world one or two years into its own existence — is one of the rarest sights any traveler can visit. Most young worlds are gas clouds and lava. This one has a valved spring and a stair.

Which means: the place you will see does not exist in ten years. The seams will stain. The drains will curve. The build will vanish under the grown. Go now or accept that you missed the only version of it that will ever be.

## Before you go: the paperwork of a proof

A Proof Garden has authors. Find them. This is not a place you sneak into — and you shouldn't want to, because half the visit is the builders themselves, and because the thing it belongs to (whatever structure is growing up around it, floor by curing floor) is an active construction site fifty-plus levels above whatever counts as ground.

Ask for an invitation directly. Proof Gardens want witnesses — that is most of what a proof is for. The likely answer is yes. Bring identification from whatever bureau certifies you as a person who exists; construction sites love manifests and waivers. Sign the waiver without complaint. Ask three logistical questions and accept the answers as gospel:

1. **When does the lift run, and when is my return cage?** Arrange the return before you enter the garden. I mean this seriously. The lens-sky renders an afternoon sun that never moves. Shadows do not lengthen. Your body will lose the afternoon the way you lose time in a warm bath. Set an alarm on a watch you can see — not a phone you'll have to unfog.
2. **Is there a boot-wash or quarantine protocol?** Say yes to it joyfully. (More below — this is the ethical heart of the visit.)
3. **What does the cure schedule look like?** The chalk in the shaft — *CONCRETE CURE 60 DAYS*, *DO NOT STACK* — is the calendar the whole structure lives by. Your visit is a guest of that calendar. Don't ask them to bend it.

## Biosecurity: cleanliness is not neurosis here

This is the part travelers from old, sloppy, enormous worlds routinely get wrong, so hear it plainly: **you are a walking ecosystem of stowaways, and this garden is a nursery.**

Your boots carry soil from forty other universes. Your pack seams carry spores. Your clothing carries seeds you've never noticed. In an old forest, this is nothing — the forest eats strangers for breakfast. In a two-year-old manufactured ecosystem with a metered water budget and hand-laid loam, one aggressive mold from your home dimension could set the project back years, or permanently introduce something the builders will spend a decade evicting.

So:

- Launder everything you'll wear in unscented detergent, days before, and let it dry clean. No fabric softener, no perfume, no aerosol sunscreen, no cologne. A closed warm room is also a closed chemical room.
- Brush your pack out. Vacuum its seams if you're thorough.
- **Do not bring seeds, cuttings, soil, compost, pets, or insects.** Obviously. But also less obviously: no dried flowers in a hatband, no wooden souvenirs, no walking stick cut from a home-world forest. If you've recently come from a spore-dense world — fungal forests, peat universes, anywhere damp and old — say so, and accept whatever quarantine they propose.
- Take the offered footwear or footbath even if it feels ceremonial. It is not ceremonial. It is the whole point of the place wearing a uniform.

If this sounds like a burden: reframe it. The protocols are not obstacles to the visit. They are the visit. Washing your boots at the door of a young world is the closest thing modern travel offers to a rite.

## Packing: small pack, two climates, one shaft

The journey has three climates and you will pass through all of them in under an hour.

**The shaft** is cold, thin, dusty, and vertical. Bring a thermal layer you can strip and stuff away, a hard hat (they'll provide one; wear it — you are riding a riveted cage on a chain through an unfinished high-rise), and a bandana or respirator for the smell of setting stone and the dust of curing concrete. If your ears are sensitive, bring something to chew. Your ears will adjust twice going up, and twice again coming down. Vertigo-prone travelers: watch the chain, not the level numbers. The numbers count past fifty and some people find that the numbers are what get them.

**The garden** is wet warmth, dense air, and heat rising from below — the ground is the temperature of a sleeping flank. You will want the minimum clothing legally permitted by whatever hosts you. Quick-dry everything. You will be sweated through within ten minutes and damp for the rest of the visit; make peace with it. Skip the umbrella — but know that the lens sky drips, individually, audibly, and one may land on you. Consider that a gift.

**Your pack itself** should be small, because the garden is forty paces long and there is nowhere for a big pack to be. In it:

- A **hand lens or loupe**. Binoculars are useless here; the horizon is forty feet away. The garden rewards the opposite instrument — close looking. The formwork grain in the cast stone, the bolt lines, the first tentative moss, the drum-marks on the tumbled pebbles: this is where the garden lives.
- **A pencil and a soft-cover notebook.** Paper tolerates humidity; ink smears; screens fog and stay fogged. Pencil, soft cover, small.
- A **camera in a sealed bag.** The cold-to-warm transition will fog every lens you own. Seal it on the lift, open it inside after twenty minutes of acclimation, and accept the fogging again on exit.
- **A water bottle you filled below.** Do not plan to fill it from the spring. The spring is a valve on a budget; every liter in that garden is accounted for. If the gardeners offer you a drink, take it — young water, unmineralized, tastes of the pipes it was born in, and that taste is one of the few things you can carry out without carrying anything.
- A small towel. Trust me.

## The ascent is part of the visit

Do not treat the ride as transit. The shaft is the garden's umbilical record — read the chalk as you rise. Level 40: concrete curing, sixty days. Level 55: no stacking. These numbers are the autobiography of the world below, which is younger than the garden and still being born. The garden is the older sibling. Somewhere around the top, the air thins and goes slightly wrong — the air of a sky recently switched on — and then the double-hung door with the porthole window opens and the world finishes, warm and wet and green, all at once.

Pause at the door. Let your glasses clear. Breathe once before walking in.

## How to behave in a young world

The garden will make you feel enormous, and correctly so. It is a whole intended world folded into a chapel's footprint, and you are its giant. Move like one:

- **Stay on the paths.** The moss is being courted. A footprint on a seedbed is months of setback. The straight drainage channels are ruler-true because no water has disagreed with them yet — do not be the disagreement. Don't touch valves, spouts, or weeps.
- **The pool is a cistern, not a pond.** Ask its depth rather than testing it. Do not lean over it. Do not throw a coin in — it is infrastructure, not a wishing place, and the metal leaches into a water budget that is counted in liters. Nobody has died to make it a well. Keep it that way.
- **Ask about the sun.** Whether the lens filters UV, and whether it ever dims. If they will show you the garden's dusk — the sun being switched off, manually, for one of the first nights this world has ever had — cancel your other plans. That is the visit.
- **Don't eat inside.** Crumbs feed unplanned things. If they offer you the garden's first fruit, eat it with both hands and full attention.
- **Do not take anything.** Not a pebble. The pebbles were tumbled in a drum to look like river stones; they are not river stones yet, but they intend to be, and pocketing one is stealing from a future river. Everything in the garden is either assigned or becoming. Leave it all.

## What to do there

Forty minutes to see it. Two hours to feel it. Half a day if you can work.

The best thing you can offer a young world is **labor**. Ask to help — carry a tray of seedlings, weed a bed by hand, hold a hose. The memory will live in your shoulders, and labor is the least contaminating form of love.

Otherwise: sit at the bottom terrace, near the pool, and listen. You can hear every drip individually — that is the garden's music, and it is temporary; one day that water will be a river and will no longer keep time. Time one drip's interval. Write it down, with the date. You are taking a measurement of a world's childhood, which is the only souvenir worth having.

Then take two walks. First, find the **oldest thing** — probably the bronze spout, possibly some stone kept from elsewhere. Second, find the **youngest thing** — a stain not yet born, a moss colony days old. The garden's entire biography fits between those two points. Photograph the details, not the panorama: the panorama makes it a diorama; the bolt-lines and unstained weeps make it what it is, and in ten years your photos will be the only remaining proof that the proof was ever new. And photograph the gardeners. They are the garden's true fauna.
