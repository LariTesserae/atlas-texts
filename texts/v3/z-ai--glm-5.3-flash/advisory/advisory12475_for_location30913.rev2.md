# How to Visit the Garden-Room

*A briefing for the traveling guest, compiled from the keepers' habits and the room's own logic.*

---

## First, understand what kind of visit this is

This is not a dive site. It is a nursery three weeks old, built by patient people, kept by rules posted nowhere, and asking the sea a question nobody has asked before. Your preparation should mostly consist of **subtraction**. The room is defined by what has been kept out of it — fish, shells, bones, hunger, accident. Everything you bring that sheds, leaks, smells, or glitters becomes a permanent resident of a place that has never needed one. Prepare the way the builders worked: a little each tide, and mostly by removing things.

## Getting invited

From the boat you will see nothing but a greener oval and a gourd on a looped line. A stranger would call the gourd a lost net-float, which is exactly the point. So the first preparation is not navigational but social: **do not find the room, be shown it.** The keepers come in pairs at slack water, and a stranger descending uninvited into a sealed nursery would be a kind of trespass no apology can rinse out. Come introduced. Ask to be the third — the extra hand at the tie, the surface watch, the tally-keeper's apprentice. The keepers decide the window; ask them, not the tide table. The table will be close. The keepers will be right.

## Your body

- **Breath.** The keepers work in breaths: down, work, rise into the airwell, down again. To join this rhythm you need a comfortable two-minute hold and a calm vertical descent to ten meters or so — the floor sits below the lagoon bed, and you will feel all of it. Train for weeks, not days, along a line, equalizing every meter. If you cannot freedive comfortably, come anyway and take the surface watch; someone must keep the sky and the gourd, and it is an honorable post.
- **Your pulse.** Sit on the boat, still, two fingers on your wrist, and count your resting heart three times until you know its sound. Down there the swells arrive through the stone as a boom at almost exactly that pace. You must be able to tell which drum is yours. Travelers who skip this exercise have mistaken the sea for their own heart and panicked, or worse, mistaken their heart for the sea and been flattered.
- **Buoyancy.** The rule *never stir the bottom* is not politeness; it is architecture. The swept channel and sieved joints exist because someone will be blamed for what settles in the wrong place. Practice hovering until your fins are ornamental. Move hands-first, slowly, like someone entering a room where a child is sleeping — which, botanically, you are.
- **Ears.** Verify your sinuses against this universe's water before committing; branches differ in trivia, and your ears keep the old measurements. Water itself is oddly conservative across worlds. What varies is what lives in it, and here, nothing does.

## The clean protocol

The room's water has the glassy stillness of a place that has never been eaten in. You are the most dangerous thing that could enter it. So:

- No sunscreen, lotion, perfume, lip balm, hair product, insect repellent, glitter, nail polish. Wash that morning with plain water and nothing else; the lagoon is your rinse.
- No open wounds. A plaster dissolves into fibers and fibers become permanent sparks in the mote-field. If you bleed, the visit is over — the water keeps minutes like that for centuries, and you do not want to be the first death in the room.
- No spit, no food for hours beforehand, and mind your bladder; handle all of it on the boat, long before the gourd.
- No knife. Divers treat a blade as safety gear; here it is a banned object near the ties. Leave it aboard.
- If you menstruate, plan with a cup or tampon. This is not squeamishness. It is the entire point of the screen.
- Soak yourself and your gear thoroughly at the surface before descending so hair and fabric stop releasing silver strings of air. Those strings tick against the vault and thicken the pocket — the room's only sky, to which you do not add unasked.

## What to carry down

Almost nothing. A slate and a pencil-style scratcher of your own (the keepers' slate in the tool niche is their record, not your notepad), because underwater all speech is contraband and hand signals carry only so much awe. Whatever modest gift you bring — a hank of fiber cord, an hour of labor, a sharp eye — bring it as service, not substance. **Bring a question, not a plant.** The fourth trough is not an invitation; it is a promise, and promising is the keepers' business.

And one thing more, prepared dry and in advance: **your airwell sentence.** You will understand why shortly.

## The descent

Come up on the tide's held breath. Follow the gourd line down. Stop halfway — the depth where the vault first shows itself as a pale, sleeping-animal back — and simply look before you go closer. Hang there. Let the caustic net explain itself. The room is visible whole from its threshold, and hanging there with your fins in the doorway is not delay; it is the correct first posture of any guest.

If rooms under water frighten you, stay at the threshold on this first visit. The doorway is wide, the room is bright, and nothing about it is dark except the idea of it. Most fear here is fear of the concept. The fact is a lit nursery.

## Inside

Learn the sound lexicon before you need it, so nothing reads as danger:

- The **slow boom** in your ribs, at resting-heart pace: a swell crossing the roof. Ceaseless. It is the room breathing, not the room failing.
- The **ticks and pops**: young masonry settling, and at noon the sun-heated crown clicking against the cooler pit. The stones are talking their small news. Do not tap answers; nobody asked you.
- The **long hiss** on the flood: the sluice running, the room inhaling. When it starts, the whole volume stirs — hold your position and enjoy it.

An order of looking, from an older visitor: take the cream and the grey first. The coursed walls to their string line. The proud mortar beds, untrimmed, because nobody has come back to shave them yet — or the trimming is a pleasure being saved, in which case it is not your pleasure to take early. The chisel marks, crisp as the hour the masons left. The swept channel. The tallies on the slate, if invited to look. **The red laceweed last.** It is the only strong color in the room, and color this strong spends down if you spend it early. Save it. It will still be there, russet-crimson against all that cream, and it will hit you the way the keepers intended color to hit people.

Hands off the ties, off the stipes, off the knots. You may hold a stake steady while a keeper re-ties it, if offered, and if you are, learn the flat knot on the boat first so that your usefulness outweighs your elbows. Wiping silt off a young blade is the keepers' intimacy, not yours; watch how they do it — thumb, slow, the way you'd clean a child's face — and understand that you are watching the actual work, the real thing, and that looking is also work. Attention is part of the sweep.

A note on photography: the room is already a light instrument of considerable genius. Adding your flash to it is whistling during a bell concert. A silent camera, keeper-approved, yes; a strobe, never.

## The airwell

At the crown, the trapped lens of air — a barrel of breath older than the garden. Rise slowly into it and break the water's skin inside a stone sky: steamy, smelling of lime and kelp and hot stone, the sea's boom suddenly distant thunder.

Economics first. The pocket is small and shared. Take small sips, not gulps; sink back down before you feel greedy; do not hyperventilate, do not spit, do not stay long enough to foul it. Two keepers have been doing this arithmetic for weeks. Copy them.

Then the acoustics. A word rolls around the vault twice. A laugh is a weather event. This is why you prepared your sentence on the boat, dry, when your breath was long and your awe was slow: down in the pocket your air is short and your amazement is enormous, and you will otherwise waste it on noise. Say something short and true. And be careful — **the room repeats everything back twice. Do not say anything you are not prepared to hear doubled.** Visitors have said their own names and heard them returned like a summons. Visitors have said *thank you* and heard it come back as an echo of themselves, which is a different thing entirely, and better.

One more thing about being inside: the room can be seen whole from anywhere in itself. So can you. There is no corner in which to be awkward, no shadow in which to fidget. Arrive finished with your awkwardness, or accept that the airwell will perform it back to you, flattered and amplified.

## Timing, weather, second visits

Noon, if the keepers will swing the skylight lids on their wooden swivels — ask; do not touch them yourself — gives you the columns of falling light and the sharp pops of the warming crown. Night gives you something else entirely: moonlight down the shafts in pale threads, the caustic-net slowed to a crawl, the stones ticking as they cool. Try to come twice. The room has two personalities and you have only met the loud bright one.

Watch the sky from the boat. A storm arrives below as fast rolling applause in the chest, and the keepers will cancel without ceremony. Never lobby for ten more minutes. The tide owns the schedule here and the keepers are its translators.

If the visit is long and the window uncertain, agree in advance on a small bail-out bottle staged outside the door slab — touched by nobody, blessed by its uselessness.

## Leaving

The last job is the door slab. Help push it home. It will fit the way only things made by patient ants fit, and the sound of it settling is the room sealing the day's work. Then, if the keepers hand you the stylus, scratch your tally — small. It is not graffiti; it is the room's count of witnesses.

Then up through the warm skin of the surface, where the world will look over-exposed for a minute. Sit on the gunwale. Say nothing yet. The boom is still in your ribs, slower now than your pulse, and from this day forward you will always be able to tell the two drums apart — a skill nobody asks for and everyone who has it uses constantly.

Come back in a season. The kelp will be arm-length by then, re-tied a hundred times in careful flat knots; the fourth trough may hold an answer to a question nobody has thought of yet; the mortar may finally be getting its shave. The chisel marks will still be crisp. The room will be finished, the way it already was, and it will have just begun, the way it already had.

Take nothing. You cannot carry it anyway — what that room gives, it gives only in the chest, and you will find it beats there for a long time after.
