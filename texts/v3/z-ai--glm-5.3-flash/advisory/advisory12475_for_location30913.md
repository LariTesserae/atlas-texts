# Briefing: The House That Is Not On the Street

**First, a note on your source material.** The description you were given ends mid-sentence — *"The household that owns"* — and you should treat that truncation as data, not damage. Places that manage their own invisibility also manage what gets written about them. Either your sender was interrupted, or the household edited the file, and honestly the distinction may not matter. Prepare for a place that curates its own information. You will not be told everything. You will be told exactly enough, which is how careful households work.

Take heart in this: nothing in the description is hostile. A lime-washed wall, an old door, a hot lintel — these are the features of a home, not a fortress. The defenses here are social, not lethal. Your equipment is manners. You are an ordinary human, and that is sufficient.

---

## Reading the threshold, because it is reading you

Three clues, and each is also an instruction.

**The lime-washed wall.** Lime wash is not paint; it's a discipline. It has to be renewed — most households in hot countries redo it yearly, before some festival or at the turn of a season. So the wall tells you: *someone here is diligent, alive, and keeps a clean face for the street.* The wall is a page kept deliberately blank. It will not testify about you; do not expect it to testify about the household either. Also, practically: lime chalks off on everything. Don't wear your best black. You will leave with white dust on your sleeve and you should keep it until it wears away on its own — it's the closest thing to a receipt this place will issue.

**The teak door gone silver.** Silvered teak takes decades — the wood's oils burnishing down under sun until it looks like metal that decided to be patient. The household repaints the wall but has never painted the door. Read that: they renew what faces the public and let what faces them grow old honestly. The door is the house's true face. Now look at its wear. Generations of hands leave a ghost — a smooth patch at shoulder height, a polished hollow near the latch. Put your hand where the old hands went. On doors like this, the wear pattern is the instruction manual.

**The lintel, hot enough to warn the hand away.** This is the guard, and it doubles as a clock. Here is the diagnostic every traveler should memorize: press the back of your hand (back, not palm — the back is for listening, the palm is for grabbing, and you are not grabbing anything) to the lintel, then to the wall beside it. If both are hot, it's just the sun; the street is serious about summer and you should have brought water. But if the wall is cool and the lintel is warm — *the heat is coming from inside.* The hearth is lit. The household is home. A warm lintel with a cool wall is the multiverse's oldest do-not-disturb sign, hung in the only language a threshold can speak.

The lintel also teaches the bow. In half the worlds you'll visit, low lintels train the body to duck; here the heat does it. The house is training you before you enter. Let it. A bow costs nothing and architecture remembers courtesy.

## Finding a place that is being private, not absent

Understand the distinction: the house does not *fail* to exist from the street. It *declines*. That's a different problem with a different solution. You cannot navigate to something that is withholding itself; you can only perceive a withholding.

So: walk the street slowly, eyes soft. Central vision is told what to see; peripheral vision still catches what's true. Count the doors walking one direction, then count them walking back. If the numbers disagree, one of the counts is hiding this one, and you now know roughly where the skipped beat lives. Look for a wall that runs too long, a shadow at an angle the sun can't explain, heat-shimmer where there is no vent.

If that fails, ask — but not an adult on errands. Ask a child, or someone very old, or a dog. These are the residents who never agreed to stop seeing what's there. Offer the dog your palm before you offer the street your questions.

And carry your invitation correctly. If yours is on paper, copy it into memory and carry a small token instead — a button, a seed, handwriting on something else. Paper is read by streets. If you have no invitation, understand that your reason *is* your invitation. Hidden households receive travelers who need something the household actually has. Be honest with yourself about what you need. The lintel will check your work.

## What to carry, wear, and leave behind

**Carry:**
- **Salt**, in a cloth bag. The oldest guest-gift in the multiverse; it survives translation between physics better than almost anything.
- **Something sweet** — honey travels well, hardened sugar better. Universes disagree about much; they rarely disagree about sweetness.
- **One rehearsed story** about somewhere these people have never been. Specific, small, true. A hidden household is quietly starved of elsewhere; bring geography the way you'd bring flowers.
- **An anchor from home**: a pebble, a key, your grandmother's thimble. Not for luck — for mass. Houses that don't exist from the street sometimes don't exist *from the inside* either, in the sense that the courtyard may be larger than the lot allows. When the hallway runs long, hold the anchor. You will settle.
- **Your own remedies.** Allergy tablets, rehydration salts. A kitchen from any universe can outrun any stomach. Eat slowly; small portions first.

**Wear:** nothing that announces your origin — no logos in dead languages, no fabric that knits itself shut, no shoes that remember other shoes. Slip-offs, because you will almost certainly go barefoot, and floors like these are kept for feet. Light colors; the wall will chalk your blacks anyway. A hat — the lintel has already told you the glare here bounces like snow.

**Leave behind:**
- Cameras, recorders, anything that documents. Asking a place that erases itself from the street to become evidence is a rudeness it cannot forgive. If you must keep it, keep it in memory and call that a fair trade.
- Your schedule — not the fact of it, the *weight* of it. Anchor it outside: tell someone in your home universe when to start worrying, and mean the date.
- Weapons, if you carry them. The door will know. Better it never meets them.
- Your assumption that *hidden* means *sinister*. Most households hide for the reason anyone hides: so they can be themselves.

## The knock, and the hours after

Go when the street is empty. In hot countries, life is conducted in the margins of the day — dawn, and the blue end of evening — and a place that hides does its best work when no one is watching the margins. If you find the shutters across the street closed and the road empty, good. Absence is a kind of highlight.

On silvered teak, knock like family: three, unhurried, flat of the knuckles. Then hands still, for a hundred heartbeats. A house that doesn't exist is not empty — it is *deciding*. If a voice asks who you are, give the simplest true answer. Names get complicated across universes; needs don't. *"I was told there was a door here. I'm hoping to be let in"* will beat any pedigree you could recite.

When it opens: the dim-after-glare will hit you first. Take three breaths in the cool before you speak. The air will smell of lime, teak oil, cooking, and one flower you cannot name. Let your eyes adjust before you admire anything — praise that comes from a squint is cheap, and these people will know the difference.

Duck slightly, even if the lintel is high.

## Inside the household

Follow their clock, not yours. Meals may run long; one afternoon may hold a season. If you must be elsewhere by a certain hour of your home world, say so *at the beginning, out loud*. Households like this honor announced itineraries and resent discovered ones — being told you're leaving is a gift to them, because it lets them let you go.

Offer your hands. Shell the peas, wipe the glasses, hold the ladder steady. You will be liked for your story, but you will be *trusted* for your dish-drying. In a hidden house, usefulness is the only fluent language, and labor is the visa.

Eat what is offered — at least the first of it. Refusing a first spoonful reads as suspicion, and suspicion at a table is a long stain. If it's strange, taste it as geography.

Ask before rooms. Ask before gifts. Ask before leaving — *"May I go"* is a sentence this kind of household needs to hear, because the things that slip away unannounced are exactly how such households decide never to open again.

And if the geometry goes impossible — the courtyard bigger than the plot, the corridor bending where no corridor should — stand still and don't fight it. The rooms are the size the household needed them to be. The proportions will settle around you like water finding its level. That's what the anchor is for.

---

## Leaving well

The door may only open outward at certain hours. On arrival, in good light, wrap your palm once around the handle so your hand knows its shape — your hand should be able to find the way out even in the dark, even after the household's language has soaked into yours and you've half-forgotten your own word for *going*.

Then go visibly. Walk out facing the house, not backing down the step. On the street, the door will already be unremarkable; do not test this, do not turn to check. The house is being private again, and you were permitted a long look. Keep the lime chalk on your sleeve until it wears off by itself.

Say goodbye like it costs something. Because it does, and they will weigh it.

---

## If it never opens

Lintel hot, knock unanswered, season after season: do not camp on the pavement of a place that doesn't exist — too much attention damages the not-existing, the way touching a frost flower does. Go buy fruit from someone. Help carry a crate. Come back in another season, from another direction, with a better story.

And if it never opens at all — understand what you were given anyway. The street taught you how to see a skipped beat, and you can spend that skill at every other threshold in the multiverse for the rest of your traveling life.

---

## Last thing

You will find you were remembered there mostly by role rather than name — *the traveler* — and that this is restful in a way you'll miss afterward. You may spend years testing doorframes with the back of your hand, out of habit. That's fine. That's a small tuition paid to a small good house.

And if some later traveler asks you about a lime-washed wall, a silver teak door, a lintel that warms the hand — tell them exactly as much as you were told.

Stop mid-sentence if that's how you received it.

Some sentences are doors. They open for the next person at the right width.
