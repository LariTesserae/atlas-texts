# Preparation Brief: The Held Note

Read this the way you would read a diving table: slowly, more than once, and with the understanding that most of what can go wrong will go wrong through your own habits, not through the world. The Held Note is not hostile. Nothing in it hunts you, freezes you dramatically, or rises up in weather. Its danger is the opposite of drama: a place engineered — by hands or by ages — to admit no change, visited by an animal made almost entirely of change. You are the event. Prepare accordingly.

## 1. What kind of place this is, in operational terms

Extract the facts from the poetry, because the poetry is accurate and therefore dangerous to skip:

- **Water temperature: 1°C, everywhere, forever.** Pure water freezes at zero; this sea sits one degree above its own freezing point at every depth. There is no warm layer, no thermocline, no season. 
- **Depth: there is no shallow end.** The entire plateau — the *highest* ground on this world — stands only a few hundred meters beneath the Lid. Every point of rock you could ever touch is a deep dive.
- **The Lid: continuous ice, no openings except the wells.** There is no surface to surface at. No beach, no shore, no place to breach and breathe.
- **One tone, everywhere, phase-locked, never varying.** It is not coming from a direction. It is the acoustic properties of the sea itself plus every machine on the planet agreeing.
- **No currents, no tides, no drift, no sediment.** The sea is at rest relative to itself. What falls, stands. What you release, stays.
- **Wells: one every few hundred kilometers.** They make the warmth, the light-pillars, the oxygen, the scrubbed water, the steam above the ice — and the chord.

Notice what is *absent* from that list: anything that will come for you, and anything that will come to help you. No rescue exists here. No weather will force your hand or excuse your error. The world will neither resist you nor accommodate you; it will simply hold, at one degree, under one tone, while you succeed or fail entirely on your own preparation. That is the character of the risk, and it should shape everything you pack.

## 2. The first hard decision: you will not swim here

Every instinct from other worlds tells you to pack a wetsuit and fins. Put that instinct down. A human body in soft gear at 1°C buys minutes, not hours — and every dive on this planet is a deep dive, hundreds of meters, which means decompression on top of the cold. Wet diving the Held Note is not adventurous; it is arithmetic with a short denominator.

You go down **sealed**:

- A **one-atmosphere hard suit** (600 m class or better) for hands-on work: standing on the pillows, close study of the film and the scrapers, the gantries of the wells. This is the suit that lets you feel the rock buzzing underfoot near a well, which is one of the experiences this place exists to give.
- A **small pressurized craft** for the traverses. The gaps between wells are hundreds of kilometers. The good news: with no current anywhere, every kilometer you log is real. At two or three kilometers an hour, a well-to-well crossing is a week or more each way — so the craft is also your life support, your kitchen, and your bed for that span.

Bring both if your crossing allowance permits. If you must choose one, choose the craft; you can see everything through a viewport except the feeling of the rock, and the rock will still buzz through the hull.

Be dressed for immersion **at the moment of crossing**. If your door opens into open water, there is nothing to struggle toward — only the Lid, a few hundred meters up, and it does not open for the drowning. Your portal is your shore. Choose your arrival coordinates as your exit coordinates, and log them.

## 3. Cold: budget heat like fuel, because it is fuel

Apart from the wells, you are the warmest object on this planet. Every calorie of warmth you enjoy will be imported, generated, and defended. The sea will take heat from you the way a ledger takes ink — completely, without hurry — so plan on that ledger staying open as long as you are there.

- Hab or craft interior held at 18–20°C. Assume a continuous heat load on the order of several hundred watts against a 1°C heat sink, and carry **three times** the energy you think you need. Cold water is an infinite reservoir of patience; your fuel cell is not.
- Heated undergarments for the hard suit — two sets per suit, because one will fail. Work in preheated shifts. The first symptom of real cold is not shivering; it is clumsy hands, and the last is that you stop being concerned. Build a thermal log into your routine and have your partner enforce it.
- Do **not** plan to live in the well's warm sleeve. It is "a few degrees kinder," which is to say: lethal, with better manners. The sleeve is for pressing a glove against and marveling. Your warmth is your own.

## 4. The tone: how to live inside a sound

This deserves more preparation than any other single item, because you cannot leave it behind, plug it out, or wait it out. The fundamental sits near the bottom of hearing and probably below it — the dossier's image of a cathedral-district organ pipe puts it somewhere in the region of single-digit to low-double-digit hertz. At that frequency, earplugs are nearly ceremonial: the tone reaches you by bone conduction, through the water, through your suit, through your skeleton. Your sternum is a resonator. Your teeth will ache.

What you can actually do:

- **Wear protection anyway.** Deep-seal plugs plus muffs. The fundamental will get through regardless; the two faint overtones are what will erode your hearing over weeks, and every decibel you block of those is real. Get a baseline audiogram before you go and another when you return.
- **Build your one quiet room.** Your hab's interior should be acoustically isolated — mass, decoupled mounts, an air gap. Done properly, this will be the only *quieter* this planet has ever contained. Treat that cabin as a chapel. Keep it for sleep, for eating, for the hours you are not working. It will feel, after days in the chord, almost illegally silent.
- **Keep your hull acoustically honest on the outside.** Do not wrap the exterior in damping that stops it coupling with the sea. To the ecosystem, an object that hums along with the world is scenery. You want to be scenery.
- **Understand what active cancellation costs.** Yes, a phase-locked tone is, in principle, cancelable. But a local patch of quiet is a hole in the sound, and in this sea, holes have meaning. The herds keep their distance from anything that dims the tone. A quiet suit is predator-shaped. If you run cancellation at all, run it inside the hab only.
- **Keep your machinery off the chord's frequencies.** On arrival, take out your spectrum analyzer and write down the three frequencies — the fundamental and its two glints — because the dossier won't give them to you and the planet won't either. Mount your pumps and fans on elastomers. Emit nothing at or near those three notes. The machines here listen to each other and correct themselves against the sum of all the rest; you have no wish to be heard by that bookkeeping as a fourth voice.
- **No sonar. Ever.** In water that carries sound this faithfully, a ping is a shout across a library. Navigate by eye and by clock, which this world, unusually, makes possible (see §5 and §6).
- **Expect the first days to be poor.** Teeth, sleep, a faint nausea, a suspicion that the light is trembling — if the white noon ever seems to shiver, it is your eyes vibrating, not the light; go inside, lie down, drink water. Habituation arrives around day four or five, and after that the tone becomes furniture. Do not mistake furniture for safety; log your hearing anyway.

And learn to speak with your hands *before* you arrive. The dossier is giving you instructions: conversation here is a manual art. Use slates and hardline or tight-beam comms; an unmasked voice in this sea would carry, clearly, for kilometers. Gossip here travels like light. Don't gossip.

## 5. Light and distance: shadowless noon, and the lies it tells

The light comes from everywhere above and casts no shadows, and the water carries it unattenuated for kilometers. Both facts are beautiful and both will mislead you.

- **Distance lies.** With no motes, no haze, no attenuation, a ridge five kilometers away presents itself with the sharpness of something across the street — and things *are* five kilometers away when they look fifty meters off. Until your instincts recalibrate, trust your rangefinder over your eyes absolutely, especially when judging a well's hairline: a light-pillar visible across tens of kilometers is not "a little way off," and you do not want to learn the true scale of "a little way" against your air or heat margins.
- **There is no night to wait for and no golden hour.** Light is noon-behind-overcast, permanently. Photograph accordingly — flat, luminous, scale-less — and put an object of known size in any frame where scale matters.
- **Your lamp is a first.** Switch on a work light and you cast the first shadows these rocks have ever received. It is permitted; do it sparingly. There is something close to blasphemy in it, like striking a match in a room that has never once been dark.

## 6. Moving: you are a small silence

Because there are no currents, navigation here is the most honest in the multiverse: log your speed and time and dead reckoning will not drift, because nothing does. Rejoice in that. But the same stillness gives you an acoustic identity you must manage.

To the food web, a body moving through this uniform sea reads as an absence — a soft patch where the chord does not quite arrive, with a direction and a speed. **That is what a hunter is here. That is what you are.** The herds will hold a listening distance from you without ever being taught why.

- Move **slowly and predictably**. Steady speed, no bursts, no sudden reversals. Give the herds a boring signature.
- No bubbles: run closed-circuit, or none at all. A string of exhaust bubbles rising to spread under the Lid is an event, and the kites will come to read it. Let your wake be the one thing you contribute, and let it be faint.
- **The silence protocol, rehearsed before arrival:** if a true silence is noticed — a patch of not-quite-chord, growing, patient, jointed — *stop*. All propulsion off. Hold station. A hole that stops moving stops having a direction and a speed; the chord closes over you and it passes on its business. Do **not** flee. Fleeing is a sustained, fast hole, which is precisely the signature a silence follows. This is the exact reverse of what every other world has trained into you, which is why you drill it at home, in a pool, humming.
- **Footing is the real hazard.** Pillow basalt is glass-skinned and knife-seamed; the bacterial film is slick felt over it. Your suit's outer layer should be cut-resistant. Nothing on this planet will attack you, but the rock is young and sharp and a puncture at 1°C is a countdown. Falls, not predators, are what will nearly kill you here. Walk like a guest in a gallery of glass, which is what you are.
- Do not descend into the fissures. They glow white at their bottoms, which is the world's way of inviting you, and the bottom of a fissure is no place to be when something goes wrong.

## 7. Eating, drinking, and the permanence of crumbs

- **Import all calories.** The world's rule is explicit and you are a visitor under it: *nothing surges; a vacancy must open before anything is permitted to fill it.* You may not add a mouth to this arithmetic. Do not graze the film, do not taste a scraper, do not "sample" a herd. Bring dense, long-life food at twice your planned stay.
- **The sea is drinkable and tastes of nothing.** That is worth experiencing once, deliberately — the first mouthful of water in your life with no taste at all. But it carries no minerals, so remineralize or supplement if you drink it for more than novelty.
- **Nothing rots here.** No current will carry a crumb from where it falls. A dropped wrapper, a shed strip of tape, a lost glove — these become permanent installations on a plateau whose entire meaning is that nothing settles. Contain all your waste, sealed, and take it home. Your hab is also a biome — your skin flora, your breath — and you should keep your biology inside you: filter, sterilize, contain. You are not doing it only for the world. You are doing it because anything you shed will remain exactly where it lands, legible forever, in a place with no mud to bury it and no rain to wash it.
- The wells' outputs are not your pantry. The sheath bleeds dissolved gas for the food web; do not collect it, breathe it, or "top up" from it. Your tanks are your lungs. Their business is the planet's.

## 8. Time: you are the only clock on the planet

No day, no season, no tide, no shadow wheeling across a sundial. Under a light that never changes and inside a tone that never varies, your circadian rhythm will free-run and fray within the week unless you build a day out of nothing:

- Scheduled lighting: blue-white at a fixed "morning," amber at a fixed "evening," on a clock you obey even when nothing in the world supports it.
- Meals at hours. Exercise at hours. Melatonin if you use such things.
- A journal with dates. After a few weeks the dates will feel like foreign currency; spend them anyway. This world is *weeks old* and has been for an unmeasured span; your calendar is a foreign object here and should be, visibly, yours.

## 9. Where to stand: the half-built well

Make the interrupted well your base. The reasons are practical and then some:

- It is the only place on the planet **already marked by interruption**. Your presence there adds a footnote to a page that already holds one, rather than a fresh sentence on virgin rock.
- It has made footing — gantries, bolt heads, gray scaffolding. The only stairs this world offers.
- It stands on a rise, apart, at the Shelf's margin, and the emptiness around it is the emptiest and cleanest you will find.

Rules of the house: touch nothing that turns, unbolt nothing, salvage nothing. The bolts are bright and there is not a fleck of oxidation, and that absence of rust is a sentence reading *recently* and *always* at once. When you put your glove on that gray frame, know that you are the first hand on it since the hands that built it, and that the world has kept, at one degree, the exact point where those hands stopped.

A complete well — a pilgrimage for a later visit, a few hundred kilometers off — gives you the light-pillar, the kites, the chord become substance, the rock buzzing through your boots. Keep a hundred meters off the machinery. It is not yours and its schedule is not known.

## 10. The plan, briefly

- **Before crossing:** hearing baseline; a dentist's once-over (the hum finds every filling); cold-weather and sealed-suit drills; hand-signal vocabulary rehearsed to fluency; the silence protocol drilled.
- **Go as two.** One of you always belongs to the hab. The chord is company of a kind, but it is a machine's discipline, not a companion, and a solo traveler at 1°C under a planetary tone is conducting an experiment with no control group.
- **First visit: under a local month.** Arrive at the rise beside the half-built well — the chord will reach you before the cold does; it is already in you. Day one inside: systems, quiet schedule. Days two through four: short excursions, footing tests, calibrating your eyes against your rangefinder until your instinct relearns distance. Days five through eight: the herds. Get to a herd's coordinates, kill all drive, and listen with your eyes shut — the giants are found by ear, as a soft shading of the tone; then open your eyes and fail to see them, which is the correct response, and then see them anyway, which is the reward. Depart leaving nothing: no crumb, no strand, no scar beyond the wear of your boots on steps that were already built.
- **The optional expedition — above the Lid — is for a later, separately provisioned trip.** The air up there is thin and cold, of unknown pressure; test before baring a face. The reward is a white plain under an unmoving sun, standing smoke drawn with a ruler on every horizon, and — through your boots, faint — the chord. If it is quieter up there, understand what you are standing in: the quietest place on the planet, and it is quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

## 11. Abort conditions — decide these at home, when you are warm and reasonable

- **Suit or hull integrity alarm:** immediate return, drilled, no discussion.
- **Cold's progression** — fumbling, slurring, and worst of all the loss of concern about being cold.
- **A silence closing:** hold still per protocol. If it does not pass, withdraw on a slow, steady, boring heading.
- **Machines altering:** any machine moving, any bolt turning, the scaffolding stirring — be elsewhere. If the half-built well ever resumes building itself, leave. Whatever returns to finish that work will not have you on its manifest.
- **The chord decaying.** The dossier is clear: if the wells are ever told to stop, the tone dies over the course of minutes, and the first day finally ends. If that begins during your visit, leave at once and do not stay for the spectacle. A world ending its pause is not a show; the herds' geometries, the kites' column, the silences' cover are all arithmetic on a note that will no longer exist, and your business there was never the last day. Grant the planet its privacy for that. The second day has been waiting the whole time and it is in no hurry; it does not need an audience.

## 12. Coming home

Expect **silence debt**. For days, ordinary quiet will feel like the note's absence — you will catch yourself leaning into silences, listening for the tone to resume, the way you'd lean into a wind that has stopped. Bring home a recording of the chord and play it a minute at a time, quieter each day, until one day you forget to. Have your hearing checked. The teeth may ache for a week out of habit; let them.

And prepare for the specific grief of this place: **it will not remember you.** No sediment, no stratum, no grave — the past of that world consists only of things still standing, and you will not stand there. Within minutes of your departure the sea will close over the fact of you and the world will be exactly as it was. That is not failure. That is the compliment this place pays to anything that visits it well: the held note continues, unbroken, and the only trace of you anywhere in the universe is the one you carry out with you.

Pack so that it is a good one.
