# Preparing for the High Water Gardens

Most of what you need to prepare for this place is not equipment. Start with that. The Gardens will not hurt you quickly, will not chase you, will not test you in any way a checklist can anticipate. What they will do is hand you an enormous, warm, amber silence and then wait to see what you do inside it. Prepare accordingly: half your packing list is technical, and half is a decision about what kind of visitor you intend to be.

## First, solve the body problem

You are an ordinary human. The water is warm as bathwater, but it is still water, and it is still hundreds of meters deep over even the tallest summits — figure thirty to seventy atmospheres at the garden level, more in the drowned valleys between peaks. This rules out, permanently, the thing every diver on your home world is trained to do: *go up*. There is no up. Up is a long vertical journey through a glowing medium with no surface texture, no waves, no visible gradient, and a decompression schedule you do not want to improvise. Plan your existence there from the first hour as if the surface does not exist.

The clean solution is a rigid one-atmosphere suit, rated to double whatever depth your first survey shows, with redundant scrubbers and — this is the inversion most travelers get wrong — **cooling, not heating**. The sea will not chill you. Your own suit may cook you. Pack for the tropics you're standing in, not the depth you're at.

The best architecture, if your portal tech allows it: live *above*, visit *below*. Park a surface craft on the ocean far overhead and commute daily through the warm luminous column. You lose nothing — the descent itself becomes the ritual, the day's opening ceremony — and you gain an air-filled, wind-sounding place to sleep and think. Never sleep in open water. The current is gentle, but it is constant, and you will wake somewhere you did not choose, possibly inside a hedge.

You will be tempted, somewhere in your gear room, by some charm of artificial gills, some membrane suit, the fantasy of swimming through honey with bare skin in bathwater warmth. Do not. The spore-fuzz is ubiquitous, the biochemistry is young and unread, and the water *glows with something dissolved in it that you cannot name*. Ordinary humans do not gamble their lungs on a biosphere that has not yet grown hands. Seal yourself.

## Arrival: the first minute belongs to the water

Whatever your entry — a gate opening in the water column, a hard-suit descent from the surface craft — set your buoyancy neutral *before* you arrive, arms uncrossed, thrusters off. The first minute is not for moving. The water will take your weight the way a held breath takes a whisper. Let it.

Two things will immediately go wrong with your senses, and you should be warned by someone:

**The light is a liar with good intentions.** It reads as normal light, but it scatters strangely, pools where it gathers, and may not cast shadows the way your visual cortex expects. Your depth perception will be subtly wrong for the first half hour. Move slowly. Do not switch on lamps. Let your pupils open; the amber is faint and your eyes will find more in it than you believe. And when your light meter returns a reading that is physically impossible — and it may, because this light has no right to exist — do not recalibrate five times in a panic. Log it. Move on. This world does not owe your instruments agreement.

**There is no weather, and you are it.** No wind, no rain, no falling — and if your suit vents anything, your bubbles will be the only rising things in the range. Learn the trick early: it is also your compass. When "up" becomes uncertain, and in this pooled amber glow it will, purge a whisper of gas and follow it. Keep a short inert guide line and retrievable acoustic markers for routes — placed quietly, collected on exit. Sound carries here, and the world's silence is its one possession; a loud ping is vandalism of the atmosphere.

## Moving through: travel above the world, not across it

Everything here is upholstered, and everything upholstered here heals on geological time. A stone lasts a season before something green claims it — which means a frond you snap may not regrow before the season turns. So the cardinal rule of locomotion: **swim in the water column. Never walk the bottom. Never touch unless touched-upon.**

Route over ridgelines and plateaus rather than through valleys. The valleys are hedges now — corridors of living bone-and-rose tissue — and parting them is surgery on a world that sutured itself for a million years to make them. Even the kelp-grass plateau is not a free lawn; the blades stand upright because nothing has ever bent them, and your passage will be written there for months. Give the kelp-grass its posture. Float above it.

Watch your lines and tethers obsessively. The tethered siphons breathe the slow current and do not appreciate sharing their rigging; if you snag, cut *your* line, never the siphon. And respect the text's own warning: the rock is young, its fracture faces unrounded, its flutes skin-smooth but its edges knife-fresh. Cut-resistant everything. Inspect your suit after every outing — a slow leak in paradise is still a leak at fifty atmospheres.

Learn the palette before you need it, because the light is your map: electric green marks the basins where brightness pools; russet and wine-dark mark the tall shadowed walls. Once you can *read* the color of the water column, the drowned topography announces itself, and the ridgelines stop being identical strangers.

## The hazards that fit on one hand

- **The birth pains.** Once in a great while the seabed below vents — a low percussive thump that will roll through your bones like a struck bell. The first one will frighten you badly. After that you will wait for them like weather. But stay clear of fresh scarps and any place the rock looks newly broken; the range is still forming, and formation is not gentle at close range.
- **The fizz.** If the water around you suddenly effervesces — gas seeping from below — the density of your medium is dropping under you. Do not ascend through the column; swim out horizontally, unhurried, and look back at it from a distance. It is a miracle. Watch it from a distance.
- **The clock.** If the light here is constant — and by every account it is — your circadian rhythm will free-run within two days and you will not notice it happening. Keep artificial days in your suit and vessel. Dim your interior lights on schedule like a religious observance.
- **Phantom sound.** In near-total silence, the human brain begins composing. Expect faint chimes, distant voices, music with no source. Know them for the artifacts they are, so that the *real* sounds — the tidal hum felt in stone, the century-slow settling crack — land with the weight they deserve. Press your glove to a ridgeline once, deliberately, and let the mountain speak through your skeleton. That is worth the trip.
- **Your own breathing.** In a sealed suit, in the quietest world you will ever enter, your breath will be the loudest thing for kilometers. It will irritate you the first day. Practice slow tidal breathing before you arrive, and by the second day you will have matched it to the place, and it will feel like participation rather than noise.

## The fauna protocol, and why it matters

The animals here are rare, solitary, and — the text is careful to say this — their distance from one another *seems chosen*. Solitude is their law. You are under it too.
