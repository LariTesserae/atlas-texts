# Preparation Briefing: The Draft

## I. Before you go: understand what you are walking into

You are going to visit a world that is not finished. Sit with that sentence until it stops sounding like a flaw and starts sounding like a fact, because everything else — your packing list, your schedule, your manners, your exit strategy — has to be chosen in that light.

The Draft is not dangerous the way a cliff is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a wet plaster wall is dangerous: gently, affectionately, by accepting your handprint. The plateau's whole disposition is to finish things, slowly, on schedule, and you, arriving from a decided world, are the most decided thing for a thousand miles in every direction except up. Your risk is not that the plateau will hurt you. Your risk is that the plateau will *include* you — will begin, without malice and at its own pace, to finish you. Every rule the wardens teach is doubled: it protects the world from you, and it protects you from the world. Learn them as courtesies, because that is what they are. They are load-bearing politeness. They hold the ground up, and they hold you apart from it, so that both of you get to remain yourselves.

Say this to yourself on the trailhead, and mean it: *I am going as a guest of a process, not a spectator of a place.* You will not see anything finish. The pace is decades; your visit is days. Come to watch things *begin* — a ridgeline making up its mind, a herd resolving, a valley being subtracted somewhere to the north — and you will be richly paid. Come to see sights, finished and framed, and the gloam will disappoint you in ways you won't be able to name (and please, don't try).

## II. Choosing your window

Check the flywheel first. The great wheel turns once in sixty-two years; find out where it is in its circuit. If a Return — the completion festival — falls within your travel window, understand what you are signing up for: crowds, long silences, grown warden-stamped men and women weeping in the streets for reasons nobody can explain to them. It is the single most moving thing on the plateau, and it is not for everyone. First-timers with soft hearts and flexible calendars: consider coming anyway. You will not get a second chance at this one.

Check the Wet next. It comes roughly twice a year, when the Roof briefly fails, and it is the only sky-event this world holds. If your dates can bend to catch it, bend them. Pack one set of feast clothes — wool, something that dries gracefully — because you will be standing under eaves in your best, like everyone else, and you will not want to be indoors for it. Travelers who miss the Wet and then hear the locals talk about it describe a very specific regret.

Budget nine to fourteen days for a first visit. That is enough to acclimatize, to learn the walk, to attend the dusk stamp without flinching, and to see one thing genuinely begin. It is short enough that the plateau will still be considering you, rather than drafting you. Longer than a season and you should not come as a tourist at all; you should come as a petitioner, with different paperwork and a different heart.

Plan for a useless first hour. This is not negotiable. You will come up out of the Roof, see the pale coin of the sun, the milk-glow of the stone, the strung beads of Assay in the middle distance, the hard forge-shadows swinging their long sure arcs — and you will not be able to do anything at all except stand there. Everyone stands there. Budget the hour. Locals will give you room; they remember their own first hour.

## III. Papers, permissions, and the fine print

Register at the Ledger Hall before you do anything else, including resting. You will be logged, photographed once, and asked for your name. Give it once, on paper, with witnesses, and then let it go. After that you are *the Visitor*, *the guest*, *the one from the low world*, and you should not scatter your name around the way travelers usually do. Names are bullion here. The Hall holds them the way other cities hold gold, and they will treat yours with respect — which means you should too. Do not sign things casually. A signature is a small naming of yourself, and naming is finishing.

You will be issued a walking-permit listing which rails and causeways you may use unescorted. Tender-zone access requires a warden escort, always, no exceptions, and no amount of traveler confidence changes this. The escort is not for your safety in the way you think; it is for the ground's. You will also be asked to declare any hard-light apparatus you carry. More on that under packing.

Read your insurance policy. Most multiverse carriers class the Draft as *ontologically active terrain*, and the exclusions are worth your attention: coverage is typically void in cases of "partial commitment of the insured," "premature finalization," and — read this twice — "loss of the insured to landscape, gradual." Note also that most policies void automatically if you accept food in an errata. The underwriters know the rules. Make sure you do too.

## IV. What to pack, and what to leave

**Boots.** Broad-soled, soft-flexing, low-pressure. No heels of any kind — a heel is a chisel with your body weight behind it, and on tenderstone that makes it a geological instrument. If your favorite boots are stiff-soled and narrow, buy new ones and break them in *before* the rimwall, because you will be walking two days of switchbacks in them and then walking on ground that remembers feet.

**Clothing.** Wool and waxed canvas, in layers. It is cool and damp up there in the way of places where the air holds everything and gives back nothing. Everything you own should tolerate permanent condensation without rotting. No suede. No untreated leather. Two of every small thing you care about, because things get rearranged (see: Amendments, below).

**Pencils, not pens.** And bring a good eraser, and a spare. On the Draft the eraser is a sacred instrument — the only tool that un-thinks. Use soft pencil in a waterproof notebook. Locals will notice your eraser. This is the single best small trade good you can carry; a dozen quality erasers will open more doors than any currency.

**Light.** Frosted glass, wide throw, dim. Bring light that asks permission. A hard focused beam on the plateau is not merely rude; it is ontologically loud — it casts the only kind of shadow the ground respects, and the locals collect those shadows as relics, so a careless traveler's beam is somebody else's miracle, unearned and unlogged. If your equipment forces you to a hard light, you will file a *declaration of hard shadows cast* with the wardens, and the shadows will be collected. Yes, it's a form. Yes, they're serious.

**Nothing with your name on it.** Strip the monograms, the dog tags, the embroidered initials, the engraved flasks. Gear left in tender zones starts becoming the idea of the gear, and if your name is stitched into it, your name goes with it, into the ground, where it begins to finish. Mark your kit with color, pattern, and notches instead. This will feel backwards. Do it anyway.

**A metronome** (wound, mechanical — your electronics will misbehave here in ways that are instructive but inconvenient). For errata transit, you will want a pace you can hold without thinking, and a machine that ticks is better than a heart that wonders.

**Counting gear.** Tally sticks, counting beads, and — if you travel with children — a small bell for each child. You will understand why within a day.

**Gifts for Assay.** Erasers, as noted. Straightedges, plumb lines, fine survey chalk, good lamp oil (lamp oil is nearly currency; the bell in the Ledger Hall is timed by it). And one finished thing: a pebble from a world where everything is decided, a pressed flower, a photograph from home of something that will never change. A finished gift is an exotic treasure here. Do not bring two; one is generous, two is showing off.

**Do not bring:** engraving tools or name-stamps (licensed); hard-light lamps and lasers (declared); fast-growing seeds or plants (speed is a kind of rudeness here, and a lowland seed would rush, which is the one thing the Draft does not do); your mule (hooves concentrate weight; your mule will leave a line of dents that in fifty years is a tunnel; hire local hogs, whose digestion is the local heavy industry and whose castings are the first true sand this world has made); your dog. I know. Leave the dog with family. A dog is a small thing you love, and the rule about what you may leave overnight in tender zones is written in the imperative for a reason.

## V. The climb, and the crossing of the Roof

Take three days for the two-day climb if your schedule allows. The air thins as you rise, and somewhere in the second day it goes sweet — like the inside of a bell, they say, and they're right, and you will spend the rest of your life occasionally meeting that smell in other places and being briefly unable to speak. Lightheadedness in the sweet air is normal. Ascend slowly. The one tone the plateau cannot hear is haste; it will simply fail to schedule you, and you will find your hurry mysteriously, politely ignored until it dies of embarrassment.

Rope up with your party for the Roof crossing. Inside the Roof is a held breath — near-total humidity, low visibility, everything beading — and this is where your counting discipline begins. Count your party entering the Roof. Count them again leaving it. Reconcile *before* you move on, and if the counts disagree with each other, you do not have a math problem, you have a situation, and you stand there, all of you, until it resolves. Children who have grown up here outrank you at counting. Defer to them.

Above the Roof, when you break into the gloaming, leave your lamp holstered. Your eyes will need an hour, maybe two, to adjust to a light that never quite commits to being day. Do not cheat with a beam out of homesickness. Locals will forgive a newcomer once, quietly, but a hard light at full dark up there is like shouting in a library — except the library is made of a material that listens, and remembers, and considers.

## VI. Moving around: rails, weight, and honest miles

Distance on the Draft is not real until it has been surveyed twice. This is not a saying; it is a physics. The rails and causeways are straightedges, and a mile walked on a rail is a mile. A mile walked off-rail across the tender flats can be a mile, or a season, or a rumor of a mile that you are still walking when your return date passes without you. So: walk the rails. Off-rail travel happens only with a warden escort, and you will quickly notice the escort does three things — keeps the pace, varies the line, and carries a horn of true sand.

The sand matters. Here is a thing no guidebook told me before my first visit: on truly tender ground, your footprints persist, and they *begin*. A deep impression is a proposal to the ground, and the ground, given time, accepts proposals. A contour-walker's footprint becomes a valley that deepens for five hundred years; your boot-print will not become a valley, but it may become a dibble, a seed-spot, the start of something the ground was already thinking about. The wardens' crews counterwalk impressions and pay them back with true sand — marl-hog castings, the first finished sand this world has produced. Report any print deeper than a knuckle. The crew will sigh, the way they sigh every morning putting the amendments' work back, and they will handle it, and you will feel — rightly — that you have been gently forgiven.

So the movement discipline is: walk where many have walked; keep moving on tender flats; do not stand in one spot longer than a conversation; and if you must stop to listen at a south-facing slope at dusk (you will want to; everyone wants to; you can feel the slope thinking about being a cliff), do it on a marked listening-stone, with a warden, and step away when the warden says. Shift your weight often. Distribute it. You are, from the ground's point of view, a heavy, hot, decided thing, and courtesy means being a light one.

Twice a day, at shift change, the emptiness fills — the hog herds pouring down to the seeps, the feeder-flocks lifting off the mesh in gray spirals, the amendments running the ridgelines like sparks along a wire. It is the only fast thing on the plateau and it has absolute right-of-way. Ask your escort where the herd-roads cross your route and be off them by the change. Standing on a rail while four hundred marl-hogs conduct their twice-daily commute around you is a story you will tell forever, but it is not a story you want to *star* in.

## VII. Camping, smoke, and the law of the emptiness

Camp in licensed campfields — marked tent-pads on stamped, cured ground, set in Assay's legal, metaphysical emptiness. The emptiness is not waste; it is room, and the buildings need it, and so, it turns out, do visitors. Do not camp in a young valley (a fresh contour-walker print) even if it looks like perfect shelter, because it is not shelter, it is a *schedule*, and in a decade it is a gorge and your tent is an errata.

Do not chalk your name; do chalk your kit's outlines. A chalked outline is a proposal — *the kettle was here* — and proposals are welcome on the Draft; they are the local grammar. The Amendments will visit your camp at night anyway. They are small, quick, morally neutral thieves of geometry, and they will rearrange your cairns, your boots, your stacked pans, because they like the world redrawn. Do not chase them. Do not curse them; they are not malicious, they are *editorial*. Chalk everything, inventory every morning, and count twice.

About your fire: cook-smoke is exempt from the hearth laws; nobody expects your camp stove to raise polite smoke. But know the local doctrine — smoke that rises in its assigned shape proves a true hearth, and a true hearth will eventually finish the house around it. If you stay a week in one tent, light a real fire in a real stove one evening, and let the plateau consider your shelter. It will remain a proposal. That is correct and honorable. You are a proposal too, and the plateau knows it, and it is being very polite about it, and you should be equally polite back: no concrete, no fast-setting anything, no kiln-drying the ground beneath your pad. You cannot fast-track a world. People tried, in the old days. The errata are what that looks like.

## VIII. The wardens' rules, annotated

Memorize them verbatim before you arrive, because paraphrasing a rule on the plateau is like whistling in a church — technically possible, spiritually wrong.

**1. Name nothing hastily.** Naming is finishing. A named thing hardens, commits, stops considering alternatives. So your speech discipline is: *describe, don't define.* "The stone that is still asking." "The water that is nearly water." "The hill with two opinions." Use *so-called* the way a careful lawyer does. Do not name your tent, your mule (which you left home, good), your campfire, the local features, other travelers, or anyone's children. And — this is the one nobody warns you about — do not name what you feel about the place. Do not stand in the emptiness at dusk, feel the ground becoming true under you, and say to your companion, "This is *home*." The plateau will hear you. The plateau accepts proposals. There are people in Assay who arrived as tourists, said one sentence on their third evening, and are now in the census.

**2. Count your children coming down from the fog fields. All of them, twice.** Generalize this: count your party at every transition — through the Roof, in and out of the mesh rows, across any errata boundary the warden marks. Assign one Counter per group and let them do nothing else. Reconcile counts out loud. If you travel with children, bells on each; the fog fields are the greenest, loudest, most wonderful place on the plateau and children vanish into wonder the way the frost sublimes — completely, and by mid-morning.

**3. Do not leave anything you love overnight in the tender zones.** A tool left on a south slope will be partially become the idea of the tool by morning. The rule says *anything you love*, and it means people. Do not let a companion nap on a south slope. Do not leave a scarf your mother knitted drying on a tender stone. Inventory everything you own every morning by feel, not by sight — sight can be amended; the weight of a thing in your hand is harder to fake.

**4. Errata.** If you come to a soft spot — where the air echoes before you speak, where a hillside exists in two opinions — walk through at a steady pace, look at nothing twice, and do not accept any food. Let me expand this from hard experience. The steady pace should come from your metronome or your beads, not your nerves. Soft focus, eyes forward, head still. *Look at nothing twice* includes not looking back — a double-take is a mini-survey, and in an errata, a survey is a vote. The echo that arrives before your voice: do not answer it, do not let it finish your sentences for you. And the offered food: it will not look like a trap. It will look like a stall, a kind farmer, a companion who has trail mix and is offering. If food is offered inside an errata, you are being priced. Decline with the most important sentence you will learn on the plateau: **"I am still considering."** It declines everything — food, names, gifts, proposals, the plateau itself — gracefully and without insult. Learn it in the local tongue and say it slowly.

And the herds. You may see a herd of nine animals standing in eleven positions, resolving as you watch, and the resolution is beautiful, and you should not stay for it. This is the premier wonder of the Draft and the rule is *look away*, so prepare your willpower before you arrive, in advance, at home, where deciding is easy. Agree on a code-word with your group that means *turn my shoulders for me*. Appoint a rear-guard whose whole job is physically turning people. Everyone who has visited wants to describe the resolving herd. Nobody who stayed to watch it can describe it. Think about what that sentence means, and then don't test it.

**If you brush the errata anyway** — symptoms: your echo arriving early, tasting your own words, seeing your companion slightly to the left of where they sound — stop. Take out the cured-stone token the wardens issue you at registration. Hold it in your bare hand. Eat nothing. Breathe. Wait for the ground's second opinion to go away. If it doesn't go, find a warden. They are the plateau's medics, they have seen this, they will give you broth and sleep and, if needed, a light ceremonial stamping, and you will be fine, and you will be *slightly more definite* afterward, which is either the cure or the disease depending on how long you plan to stay.

## IX. A word on what you are

Here is the thing the briefing-writers dance around, so I will say it plainly.

The stamp-wardens who went unfinished in the old way — the translucent hands, the faces that photograph differently each year — are the plateau's most trusted citizens, and *it takes one to know one*. And you, multiverse traveler, walking around with your edges full of alternatives, your loyalties divided between worlds, your self not committed to any single geography — you are, from the plateau's point of view, unfinished in the old way. The wardens will see it immediately. They will treat you as kin. Accept this graciously; it is your passport. But understand it is also your file: the most trusted citizens are the most closely watched, and rightly, because they know best what the plateau does to things it loves.

Everything on the Draft is offered finishing, eventually, on schedule. Including you. The plateau is patient with everything except haste, and it has all the time there is.

So your anchors, your daily practice, your small stubborn rituals:

- **The return date.** Write it before you leave home, in your home ink, on your home paper, and carry it. A date set *on* the plateau may not finish; yours might stay tentative, might keep considering. Anchor it in a decided world.
- **The unnamed token.** Carry one thing from home whose meaning you have deliberately never settled — a ticket stub, a key to a door that may or may not be yours anymore — and never name it aloud, never decide it, all the time you are there. It is your ballast of ambiguity. When the plateau checks your edges, it finds one thing still open, and it moves along politely.
- **The edge-check.** Each evening, look at your handwriting — is it still yours? Look at your shadow — still soft? Recall one open question from home, deliberately, and confirm it still has both possibilities inside it. The night one of your open questions closes *by itself*, gently, without your consent — that is the plateau working on you. Tell your escort. This is what they are for. There is a longer version of this advice, and you do not want to need it.

## X. Eating, drinking, and the teeth

Drink mesh-water and terrace-water — the fog-harvest drip, caught, settled, counted. Seep-water from the young seams is clean and cold and fine in small amounts, but a steady diet of water that is still becoming water, and you will start leaving soft prints of your own. Eat ledgered food in Assay, from stalls on the causeways, with provenance you can ask about twice. Meat from the herds is safe *outside* errata — those herds are singular and counted — but meat has to be provenance-meat; never eat anything that offered itself, and never eat anything growing directly from a tender slope, because a slope may grow food in order to get eaten in order to get decided, and you do not want to be the decision.

Practicalities: the damp gets into everything; dry your feet nightly, ruthlessly; foot powder is worth its weight in cured sand. The great stamp at dusk comes up through the ground and arrives in your molars — earplugs are useless against it, so don't bother; if your jaw aches by day three, chew a little wax at night and stop clenching. Let the stamp into your chest the first few evenings. It is a single deep note that takes minutes to die, and locals keep working through it, but you are allowed to stand still for it, and they will find it charming that you do. Do not try to hear the end of the Ledger Hall bell. You will fail; everyone fails; the decay is measured in lamp-oil. Give up by day three or the failure will start to feel like a personal debt.

One more: the drone. The works' drone never stops; you feel it before you identify it; and by the end of your visit you will not be able to sleep without it. This reverses at home in about a week — the week, ironically, when you start missing it.

## XI. Money, gifts, and what you may carry home

Currency is stamped tokens of cured stone and lamp-oil scrip; count your change aloud, twice, and merchants will respect you for it. Barter goods as listed: erasers above all, straightedges, plumb lines, chalk, lamp oil. Tipping is done in lamp oil, lightly, the way the wardens are stamped.

Souvenirs, in order of perfection: **certified true sand** — marl-hog castings, the first true sand this world has ever made, each batch stamped and counted; it is literally a souvenir of a world *deciding to have a ground*, and it is the best gift you will ever bring home to someone who builds things. **A boxed hard shadow** — regulated, stamped, expensive, and the most memorable object you will ever own: a shadow with edges, from a world where nothing else has any. Cured-stone trinkets. Mesh-woven cloth from the terraces. A dwarf-alder carving under permit — wood a century old from a tree knee-high, which is a sentence you will be explaining at dinner parties for the rest of your life.

What will be confiscated at the rim: uncured tenderstone (every rock up there is a proposal; do not pocket "a rock"), uncounted animals, anything whose second survey hasn't happened, and — they will be kind about it, but firm — any photograph still in the act of deciding.

## XII. Cameras, clocks, and other liars

Leave the camera in the bag inside errata — the camera is a reason to stay, and a second look with a glass eye. Everywhere else, photograph freely, but understand: the Draft does not photograph truly. Photos of tender zones will come home soft, half-committed, and some of them will *continue developing for years*, the print slowly deciding what it saw. Label and date them. Never cite them as evidence of anything. One portrait per year of a stamp-warden, with permission, is a courtesy; a burst of frames is a survey, and surveying a warden without consent is a serious discourtesy from someone they were beginning to think of as unfinished kin.

Your instruments will lie the way the ground lies: gently, and from a point of view. Altimeters will disagree with themselves and both readings will be true — the ridgelines are unsure of their own height. Compasses wander where north hasn't decided. GPS-class devices will produce laughter, if devices can. Trust the rails, the beacons, the wardens, and your own counted feet. Do keep a plain paper log, in pencil — the Ledger Hall prizes traveler records because you see from outside the survey lines, and gifting them a copy is a gesture they will repay in lamp oil, or, if they are very taken with you, in a *name*.

If they offer you a name, decide before your trip — now, tonight, at home — whether you want one. A name given by the Ledger Hall is a beautiful, binding thing. Travelers who accept describe the journey home as slower, more deliberate, more *final*. If you are the kind of traveler with a home to keep returning to, decline gracefully, with the sentence you already know.

## XIII. Etiquette in Assay

Patience is the local courtesy; nothing here is rushed, and treating slowness as a favor being done you — rather than an obstacle to you — is the whole social compact. Never ask when anything will be done. Not houses, not valleys, not anyone's projects, not anyone's children. It is the local equivalent of asking a stranger when the baby is due: occasionally technically answerable, always intrusive. Praise provisionally for unfinished things — *it's sitting well*, *it's taking its time beautifully* — and definitively only for finished things: the engines, a true hearth, a finished wall. Compliment the watch family on the rim in the traditional formula if you pass their crack — *four generations, and a proud finger's width* — and do not touch the crack. It is their crack. It is the only thing in the world they are minding.

Do not suggest the city could be denser. Do not hum a fixed, absolute-pitch tune near the Organ Fissures unless invited; the fissures are instruments, and playing someone's instrument uninvited is rude — but if invited, sing something you are willing to have retuned, because the fissure will keep your song and change its key for a hundred years, and you should choose your gift accordingly. Never step into the long arc of a forge-shadow at dusk, and never block one. And if the fringe-feeders hanging under a causeway hum in their sleep as you pass, it is acceptable, and I would even say correct, to hum back, quietly, in whatever key you have.

## XIV. Leaving, and the week after

The climb down is two days, and the sweet thin air thickens as you drop, and the sweetness goes out of it like a note ending. Somewhere on the switchbacks you will pass back through the Roof — count your party in, count them out, reconcile — and then the lowlands will hit you like a slab of decided matter. Everything finished. Everything shouting its conclusions. People at the transit station committing to things, in full sentences, *aloud*. You will find yourself touching furniture to see if it dents. It doesn't. Let it be. The lowlands will feel overdecided, garish with finality, for about a week, and then you will be home.

Check your edges against a mirror for a month — gently, without paranoia; if your reflection starts proposing alternatives, that is not a style choice, go back and see the wardens. Your photographs may still be developing; date them and let them. And at some point, usually at dusk, usually after the hour when a stamp would fall if the world you were in had one, you will miss it — the drone, the gloam, the groan under everything, the ridgeline making up its mind a finger's width at a time. Most visitors come home lighter and stranger and kinder to their own unfinished projects. Some come home later than they said they would. The difference, as far as I can tell, is whether they wrote the return date in home ink, and whether, on some third evening, standing in the emptiness between districts with the ground becoming true under their feet, they said one sentence too many.

Say nothing. Stand still. Watch a shore decide. Go home on schedule, still considering.

## XV. The Pocket Card

Copy this by hand, in pencil, and carry it:

1. Name nothing hastily. I am still considering.
2. Count everyone, at every threshold, twice. Children outrank me at counting.
3. Nothing I love sleeps in the tender zones. Neither do I, unanchored.
4. Errata: steady pace, soft eyes, nothing twice, no food. The resolving herd is beautiful, and I do not stay for it.
5. My name was given once, at the Hall, with witnesses.
6. Pencil, never pen. The eraser is sacred.
7. Soft light only. Declare my hard shadows.
8. The stamp comes through the ground; let it into my chest.
9. The return date is written in home ink.
10. The ground under me is becoming true, a finger's width at a time — and so, gently, am I.

Go well. Go slowly. It is a world's morning, and you have been invited to stand quietly at the back of it, near the door, with your edges open.
