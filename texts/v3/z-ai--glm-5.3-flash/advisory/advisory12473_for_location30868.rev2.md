# Preparing for the Cloudstead: A Traveler's Briefing

## First, adjust your sense of what "old" means

Everything there is younger than you. Not the buildings — everything. The stone, the soil, the weather, the light, the calendar. The oldest document on the plateau is a chalkboard, and it is thirty-four days old. The oldest object is a marker stone that says *Day One*.

If you come from worlds of strata, ruins, deep time, places where the ground remembers ice ages — this will do something to you that you cannot fully predict. Some travelers report a low, unplaceable grief in places with no past, as if they'd brought their own history and it had nowhere to set down. Others find it euphoric: a world with no ruins is a world with no guilt. You will not know which traveler you are until you're standing on warm stone that is still deciding its shape. Plan accordingly: book a short first stay. You can extend it by asking; leaving early is understood here, because everything here is new enough that no one pretends to know how visits go.

## Pack for a world where nothing ever dries

The permanent condition is saturation. The air itself is always condensing; the rain comes warm as bathwater on a schedule nobody controls; everything — rail, leaf, cable, window — beads with water. You will never be fully dry during your stay. Accept this now, on the plane of preparation, and it will not defeat you there.

So:

- **Three of everything wearable.** Wool and quick-dry synthetics; nothing heavy cotton, which will stay wet against your skin for days and quietly ruin you. Socks especially. Rotate them; dry the pair you're not wearing against the warm stone seams near the pump-house — the floor radiates heat like the middle of a loaf, and it's the only gentle drying surface the place offers.
- **Boots with real grip.** The floor is cast manufactured stone, seamed with pitch, and the moss has found every joint. Wet moss on smooth stone is the local way of falling. Tread matters more than waterproofing; you cannot keep the water out, only keep your feet under you.
- **A brim hat and eye protection for the noon-open.** One hour a day the cloud thins and true sunlight comes straight down, and the entire plateau glitters — every drip lit, every mesh screen burning silver. It is the most beautiful hour and it will strike you blind if you're not ready for it.
- **Antifungal cream. Use it nightly.** Constant wet warmth is a foot's enemy. This is not a suggestion; it is the difference between a good trip and a hobbling one.
- **A waterproof notebook and a pencil, not a pen.** Ink and humidity have opinions. Pencil survives being rained on.
- **Desiccant, in quantity, in a sealed box, rotated daily.** Your optics and electronics will fog and corrode otherwise. Honestly: consider leaving electronics behind. The place defeats them. A hand lens and a notebook serve better.

One more thing about the water: it is condensed cloud, and cloud has never passed through earth. It is the softest water you will ever drink — no minerals, no taste, almost nothing. Some travelers find it delicious; some find it faintly unnerving, water with no history. Bring tea. Good tea, the kind you'd save. The steam economy means hot water is available, and soft water makes extraordinary tea. It is also an excellent gift, and I'll come back to gifts.

## The rail is not a suggestion

Past the last rail, the built world ends and the raw world begins, and the raw world is *unfinished*. New stone out there rises in steaming domes, glassy in patches, ticking as it cools. A crack measured last week is a fold this week. Survey stakes stand in it with dates chalked on them — those stakes are someone's handwriting, someone's month of careful watching. Do not step past the rail. Not because you're clumsy, but because the ground itself has not finished being ground. Fresh glassy stone can be razor-edged and still warm. If you are the sort of traveler who carries instruments, ask before staking anything; the keepers redraw their map monthly and will not appreciate a stranger's bolt in a mountain that is mid-thought.

Similarly: the pump-engine is the heart of the place and it is quiet on purpose. It does not roar; it breathes. Do not tamper, do not test, do not "just take a quick reading." If you are an engineer, your curiosity is welcome — as questions, asked of people, not experiments, run on their machine.

## Learn to whisper

There is a rule, posted and also simply understood, that you keep your voice down near the young stone. Nobody knows whether noise genuinely startles rock while it sets. Everyone whispers anyway. This is the local culture in a sentence: behave as if the world can hear you, because it is young and might.

Understand what this means practically. Sound carries enormously across a landscape built for quiet. Your footsteps will be audible a long way. There is nowhere to be anonymous — the whole plateau takes twenty minutes to walk and can be seen from any point on it. You will be heard arriving, heard sleeping-turning, heard leaving. Come comfortable with being audible. The crew will be just as audible; that's the point; you'll all be part of the same quiet.

Bring filtered earplugs anyway — not for noise but for irregularity. The sound of the place is a floor, not an event: arrhythmic drips from a hundred leaf-tips, the pump's slow breath, the tick of cooling stone, the swifts. The swifts are *outrageously* loud, and they are precious — two arrived uninvited, they nest in machinery younger than they are, and they are the only wild voice on the plateau. Do not shush them. If you are a sleeper who needs regularity, the drip will fight you for a night or two. The earplugs, paradoxically, are how you earn the right to listen to it.

And about that lamp: on still evenings, far out in the cloud, a light blinks. Someone else's platform, perhaps. No lamp here answers it, and this is a decision the keepers have made — you'll feel the weight of it if you're there at night, everyone does. Do not answer it. Do not rig a signal. Do not ask about it more than once. It is theirs.

## Biology runs in both directions, and this is the one law with teeth

These people distributed forty worms by hand and know where each one was released. There is one fish, in one tank. The fauna can be counted, which means every living thing is known, which means every living thing is precious, which means the cardinal sin of this place is uninvited biology.

So: scrub your boots, your cuffs, your pack seams, your gear straps before you arrive. You are a multiverse traveler; you may be carrying spores from worlds that would find a first-generation biome delicious, and a biome of morning has no defenses and no history of restraint. Expect a rinse on arrival and make it easy — bring gear that scrubs clean, declare anything organic, and if you carry a favorite houseplant cutting or a vial of soil from home, leave it home. Seeds as gifts are not gifts here. They are weather.

Going the other way: you will want a cutting. Everyone does. The vines grow a hand-span a day, the keepers trim every morning, and the temptation to carry a piece of botanical morning out into the multiverse is enormous. Ask. Ask before touching the tagged fruit on the south deck — the tag with a date on it is a document, the closest thing this place has to a deed. A cutting given freely is a treasure; a cutting taken is a theft from a garden that hasn't finished existing.

For gifts, bring what they use: **chalk** — their entire culture runs on chalk calendars and chalked dates, and good chalk is currency of the heart here. Brass fittings, good twine, paper for tags, a hand lens for the survey crew. And tea, as I said. Inert, useful, documented.

## Learn the weathers before you need them

The day has a shape, and the shape has names once you've been there a day:

- **The mist front**, gray morning: a single long breath of wind and then visibility collapses. Hold the rails. Do not wander. The boundaries everywhere — rails, lips, gutters, the pale strut-fence — were placed by people who know the ground is provisional, and during a mist front you will want every one of them.
- **Warm drizzle**: an hour of it. Useful hours; run your errands, talk with the crew.
- **The noon-open**: one fierce hour of true sun, straight down, everything glittering, steam visibly rising off the young stone. Spend it at the rail. Drink water — saturation impairs your cooling and you'll sweat without noticing it evaporates. This is also when the light can sear; hat on.
- **The drip-hours**: the default condition. This is when the place is itself. Do nothing at least once during a drip-hour. Stand at the rail. Twenty minutes, no errand. Let the sound-floor become background and then notice you cannot un-hear it.
- **The pollination fog**, dawn, twice a week: gold mist along the upper terraces, the whole plateau briefly sweet and amber-lit. Manufactured weather, and the single most beautiful thing you will see there. If your lungs are sensitive, mask up or stay in the pump-house that hour — the dust touches every flower, open and closed. Everyone else: dawn, fog day, be there. Plan your whole trip around seeing at least one.

## Timekeeping is the local religion — convert before you land

Learn the Day count before you arrive. (By my reckoning, tomorrow is Day Thirty-Five.) Use the number. Say "Day Thirty-Six," not "the day after tomorrow." The keepers will chalk your arrival onto a board; offer your departure date too, and put it up yourself — partly out of courtesy, partly because a place this gentle can dissolve you, and a departure date on a public chalkboard is a traveler's best insurance against never leaving. There is a condition, informal, that long-stay travelers get: a hunger to witness every first time — first brood, first harvest, first hundred. It's real. The chalk date is your antidote.

If you can time your visit to attend the chalk calendar's morning update — the oldest document on the plateau gaining a new line — do. It is the closest thing the place has to a liturgy, and it lasts about a minute.

## What it will do to you, so you're not surprised

- **No horizons.** Nothing here has ever seen one, and neither, from the rail, will you. Depth perception gives up; the cloud below is the bright underside of cloud and looks deceptively solid. Some travelers get a white-vertigo at the edge. Hold the rail and let your eyes adjust slowly; look at the cloud underside the way you'd look at deep water, not down a cliff.
- **Total visibility of the whole.** From any point you can see nearly everything, which is only possible because the project is small. People from big worlds find this unexpectedly moving — an entire civilization you can see at a glance, garden outrunning its own mountain. Don't photograph it first. Look first. Cameras take one thing at a time, and this place is explicitly not one thing at a time — it's rail, vine, mist; pipe, fern, drip, all at once, three layers deep everywhere. A photo will flatten it into a picture of a garden. Your memory won't.
- **The hum.** The pump breathes and you feel it in your molars more than your ears. For two days it will be noticeable; after that it becomes your pulse, and leaving is when you notice it's gone.
- **The drips come home with you.** Travelers reliably report phantom drips — arrhythmic, patient — haunting dry rooms for a week after departure. This is not a malfunction. Consider it the place's parting gift, and let it keep going.

## The right spirit to arrive in

Do not call it small, and do not call it quaint, and do not — under any circumstance — call it empty. It is young, and young is not a lesser form of old; it is a form old worlds have entirely forgotten how to be. The correct posture here is witness, not judge. Praise specifically, not generally: ask about the mist-combs, ask how they set the brass collars where the vines may cross, ask where each worm went. Curiosity flatters a place of beginnings; awe just makes the keepers glance at their boots.

Best of all, be useful quietly. Offer an hour of vine-trimming. The watering schedule is chalked on a board; ask if you may carry water under it. In a place where footsteps are audible for a long way, the sound of a visitor's hands working alongside the crew's is the truest visa there is.

You will arrive — coordinate the arrival window through the keepers' protocol before you set out, and aim for a drip-hour or the drizzle, not a mist front — rinsed, counted, chalked onto a board, whispered to. Walk the twenty minutes once, slowly. Touch nothing with a tag. At the rail, in a drip-hour, look down at the bright underside of cloud, then at the small hard green fruit with its dated tag, then at the survey stakes chalked with days.

Everything there is a seedling — the ground, the light, the time. You are, for the length of your stay, one of the older things on the plateau, and that is exactly the right thing to be: not a marvel, just someone old enough to know how rare a morning is, standing quietly in one.
