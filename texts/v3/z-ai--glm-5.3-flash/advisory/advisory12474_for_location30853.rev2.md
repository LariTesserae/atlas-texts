# Preparation Notes for the Cup

*Being a briefing for the ordinary traveler — the kind with one heartbeat, two hands, and a head full of noise.*

Before you pack anything, understand what you are actually preparing for. It is not cold, though there is cold. It is not distance, though the plain will try to make you walk forever. It is this: you are about to visit the quietest inhabited place you will ever stand in — a town that has traded its voice for permanence — and almost none of your preparation will be gear. Most of it is the recalibration of an animal that has never once, from the moment of birth, been without sound.

The crossing itself, whatever method you use between worlds, is the easy part. The Cup begins working on you at the tenth step of a stair.

---

## 1. First, decide whether you should go

Screen yourself honestly. The Cup does not attack visitors. It simply removes the world's masking and hands you back to yourself.

If you carry tinnitus, hear this now: there, it will be the only sound in town that belongs to you — unswallowed, unrepeated, and unheard by anyone, because no one can hear anything. If silence makes you panic — the real panic, the kind documented in anechoic chambers, where people hear their own hearts and flee within minutes — know that the Cup is that, worse and better. Worse, because it is not an empty room; it is a listening town. Better, because it has weight and warmth and smell and moths and a purpose to its stillness.

Some people should not go. Some people *must*. If you are noise-sick, grief-stiffened, overstimulated by the loud worlds, the Cup may be the kindest place you will ever visit — with one caveat: the Cup keeps what it is given. It will keep your grief too. It does not dissolve things. It mosses them over, at about a lifetime's pace.

Decide the length of stay in advance. The tinker lasted three days, and his bell took all three to be forgiven. Treat three days as a respectable first visit. A lifetime is a different application entirely.

And go alone. Two travelers are a small loud island of hands — always gesturing, always a knot the town must part around. One guest is absorbable. One guest is a guest.

---

## 2. Training, weeks before you leave

**Practice silence.** Find the quietest room available to you and sit in it for hours. Learn what your skull sounds like: the heartbeat, the blood, the joints, the ringing-or-not-ringing. The Cup will hand you this soundtrack at full volume on step ten of the weather stair. Meet it first in a place you can leave.

**Learn to speak with your hands.** Their language is mostly hands — and here is the strange preparatory truth: *you cannot study it in advance*, because it has no recordings. A language with no audio cannot be exported. Bring fluency in whatever hand-speech you know; it will be wrong in every particular and right in every principle. Learn the principles: slow, clear, deliberate, hands in the light, face toward the person.

(If you are deaf, you may be the most qualified visitor the Cup receives in a generation. The rest of us are learning to visit your country.)

**Learn quiet movement.** You will be a large, visually loud animal among people whose whole lives are gesture. Fidgeting reads as speech there. Practice stillness the way you'd practice a language.

**Practice damp cold.** Sleep out in wet cold in your intended kit before you go. The fog is a blanket, but you are not.

---

## 3. What to carry

The governing physics: *the Cup keeps everything it is given, in matter.* Your sheddings — hair, skin, breath, boot-prints — stay whether you like it or not; the elders will one day know which patch of green you are. Your possessions leave only if they leave on your back. From these two facts, everything else follows.

**Everything must be capable of silence.** Test each item by shaking it beside your open ear. Buckles, straps, pot handles, zips — felt them, wax them, or leave them behind. Anything that can ring, tick, click, or rustle *will* do it at the worst possible moment, and the town will spend days slowly forgiving it. Secure your kit against accidental noise the way you would secure a blade.

**No watch.** A ticking watch in the Cup is either a private metronome eating your sanity from inside your own hand-span, or — set down on a shelf — a second tinker's bell: a small condemned noise being slowly pardoned. The Cup will tell you time by smells and birds. Let it.

**No recording equipment.** Field recorders, parabolic microphones, phones: useless. There is almost nothing to record. Your machine will come home holding the only sound it could catch — its own hiss. The Cup's entire audio archive lives in skulls. Leave the dead weight, and more importantly, leave the temptation.

**No stove, no lamp, no candle.** One hearth is permitted in that town. It is already lit. It is not yours. Warmth is wool and stillness; light is the pearl-dark, which is enough. Bring a plain cloth eye-mask if you need blackness to sleep — and know that you'll be trading away the moths.

**Clothing:** felted wool, layered, muted, no crinkle-fabrics (you will hear your own jacket rustle for the rest of your life, and no one else ever will). Spare socks matter more than you think; fog-softened feet fail travelers. Footwear with quiet soles and real grip, because the stairs are stone worn glassy-smooth and slicked with fog every night. Consider felt overshoes for the town itself — partly for traction, partly courtesy: *your soles are pens.* The tinker's boot-prints stayed on the High Stair for years before the moss closed over them. Yours will too. Decide how heavy you want your handwriting to be.

**Food:** dense, silent, wrapper-free. Nut paste, hard cheese, dried fruit in a cloth roll. You will do all your chewing inside your own skull — the only diner who hears you is you — but crumbs and wrappers are matter, and matter stays. Pack out what you pack in, and accept that your body's exports are beyond your control.

**Water bottle: carry it empty.** Water in the Cup is counted by the drop. The fog conducts the census; there is exactly enough for exactly the people who are there. Imported water is either pointless (surplus weeps away through the stone by evening) or theft (someone's counted cups). You will be counted and rationed. Empty bottle, full patience.

**Medicine:** if your body runs on a schedule, declare it on the first day, in hands, and work out the water arithmetic with the elders *before* you need it. Do not improvise at the eye-pool.

**Pen and paper.** The one machine worth bringing. It is your only echo.

**For the plain above:** storm shelter, real insulation, sunglasses against the flat white glare, and a staff — wrapped in felt at both ends so it cannot tap — for probing the gravel, and for finding a seam in the ground before it finds your ankle. The rim of the Cup is flush with the plain. In night fog, a misstep off the lip drops you onto someone's roof garden. Probe.

One more item, invisible: **decide in advance what illness means to you there.** If you fall sick, you become a mouth on counted water and hands that cannot work. Know your threshold for climbing back out, before you are feverish and cannot choose clearly.

---

## 4. Finding the door

The plain is the dangerous half of this journey. It is featureless, wind-scoured, glare-white; you could walk it for years and cross the Cup without knowing. So:

**Never navigate by hope. Use the signs.** At dusk: the lantern-birds coming home, low and purposeful, full-cropped — walk beneath their line and you will end at the rim, because the birds always find it. In the bright hours: the green haze hanging over a bowl that isn't visibly there. At night: the one place on the whole plain where the fog stops sliding past, pools, and pours itself over a lip and sinks — the only spot where the fog is going *somewhere*.

**And the nose.** The Cup is loud with scent — crushed cress, wet chalk, old smoke, deep moss. With the wind behind you it will find you before you find it. Cold air kills odor, so trust your nose in the milder hours and your eyes at the bright hour.

**Do not sleep on the open plain in the fog.** The night banks are wet, slow, and freezing; they will strip the heat out of you while you lie there politely. Bivouac low and sheltered, or time your walk so you arrive at dusk and go down with the birds.

**Use the weather stair and nothing else.** It is a slot in the white rim, cut narrow. Everywhere else, the rim is people's roofs. A visitor who lowers himself into someone's garden is remembered in wear.

---

## 5. Going down

Descend in daylight if you can, once the night's fog has thinned. The stair is narrow and slick — the fog slides down it like a slow waterfall of milk. One hand on the stone. Slowly.

You need no rope, no harness, no climbing skill. The bowl is only a few tall men deep. *The depth is acoustic, not vertical.*

What happens on the stairs has been reported the same way by everyone who has done it: the wind's noise thins, your boots stop reporting to you around the fifth step, and by the tenth you are inside the quiet, and the quiet has weight — a pressure on the eardrums like deep water. Yawn, swallow, equalize. It eases but never entirely leaves, and that is correct. You are inside something that is listening. The pressure *is* the listening.

Your first instinct, around step five or step ten, will be to make a sound — cough, hum, shout — to confirm you still exist. If you must, do it once, softly, and attend to what happens: your voice is born and dies inside a hand-span. You will hear almost none of your own shout. This is the door-tax of the Cup, and everyone pays it, and the ones who pay it twice in panic spend the town's forgiveness — because every sound you make there is being slowly pardoned over days, and somewhere in that town a ledger of wear is keeping the account. Make your peace on the stair. Then put your hand back on the rock and finish going down.

Turning back is permitted — on the stair, slowly, visibly. No shame attaches. But know that even retreat is kept: your boot-prints climbing out will close over at about a lifetime's pace, and the elders will know which patch of green the almost-visitor is.

That is the kind of place this is.

---

## 6. First hours

Stand at the edge of the plaza, beneath the birds' column, hands visible and open, and be still. You were seen before you cleared the stair — a town that reads wear and smoke and snail-tracks sees everything. Wait to be addressed. When you are addressed it will be by hands; answer slowly, in whatever hand-speech you have: *I am a guest. I am one. I will learn.*

**You will be counted.** Accept this without negotiation — it is not bureaucracy, it is physics. Your body is a new mouth on exactly enough water. However they fit you into the arithmetic — tighter combs at dawn, shorter rinses, an elder's surrendered surplus — accept it, and drink only what is offered, only when it is offered. The wall-cups are counted. The eye-pool holds to one line. The fish have never once wrinkled it. Nothing here is unowned, including you now.

You will be thirsty the first day, because you will be polite. That is correct behavior.

**Before your first night, establish the one protocol that matters more than any greeting: distress.** You cannot call for help here. Screaming is a private ritual. Agree on a sign — a raised hand, a touch on the shoulder, something unmistakable that means *come* — and make sure the children know it too, because the children are the ones out at first light with their bone combs, and they will be your translators, your teachers, and your sentries all at once. Be patient with them. They have the best hands in town.

---

## 7. Living as a guest

**Ask, by hand, where waste goes — before you need to know.** This is not a euphemism for anything; it is the whole of it. Your body's exports are kept by the Cup, and salt kills moss. A careless traveler can write a dead patch of green into a wall that outlives everyone who watched him arrive. Follow their way exactly, whatever it is.

**Fire:** see above. One hearth, and it is not yours.

**Washing:** ask. It will be fog-water, rationed, done their way. Perfume, soap, tobacco — these are shouting in a town that hears with its nose. Arrive unscented; stay unscented; be clean in their manner, which is mostly fog.

**Walking:** walk where they walk, sit where they sit. The stairs are dished to the exact shallow curvature of Kept feet, and they *stopped wearing* generations ago. Every step you take adds a hair's width of your own dish at whatever pace stone accepts a stranger's weight. The record is being written regardless. Make it tidy.

**Do not touch:** the thread-lanes (the thousands of cords overhead are the town's fog-harvest — its throat, its nerves); the moss walls (blankets of the dead; the Soft Quarter is not a park); the crack of the weeping stone (press your ear to it — everyone does eventually, and whatever you hear, the drain or the sea or your own pulse or the nothing that is full, no one is wrong — but give nothing back); the snail-lines (correspondence, in progress, untranslateable, do not erase).

**The owl** that works the thread-lanes navigates by silence — yours as much as its own. Hold still when the wings brush your neck. For a moment, you are part of the stillness it hunts in.

**Pearl-dark:** the moths rise off the ferns in curtains. Let them land on you. It is the closest thing the night has to weather, and it does not happen twice for anyone.

**Eat what is offered** unless your body truly cannot. Cress, the pale fish, what the birds' economy provides. Your gut will complain, and the complaint will be silent, like everything else.

**Time:** there is no clock here, and you may not carry a ticking one anyway. Let the Cup keep time for you — by the birds' commute, by the combing, by smell. If the outer worlds have set you a deadline, build a wide margin around it. Time is a pool here, and pools do not honor schedules.

**Resist the scientist's itch.** Do not run experiments on the balance; do not drop things into the weeping stone; do not try to manufacture an echo. The Cup has been running its experiment for millennia without contamination. Your role is to not be the contaminant.

**Gifts:** give nothing material on a first visit. Anything you hand over is kept *forever* — an object given in the Cup becomes part of its permanent record, a commitment you cannot take back. If you must give, give work: a morning of combing, a mended hinge, a pair of hands held out at the right moment. The tinker was remembered not for what he brought but for what he stopped doing.

---

## 8. Speech, and what will be kept of it

Your voice will feel dead in your mouth. Speak anyway, when hands fail — softly, knowing the words are born and die within a hand-span. It is a private liturgy, and there is no shame in it: many visitors keep a daily appointment with a quiet corner and say a few sentences aloud to no one, the way you'd feed something small and hungry.

**The speaking stones:** enter only by invitation, and understand what they are — the only chambers where a voice lasts. A child's first word goes into a stone so the stone can keep it. Once a year comes the Naming, and the names of the dead are said aloud, once each, whole, softly, for a full day. If you are given a stone to speak into, know that what you say will be kept the way the moss keeps the dead. Say something you would consent to have grow over. And do not ask for the town's real name. It cannot live in your mouth; it exists only inside the stone's keeping, which is the entire point of it.

**If your stay crosses the Naming — go.** Stand at the back. It will be the loudest thing you ever witness precisely because it is the quietest. And if something in you breaks a little on that day, that is not damage. That is the intended use.

**Keep the paper journal.** Write everything: the ten thousand drips that land without landing-audibly; what the snail-lines said that morning, and the children's theories about it; which patch of green the elders showed you, if they show you, and whether they smiled when they did. Paper is the one echo the Cup will let you carry out.

---

## 9. Leaving

**Tell the elders before you go, in hands, with time to spare.** Vanishing from a census is a wound here.

**Take nothing that was not given.** If something is given — weigh it before you accept, because a gift given in the Cup is kept, and its absence is a gap in the record forever. If you accept, take it with two hands.

Then understand what stays of you regardless: boot-prints closing over at a lifetime's pace; perhaps a hair in the moss at the Soft Quarter's edge; a thread in the smoke-lens if you were warmed at the hearth even once — *this layer, the traveler*; a word in a stone, if you were given one. You are being written into a place that does not forget, in its native medium, which is wear. Make it good writing. Mend something quietly. Comb fog one morning without being asked. Sit with the children and hear their snail-theories and add nothing to the debate.

**What you may take freely:** the smell — your clothes will carry crushed cress and wet chalk and old smoke for months, and it will ambush you without warning in other worlds' markets. The pressure-memory in your ears. And the habit of listening, which takes longer than the smell to fade, and is worth keeping for good.

**Expect the loud world to be unbearable for a while.** Doors will make you flinch. You will catch yourself signing at people who aren't looking. Rain will sound obscene. Give it a week. Schedule nothing.

---

## 10. A last word

The elders say the outside world is a river, and noise is the sound of things being carried off. You are from the river, and you will arrive full of river-habits: the impulse to narrate, to fix, to improve, to take, to be *heard*. The whole of your preparation — the wool, the empty bottle, the practiced hands, the silent kit, the rehearsed skull — amounts to one act performed at the top of the weather stair: setting all of that down, and picking it up again, changed, when you climb out.

The Cup will ask nothing of you but what it asks of itself: that you add nothing, take nothing, and be kept a little while, gently, and then be let go.

Press your ear to the weeping stone before you leave. Everyone does, eventually. Whatever you hear — no one is wrong.
