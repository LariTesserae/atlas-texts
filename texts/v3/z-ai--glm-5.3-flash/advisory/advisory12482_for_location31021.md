## What you're actually walking into

Read the place correctly before you pack, because everything else follows from three facts.

**It is two climates separated by an inch.** Outside: a cold seeping plain, wind, wet ground, ordinary weather. Inside: body temperature, near-total saturation, no wind, no seasons. You are not visiting a building; you are crossing a skin. Every packing decision is really a decision about that seam.

**It is a closed economy.** Water goes around, and around; nothing enters and nothing leaves except what the flume carries in and the culvert carries out. Everything in that room — the moisture, the mineral film, the snail shells, the fallen leaf the mat takes and raises again — is circulating material. Whatever you add joins the circulation. Whatever you remove is an amputation.

**It is one organism.** Every leaf is the same species, graft fused to graft, no tissue rejecting tissue. The grove is a single body wearing the shape of a garden, and bodies can sicken. This is the fact most travelers underestimate, so hold it firmly: there is no "a leaf" here. There is only the body.

This reads as a near-Earth world — iron, bronze, enamel, a moth, snails — so the standard traveler's kit applies, with the modifications below. Nothing here will hurt you if you're careful. Nearly everything here will punish carelessness gently, by absorbing it.

## Before you cross the plain

**Clean and disinfect your boots and gear.** Actually do this; it's the single most important act of preparation. No soil exists anywhere inside, the place has no decomposers but itself, and a fungus or bacterium riding your boot sole would be a disease in a monoculture of one. You would be the first pathogen in a century of balance. Rinse, scrub, treat. Give it no soil.

**Plan a short visit.** There is no food, no water you should drink untreated, no latrine that isn't inside a closed nutrient loop, and no litter that won't be digested into the circulation. Everything you carry in, you carry out — including, frankly, your own waste for any stay longer than a few hours. This caps the visit naturally. Plan for a half day, no more, and pack accordingly: food eaten before or after, not inside.

**Tell yourself the pace now, at home.** The room runs on durations, not events. Its slowest cycle you'll feel is a pump stroke; its fastest is a drip falling. If you arrive with an errand energy, you'll leave having seen only a hot wet shed. Decide in advance to be slow, because the room will not speed up for you and never has.

## The approach

The plain does your navigation for you going in. Everything seeps downhill toward the bowl, and the bowl is the greenhouse; follow the wet and you cannot fail to arrive. Wear boots you've accepted will be wet the entire way — rush-flat, seepage, water standing in the hoofprints of no animals. A pole is useful on the rush-flat. Note, though, that the walk *out* is uphill along the same seep, muddier and slower than the walk in; budget energy and daylight for the return, and start back before you're tired rather than after.

Time your arrival for morning if you can. That's when the condensation tide runs inward and the walls rain; it's the version of the room worth seeing first.

## What to wear

Inside, you will bead within a minute. So will everything. Fight this nowhere except at the exit.

- **Inside layers: minimum.** A thin synthetic base layer and quick-dry trousers. No cotton — it will never dry and it will chill you fatally on exit. No fleece — it becomes a wet pelt that weighs kilos. The air is the temperature of a body; you need almost nothing, and whatever you wear becomes a condensation surface.
- **The most important garment is the one you don't wear yet:** a dry base layer plus a windproof, sealed in your pack, untouched for the whole visit. When you step back out through the seam, you will be filmed in warm water, and the plain's dry coolness plus wind will strip heat off you faster than you expect. Changing into dry clothes at the seam is the difference between a fond walk home and genuine hypothermia on the rush-flat.
- **Footwear:** grippy, drained shoes. There is no floor — there is a root-mat, sprung and drum-tight, that receives your footfall a centimeter down, with a skin of moving water running channels through it. It's pleasant walking, like a stretched floor, but everything in the room is wet-filmed. Watch the channels; they're shallow but slick.
- Long hair: tie it back. It becomes a condensation mop otherwise. If you habitually grab things for balance, thin gloves; the iron ribs are cold, wet, and furred.

## Gear, and the double fog

This room is a dew-point machine, and your equipment will demonstrate the lesson whether you consent or not. Anything colder than the room fogs instantly on entry. Anything warmer than the plain fogs instantly on exit. The protocol:

- **Bag everything airtight before crossing.** Camera, phone, recorder, spare lenses, notebook. Give the bag twenty to thirty minutes inside to let the contents reach room temperature before you open it. Reverse the process leaving: bag the warm, damp gear, let it cool sealed. If you skip this, you'll spend your first half hour wiping a lens that re-fogs with every pump stroke.
- **Paper:** waterproof notebook and pencil only. Ink runs on beaded pages. Everything you write will be written on a wet surface; accept it.
- **Sound recordists, pay attention:** this may be one of the great recording rooms in any reach — the pump's deep stroke as bass, the flume's rain-on-wood, the ten-thousand-drip hiss, and threading through it, the growth creaks, each one arriving, completing itself over half a minute, and gone. You will never get a cleaner recording of a machine becoming a plant. But condensation kills microphone capsules: let the recorder acclimate in its case, use a hydrophobic windscreen, and consider one microphone on the north condensers and one on the flume.
- **Photographers:** the light is diffuse, shadowless, sourceless — bright the way a fog is lit. There are no dramatic shadows to chase, so chase edges instead: the seam, the flake-shadowed enamel letters, a snail shell thin as a fingernail, the handwheel rim worn bright. And know that the whole room vibrates with the pump stroke through the mat; long exposures will breathe with it.
- A small light is worth carrying; the chamber is bright but even, and under the wall-growth the green goes deep. A max-min thermometer, held and read, will capture the tide in a single number pair if you're the measuring kind. A sling psychrometer is useless here — wet bulb equals dry bulb; the air has nothing left to give. Which is itself worth knowing you'll see.

## Nothing in, nothing out

**In:** no soil, no plant material, no food opened inside, no litter of any kind. Zip your pockets. Leash anything dangling. A dropped wrapper does not lie on the floor here — the floor takes it in, digests it, raises it again, and it enters the loop that goes around, and around.
