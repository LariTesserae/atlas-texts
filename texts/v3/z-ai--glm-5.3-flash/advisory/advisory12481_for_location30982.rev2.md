# Field Briefing: Graftwick, on the Lode

First, adjust the picture in your head. You are not visiting a town with a remarkable tree in it. You are visiting the inside of one organism — a single plant, four centuries old, grafted into itself until every wall, gutter, pipe, stair, and doorjamb is the same body — and two hundred and fifty people live inside that body the way birds live in a hedge. Everything below follows from that one fact. Read it twice. Nearly every mistake travelers make there is a mistake of forgetting it.

## When to go, and how to arrive

Go in a warm, dry-ish season, and plan to stay at least four days — a week if you can manage it. Fewer than four and you'll have heard the drum without hearing the melody. Time your exit around the barge, which rides the Lode twice a month, or make certain your own way out doesn't depend on the weather, because some springs the river comes up over its bars and stands among the outer stems, and you do not want to be a traveler with a fixed departure date during a flood you could have checked.

If you open your own door between worlds — and I assume you do, or you wouldn't be reading this — open it out on the plain, a mile or more from the dome, and *walk in*. This is not sentiment. The town is flat, small, and visible for a mile in every direction; there is no such thing as arriving unobserved, and appearing out of a hole in the air inside a town that is one continuous piece of flammable living wood is the single rudest thing you could do. Walk out of the weather like a person. It costs you nothing and buys everything.

Also: do not try to shortcut the wet meadow. The sedges stand in standing water and every hollow holds last night's rain. The corduroy path is on the **north** side. Take it.

And stop when you first hear the Stamps. This will be about a mile out — a deep wooden heartbeat, lift and fall, lift and fall, with the thinner double-knock of the ram-pumps underneath. Stand there a moment and let it find your pulse. You are about to live inside that heartbeat for the duration. Make introductions properly. Your eyes will keep insisting the last mile is just a wooded hummock; your ears are right and your eyes are wrong. Follow the sound.

## What to leave behind

**Fire. All of it.** No torches, no smoking, no pocket-stoves, no candles, nothing that strikes or glows or vents heat. Every hearth in Graftwick sits on a clay platform in an open court, with sand and a watcher beside it, and the water-wards who enforce this are a kind of priesthood who are never wholly dry. You will be the single largest fire risk in town the moment you arrive — an unknown person with unknown gear inside one enormous bale of the only thing the town cannot lose. If any of your equipment so much as warms strangely, report it to the water-wards *immediately*. They will be more grateful than frightened, which tells you everything about a town that has survived itself for four hundred years.

**Living things.** No seeds in your cuff-edges, no soil on your boots, no cuttings, no houseplants, no pets — and absolutely no pets that eat insects, because the sap-moths are on the payroll and the gall-wasps are livestock. The ground under the canopy is strangely empty; most small life declines whatever the roots put out, and that emptiness is a four-century-old equilibrium. You are a plague vector until proven otherwise, in both directions. This is not paranoia; it is the water-wards' oldest duty, and they will want to look at your boots before they want to know your name. Let them.

**Hurry.** You may keep your itinerary, but leave its authority at the door. An order in the Counting Room ledger sits on its page with a date like *cut ready, ninth year*, and no one chases it, because chasing is not a thing time does here. Impatience, in Graftwick, reads as a foreign accent.

**Secrets.** News travels along the rills, door to door, at the speed of water. Whatever you tell one person at breakfast will have reached the last house by supper — not through malice, but through hydraulics. Say nothing you wouldn't be content to hear repeated back to you warmly.

**Hard heels, hobnails, hooks, and anything that scars wood.** You will be walking on floors that are skin.

## What to bring

The barge — the town's entire mineral import, twice a month — is small as a coffin. That means a traveler's pack of iron and salt is roughly a barge's worth of wealth. Bring generously but not absurdly; you cannot buy the town, and trying will be remembered longer than you will.

In rough order of usefulness:

- **Salt**, coarse, well-sealed. It is on their import list, always has been, always will be.
- **Needles, files, whetstones, a good saw or adze** — iron edge tools for dead timber only, and say so when you give it. The distinction matters to them and should to you.
- **A hand-lens, and above all a proper brass listening-horn.** The water-wards walk the pipe-mains each morning with a listening-horn and can name a failing joint by its drip-count. A genuinely good horn will make you friends in the priesthood for life, and their friendship is the most useful friendship in town.
- **Blue cloth.** They buy it from the barge and no one will explain entirely why. Bring some anyway.
- **A book for the school**, if you can carry one — pictures of other places. This is a town that trades in patience; a book is a patient object, and children who learn graft-cuts at eight will still be reading it at eighty.
- **Kit for your body:** spare socks in oilskin, blister care, antifungal, antihistamine, tweezers (the leeches in the retting pits are sworn at with feeling, and you will want to swear too). If your lungs are delicate, bring what your world packs for constant warm humidity, fermentation-breath off the vat-rooms, and mold-sweet air. And if your world can test you for urushiol-type sensitivity, do it before you go — the lacquer is real lacquer, and raw sap is not for everyone. Sensitive travelers buy only cured ware and stay out of the vat-rooms on still days.
- **Earplugs — for the first night only.** After that you won't want them. I'll explain.
- Coin, for courtesy. But understand that the real money in Graftwick is the ledger, and a dated promise from you is worth more than any coin you carry. It is also remembered longer. See *Leaving*, below.

One candid note: Graftwick is vertical. Workshops at the ground where the weight is, living rooms a tier up, lofts in the small branches, and the ladders are living. If you have weak knees or need level ground, send word ahead by the barge — the barge runs twice a month, so a letter travels faster than you can — and give them time. They grow what is needed, given time. That is, in fact, the entire town.

## Arrival, and the ledger

You will be seen crossing the plain long before you arrive; by the time you step onto the boardwalk there will be children, and behind the children an elder, and behind the elder, eventually, a water-ward wanting your boots. Answer the boot question first and fully. Then go to the Counting Room and get yourself **into the ledger** — name, origin (be specific; they are unshockable), what you carry, what you'll do, what you owe. Being in the ledger is the whole of the law and the whole of the hospitality in one gesture. It means you exist and will be looked for. An un-ledgered stranger in Graftwick is a ghost, and towns that are one body do not care for ghosts.

Then — and this is the hardest instruction in this briefing — **do nothing**. Walk the three streets and the dozen lanes. Lose them and find them again. Put your palm flat on a wall and hold it there until it comes to you that the wall is also holding you. Drink the water: cold, faintly sweet, faintly of bark. Get rained on, notice that the rain lands on you already warm, and stop minding. Let the cadence in. Day one is not for asking questions. It is for becoming quiet enough to hear the answers.

## The laws, in traveler's form

The laws are few, old, and all concern keeping time with the plant. Translated for a visitor:

- **No flame**, ever, anywhere, including your gear. Already covered. It bears repeating because everything else is forgivable and this is not.
- **No cutting.** Every cut is a tap, every tap is counted, and the Counting Room knows the count. Don't prune, trim, carve, notch, or initial anything — there is no "anything" in Graftwick that is not the town. If something has grown onto your pack strap or your bootlace, do not free it yourself; find a grafter. They will make one small cut that is theirs to make, say something that is probably a joke, and think no more of it. You should think a great deal of it.
- **No careless weight.** Load one limb and a wall two streets away eases its strain. Your pack is a load; set it down where you're shown. Don't hang things on rails.
- **Respect the water.** The leat, the wheel, the proving-tanks: look, don't swim. The retting pits: absolutely not. If you go into the Lode by accident, catch the root-cliff — it has never lost an argument with the river — and expect to be fished out by children who will dine on the story for a year.
- **The livestock is livestock.** The gall-wasps are a farm. The sap-moths are employees. The Gray, the old heron who holds station at the proving-tank overflow, is a pensioner. Do not feed, swat, or collect any of them.
- **Rest-years are sabbaths.** If a stand stands dark and untapped with its leaves come in deeper green, that is not neglect. Never be the visitor who suggests pruning.
- **Your word is a dated entry.** Make no promise you don't intend to keep across years. They will not chase you. They will simply still be keeping their side when you return — and that is how yours gets measured.

## Living there

**Food** grows on the plain, outside the dome, because under the canopy almost nothing volunteers to grow — the Stock keeps the ground to itself. Expect precision and salt-sparing: seasoning is a mineral here, imported coffin-sized, and the cooking is better than it has any right to be. Cress off the rills, water from the Weep, and if you are lucky enough to be there on one of the two days a year when someone takes an eel in the leat, cancel every other plan — the whole town will have heard by supper, and the supper is the point. By day three everything will taste faintly of bark, including your own hands. This is correct and should be trusted.

**Sleep:** you'll be lodged a tier up in the weave, in a room that someone's grandmother tied into shape, bed grown to its own floor, window glazed in sap the color of cider. The first two nights the Stamps will knock in your sternum and the rams will double-knock until dawn. Use the earplugs if you must. By the third night you will sleep like a child in a choir loft, and you will leave the earplugs behind as a gift.

**Weather:** rain comes often and means nothing by it. Be wet and warm and glad. But when a shower ends and the sun comes back — go outside and stand still. The whole town begins to exhale: every wall breathing its wet back out, the warm air taking it up, a rising veil of the town's own breath, gold-green, dripping, lit from inside its leaves. The town has its own name for that half-hour; ask for it. Stand in it. Whatever your chest does, that is the correct response.

**The Nooning:** once a day the Stamps are stilled, and the silence is so total that babies wake and visitors laugh. Be sitting down for your first one. Then listen — for the first time you will hear the Weep, which is the truest sound the place has. Laugh or weep; both are traditional.

## The two clocks, and your money

Graftwick keeps the day's clock and the generation's clock, and you must match your questions to the right one. "How long?" answered in years is not evasion; it is exactness. A grafter plants the walls of a house she will not live to roof and says so without grief.

Practically, this gives you the single best opportunity Graftwick offers a traveler with a return ticket: **buy what's on the shelf, and commission one thing on purpose.** Cordage, bark-cloth, cured lacquerware, ink, dyes — take those now, heavy and lovely. But then sit in the Counting Room and place one considered order with a date on it years away. This is one of the very few economies in any ring of worlds where *showing up again in a decade* is enforceable — where your return trip is, functionally, a bank. The price will be honest, the waiting will be shared, and the object will arrive finished to the last coat. And offer your hands while you wait: the pit-saw is always short an under-hand, the rope-walk's top turn wants turning, the steaming heaps of prunings want hauling. A day of sawdust buys a week's lodging and a place at a table. Do not sell speed here. It is the one commodity with no market.

What to see, all free: the Nooning from beneath the stilled galleries; the exhalation after rain; the Weep from the boardwalk at night while you count every lamp in town (you can; there are only two hundred and fifty souls' worth); a pipe-proving at the tanks if one is due — a season's head of water held back by wood and patience, with the Gray standing his post; and the school on a tying day. If a child is shown holding their first tie, carefully dead and carefully kept, understand that you are being shown a wedding invitation a decade early, and answer correctly.

## Leaving

Settle the ledger. Tell one person you're going; the water will do the rest, and the town will watch you the whole mile out from the boardwalk. Let them. Don't open your door inside the town on the way out either — walk back across the corduroy, out of the Stock's weather and into the plain's, and hear the Stamps fade behind you at the same point where they first found you.

**Take:** oiled bark-cloth — the best rain gear you will own in any world, and it will smell faintly of bark forever after; a coil of cordage; cured lacquer; ink. If — and only if — it is offered, take **seed-stock**, bundled and labeled for stands that do not yet exist. If it is offered, the town is trusting you with its future; plant it somewhere with the patience to deserve it, and send word of where.

**Do not take:** cuttings of the Stock, gall-wasps, moth larvae, the Gray (obviously), anyone's first tie, or anything you did not pay for or grieve for.

**And if you promised to return, keep the date or come update it.** An unkept promise in Graftwick is not resented. It is kept in trust, on the page, with the date showing. That is heavier than resentment, and kinder, and you will feel it from three worlds away.

## The part no checklist covers

Prepare your tempo, because the town will change it. Graftwick is loud and slow, and the loudness *is* the slowness made audible — every knock is a tree agreeing to be a house, every drip a joint confessing to a listening-horn. You will arrive measuring days and leave measuring decades. That is not a side effect of the visit. It is the visit.

Know too that some travelers feel claustrophobic inside one shared body — a hand on any surface touching the same living thing as the hand on the far side of the wall. Some feel held. If it alarms you, budget your evenings for the boardwalk and the plain's open sky; no one will follow, and no one will be hurt, because a town that built its whole existence on patience understands needing air. If it does not alarm you — if instead you find yourself resting your palm on doorjambs the way the locals do, adding your few thousand touches to the dark polish of generations — then you have already understood the place better than any briefing can teach it.

And prepare, finally, to be *remembered*. In most worlds you visit, you pass through and the world closes over you like water. In Graftwick the ledger stays open. Twenty years on, someone will tap a wall and say a traveler once hauled prunings to the heaps, or taught the school a song, or left a listening-horn that has named forty failing joints since. You will be one of the hands that polished a doorjamb — and the doorway, which grows a hand's-breadth of a hand's-breadth closer every morning to the shape of the hands that hold it, will hold your shape too.

That is what Graftwick costs and pays. Not souvenirs. Membership — in the only currency the place has ever issued, which is time.
