Here is the paradox to hold while you pack: the High Water Gardens may be the gentlest world you will ever visit, and among the least forgiving. Nothing in it is hostile. Nothing in it is *capable* of hostility. It is also a place with no walls, no tools, no one to trade with, nothing to burn, and — several hundred meters of warm amber water above every point of interest — no air. The Gardens will never hurt you. They will simply, quietly, decline to keep you alive. Your preparation is the difference between those two sentences.

## Be honest about what you are

You are the only dry-remembering thing in that ocean, and your body is built for one atmosphere. The summits — the *highest* ground — lie hundreds of meters down. There are no shallows to acclimate in; the shallowest point of this world is deeper than most of the diving you have ever done, by an order of magnitude.

So choose your method honestly, before you fall in love with the place:

**A hard-shelled one-atmosphere suit**, rated to 600–800 meters with margin, low-thrust propulsion, articulated through the shoulders and hips. This is the visitor's option. You keep your air at surface pressure, you leave when you want, and you never carry the sea home in your blood. The cost is bulk: you will move through the Gardens like a slow, polite machine.

**Full saturation**, from a habitat vessel moored above, with umbilical excursions. This is the *resident's* option. You compress over a full day — slowly, slowly; the bathwater warmth is this world's single physiological kindness, because the deep-pressure trembling of the nervous system is far worse in cold water — and then you can swim freely for weeks. The price is written at the end: days, perhaps a week, of decompression before you may breathe surface air again. You cannot come here for an afternoon. The Gardens' tempo is not a metaphor; it becomes your circulatory system. You will leave the way light enters this place: gradually.

One warning if your multiverse kit includes a "breathe water" apparatus: test it *elsewhere* first, and hard. At thirty atmospheres, the dissolved oxygen in seawater carries a partial pressure several times past what the human nervous system tolerates. A naive gill in a beautiful place is a convulsion. Any water-breathing rig must meter gas like a rebreather does, or it is a coffin painted the color of honey.

Whatever you choose: everything in twos, the critical things in threes. There is no salvage market at the bottom of a private ocean. Budget this trip as pure expenditure — nothing can be recouped, resupplied, or repaired by local means, because there are no local means. There is nothing here but life and young stone, and neither of them fixes regulators.

## Test the honeyed light before you trust it

The water glows with *normal light* where normal light has no right to be. That is either a wonder or a warning, and you cannot yet know which. So the first days of any visit are an audit, not a tour:

Sample the water and run full chemistry before you assume it is ordinary seawater with a cosmetic quirk. Check whether radio behaves. Check whether your compass does — a medium with anomalous interaction with light may have opinions about electromagnetism generally. Until proven otherwise, plan all communication between travelers by acoustic modem, all terrain-sensing by sonar, all position-keeping by inertial dead-reckoning plus pressure.

And here is the disorientation no one warns you about: the light comes from the water itself, evenly, in all directions. There is no bright above, no dim below, no shadow anywhere. Depth perception loses its usual gradient; up and down become negotiable within an hour. Carry an artificial horizon — a small level, a tethered bead, anything — for the moments when the amber goes everywhere at once and your inner ear, offered no darkness either way, quietly resigns. Trust your pressure gauge for depth, never your eyes.

Bring backup lamps. You almost certainly will not need them, which is exactly when you will.

## Choose your ground the way a guest chooses

You are the first visitor to a world with no artifacts. *Placement is ethics here.*

Camp high — on the plateaus among the kelp-grass, near but not at the hollow sphere. Plateau ground is the gentlest: moderate slopes, upright soft-bladed growth, well away from the fracture-sharp ridgelines and — this matters — away from the base of any sheer face. Young rock settles in centimeter events. Do not camp beneath anything that has not finished becoming a mountain.

No anchors. An anchor wound gets upholstered, like everything here, and the world will wear your scar at its own pace, which is to say for a very long time. Use a ballast plate laid flat on the sparsest ground you can find, and accept that it will begin to green within the season — stone stays bare here for about as long as a fallen branch stays bare in a rainforest. Schedule gentle clearing, or let the Gardens have it and plan to take the plate home when you leave. Decide in advance which, because everything you abandon becomes an artifact, and this world has none. Think carefully before you take responsibility for its first.

And keep your noise inside the pressure hull. The local soundscape is a tidal hum, a settling crack, perhaps one thump a year. Your habitat's pumps should not be the loudest event in an ocean. Choose your machinery for quietness the way you would choose it for reliability. This includes your music, your arguments, your voice. Outside, be fauna-quiet.

## Learn to move like a rare thing

Buoyancy is the local morality. Every cubic meter holds something. Stone does not stay stone for a season. Fin wash, a braced palm, a dropped knee — these are events at the scale this world runs on, and its fronds do not recover at your pace.

So arrive already skilled, or arrive in the suit, but do not arrive sloppy. Hover motionless for minutes at a time. Frog-kick. Approach without surge. If you cannot do these in your sleep, do the training *before* you come; the Gardens do not grade on a curve.

Keep your hands to yourself. A hedge of coral-anemone — pale rose, bone-white, presumably well supplied with stinging cells — is an invitation you should decline. Part a living corridor only if survival demands it, which it almost never will; take the ridgelines instead and accept their sharpness. Young rock keeps its original fracture edges, and you are, at last, the thing the landscape has been waiting for: something soft, something that can be cut. Gauntlets, shin guards, a thin abrasion layer over your skin — the warm water means you need almost no thermal protection, which frees you to wear armor instead.

And the sphere: visit it, hover at its threshold, and do not go in. It is not a dwelling. It is the *premonition* of a dwelling — the first time this world has folded living tissue around a hollow and left a door. Your exhaust, your body heat, your grip could end the premonition. Some doors are for looking at.

Take your tempo from the fauna. Solitude is their default condition and it looks chosen; they keep their distance out of an instinct none of them could name — keep yours out of one you can. The rule is simple: **all approaches are initiated by the resident.** When the pale eel finds you — and after a day of nothing, it will feel like a coronation — stop. Hover. Let it finish looking. If it pours closer, that is the event of your trip; receive it. Never follow. Slow movement has a second grace: through the spore-fuzz and the animalcule motes you will trail a faint shimmering signature of disturbed water, and a slow traveler leaves almost no signature at all. Be an almost-imperceptible current. It is the closest thing this world has to a way of being welcomed.

## What can hurt you, and how slowly

**Edges.** Young stone is sharp; treat every cut as urgent, because warm luminous water is a culture medium and nothing in your med kit has ever met this biota. Wound closure supplies, generously, and the discipline to use them immediately.

**Seeps and thumps.** Most of the birth-pain thumps are exactly what they sound like: the mountains being born, felt in your sternum, startling, harmless at any distance. Near an active seep they are not harmless. Rising bubbles make rising water, and rising water makes an unplanned ascent, and an unplanned ascent while saturated is fatal. The protocol: any column of beads, any shimmer in the sediment, any water that tastes — metaphorically — of the deep's business: leave, horizontally, now. When a thump rolls through, stop swimming, hold your depth, vent nothing, watch for rising gas before you move on.

**Your own gas.** Oxygen ceilings, decompression obligations, the gill-trap above. Redundant life support is not paranoia here; it is grammar.

**The silence.** Expect your ears to invent sound for the first few days — ringing, whispers, a fan that isn't running. That is an auditory system going hungry; it fades. Then something better happens: you begin to hear the tidal hum with your skeleton, and later, back in your own loud world, you will miss it.

**Time without a sky.** If the light here knows no day and no night — and it may not — the seasons are measured in sediment while your body still needs a Tuesday. Bring a clock. Bring a lamp schedule inside the habitat, rituals at fixed hours, and the small stubbornness of a person who makes their own mornings when the world declines to provide any.

## The hazard is you

Quarantine runs both directions, and the second direction is the one that matters.

Nothing unboiled leaves your hull: closed waste, sealed graywater, filtered intakes — the spore-fuzz will find any opening. No bare-hand sampling; sealed containers only. And think about what you *carry*: a young biosphere with no competitors, no immune history, nothing that has ever met a traveler's microbiome. A world this young, this dense with unguarded life, could be undone not by a predator but by a guest's rinse water.

Remember the last line of any honest description of this place: it is waiting, not urgently, for its first real change. The most likely candidate to be that change is the thing reading this guide. Decline the role. If your visit must change something, let it be you.

The same courtesy applies to light and sound. Your lamp is a local sun; the fauna here have grown up under a directionless amber sky, and a beam is a violent weather event to a coin-eyed eel. Use it sparingly, briefly, and never at an animal.

## How to spend a month of almost nothing

Schedule nothing. That is not laziness; it is the itinerary. The Gardens are dense with everything except event, and event is the only currency worth having there. The single eel after a day of empty water. A thump, if you are lucky, once. A slope of russet that will not visibly move in your lifetime no matter how patiently you watch. Your stillness is how you pay for these things; spend accordingly.

Bring pen and paper and write inside the habitat, at one atmosphere, where ink behaves and paper stays dry — you are the only thing in this world that remembers dryness, and the homesickness you feel here will not be for people. It will be for wind. You may keep a recording of wind and play it inside the hull. Never outside.

Photograph sparingly. Set a white-balance card if you must, but know in advance that your footage will not match your memory — the russet and wine-dark and bone will not survive the translation — and choose which one you intend to keep.

Consider going with one companion. The fauna keep their solitude by instinct; you are not required to. If you go alone, schedule acoustic contact with home on fixed days and keep it religiously — a voice on schedule is the mooring line that matters most down there.

## A packing list, roughly

- A one-atmosphere hard suit rated to 800 m, redundant life support, gentle thrusters — *or* a full saturation rig and a calendar with a week of decompression on the far side
- Depth from pressure, position from sonar and inertia, comms by acoustic modem; verify all electromagnetic tools before trusting any
- Two backup lamps, sealed (you won't need them; that's the point)
- Thin cut-resistant skin layer, gauntlets, shin guards; cooling, not heating
- Wound kit sized for weeks; treat every breach of skin as urgent
- All food, all water, all spares — doubled; nothing here is edible, drinkable, or fixable
- Sealed waste and sample systems; nothing unboiled leaves the hull
- Ballast plate, not an anchor; fouling tools; the quietest pumps you can buy
- An artificial horizon; a clock; pen and paper; one recording of wind

## Leaving

The exit is part of the visit. Days of stops, ascending by increments, and somewhere on the way up — note the depth — the amber will stop, and ordinary dark or ordinary blue will begin. That boundary may be the strangest fact in the whole place, and someone ought to record it. Record it.

Then the last of the gas leaves your blood, and that will be the true departure, days after your body has broken the surface: the Gardens' tempo exiting you the way it entered, slowly, mineral by mineral. Nothing hurries. Nothing needs to. Arrive with that already in your blood, and the water will only confirm it.
