# Preparation Brief: The Weeping House

*(the self-throttling wetland engine; the greenhouse that kept itself)*

---

## I. What you are actually walking into

Classify it correctly and everything else follows. This is not a ruin, not a garden, not a machine you visit. It is a **body that has agreed to be shaped like a building** — one organism and one engine fused into a single metabolism, running at blood heat, breathing its own water in circles. So your preparation is a cross between two disciplines that travelers rarely combine: **sauna physiology** and **etiquette inside another creature**.

The hazards, ranked honestly:

1. **The climate.** Near-total saturation at body-plus temperature. Nothing in there will attack you; the air itself is the danger, because at full humidity your sweat stops being air conditioning and becomes costume. This is the most likely way to be hurt, and it is entirely preventable.
2. **The floor.** A living root-mat, sprung like a drumhead, with two centimeters of moving water on it. Wet, giving, strange underfoot. Faint on it and you land face-first on living tissue in a warm incubator.
3. **Fragility of the place itself.** A monoculture is a monoculture *because* nothing else can live there — which means it has no immune diversity. You are the most dangerous object in the room. Every rule below is partly about not being that.
4. **The mechanism's controls.** The handwheel turns itself, weekly, answering the pressure of an entire plain's water. It is the only place in this world where one casual human gesture could do real damage. Hands off is not etiquette; it is load-bearing.
5. **The silence of missing animals.** Not a physical danger, but it will get under your skin if you don't expect it. Prepare for it like weather.

No predators. No parasites visible. No insects to bite you — one pale moth and some fingernail-thin snails, that is the entire animal kingdom on offer. The danger is that the place is *pleasant*. It will invite you to stay.

## II. Your body, before you go

- **Heat-readiness.** Inside is roughly 30–36 °C at humidity approaching 100%. Your cooling system is effectively disabled; you lose heat only by breathing and by contact. A healthy adult standing quietly manages about 90 minutes comfortably. Anyone with heart trouble, heat-illness history, or significant asthma should treat this as a marginal environment and plan a shorter visit or none. There is no shame in standing at the seam and going no further; the place loses nothing.
- **Conditioning.** If your home world has saunas or steam rooms, use them for the week beforehand. If not, arrive rested, sober (no alcohol for 24 hours prior — it sabotages exactly the regulation you'll need), and having eaten lightly a few hours before, not immediately before.
- **Hydration discipline.** This is the whole game. You will be soaked within a minute and *will not feel thirsty*, because thirst tracks dry mouths and yours will be wet. Drink on schedule, not on desire: roughly 250 ml per half hour inside, with electrolytes. Pre-hydrate an hour before entry. Do not chug plain water in quantity — that trades heat risk for sodium risk.
- **Skin and small wounds.** Bandage any blister, hangnail, or scrape *before* entry. The air will macerate softened skin, and — this deserves its own line — **the mat digests.** Nothing fallen lingers there; a leaf is taken in the same day. You do not want to conduct the experiment of whether a drop of your blood is metabolically interesting. Keep your skin sealed, your fingernails short, and your hands off your face.
- **Skip fragrances.** Perfumed skin in saturated air means the chemistry clings to you for a day and introduces compounds the place has never met. Unscented everything.

## III. Biosecurity — in both directions

Multiverse travelers are habitually careless about this, and here it matters more than almost anywhere, in *both* directions.

**What you carry in.** Seeds in a cuff. Spores in boot treads. Mud from last week's world. A mold spore asleep in a knife's handle-wrap. This grove is one genome wearing the shape of a garden — every graft fused, no tissue rejecting tissue. That is its glory and its single point of failure. One visitor's blight could end a century of balance. So: wash boot treads, turn out pockets, inspect pack seams and gear straps, and **leave organic-material gear on the plain side of the curtain** — a wooden walking stick, a leather satchel, a straw hat. The roots here threated their way into iron pipes; you do not want to find out what they do to a hazel haft left against the mat, and you especially don't want to find out what your stick's microbes do to them.

**What you carry out.** Its condensation, for a start — your clothes will be soaked in it. This is harmless and even fitting: it is the plain's own water, slightly warmed, and your dripping shirt does exactly what the culvert does, returning it to the downhill seep. Carry that thought; it makes the exit feel honest.

But also: **check for hitchhikers.** Snails graze the panes; a fingernail-thin shell rides beautifully in a boot tread. Inspect collar, cuffs, treads, and pack seams at the threshold, and return any passengers gently to a pane — they belong to the condensation layer and nowhere else. Then decontaminate gear properly before it goes to any other world: hot wash or a dilute bleach soak for anything that touched the mat. If you expect to visit often, dedicate a "wet kit" — boots, layer, notebook — that lives between visits and never travels elsewhere. A traveler's small courtesy with large consequences.

**The cutting.** Someone always wants one. A leaf that crosses the seam withers back to the line — the organism polices its own boundary. A cutting sealed in a jar of its own water is a different question, and the answer is still no. You would be carrying out either a dead stem or a live empire, and you cannot know which until it is too late to matter.

## IV. Packing list

- **Water**, 1.5–2 L, with electrolyte tablets. A thermos of something cool: warm water is everywhere here, and coolness is the one luxury the house does not stock.
- **Clothing:** one smooth, quick-drying long-sleeve layer and long trousers. You will brush against stem walls constantly; sleeves are your chemical and mechanical buffer. No fuzz, no loose weave, no dangling straps — lint sheds into the wet and fibers snag on living bark. Hair tied. A sealed spare shirt for the exit; putting on a dry shirt in dry wind after the chamber is one of the great small pleasures available to a traveler.
- **Footwear:** closed boots with clean treads and real grip. The mat is taut, not boggy, but it is wet, and there are ceramic gutter lips and copper edges to find with a toe. No spikes — it is a drum, not mud, and drumming on it is rude.
- **Electronics** in sealable bags with desiccant. Anything you carry in cold will fog the moment you cross and stay wet for half an hour. The camera trick: let it equilibrate *sealed* inside the chamber for 30–40 minutes, then open and shoot. A cheap mechanical watch survives the damp better than a smart one, and you will want timekeeping — see below. Spectacle-wearers: pre-warm your lenses against your skin before entry and accept five blind minutes regardless; contact lenses will collect a film in that air, so glasses win.
- **Notes:** pencil on slate, or on waterproof stone-paper. Ink runs, phones sweat, paper pulps.
- **Med kit:** oral rehydration salts, antiseptic, dressings, antihistamine, hydrocortisone, inhaler if prescribed, current tetanus protection.
- **A whistle.** The chamber is full-loud — broadband drip-hiss over a bass stroke — and it swallows the human voice at six paces. A whistle's narrow note cuts through. A folded band of bright cloth works as a visual beacon; lay it *on* the mat only if you will retrieve it, or hang it from a rib.
- **No food inside.** Eat beforehand. Crumbs here are not litter; they are *intake*, and the mat's metabolism does not need your contribution and should not get it. If you must carry calories, a squeeze pouch, resealed, hung from a rib.
- **A sound recorder**, sealed, is the underrated item. The fog-lit, shadowless interior photographs as pale green white; beautiful, but flat. What this place *is* is its sound — the stroke, the flume, the ten-thousand-tip hiss, the growth creaks — and a recording weighs nothing and is the only souvenir you can legitimately carry out.
- **Bring nothing living, nothing once-living, nothing that was ever a seed.** This is the short version of Sections III and X.

## V. Timing

The house has a daily breath. Mornings, condensation rains *inward* on the walls; afternoons, the air drinks it back off the glass. The description's own thermometer — "a hothouse at four in the afternoon" — tells you when the heat peaks. So:

- **First visit:** arrive mid-morning, sit out the acclimatization, enter around the wall-rain, stay **60 to 90 minutes**, and be out before the afternoon turn. You will get the tide, two or three strokes of the pump, the drip at full voice, and a safe thermal margin.
- **Longer study:** many short visits beat one long one, always. The cycles run on hours, weeks, and years; they will keep. This is, notably, the one place in the multiverse that is guaranteed to still be patient with you later.
- The exterior plain browns seasonally while the interior never fades — the seam's contrast is starkest in the outside world's late summer, if you care about such things for pictures.

## VI. Entry protocol, step by step

1. **Cross the plain slowly.** Watch the seam before you touch it. If you are lucky you will see the trim happen — a leaf reaching past the plane and withering back to the line — and you will understand that the boundary is maintained, not inherited. Lay a palm on a surviving pane: two climates an inch apart. That inch is what your lungs are about to cross.
2. **Sit at the threshold twenty to thirty minutes.** Hydrate. Shed layers into your pack. Seal electronics. Check each other's cuffs, hems, and treads.
3. **Fix the door before you enter.** There are no landmarks inside — every leaf is the same species, the light is shadowless, and depth perception goes soft within minutes. Your navigation will be *bones and sound*, so learn both now. The bones: beneath every wall of green there runs iron, the old frame, and the doorway is an iron rectangle. If you lose your way, you do not follow sightlines; you follow the frame until your hand finds bare metal and then follows metal until it finds the rectangle. The sound: the loudest rain-on-wood is the flume, which falls at the basin's heart; the deep stroke is the engine, same direction; a thin cold dripping line is the culvert's outflow, which leads away. Three points on a compass made of noise. Learn them standing still.
4. **Enter sideways through the stem-curtain, slowly.** It gives with a woody creak; that is its elasticity, and it is the same organism as the walls. Do not yank it, do not hold it for the person behind — let each arrival push through their own way and let it fall closed on its own.
5. **Stand still for the first five minutes.** Let your arms bead. Pick the sound apart into its four layers — stroke, flume, drip-hiss, creaks — and then find the fifth layer, the one that is a *missing* layer: the animal-silence underneath, where birdsong and insect-drone should be and is not. It arrives like a floor that isn't there. Everyone feels it. Knowing it is coming is the difference between a shiver and a bad half hour.

## VII. Inside: moving, touching, drinking, waiting

**Move softly.** The mat takes a centimeter of your weight and gives it back; walk with that spring rather than against it. It will swallow your footprints by evening — that is its housekeeping, not an invitation to loiter on it. Keep your pack hung from a rib, everything off the floor. A dropped glove dams a channel; a dropped knife is *taken in*. If you drop something, retrieve it immediately and completely.

**Touch policy.** Stem walls and iron ribs: fine, gently, palm-flat, and notice what your hand tells you — living stem is blood-warm, iron on the north face is cool, and that contrast is one of the few *textures of information* in a shadowless room. Do not grip, do not lean, do not rest a hand on the condenser fins — the moss on them is furred to exactly the right depth, and a handprint is a small wound to a balance.

**The two forbiddens:** the handwheel and the valves. The wheel's arc-marks are its own handwriting; it turns a few degrees a week as the pressure asks, and it is throttling the descent of an entire plain's water — the meadow you camped on is downstream of your discretion. The gauges you may read with your eyes. Do not tap them; uncracked glass is rare enough to protect.

**Do not maintain anything.** No oil (foreign lubricant into a root-pipe circulatory system is poisoning, not help), no tightening (the plant is the fastener now), no scraping patina, no clearing moss. Bright brass and a clean gauge glass mean *someone's* maintenance already happens here, and it isn't yours. The place heals the way a body does; your intervention is contamination, not kindness.

**Water.** Everything in here has been through the plant — the air's moisture is effectively distilled, and a bead at a leaf-tip is the purest water in the room. But purest is relative in a warm wet chamber furred with biofilm: drink your own supply, and if you must refill, take beads from leaf-tips into a clean container rather than dipping from the channels, and treat anything you drink. One more caution: keep your face out of the flume's spray. Any warm aerosol is a lung's gamble, whatever world you're from.

**The visit's spine.** Go to the flume and wait for the stroke. It comes up through the mat into your feet before your ears have it — a held breath released through iron — and for half a beat the ten-thousand-tip hiss holds its own breath, and the moth adjusts on its pane, and the whole warm chamber rises and settles. Time your exit to a stroke's release; it is the correct way to leave, like leaving between movements.

**The half-hour check.** Notice whether you have stopped noticing the heat. That is not acclimation; that is the early stage of the problem. If you feel chill, gooseflesh, headache, or a strange elation, leave *now* — the exit is thirty steps and a vine curtain away, and dry wind does in a minute what your skin cannot.

**The one rule with no interpretive room:** if you cut yourself inside, finish the visit. Clean it, bandage it, and go. Warmth, saturation, and a floor that digests make a wound a different object here than anywhere else you'll stand this year.

## VIII. Exit and aftercare

Push out into the dry wind and let it do in a minute what your body couldn't in ninety. The plain will seem *loud* — real wind, real emptiness — after the chamber-full noise; the contrast is part of the visit, so don't rush it. Sit on the seam with a palm on each climate, the way you did coming in. The symmetry matters more than you'd think.

Then, before you leave the world entirely: hitchhiker inspection — collar, cuffs, treads, pack seams; snails back to a pane, the moth gently back through the curtain if it rode out on your shoulder. Swap to the dry shirt. Rehydrate with electrolytes over two hours, not all at once. Your skin will keep beading for an hour afterward as it recalibrates what "air" means; a hot shower helps it along. Watch any broken skin and your lungs for a few days — a cough or fever after a long visit earns a professional's attention, and you should tell them where you have been in whatever terms your world accepts.

## IX. If you meet the tender

The evidence is unambiguous: a brass fitting bright under patina, a clean gauge glass, a dry unchipped mug on a grown-over ledge. Someone comes, at some interval the mat erases the memory of, and does small work and leaves almost nothing — not even footprints by nightfall.

If you meet them: let them come to you. Speak close, because the noise eats ordinary voices at arm's length and closeness is simply the house style here — everything in this room is intimate, water and warmth and sound included. Offer nothing living. Take nothing but attention. Do not follow them. Do not ask to stay past the hour they indicate, and read their silences the way you'd read gauges.

If you never meet them: leave your visit shaped like theirs. A day-swallowed footprint's worth of trace, a few degrees of attention spent, nothing else. And ration the coordinates — this is a monoculture that spent a century becoming invulnerable, and it is precisely therefore fragile to traffic. Bring the next traveler only after they have read this brief and meant it.

## X. What the visit will be like, so you can prepare for that too

Decide your exit time before you enter and obey it, because the place hypnotizes gently. Every cycle ends with an invitation to one more — one more stroke, one more minute of the hiss, maybe a growth-creak if you're lucky, half a minute of a thing audibly becoming larger. "Just one more stroke" is how ninety minutes becomes three hours and a headache. Set a soft alarm. The pump gives you a clock anyway — a metronome of long moments — which is a strange mercy in a place with no other timekeeping; you will feel time as durations, and it is good to have one small number chained to it.

The landmarklessness and the missing animals, together, produce a mild altered state in most visitors: floating, dream-adjacent, slightly unmoored from ordinary scale. This is not danger; it is the visit's actual gift. But move deliberately in it, don't trust a quick glance at distance, and don't make decisions inside that you could make better outside — including the decision to stay.

And when you go, go the way the place's own water goes: the culvert's outflow, slightly warmed, slipping back under the plain's edge to rejoin the seep without changing its course. Come out a degree or two warmer in spirit. Leave no forecast behind you. Everything in there already has somewhere to be — around, and around, and around — and it does not need you to stay. It only needs you to pass through kindly.
