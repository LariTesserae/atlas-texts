# Briefing: The Fumarole Market
### For the ordinary human traveler — what to know, what to carry, and what it will do to you

---

## First, a correction to your instincts

Everything in you that says *underground = danger* is calibrated for old caves: cold, dry, dark, dead. This is not that. The Fumarole Market is warm, wet, lit, and alive — more like being inside a lung than inside a hole. Your instincts will misfire in both directions. They will tell you the heat is a threat (it is manageable) and the dimness is a hazard (it is mostly a courtesy). Rewrite your reflexes before you go, and you will be fine. Arrive unprepared in spirit, and you'll spend your whole visit flinching.

Forty meters. That's the whole depth. Two hundred steps down a spiral stair, and the sky is a coin behind you. This is the easiest subterranean city in any catalog — and the most claustrophobic per cubic meter. Both things are true. Prepare for both.

---

## Before you go: the body

**Heat and water discipline.** You will sweat from the first rung to the last, constantly, in a way your body is probably not used to. The ambient air cycles between dripping humid and merely warm-dry depending on where the fans are pulling. Acclimatize if you can: a week of sauna or hot-yoga beforehand genuinely helps. Your first 48 hours down there should be deliberately lazy.

Here is the trap most travelers walk into: **the water is distilled.** It condensed out of vapor onto copper. It is pure — probably the cleanest water you'll ever drink — and nearly mineral-free. Meanwhile your body is dumping salt and potassium through your skin all day. Drink their water, sweat it out, drink more, and within three days you can make yourself genuinely ill with hyponatremia while being technically surrounded by liquid wealth. **Pack electrolyte sachets — twenty at minimum.** This is the single most important item in your bag. Add a rehydration formula and, if you have any history of it, an inhaler: the air is warm, wet, spore-rich, and full of people. Asthmatics and mold-allergy sufferers should think hard before committing. The biofilm terraces are the light source and the bread source, and their airborne load is not subtle.

**Skin.** Constant damp means fungal joy. Pack antifungal powder, rotate four pairs of socks, dry your feet whenever you're stationary, wear loose quick-drying layers. Linen or modern synthetics; nothing heavy. You will do laundry constantly and your neighbors' laundry will touch yours on the line. This is normal. Let it be normal.

**Respiratory courtesy — both directions.** The city's entire ecology is human plus managed slime. There are no rats, no insects, no detritivores. Which means disease has nowhere to hide *except the people*, and the ecology has no buffer against invaders. Before you descend: vaccinate what's vaccinable, treat any cough, any fungal patch, any open sore as a disqualifier for the mat terraces. Vacuum your pack, freeze-wash your clothes if you can. You are walking biocargo. And when you leave, launder everything — their spores in your collar could colonize some other world's cellar.

**Leave the fire home.** This cannot be overstated. The forges are hooded, engineered, fiercely regulated — fire exists down there the way a vaccine exists: deliberately, in controlled doses. A traveler with a lighter, a vaping rig, even matches in a coat pocket is walking through a city carrying a small bomb. Expect ignition sources to be surrendered at the stair. Surrender them cheerfully. Also: soap, not sanitizer — alcohol gel is flammable and greasy films foul the copper fins, which are the city's throat. Wash your hands before you touch *anything* copper.

## Before you go: the wallet

**Your money is a flask.** Wages, prices, debts — all of it is condensate, measured in the local gradations of volume (learn the measures your first day: there will be a cup, a jug, a run, a cistern, and the differences will matter). Practical consequences:

- **Wealth has weight.** A liter is a kilogram. Twenty liters of capital is a small fortune and an aching shoulder. Keep your flask modest, collapsible, and close to your body — a single-file tunnel is a terrible place to be robbed and an even worse place to be *known* as rich.
- **Never dip your personal vessel into anyone's cistern.** Lips-off, hands-off: pour or accept a ladle. This is the hygiene rule that keeps a hive healthy, and breaking it will mark you faster than any accent.
- **Do not import water as capital.** It's not illegal; it's tone-deaf. Their earth sweats. Arriving with a wagon of bottled water is like bringing sand to a beach — and it subtly insults the entire reason the city exists.

Bridge money, yes: a liter or two sealed, to cover your first night. Then earn. This is the friendliest labor economy you will ever enter — the rails are pushed by hand, by children, by the old, which means an able-bodied adult is genuinely useful on day one. A shift on the bucket-chain, a day scraping mats, an hour oiling fan belts if you know belts. Work is the fastest way to stop being a stranger, and it pays in the only currency that exists.

**Gifts worth carrying in:** fine needles and thread, spice, tea, dried fruit, good pencil lead and waterproof paper (ink weeps in this air), playing cards, quality sewing scissors, spare fan-belt stock or bearings if you can get them — the fan-halls are the city's heart and every fan-wright is hungry for good belting. **Gifts not worth carrying:** anything oily (fins, remember), most electronics (heat and humidity eat them), anything that needs batteries charged overnight in a stacked bunk-niche.

## The descent, and what it feels like

At roughly the twelfth rung the air goes velvet. Your clothes stop fitting — not tightly, just *closely*, like the city is holding you. By the bottom, your surface skin is a memory. Then the light registers, and it takes a while: green-gold, sourceless-seeming, actually coming from the mat terraces themselves — you are lit by the food. The orange pools you'll see are civic anchors: drinking stations, the forge-hall. Learn to navigate by warmth of light.

The thrum arrives next — the fans, the belts, the rails — and then, within your first eight minutes, the hiss. The whole city exhales at once and everything stops. **You will be mid-step. Someone will notice.** Stop. Hold still until the floor finishes breathing. This is the closest thing they have to a bell, a clock, and a prayer, and it is all three at once, and the fastest way to become a local is to start stopping without being reminded. Set your watch to it. Count your errands in hiss-units. By day three you'll stop before it comes, slightly ahead of the room, and feel unreasonably proud.

Sleep, incidentally, is a real consideration. The thrum never fully dies. If you need silence to sleep, you will not get silence; you will get a hive-sized white noise machine. Practice beforehand, or bring wax earplugs — and note that the hiss reaches you through the floor, not the air, so even plugged ears won't leave you deaf to the city's clock.

## The grammar of the place

Three rules, and each one is bigger than it sounds:

**You do not dam another district's seep.** On the surface this means: don't touch water infrastructure that isn't yours. In practice it means don't tamper with fins, don't redirect drips, don't let your cistern overflow into a neighbor's channel, don't hang laundry on copper you didn't earn the right to hang on. Water *is* work *is* law *is* ancestry here — the whole city was dug so water would flow a certain way, and the map of the seeps is the map of the politics. When in doubt, ask before you touch anything wet.

**You listen before you dig.** You're not going to dig. But the rule scales down: listen before you drill an anchor, hammer a bracket, drag a heavy pack through a single-file tunnel. The rock is one generation old and it grumbles — it is still settling into being a ceiling. Micro-tremors are normal; learn which rattles the locals ignore and which make them drift toward the wide galleries. Ask, on day one, what the exits are and where the wideness is. Also, mechanically: bulky packs in single-file tunnels make you a moving wall. Carry compact, step into niches to let others pass, and learn the local "behind you" call.

**When the breathing-hiss comes, you stop moving.** Covered above, but it goes deeper than etiquette — the hiss structures time. Meetings begin at a hiss. Kettles go on at a hiss. If you're bargaining in the market, pauses land on hisses. Don't rush through one.
