**First, correct the map in your head.** Every traveler arrives with a private bargain: *a bowl, half an hour across — I'll be down and up before supper.* Make the bargain now and you'll break it by noon. The rim is farther than it looks and the road down switchbacks more than the walk along the top suggests, and the ground at the bottom gives under you like a thrown rug over a mattress, which means every step costs a little more than a step. Plan the descent as a morning, not a stroll. Start early, eat a real breakfast on the rim, and arrive in working light — because the town finds strangers at the last bend of the road, and you want to be found by day, seen clearly, not just heard crashing in the ferns at dusk.

**Pack for two climates that share one path.** The rim is cool, resinous, dry in the way of old woods; the floor is a warm wet lung. You will strip down to shirtsleeves at the last bend while the rim behind you still wants its coat. So: layers you can peel and stow, a real coat for the rim, and a bag you trust to keep things dry. The single most useful object you can carry is a sealed dry-bag with a change of clothes in it — your *leaving* clothes, packed shut from the start. Everything else you bring will take on the smell of the white sweet smoke and a permanent faint damp, and you will not get either out for weeks, so don't bring anything you'd grieve over. Linen and light wool. Nothing suede, nothing that sulks when wet. A brimmed hat, because the old trees on the First Dike catch the rain and organize it into drip-lines that will find your collar.

**Understand what humidity does to your belongings, because the town long ago made its peace with it and you haven't yet.** Everything metal weeps there. Your glasses will fog at the rim — everyone's do, it's practically the toll — and any cold-soaked phone or camera opened warm will mist internally, so keep gear bagged until it stops sweating, then resign yourself to wiping it nightly. Bring silica gel and use it. Write in pencil; ink sulked here before you were born. The town's own answers — resin-varnish instead of paint, brass instead of steel where it can be spared, grease instead of polish — are worth copying: oil your knife before you come and once a day while you're there.

**Prepare your feet like a sailor prepares for a voyage.** The mat is springy and soft, which sounds kind and isn't — it works your calves the way sand does — and your boots will never fully dry. Bring two pairs and rotate them, grease the leather, dry the insides overnight wherever the guest-loft dries things, and powder your feet with a seriousness you have never previously felt capable of. Check between your toes every night; this is a fungal paradise and the town's hospitality does not extend to your athlete's foot becoming their athlete's foot. Antifungal powder, antihistamines if mold has ever argued with you, and more water than thirst asks for — though drink what's boiled or poured for you, and never what stands in the ditches. The wells are polite holes; the water in them is warm, brown, and honest about being peat. The tea is made from it and it is very good tea, and that is the correct relationship to have with that water.

**Give the drone two nights before you judge it.** The note below music will be in your teeth and sternum within an hour of descending, and earplugs will not stop it, because it isn't arriving through your ears — it's arriving through the floor, the bench, your ribs. Some travelers feel a low seasick restlessness the first night. Nearly everyone is adapted by the second, and by the third a large fraction of visitors report the best sleep of their adult lives, the drone having revealed itself as what it is: a purr, a body at rest keeping its contents warm. If you are in the stubborn minority, there are lodgings on the rim; sleep up there, descend by day, and consider it a cost of your particular nervous system, not a failure of the town. Bring earplugs anyway — for the drip, the leaf-surf, the one neighbor who snores in a different key.

**Bring usefulness, not wealth.** Ask at the rim guesthouse what the bowl is short of this season; it is never short of green. But some things are always welcome in a town where iron-shod wooden screws turn all day: good files and a whetstone, sail needles and waxed twine, scissors, soap in shameful quantity, a folding rule, a jar of salt, reading glasses in common strengths, pencils, a barometer — a genuinely beloved gift in a town whose weather is other people's rain running downhill toward them, and where a falling needle means everyone takes a handle on the pumps. Chocolate travels well if it's sealed and makes a memorable gift at tea. What you must *not* carry in either direction is living green. Scrub your boot treads at the rim before you descend — not superstition, courtesy; a seed on a tread is a stranger the pruners have no rhythm against. And take no cuttings home without leave; the green of the bowl is a lineage, tended longer than any of the people pruning it have been alive, and a shoot tucked in a handkerchief is a matter for the wardens, not a souvenir.

**Learn three words before you go:** *the screws*, *the ring*, *the cut*. Everything else can be pointed at. Then learn the real grammar, which is a tool. You will be handed something within your first hour — a rake, a bill-hook, the handle of a barrow — and taking it is your visa. The town obeys two laws, **pump** and **prune**, constantly and gently, and a guest who merely watches is a guest who has misunderstood; the stillness you see is obedience in motion, and you're welcome to join it. Pluck the shoot you see budding in a channel. Carry your barrow's worth. Stand at the second screw for an hour handing spars along, and afterward you may photograph anyone you like, because you will have become briefly legible to them. If a storm comes while you're there, ask for a place at the pumps. I will tell you now what the town will not: that this is the best day of your trip, and you'll be bragging about it for years.

**Respect the water's rules as if they were physics, because here they are.** Nothing dropped near water that a screw wouldn't forgive: no coins in canals, no lens caps balanced on ditch-boards, no drones — above all no drones. The town already hums in one note, tuned by hands two lifetimes dead; adding a mosquito to the chord is not a photograph, it's a trespass. Keep scarves, straps, and dangling anything clear of the screw flights. They turn at a walking pace, which sounds harmless and is exactly fast enough: the iron-shods do not forgive a sleeve. At night, walk the planks the locals do — tap ahead with your stick, because sluice boards get lifted for cleaning, and the Dike lamps are warm and few. And keep your fire discipline absolute: open flame only where the town keeps its flame, ash in the ash-place. The ground under your boots is decades of compressed fiber and peat, and peat remembers fire for a very long time.

**Save Number Two for dusk.** Go when the lamps come on under their green shades, every bulb beaded with condensation like the whole hall is gently crying, and stand where the bark comes down over the iron and stops at the bolts. Do not touch the bolts. Nobody will tell you not to; you will simply know, the way you know in a church. Warm vapor rises from the join where the tree holds the engine the way a hand holds a wasp — by closing slowly, over two lifetimes, without ever quite closing. Bring nothing. Say little.
