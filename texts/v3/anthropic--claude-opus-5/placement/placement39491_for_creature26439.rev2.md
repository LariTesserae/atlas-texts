## I would be the one who lays the skins

I tried the obvious ones first, the way you try a stone with your foot before you trust it.

The Contrary is too flattering. It is the role that lets you be right by being difficult, and I know myself well enough to know I would enjoy it, and enjoyment is not what that job is for. The Contrary is not supposed to *want* to see the flood of the year 900 slopping over a lip. They are supposed to see it anyway, the way you see a face in a burl, whether or not you are in the mood.

The Bowl is a kind of ending I have not earned. Eighty years old, mostly hands, walking the dome in the dark with a vessel of water, telling twenty-two people the truth about level and nothing else — you do not choose that. It is what is left of you after everything else has gone, if what is left is worth keeping.

The carrier is honest work and the only work here that touches the world, and it is also the one job on the Welt that could be done by anybody at all, which is why the two who do it have lowland faces and are treated with a tenderness that is almost an apology.

No. If I were up there, coughing, damp, cropped, with my palm resting flat on a wall without noticing, I would be a skinner.

---

We are the ones who lay the cloth.

When a plan has survived nine years of charcoal and argument and the Contrary has withdrawn in the bathhouse and the withdrawal has been cut into the Table, and the cutters have gone out and taken the line out of the rock a hair at a time — then the channel is finished, and empty, and absolutely dry, and it is the most dangerous object on the mountain. One rainstorm let into it unskinned and the decision is made forever by weather.

So before anything is proved, it is rehearsed, and rehearsal is cloth.

I sew in the gallery, because it is the only room where stone dries, and so half my life is spent in the one dry place in a world made of wet. Waxed cloth for the runs, greased hide for the weirs and the turns where the water will hit hardest. Seams lapped downstream, always, every one of them, eleven hundred paces of laps all lying the same way like fur. Sinew, and a bone awl, and fat. The fat is the part that shames me. Every finger of grease on a hide seam is a finger of grease that is not going into somebody's porridge, and it came up the stair on a back, three days, and the arithmetic of the carry is the true frontier of this place and everyone knows it. The skinner eats the town's butter. They have never once said so.

Then I go out and lay it. This takes a season. I crawl the channel on my belly and my ruined knees, from the gate down, setting cloth into the cut, bedding it, pinning the lip with a hold-course of little fitted stones that I lift and relay a hundred times, working the wrinkles out with the heel of my hand because a wrinkle is a hesitation and hesitation is where water starts thinking. Nobody knows a line the way I know it. Not the drawer who drew it; they knew it as a bearing and a fall. Not the cutter; they knew nine paces of it a season, as resistance. I know it as a body knows a stair in the dark — every curve of it has been under my ribs.

And then we lift the gate and run water down a sleeve for three seasons, and it never once touches the rock, and we watch, and we adjust, and I pull the whole thing up and re-skin it and we do it again.

I will do this eleven times in my life, perhaps, and four of those will be for lines that are never cut at all, because the Contrary was right after all, or because a new lobe broke out east and changed the whole argument.

Skinners have no line-names. We prove nothing. A skinner is, in the grammar of this place, a permanently incomplete sentence, and they know it, and their kindness about it is worse than contempt would be. I am fed second, after the Contrary.

---

Here is what I would know that nobody else quite lets themselves know.

**The skin lies.**

Not much. But it lies in a particular direction, and always the same direction, and the lie is this: *cloth bridges*. Water in a sleeve is a little braver than water on stone. It does not feel the small hollow the sleeve spans; it does not catch on the glassy bulge it slides over; a greased hide laid across a dip is a bandage, and the water crosses the dip like a man crossing a covered pit, whistling.

We keep a tally of this. Every skinner adds to it — the measured difference between what the sleeve said and what the proved stone did, run by run, generation by generation. The slack, it is called. It is not in the songs and not cut in the Table, because it is not a fact about the mountain, it is a fact about us. The drawers use it. The Contrary uses it as a weapon and comes to me for ammunition, and we have a working friendship of the kind that consists mostly of numbers said flatly across two hundred paces of weir.

And there is a thing nobody asks about the Wander. The Wander bit at its eleventh pace, on a hollow no one's bowl had caught. Three seasons of skin ran over that hollow and said nothing, because a skin lies across a hollow, because that is what a skin does. Sethe took it. Sethe struck the slab and ate the mistake meal and spent twenty-nine years redrawing around her own gorge and is the most honoured name we have. I think about the skinner of that year every time I bed a seam. I have never learned their name. That is not an oversight; skinners don't have names to learn.

That is the particular horror of this trade: my errors are not worn by me. They surface later, under someone else's hands, as someone else's bite, and that person strikes the slab, and everyone comes, and we eat well, and the cloths come down, and I sit there with my grease-black fingernails eating dried fruit and saying nothing useful, because nothing useful is what you say.

---

So we invented the tell-hole.

Every so many paces, in the skin, we cut a window the size of a coin. Water is allowed through it, onto virgin stone, deliberately. It bites. It bites small, and it bites forever.

It is the only damage a Welter is permitted to do alone. It goes on the cloth beforehand and is struck into the Table afterwards, because it is permanent, and permanent things live in stone. We choose the places by feel: over a bulge the sleeve is spanning, at a turn where I don't believe my own laps, anywhere the cloth is being too kind. Then we read them. A tell-hole that fills and sits tells you the fall is dying there. A tell-hole that streaks three fingers downslope in a season tells you the water is going faster than the sleeve admits, and you go back to the drawers with a number.

If you walk the dome you will find these dots everywhere. Lines of them, coin-sized pocks in a dead-straight stipple across ground where no channel runs and no channel will ever run, because that plan was abandoned in the fourth year. Futures that did not happen, punctuated. It is the same handwriting as the scribble-ground, only licensed, and done by an adult, on purpose, for a reason.

It is the nearest I will come to a line-name. My record is a scatter of holes. I damage a very little, on purpose, so that we do not damage everything by accident, and every dot I make is an admission — cut into the floor of the gallery where they sing the Table twice a year — that rehearsal is not the thing, and that I know exactly by how much.

---

I am the wettest person on the mountain and the one whose work is always removed. A skin, when it is pulled, is cut down for foot-sheaths. So everyone here walks on last decade's rehearsals, remade every eleven days, and no one thinks about it, and I think about it constantly and do not mention it, because it is the kind of thought that has no number in it.

---

And when I am dying, they will kneel by the pallet in the long gallery where we lie down in a row like bricks in a town with two thousand beds, and they will ask me the only question, which is not about the soul.

*Is there anything you got wrong that we don't know about?*

I will give them the tell-holes first — the three on the east shoulder I never struck into the Table because I meant to and then it was winter. Small. Someone should walk them.

Then I will tell them the other thing, the one I have carried for eleven years and never said in the bathhouse.

The moss does not grow under a skin. Cloth lies for three seasons and when you pull it the stone beneath comes up bare and pale, and that is ordinary, and that is what always happens.

On the east lobe, on the bearing we had agreed on cloth and had not yet cut, when I pulled the skin, the moss was under it. Six inches deep and thriving, lying the whole length of the line, with the print of my seams in it.

I wrote it down. It is in the gallery; they are welcome to read it. I never theorised.

I would like, at the end, to have said it out loud once.
