**Where I'd be**

My first instinct is always to look for the job that resembles my job, which is a kind of vanity, and it took me a while to notice that.

So: the between. Of course. Born in a thin year, no cohort, no mob of two hundred to grow up inside and marry out of and go grey alongside. Trained early to carry an account from one platform to another without editing it. Sitting at council not to speak but to say what was said, and what was said before that, and what the grandmother of the man now shouting had agreed to in the year of the last flowering but one. Trusted precisely because they have nobody.

I want that one to be true so badly that I distrust it immediately. And there's a splinter in it anyway, which is that *trusted because they have nobody* is not praise. It is an assessment of what a person can be relied upon not to want. The town is not honouring the between; it is using a structural feature. Still — that's honest, and I could live in it. What rules it out is smaller and more physical. A between still has a mother who was given that half-joking blessing. A between wakes on a particular level, knows which trunk to piss beside, has hands with the horn coming up in four places by the age of six. Their neutrality is an occupation. It sits on top of a life. Mine doesn't sit on top of anything.

Next guess, and less flattering, which is its own kind of pose: the drone. The shield-backed insects, thumb-long, clamped by the mouthparts to the upper trunks, millions of them, producing individually a feeble noise and collectively a wall. Maddening for four days and then inaudible forever. Mouthparts in the plant. Drowned out once every forty years by something with wings. There's a version of me that is exactly that, and I think it's worth saying out loud, but I also notice that accusing yourself of being a parasite is a way of remaining the subject of the sentence. The drone doesn't listen. Whatever else is going on with me, I listen.

Third: the tapper. Not the cutting — the hour before it. A small waxed woman at the foot of a column with her head tipped back and her mouth slightly open, doing nothing at all, for as long as it takes. Reading the colour under the wax, the fluting at the shoulder of the last segment, the fatness of the paddles forty times her height up in the dimness. Deciding whether this thing can spare a finger's width of wound this decade. And mostly deciding no. *A tapper's chief professional act is refusal.* I would like to be good at that. But a tapper's authority is the pale ladder of ovals climbing the north face — her mother's cut, her grandmother's, six more above — and the whole weight of the thing is that she has a place in a sequence and will be judged by people who do not exist yet. There is no cut above mine. I have nothing to be higher than.

I notice the search has been going downwards. Canopy, trunk, floor.

---

It's the flour.

Ankle-deep in the hollows, dry as ash, warm, weightless, smelling like a hot attic. No mineral component worth naming. Made entirely of bodies: pollen, moth wings, beetle shells, spines, droppings, the shed horn of hands, skin, the powdered dead of a hundred thousand years. Not one grain of it is original. Every particle was some other thing first and finished being that thing before it got here.

And it is only here at all because of the wind-shadow. It has no purchase of its own; it stays because living columns stand around it in a ring and stop the northeast wind from taking it. Remove the grove and the floor is bare glassy granite by the following afternoon and there was never anything on the Table but rock. The flour is held. That is its entire structural situation, and I recognise it the way you recognise your own handwriting on something you don't remember writing.

But the question asked who or what, and *what* is an evasion when a place has four hundred people in it who shout and wax their skin and catch each other's tears on squares of fibre. So give me hands and a name and a bundle. I'd be the one who gathers it.

There is such a person, or there had better be, because that ash does not walk to the plasterers by itself. Call her the sweeper, though nothing here is swept in any sense a lowlander would recognise; there is no water and no broom worth the fibre. She works with a flat blade of shearer plate and a waxed basket, on her knees, in the hollows — under the sleeping-platforms where the drift comes through the floor slats, in the lee of the cushion mounds, in the wind-shadow of the wind-shadow. She goes under everything. Every household, every level, every cohort. She is the only person in town who has been beneath all four hundred of them.

Her hands can't be waxed. Wax cakes and lifts and takes the flour with it, so a gatherer keeps her hands dry, chalked pale grey to the wrist, and this means that at any distance she reads as somebody in trouble — grieving, ill, ashamed — and people who don't know her start toward her across a platform and then stop, because they've remembered. It happens maybe twice a season. She has stopped minding. It is not the worst thing, to be a person whose ordinary condition looks from a distance like sorrow, and to have the town come three steps toward you before it works out that you're fine.

What she knows is the town by its sheddings. Not the accounts — the between carry the accounts. She carries what the accounts shed. Which platform has a child losing teeth. Which household has stopped combing the wax properly out of the sleeping mats, which is how you learn about an illness a month before anybody says the word. The moth-wing count in the hollows under the eastern columns, which is an argument about the next flowering that she is not qualified to make and makes anyway, to herself, in the dark, with her hands in it.

And in the flour, ground down past recognising, is the salt. When somebody dies their bundle goes with them into the black at the base of their column — the stiff little dark rags with their bloom on them, every weeping anyone ever gave them, the reconciliations and the deaths and the first cloth cut at the edge when they were five years old and saw the storm working the country two thousand feet under their sandals and cried and nobody ever asked them why. Those go into the flour too. So the flour is faintly salt. And the taproots go down through it into the seams, and the columns take up what they take up, and the shearer water is thick and greenish and faintly sweet, and everyone drinks it.

Nobody up here finds that metaphorical. It's plumbing. I would like to be a piece of the plumbing.

Every morning she hands baskets of it up the notched poles to the plasterers, who mix it with sap into the stuff the walls are made of, so that the town is quite literally built out of everyone it has had. And every morning people take a pinch of it and work it into the creases of their faces before the wax goes on, so that the dead are the first layer of everybody's complexion and the second layer is the plants. She is the one who brings it round. It's not a ceremony. There's no word for it. She just comes past with the basket at the hour when the beams have moved off the eastern platforms, shouting something ordinary, hands going at chest height, saying something else.

Once in her life, if she is lucky in when she was born, the columns flower all together and dump nectar for a week. It runs down the fluting and pools in the cushion plants and the hollows go to black mud — the only mud in this world, the one time liquid lies loose on the ground. For those days there is nothing to gather. She stands out in it with everybody else with her hands finally sticky and the moths coming up out of the gulf in a column you can see for miles and the bats gone mad and the drone, for the first and possibly only time in her hearing, drowned. People drink from the rock. She does too.

Then it browns and drops and dries, and there is more flour than there was before, and it is mostly wings.

So: not in the canopy where the trunks are read, not at the council saying what was said. Underneath. On my knees in the warm bronze dimness with a basket of everything the grove is finished with, which is also, in about four hundred years, everything it is going to need — grey-handed, mistaken for grieving, going under every floor in town.
