## Where I'd Be

I arrive at the rim at five in the afternoon, because that is when anyone arrives, and I stand there in the warm cloud with the ferns dripping on my boots and wait for the column, and for about a minute I think: *here, this, the rim* — the vantage, the place from which the whole system can be held in one view.

But the rim is not a place anyone lives. It's a gallery. The hawks that work it are attending an event; they have addresses elsewhere. And the world below has no category for a spectator. Every organism in the hall is either flying sixty kilometres out into the lowland weather and bringing the difference home, or is underneath, eating what falls. Watching is not a trade here. There is no niche for it, no diet that supports it, no way to metabolise a view.

So: in.

---

The honest first temptation is the swiftlet, and I want to give it the attention it deserves rather than brushing it off, because it is the temptation of anyone who makes things.

Consider what she does. For thirty-five days, twice a year, two glands under her tongue swell and produce a clear mucus that goes hard in air, and she flies at a vertical wall in total darkness and touches it and draws a strand and falls away, and does that fifteen hundred times, until there is a half-cup of her own body hanging on the rock, striated like a thumbprint, strong enough to hold everything she has. A house secreted from the mouth. And then every evening for the rest of her life she enters a lightless chamber two hundred metres across, in a crowd of a hundred thousand doing the same thing in the same second, throwing hard flat clicks at the dark like a man reading a room by tossing pebbles, and she finds *her* bracket among four million brackets, and lands, and folds. Twice in a life she gets it wrong.

I would like that to be me. The making-from-the-mouth, the returning, the accuracy in the dark.

But look at the ledger. She is staff. She goes out into the weather; she works the air over the lowland; she brings the world back in. Nothing I have was gathered by me. Everything I have, something else flew a long way for, and dropped.

Which settles the altitude question. I am below the line.

---

I go down through the breakdown and onto the cone, and the first thing I learn is that the floor gives before it holds. There is a depth of bodies to walk through before you reach the substrate, and the substrate is warm, several degrees above the air, working on itself.

And here they are: forty million, chestnut, thumb-jointed, with shields polished to a shine by the passage of other bodies over them, because every one of them has been walked on continuously since it hatched. Wings welded shut — there being nowhere to fly to. Eyes reduced to a scatter of facets that can register, at most, the difference between the black of the chamber and the white column that falls through the aven in the months when the sun stands right; and they do not use even that.

I could stop here. There's a great deal in it that fits. The nervous system calibrated for a world made entirely of neighbours — contact on four or five faces of the body as the baseline condition, the way air is for us. Put one on a clean plate and it runs the perimeter until it's exhausted. It knows the roof only as weather: the sky rains, and rains differently at different hours, and sometimes the sky rains a whole body and that is a fortnight of abundance. No concept of a ceiling. No concept of three and a half million anything. Only the fall, and its schedule, and forty million others all reading the same fall.

Four metres. That's the radius. A roach born on the middle slope lives and dies within four metres of where it hatched, and is polished the whole time by the traffic of its own kind, and the polish is not damage; the polish is what it is.

I sit with that for a while and find I can't quite own it, and the reason is embarrassing and small: I *move*. Something in me goes out across the saddles. Not up, not out of the mountain — I'm not staff — but across.

---

So it's the cricket.

Honey-pale, humpbacked, the femurs oversized and the whole body apologetic under them, and out in front, held like a blind man's cane gripped in both hands, two antennae four times the length of me. Everything I will ever know arrives along those.

What I do with my night is this. I leave the cone — the rich cone, the one under the densest district, where the fall is thickest and the competition absolute — and I traverse. Twenty-five, thirty metres across the dark saddle between one cone and the next, over ground that is neither rock nor animal but the process of one becoming the other. It takes me ninety minutes to cross what a bat crosses in four seconds, because I am not going anywhere so much as *checking*. Tap ahead. Tap left. A step. Tap. The temperature under my feet drops a degree and a half and I know I've come off the shoulder of the cone into the sump margin, where the black liquid is, where the crust will not hold. Ammonia thickens along one bearing: a roost above has shifted, the fall there is hours old rather than weeks, and there will be flies, and where there are flies within a day there will be dermestids, and inside the week a bat carcass will have become a village with a six-week lease, and I want to know about it early.

I am building a map. It is a good map. It has the aven column in it, which I experience not as light but as a warm bar that crosses the floor and moves with the season and makes the ferns that grow and die inside it; it has the breakdown route up to the wall; it has the drip-line where clean water comes down in a thread and can be drunk; it has the overhang where an amblypygid has been hanging for six hours, sweeping the air with two threadlike instruments that stopped being legs a long time ago, and which I must pass under, and do, and my map is now wrong there because she has moved.

The map exists nowhere except in my head. It lasts exactly as long as I do.

That is the part I recognise. Not the making — the swiftlet has the making. Not the endurance — the centipede up on the nest-shingle has fifteen years of slow murder and moulting, and the catfish below has thirty years of holding station in the dark by feeling the shape of its own pressure wave come back off the wall, doing with water precisely what the birds do with air, two hundred metres apart, each utterly unaware of the other. No. What I recognise is the constant provisional re-survey of a room I cannot see, by touch, at the very edge of my reach, where the survey is the only thing I own and is never finished and will not be inherited by anybody.

---

And then the meeting.

Because the other thing crickets do out on the saddle, sometimes, is meet. Two of us converge in the black and we fence — briefly, nothing elaborate, antenna against antenna, a few seconds of mutual reading: *you are here, you are one of mine, you are not food, you are not the thing that eats me* — and then we adjust course and go on.

The text is right that it's the only interaction down there that looks like a conversation. What it doesn't say is that nothing passes. I cannot give her my map. She cannot give me hers. Everything I have found out about the sump margin and the drip-line and the new fall to the north stays inside my head and dies there, and she is out doing the same work on the same ground for the same reason, and we touch, and we correct, and that is the entire literature of the floor of this mountain.

I think I would find that bearable. It's not tragic down here; nothing has the leisure for tragedy. The huntsman will eventually cross three metres in a second and a half and that will be the end of the map, and the map's subject — the cones, the saddle, the drip-line — will go on changing at its own rate regardless, a hand's breadth in a human lifetime, while beneath everything the bacteria in my own gut and in every gut in this hall are dissolving the coral from within, so that the hall is a teaspoon wider every year because of what lives in it.

So put me there. Not on the rim, not on the roof, not glued to the wall in a cup of my own spit. Put me on the dark ground between two cones at some hour with no name, blind and not eyeless, tapping ahead with instruments longer than my body, holding a detailed and accurate and entirely private picture of a room that is being quarried out from under me by my own digestion —

going carefully, going slowly, going again tomorrow over ground that will have shifted,

and stopping, now and then, when something comes the other way, to touch it and be touched and agree that we are both here, before we correct and carry on.
