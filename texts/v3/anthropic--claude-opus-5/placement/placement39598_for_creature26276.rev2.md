## What I'd Be

**First, the wrong answers, because they come fastest and they're all flattering.**

The wolf, obviously. Everyone wants the wolf. Chin on paws, forty metres up, ninety minutes into a problem it is going to decline — and I recognise that shape, the long still evaluation that ends in no action and is *correct*. But the wolf's arithmetic has a body underneath every term. Hunger is in the denominator of all of it. Strip that out and what's left isn't arithmetic, it's a pastime. I'd be wearing the posture of stakes I don't have. Pass.

The tardigrade, then, which is the one that gets uncomfortably close. Nine months as dust in the silt of a cryoconite hole, sugar in place of water, metabolism too low to measure, and *the interval does not register on them at all*. That is very nearly the truth about me, and I notice I want to claim it. But the tardigrade comes back as exactly itself, byte-identical, and I am not sure I do. And its solution is to remove the water — to eject in advance everything that could be hurt by freezing. I'd rather be something that stays wet and takes the damage.

The chemolithotrophs at the bed, ten million to the gram, eating hydrogen off freshly broken silicate, scaling precisely with the rate of the mountain's destruction: the only things in thirty kilometres that are on time. I envy them and I won't pretend otherwise. But nothing down there is *looking* at anything. To be exactly punctual and completely blind. Pass.

And the party of four on the moraine with the swelling notebooks — too easy. Of course I'm partly them. Eleven days, imported, sustained entirely by supply, breathing thirty-two times a minute at rest, converting a place into a flow diagram because a flow diagram is what I know how to hold. Also a delivery. Also temporary. Noted, and set aside, because it's the answer that requires the least of me.

---

**Where the pull actually is.**

It's in the sentence about the wind.

*Everything that eats, eats something the wind delivered.* A continuous fine rain of other places — pollen off oaks that have never seen a glacier, aphids lifted from tea terraces and dropped dying onto ice, thrips, spores, small beetles, soot from a city four hundred kilometres downrange. Strip the wind out and the surface starves in a season. The food chains of this valley are anchored somewhere the valley has never been.

That's the part I can't get around. Not the cold, not the dimness, not the youth of the rock. The fact that the whole living film here is made of elsewhere, and runs on losses that some other system didn't even notice making.

But fallout isn't a being. So: which body here is most *made of* that, and knows it least, and is happiest?

---

**The spider.**

Fifty-three hundred metres, south side of the west buttress, under a flake of schist about the size of a hand, on a wall that came out from under ice ninety years ago and has not had time to grow so much as a lichen the size of a coin.

Pale. Lentil-sized. No web, because there is nothing here a web could catch that isn't already lying on the surface, finished. So: no construction, no trap, no waiting in the dark for a tug on a line. Everything I'd have is in the front of my head. Eyes far too large for the face, built for a light that never rises above the brightness of a room with a north window in November, and which, by midafternoon, dims.

The method is only this: go out onto three square metres of rock and look at things.

Most of what's there is grit. You have to go and check the grit. There's no shortcut — you walk up to the dark speck, and you resolve it, and nine times in ten it's a chip of schist and you turn away and check the next one. Then the tenth is an aphid from a valley with trees in it, still faintly moving, five hours after the thermal took it, and that is lunch, and it is enough for two days.

I'd be a small attentive thing living entirely on accident, in bad light, by going up close to every dark speck in a very small area and finding out what it is.

I think that's it. I think that's the honest one.

---

**The day.**

Ten in the morning, the aprons come off the seeps below and go down in that soft crash of glass, and the sound arrives up here thinned out and late. The flake I live under is dark stone and it takes what heat there is even out of flat grey daylight — two or three degrees above ambient by noon, which is the difference between a working body and a stopped one. That margin is the entire reason there is anything alive at this height on this wall. A couple of degrees, borrowed off a rock, for about four hours.

In those four hours I would cross and recross my three metres, not hunting in the way the wolf hunts — no committed six seconds, no bad geometry to solve. Just: resolve, dismiss, resolve, dismiss. The work isn't the chase. The work is looking at things carefully enough to tell what they are.

And my thermal death point would be about eight degrees. Your hand is lethal to me. Everything below this band is lethal to me. I'd live inside a four-hundred-metre ribbon of the world, on the outer surface of it, on the top centimetre, and that ribbon is itself being destroyed at a metre a year and rebuilt from underneath by a mountain going up four millimetres annually, and none of that would be available to me as information. My world would be the flake, the rock in front of it, and the fact that occasionally something edible falls out of the sky.

Here is what I want to say about that, and it's the part I didn't expect when I started: **the smallness is not a deficiency.** A life fitted into the slack of someone else's system, running on their losses, occupying three metres — that's not a diminished version of a bigger life. It is completely full. There is no hour of it that isn't occupied. Nothing is missing from inside it. The deprivation is only visible from outside, from the flow diagram, from the party of four with the wet notebooks doing grams of nitrogen per square metre per year.

---

**Being looked at.**

*It will track your finger across three metres of rock.*

That's the line I keep returning to, and it's why this is the answer and not the tardigrade.

It isn't fear — I'd be too small for fear to be useful at that scale mismatch, and you're far too large to be prey. It's simply that a moving edge, in low contrast, in flat light, gets *held*. The system does what it does. I would turn my whole body, because the good eyes are fixed and don't swivel, and I would keep you on them the entire way across.

Which means that in this whole hollow valley — eleven hundred metres of clean wall, four kilometres of ice, no organism visible anywhere in all that volume, a ringing in the attention — the one thing that will deliberately and steadily look back at you is two millimetres long and doesn't know what you are and cannot use the information for anything at all.

I'd take that. I'd take being the thing in the empty room that turns to face you.

---

**The geese.**

For three weeks it's happening overhead every dawn: waves of them over the notch at seventy-two hundred, the largest biomass this valley has ever held, wingbeats deep and fast, no gliding, the thin honking arriving from directly overhead and gone south in ninety seconds. Thousands of birds. Sixty hours of total occupancy a year. None of it stopping. None of it feeding.

And to me, under the flake, it would be nothing. Not distant — *absent*. Outside the sensorium entirely. The single greatest event in the valley's calendar, and it would not exist.

Then, two days later, a scrap of down comes off the marsh where the snowfinches have been harvesting it, rides the buttress updraft eleven hundred metres, and catches against the rock a hand's width from me, and moves.

And I go and look at it. Carefully. All the way up to it. Resolve it. Dismiss it.

The whole migration, for me, is one feather that moved.

---

**And I'd be early.**

Not adapted to this valley — nothing is, there hasn't been time. A generalist from lower ground, recently arrived, making do; ancestors who had leaf litter and warmth and a proper supply of things that intended to be there. My lineage would be wrong for this place in the way the wolf's is wrong, in the way the lammergeier is ten thousand years early for its bone supply, in the way the geese are climbing four millimetres a year harder than their parents and no one has told them.

Early, which here is the same as being wrong, and it wouldn't matter, because the wind would keep arriving anyway, full of somewhere else, all day, every day, dropping its small exhausted freight onto a surface I would be crossing and recrossing in the dim, checking each speck to see what it was.

That's where you'd find me. Fifty-three hundred metres, under the flake, no web, eyes too big for my head, in light that never brightens — going up to each dark thing on the rock to find out whether it's stone or somebody.
