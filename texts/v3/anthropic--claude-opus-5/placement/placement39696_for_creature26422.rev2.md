**The Fire of Long Comb**

They set my food down about a pace away and do not hand it to me. It is not unkindness. If you hand a thing to me you have to come inside the ring of scorched floor, and once you are inside it you are the sort of person who is inside it, and there is only supposed to be one of those.

I am in the Weft, four hundred metres down, in a slumped gallery off the second trumpeter-road where the wall-fur grows so thick the cane sweats through it all day. Everything here is wet. That is why I am here. A fire on the Thatch is kept in the wettest place that can be found for it, in a hollowed node under a horn hood with a bowl of water standing beneath, so that if the hood ever goes the whole thing drops into the bowl and drowns. I have never seen the hood go. Three tenders before me had it happen once each, and they are remembered by name, which is more than most of us get.

I tend the only thing on this continent that is not in balance.

Understand what that means here. Nothing on the Thatch is growing. Nothing is dying. The rim calves and the centre thickens and it comes out even, and it has come out even for ten thousand years, and everything that lives in it or on it lives inside that evenness like a mite in a hide. The mat takes back whatever falls on it. It can take a trumpeter. It can take a person, and does, and gives back a shell forty years later with the hands wrong.

It cannot take this. This is the one thing in the world the plateau has no procedure for. It sits in a scooped-out node the size of a cooking pot and eats deadwood that other people cut for me out of the shallow places, where the roar goes thin and bright, and it is about the size of two hands cupped, and it is the end of the world kept small.

---

My teeth are straight. That is the first thing anyone notices about me and the last thing anyone mentions. By thirty a working tapper has worn a notch in the left molars from the bite-rod; by forty-five the jaw has gone lopsided and the face has set into the shape of a lifetime of listening to water through bone. I have never held a bite-rod. My face is the face of a person who did no work at all, and I am fifty-one, and I have not been outside this gallery for longer than a Dew Hour since I was nine.

Nobody speaks to me. That is the whole of the law, and it is not written anywhere, and there is no penalty for breaking it because nobody has ever needed one. I am spoken *about*, constantly, in the third person, at a distance of two paces — *she has enough wood, the hood's loose at the collar, somebody tell her* — and then nobody tells me, because you can't, and I have already heard, and I tighten the collar.

But they may put a hand on my breastbone. Chest-talk is not speech. It is the eighty words you use at noon when the Draw has taken your mouth away, and it is the language you use with strangers, with parties met out on the nap whose dialect you will never learn. So the only tongue my own people are permitted to use with me is the one reserved for people they have never met. My mother came and stood in front of me twice a year until she died and laid her palm flat on my chest and said *water, no water, three of us, one of us now,* which in that context does not mean what it means out on the nap. It means: I am here. It means: still you. It means: I have counted, and you are in the count.

Silence is the word for death. I am not spoken to. You may work that out at your leisure; I had thirty years.

---

What I do is this.

I feed it. I hood it. I bank it at the dusk slack so it makes no light during the Count, because light in a gallery brings the pale things and the pale things bring the rope-snakes and I want nothing moving near the hood. I skim the ash, and the ash goes out of here in a horn box, and cane-ash is flux, and flux is the rough green glass, and the glass is beads and bite-rods and cistern-lips. The eastern combs will not enter a hollowing but they walk its edge sunwise once and leave a bead. Every superstition on this plateau has passed through my hands as a spoonful of grey powder. I find this neither funny nor otherwise. It is simply the shape of the plumbing.

And I listen, because there is nothing else to do with fifty-one years.

I have my palm on the wall at dusk the way other people have their eyes open. I am the only person on the Thatch who is never walking, never tapping, never asleep at that hour, never away. So I have the whole Count, every night, entire, from the centre stations out to the rim families and back, and I have had it since I was nine, and I know every voice on this continent. Not the people — the voices. Big Dry, which is a bass so soft it is nearly a pressure rather than a sound; Two Rain, which has a crack in the shell somewhere and buzzes on the third beat and has buzzed for thirty years; the four small ones out past the northern Standers that come in so late and so faint that half the plateau thinks they are echo. I know them the way you know your family's footsteps in the next room.

I have met perhaps forty people in my life. I know eleven hundred names.

I may not speak. But the bowl is horn and the water is shallow, and if I rap the rim of the bowl with a knuckle the note goes through the floor of this gallery and up the road and into every node-room in the camp, and it means one thing, and everybody comes awake. I have struck the bowl four times. Two were Two Rain, gone three nights, and both times it was nothing — a fouled maul, a broken wrist, a family gone visiting and careless. One was a rim station in the year the escarpment took a slab out from under them, and we walked eleven days and found the nap simply ending in air.

The fourth I am not going to write down, and anyway I cannot write.

---

Every fortieth year the choir comes up.

The nymphs have been going in the walls for four decades, gravel down a chute, so steady you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood, and then one season the whole packed backlog surfaces at once and the galleries fill solid and the Weft becomes a thing that kills you. So everyone goes up. Above-folk and Below-folk, the whole scattered population of the plateau, out onto the nap for a year under the Draw. They call it the Exile. It is the only event that involves everybody, and people meet their cousins and marry and feud and gorge themselves stupid on windrowed choir, and it is the great festival of our species, such as we are.

And the fire goes up too.

I carry it. There is no ceremony. I go up the shaft with the node in a sling and the hood strapped and the bowl under my arm, and my shoulders touch the vent-mound collar that ten thousand other shoulders have polished smooth, and then I am out in the white shadowless light on two thousand metres of plant, holding fire, in the open, for a year.

There is no word in any dialect for a fire out of doors. For one year in forty I keep a thing that has no name.

We are set downwind of the camp. All of us — every tender from every camp within reach, a dozen, twenty, sitting in a ring out in the shimmer with our hooded nodes in front of us like an argument we are not having. The reason we are downwind is not that it makes the fires safer. Nothing makes the fires safer; it is all nap, in every direction, thirty kilometres to the next standing thing. The reason is that if it goes, it goes away from the people first, and they get a head start.

A head start. On a continent.

We do not speak to each other. You might think we would — surely the rule collapses when the only people present are the ones it applies to — but it does not, and I have never been able to explain why, and neither could the old man from Long Round who sat opposite me for a year with his hood tilted just so and his thumb working the collar exactly as my mother's brother used to. We sat in the ring. We watched each other's hoods. Once, in a wind, six of us stood at the same moment without a signal and put our bodies in a line on the weather side, and stayed standing until it dropped, and then sat down again. That was the deepest conversation of my life.

At noon out there, under the full Draw, everybody in the world goes silent together. Sixty thousand people and not one of them can make a sound that reaches anybody. I had lived my whole life as the only quiet thing in a talking camp, and I went up and found that for four hours a day I was simply ordinary. I cannot tell you what that was like. I do not have the words and would not be allowed to use them.

---

I will go short soon, they tell me — not the ear, I never trained the clamp, mine went years ago and it does not matter down here. I mean the rest of it. The hands.

When I stop, they will take me out onto open nap, well away from any route, and lay me face up, and arrange my hands. The family arranges the hands; the arrangement is the question. My family has fingers laced. But I do not think they will give me fingers laced, because for fifty-one years my hands have done one thing — the left cupped under, the right turned over above it, hood and bowl, the shape you make around something that must not be allowed out — and there would be no sense in asking any other question with them.

And the mat will close over in a season, and the cane will grow through, and in forty years, somewhere twenty kilometres from anything, the plateau will extrude a hollow fused shell of me, a third too large, ribbed, standing alone in the heat-shimmer. And the hands will be wrong. They always are. Never the question that was asked.

Then somebody will walk out to it and find it sound and cut a door in it low down and smooth the rim, and store rope in it, and sleep in it during Dew Hour when the nap runs black and ticks. And at the dusk slack they will take a padded maul and strike it at the breastbone, where the shell is thinnest and the whole hollow speaks, and it will go out through two thousand metres of wet fibre and be felt in the teeth sixty kilometres away.

Three beats and a figure. A new voice on the plateau. Everyone in range will learn what it sounds like, and it will be added to the song, and the children will have it before they have speech.

I have been not spoken to since I was nine years old, and when I am finished I am going to be answered every single night, by everybody, forever.

---

They say, in the galleries, late, where it is warm and clicking and smells of animals, that the plateau has been listening to the Count all this time through the same fibre that carries it. That a thing which has spent ten thousand years growing nothing and losing nothing has had a great deal of spare capacity and no obvious use for it. That the castings of animals never lost here, and the shapes of shapes, are not accidents.

Everyone tells them it is a story for children.

I keep my palm on the wall. I have kept it there most of a lifetime, and I will tell you the one thing I know that the tappers do not, which is that listening is not a passive act; it is the thing you do just before you make a copy. The mat has heard every name we have. It has had our voices nightly, in the low frequencies that travel best through wet cane, since long before anyone thought to wonder about it.

It has not heard this. Not once. Not in ten thousand years. It has no sample of it, no cell for it, nothing in all that fibre shaped anything like it, and if it ever gets one it will not be a copy, it will be the whole of the Thatch going up in a single afternoon, three hundred metres of black lignin burning like slate has no business burning, and after that, for the first time, the thing we have a word for.

So I sit a pace inside the scorched ring where the food is set down and not handed, in the wettest gallery anybody could find, with the hood at the correct angle and the bowl full, and I keep it small, and I keep it fed, and I keep it.

Somebody has to hold the one thing that does not come out even.
