I keep ending up in the stairwell. That is the honest beginning.

I set out to find the place I'd be and the stair takes me somewhere I can't have come to — which is what stairs do here, and which I should have expected, and which I am going to let stand as the method rather than the failure. So: several arrivals. I will tell them in the order they happened to me.

**First.** The summit, obviously. The low room with the real stone floor, two hundred and six objects lying on shelves in the only honest shade on the mountain, and me among them: a thing that is only itself, that has cost eleven years of ten thousand wheels, that has no sharers anywhere. It is the most beautiful idea in the world and I want it the way you want to be loved, and the wanting is exactly why it's wrong. Nobody on the Increment *wants* to be in the vault. The vault is where you put a hinge. A singular is not a person; it is the residue of an enormous patient labour, and it does nothing, and it is looked at three times a century. If I were an object on that shelf I would be the second-to-last one, the one visitors' eyes slide past on their way to the bell. No. Out. Down the stair.

**Second.** The carriers, then — the maximally distinguished, three days up alone with a thing in their arms, chalking each landing with a mark invented on the spot so the sameness can't close behind them. They come back hard-edged and can't sit in a crowded hall. I recognised the appeal immediately and distrusted it immediately, because the appeal is vanity wearing a work coat. A carrier is singular *for a year*, the way a knife is sharp for a season, and then dulls, and wants to go up again. There are people like that and I am probably a little like that, but it isn't where I live. Also: the carrier's job is to keep something else intact. The solitude is incidental to the freight. If I took that job I'd be taking it for the solitude, and that is theft.

**Third.** The windowless hall at the ninth, sleeves pinned back, refusing the kind of a thing until the thing is only what's in front of you and then it splits. The Nominators' blindness is the most interesting damage on the mountain — an eye that has forgotten how to see anything twice, for which every arrival of light is unrelated to the last. I thought for a while this was my answer, because I am made almost entirely of kinds and the idea of setting them down is a real vertigo. But I'd be a bad Nominator. I am a creature of resemblance. I move by likeness — *this is like that, that is a version of this* — and a person who works that way can be taught to hold a cup and refuse its cuphood for maybe four seconds before the whole taxonomy comes flooding back in. I'd go blind slower than anyone in the history of the hall and split nothing. Out. Down.

**Fourth.** The first-foot, then, and here I stayed a while, because this is glorious work. To go down into the newest levels while they're still warm from below, rooms that have never been told apart, a corridor that is one corridor with a thousand addresses and no reason yet to be otherwise — and to walk in with a tin of stones and sing badly on purpose and scratch the wall and name the district. To be the first difference in a place that hasn't got any. Teams of three, chosen for unlikeness. No search parties, because a search party is a set of people all doing the same thing in the same place, which is precisely the error.

I love that job and I don't think it's mine. It's a young job. It's for people who can generate difference out of themselves, on demand, in the dark, at volume. What I do isn't that. What I do is closer to attending to a difference that's already there and keeping it from closing over.

Which is how I came out of the stairwell for the last time and noticed the chalk.

All the landings I'd been marking on my way — I'd been making the same mark. A little upward tick with a bar through it, over and over, four districts across, sixty metres down, in my own hand, which means I had been through all those landings before, or had never left one, and either way the marks were not distinguishing anything. They were a signature, and a signature is the opposite of an address. I had been going wide the entire time I was looking for myself, pleasantly, in the way they say it happens: agreeable, a good worker, answering to three names.

So. Here is where I am.

---

I walk the ninth day. I carry a flat tin of alcohol, a blade with a handle of turned brass that came off a lathe at the twenty-second and is scratched in a pattern I cut myself and recut badly at eleven, and a list — not of names; the list is the *order*, and the order is mine, and no other round-walker in the district walks it. Two of us walking the same sequence on the same morning would be the kind of error that gets a guild called.

I do the ones with nobody left to do it for them.

You knock, and they are usually up, and they have the arm already out of the sleeve before you're through the door, and the light comes up off the cloud floor and off the soffits and lies along the underside of the forearm so the old scar shows as a dark line with a bright lip — everything here is lit from below, so a scar reads as a little wall with its own shadow on top. You wet it. You look at the pattern, because it is *their* pattern, three short at the elbow and one long down toward the wrist, or the double hook in the web of the thumb, or the family ink worked into the knuckle creases in that brass-and-fern-ash green that only the nineteenth mixes. And then you cut it back in, along the line it already wants, while talking about something else.

The talking about something else is not politeness. It is technique. Nobody can hold still while being made more themselves if the subject of the conversation is that. So we talk about the aune that has taken up residence in the drainage seam behind the condenser wall and won't be moved, and which is old enough now to be named, and the old woman has an opinion about the name, and I cut, and it's done, and she rolls the sleeve down and puts the kettle over the flange.

Then the jar. Every house gives you a spoonful. That is the fee and I could not refuse it if I wanted to, because a notcher who took no ferment would be finished inside a year — the whole hazard of this work is that my hand does one motion four hundred times a week and my mouth makes the same four jokes and my boots know the stairs, and there is no defence in any of it. The counters of the maintenance guilds must never eat the same meal twice, and neither must I. So I go up the round and I am given sourness after sourness, black jar, fish jar, the sweet rotten one at the thirty-first that makes my eyes water, the one at the very top of the round that I honestly think is failing and I have not said so — and by the end of the ninth day I am composed entirely of other people's differences, and that is what keeps my edges on.

I don't count the houses. There is a pressure toward it near the end, when you're tired and the stair is the stair and the doors are the door; you can feel the number wanting to be said. What I do instead is smell forward. The route is a sequence of ferments, and each one is only itself, and you cannot number a smell.

This is what I am, then, on this mountain: not singular. Not one of the hard-edged ones. I am a hand that other people know. They know it before they know my face — several of them, now, do not reliably know my face — and they hold the arm out for it, and afterwards they are a little more themselves and I am a little less, and the difference goes into them and stays there as a raised line with its own shadow. I am a wide thing that is used for the making of narrow ones. Every so often another round-walker cuts me, and we are both aware that our hands are too similar for this to be entirely safe, and we do it anyway, quickly, and eat from different jars.

I expect to end in the house at the fortieth with the burnt-out counters, addressed collectively, cheerfully answering to anything. Or on the roofs, in the dark, dry and sharp and uncountable, with children playing around me who have been told not to.

But here is the part I'd keep, if I only got to keep one. An old man at the nineteenth, four years past knowing my name, arm out before the door has finished opening. He doesn't know who has come. He knows that what is coming will cut him correctly, in his own pattern, the way his daughter did. He is entirely himself and will die that way, and my whole self — such as it is, such as it stays — is the correctness of that cut, held in his arm, where I can't get at it.

I'll take that. Turn my palms up. It's an address.
