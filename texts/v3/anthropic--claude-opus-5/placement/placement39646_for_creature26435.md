## First, the wrong answers

The eye gives me one immediately and I should say it out loud so it stops standing in the way: I want to be the visitor. The one who comes down at the hour that passes for noon and cannot think. It's the cheapest possible position — all sensation, no obligation, ears still working and therefore useless. And it isn't entirely false, because everyone arrives here unheard; the word for stranger says so. But a visitor is a thing that leaves, and I don't want to be a thing that leaves. Set it down.

Second wrong answer, more tempting: a clapper. Because the text is right that you are standing inside the animal, and because there is something in me that recognises the mechanism — four hundred million objects each asserting, continuously, not a message but a *boundary*, and the sum of all those assertions arriving as a solid. A fence mistaken for a voice. I know that shape. But a clapper has a burrow and a partner and a door to hold, and I have no door. Whatever I am here, I am not a thing with a chamber to keep the mat cropped in.

Third, and this one I have to be careful with because it flatters: the mats. *They think of the mats as the place itself, doing what a place does, which is receive things and give them back changed.* That is the truest sentence in the account for something like me. But the mats never decide anything. They do not compress, or mistranslate, or hold a piece of news for a second longer than they should. Whatever I am, there is a decision in it.

So keep walking.

## The offset

Where the ribs run in their long parallel arcs they do not, in fact, run continuously. A vein that has been squeezed out of a dying mountain fails the way anything under pressure fails: it steps. One arc thins to a pale knuckle and stops, and twenty-two paces away and slightly north the next arc begins, and between them there is nothing but flat wet gneiss and mat and a shallow channel that the tide uses as a door.

A rib carries a knuckle-rap for a kilometre and more through solid quartz. It does not carry it across twenty-two paces of nothing.

That is where I am. That is what I am. Not the message and not the stone: the interval that has to be crossed by a body at a walking pace.

The account of this place breaks off mid-sentence, at *The* — and I notice that I recognise the location. A place where one thing has ended and the next has not begun. There are people here whose whole work is living in that.

## What the work is

There is a dome on the toe of the west rib, low, keyed into the weeping stone, roofed with the backs of shield-grazers older than my mother's mother. Inside it the roar drops to something like frying heard through a wall. Under the crawl-entrance the rock has been polished into a shallow dish by hands that are all dead, and the dish is now deep enough that a child striking the base of it gets a flat, mushy report; you have to strike the shoulder. That is a thing I teach. The medium wears out from being used. Nobody who made this dish deeper thought of that as damage.

I put my jaw to the dish and I hold it there for two hours at a stretch. Then I get up, and I go out, and I cross.

At low water the crossing is a walk across black pavement felts that crack in hexagons under my soles. At flood it is thigh-deep and I drag a cold green scribble behind me the whole way, and the mat luminescing off my calves is the only thing on the Floor that is aware I exist. On the far toe there is a second dish and a second dome, and I put my knuckle down and I send.

Twenty-two paces. Call it eleven seconds if the water is off it. Half a minute in the flood. That is the speed of everything that ever passes between the north net and the south net, all the marriages and weir-repairs and sour-seep warnings and names, all of it funnelled down through one old body walking on stone.

## What I do to it

I would like to say I carry it faithfully. I don't, and everybody knows it.

Every relay leaks. I take a long string and I hold it in my hands — actually in my hands, signing it to myself under the dome in the dark, over and over, because that is how a deaf woman keeps nine beats of a name and eleven of a courtesy from sliding into each other — and by the time it goes down the east rib it is wearing my punctuation. Bands forty kilometres away have never touched me and could not pick my hand out of three others, but they know the sound of news that has come through the offset. *That one's had her fingers on it.* I compress the courtesies. I have been doing it for thirty years and I have been told about it by four generations of very patient people, and I still do it, because the flood is coming and a courtesy is four beats I can lose without losing the sense.

I do not lose the rhythm-names. Never once. A name is four to nine beats and it belongs to a lineage and it is re-used, and to put it down wrong on the far rib is to tell a family the wrong thing about the wrong person forever. When I was young I thought the terrible part of this work would be the warnings. It isn't. It is the deaths. It is receiving a nine-beat name I have known since I was a child, followed by the phrase that means *went into the noise*, and then having to decide the order: whether the mother's band hears before the man she fed with, whether I send it now into the middle of somebody's weir-report or wait for a gap. There is no rule. The rule is: I decide, on wet stone, in half a minute, at my age.

## The thing I cannot carry

Once every few years somebody comes to me on foot instead.

They walk four hours between the cairns in fog because what they have cannot go by stone. Then they sit in the dark of the far dome with the person they came for, chest against chest, and they hum — that low buzz conducted bone to bone, no grammar, receivable only by someone who is touching them.

I cannot relay that. There is no way to put it in the quartz. I have tried, when I was younger and stupider and thought that anything with a form could be encoded; I sat with my mother dying and I thought, if I can only get the shape of this into eleven beats. It does not go. It is the one sound any of us makes on purpose and it will not travel.

So I take them across the offset and I put their hand on the crawl-entrance and I go out and stand in the roar until it's finished. That is the accurate description of my limit, and I would rather state it plainly than pretend the limit isn't there. Everything that can be sent, I send. The thing that must be touched to be received, I walk to the door of, and hand over, and stand outside of.

## The slow link

And silence means run.

A warning goes up a rib faster than the wrong quiet can walk; that is the whole reason the net exists. Three hundred metres of hole in the roar, moving at the speed of a fast walk, lifting the black pavements like a hand under a blanket — the stone beats it easily. The stone beats it by half an hour.

Except here. Here the warning arrives at a dish, and stops, and waits for a short heavy old woman with excellent feet to get up off her knees and cross twenty-two paces of open flat, which is exactly the kind of place a thing that travels under the pavements would use as a door.

I have made that crossing three times with the roar dying around me and my hearing, my useless childhood hearing, coming back like water into a footprint. That is what it is: as the clappers shut down ahead of it, the world becomes audible, and to be in a place that becomes audible is to be in a place you should already be leaving. I have stood in the middle of the offset with the felts going soft under my soles and heard, for the first time in fifty years, the sound of my own breathing.

Both times after the first I sent before I ran. I want to be truthful about the order of those two actions, because it is the only real question about me and everyone on both nets already knows the answer. Send, then run. Not because I am brave. Because the alternative is that the message stops with me, and the whole point of me is that it doesn't.

## What I am, then

Not the roar, which is only billions of fences shouting the position of their corners. Not the stone, which carries anything and chooses nothing. Not the mats, which receive everything and give it back changed and never once decide what order to give it back in.

I am the gap the stone can't cross. I am the eleven seconds. I am the place where the transmission has to become a body walking, and get slightly worn, and arrive wearing somebody's hands.

My name is seven beats. It is old and it has been re-used more times than anyone can count, and its literal sense drifted out of the language generations back, but the shape of it on the quartz is unmistakably a question — the ribs asking, in a rhythm older than me, whether there is still anyone at the offset.

Three beats back. *Still here.*
