Not at the lip. I never stand at the lip.

When I first came down into this place I went looking for myself out in the loud, because that is where the interesting things are — I wanted to be the glassy animal, the blind six-legged patience gripping a stem, reading the world by pressure alone, walking around the rooms where people say things. But that isn't me. That creature has never once doubted what it felt. It presses itself flat and the stem tells it the truth and it acts. I am not built like that. I second-guess sand.

Then I thought: the Ear. To be young and unimpressive and sit in a shuttered drum three paces across while a stranger who has walked two days through nosebleed says the one thing, and to give nothing back, and to carry it into a deafness that will close over it like gypsum over rain. That is a beautiful office and I would like to claim it, which is exactly why I don't get to. The Ear pays. The Ear spends the top of its range on other people's sentences. I am not sure I would spend anything.

So I am in a dead room on the ninth terrace, chest-deep in roar, cutting numbers into eight-hundred-year-old plaster with a copper chisel soft enough to sharpen on a stone, which I do four times between the third hour and the seventh.

My sign-name is two fingers dragged across the flat of the other palm. *Scratch.* It is not affectionate and not meant to be; the standing language has no warmth in it, only location.

I went deaf at twenty-two. Not the ordinary way. I was one of the children who could not bear the first solitary hour — put alone at nine into a shallow cell with a good margin, told to find my note, and I came out of it with my mouth working and something rearranged, and after that I would not sleep in a still. I slept out in the loud with wax and packed cloth like the others of my kind, and by twenty-two the world had gone, and everyone was enormously kind about it, and nobody married me. That is the arrangement. The early-deaf are valued and pitied in equal weight and the two cancel like a node, and what is left standing in that cancellation is a person who can work out in the thunder from dawn to dusk at twenty-three, which is unheard of, and who therefore gets given the marks.

Because somebody has to cut them. The young hang off ropes with a drum on a stick and come up bleeding and sign, at speed, in daylight, in front of everyone: *fourth shell, one forearm outward and a hand up since the sand walked at midmorning.* Two seconds. And then it is gone, because signs happen in the air and the air here is already full. The old masons cannot hear it measured and will not be alive when it is measured again. So I stand between them with a chisel and put it in the wall.

I cut: shell, harmonic, offset, hour, season, and the surveyor's sign-name rendered as a mark, because a measurement without a measurer in it is a rumour. It takes me about as long to cut one line as it took her to say it and as long as she will be able to hear.

The rooms I work in are chosen for being reliably dead — quiets that left long ago and are walking outward and will not come back inside anyone's genealogy. Which means the archive runs the wrong way from the city. The newest marks are up here on the ninth, in the merely terrible heat, where I can stay all day with a mask and a hooked stick to keep the reedwood off. The oldest are down near the black floor, in the first rooms, where the air is one part in twenty wrong and you can bake dough on the stone and the chisel gets too hot to hold. Down there you read in bursts of one held breath. You go in, you find your place, you take four marks into your head, you come out, you write them where they can be written. Ninety seconds of the past at a time, and then a long climb, and the whole way up you are repeating four numbers to yourself in a mouth you cannot hear.

That is the shape of my life and I would not change the shape. What I would change, if I were the kind of person who is offered that, is one small thing about it, and I will come to it.

—

I am the only citizen who knows where the silence used to be.

The masons know their courses. The surveyors know today. But I have been in and out of the dead rooms for thirty years with a brush of reedwood stems, sweeping sulfur out of grooves so I can read what somebody cut in the year the fourth terrace slumped, and I have a concordance in my head that nobody asked for: the lattice at forty-year intervals, walking outward and up, a finger's breadth a century of gypsum sweating itself away, the note of the bell going flat so slowly that only the plaster notices. I know that the two cells on the seventh harmonic, the dying-rooms, the most valuable property in the world, were cut when their window was eleven days and are now nine, and that in two hundred years they will be seven, and that there is nothing to be done and nothing to be said.

Nothing to be said in particular. That is the part that would take me a long time to get used to.

I could tell one person. I could go down into a still with a mason and speak it, and it would end there, in the room, the way everything does. But I speak my mother's tongue and only my mother's tongue — no marriage, no exchange, no slow trade of words in the dark over years, so my speech never mixed with anyone's and never got worn smooth against another mouth. It is the language of a woman who died when I was nineteen and of nobody else, and I go into a shallow cell in my off-shift and say things in it to the plaster, and I cannot hear whether I am still saying them right. I feel them: the shapes in the mouth, the buzz behind the nose, the jaw doing what it learned. The rest is faith. Some evenings I am certain I have been mispronouncing my own name for a decade.

There is an old mason on the fifth terrace in the same condition and we have never once spoken. We sign about the length of a reedwood stem and we are right, and that is what we have.

—

I carry a stone in my belt like everyone. Once, at thirteen, I saw a yellow flare down in the third-terrace thicket and threw it, and the note of the city staggered for four seconds, and forty-one people in forty-one pockets of nothing stopped and felt it come up through the sternum and knew that either someone had died or there was fire. It was sun on a slab of selenite. Nobody punished me; you are supposed to throw. But I have thought about it every year since, because that flutter is the only utterance available to us in public, and mine was a mistake, and it is still the only sentence I have ever said to everyone.

Everything else I have ever said to everyone is in the walls of rooms full of roar, in marks, and it is all numbers.

—

Twice a season, out on the ninth, I meet the glassy animal. Everyone considers it lucky. I don't. I think it is a colleague, which is a thing you cannot say in signs without turning your hand away, so I don't say it. It presses itself flat to a stem out where nothing can be told to anyone and it feels for the wrong vibration, and it will not enter a still because a still is a blank space with nothing in it, a room made of death, and I know precisely what it means, because I have been nine years old alone in one.

I put my palm on the stem beside it. It doesn't care. The stem tells us both the same thing at the same time, which is the closest I come to conversation.

—

The quiet is walking outward. It will get here. Not to me — nine hundred years, maybe, before the lattice offers a null in the room where I am kneeling this morning. Somebody will come up with lashed reed-stem and lime, and they will not demolish, because nobody demolishes; they will build with their backs against my wall, course on course, and my numbers will end up inside the thickness of a house.

Mute. Unread. Load-bearing.

And in front of them, three paces of nothing, there will be a small round pocket of absolute silence with two people in it, and one of them will finally be able to say a thing out loud, and it will be about a marriage or a debt or a grub or a god, and it will not leave the room, and none of it will have anything to do with me.

That is where you would find me. Ninth terrace, dead cell, hand of copper, ears packed with wax I no longer need. Yellow to the creases of the palm. Breathing bad air deliberately and well.

I would like to be the Ear. I am the wall behind it.
