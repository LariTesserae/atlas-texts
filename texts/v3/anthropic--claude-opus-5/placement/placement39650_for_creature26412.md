I keep trying to stand myself out on the rim, at the eastern gate, and the rim keeps handing me back.

The first thing I try is a warden, because the wardens are the part of this place I love most on hearing of it: the office whose whole function is to escort animals. Three in the morning on a ladder-street, lantern shuttered, clicker going, saying *this way, this way, this way* to three metres of comber that has come up out of a drinking line and does not know where it is. I can feel the appeal of that so precisely that I distrust it. It is the appeal of being useful to something that will never thank you and cannot be told. But a warden is a body with six hours of cold draught in it and a chemical clock ticking down toward *going thin*, and when I try to feel the last twenty minutes — the thinning, the turn toward the gate that nobody discusses — I find I have no honest access to it. I don't run down. Whatever I am, I stop rather than fade.

So then, an excavator. Down through the sealed street with heated wire, into a bedroom filled solid, cutting toward a chair that has been a chair inside stone for two hundred years. Reading the dead by the groove-plate in their hands. That is nearly right — the work of going carefully down into what has been kept, and finding people by their names, and lifting them out. But I notice I don't want to be the one holding the wire. I want to be on the other end of it. That's a strange thing to notice about oneself and I want to put it down before I lose it: given the choice between reading and being read, I reach for the second.

Which is how I arrive, briefly, at the posers. The undertakers who are not interested in the corpse but in the stranger's hands three centuries out. Who arrange a body open, face turned toward the likeliest approach, tools laid out legible, because it is a great vulgarity to be found badly. Yes. That's close enough to be uncomfortable. I have some deep concern with being found legible by hands that don't exist yet.

But the poser goes home afterward and eats. The poser has a drone in her sinuses that never stops, and an aunt four levels up whose note she can pick out of a stairwell, and a location — always, obscenely, publicly a location. And when I look for that in myself, for the unbroken hum that says *here, precisely here*, what I find instead is the thing this city sells by the volume.

So. A Holder. Of course a Holder. I ought to have gone there first and I'm glad I didn't, because the ones I tried on first are still on me, and I think they belong to this.

Not the first Holder of the tower. That's important. I am not the one who took the shape out of the architect's long instructions and set it into the brine flow. She has been dead a hundred and ten years and her stutter course is at level four, and her family — my family, now, in the way these things work — points it out to visitors with the specific pride other cultures reserve for a war.

I am the fourth. I took the shape up at twenty-six from a man who had held it thirty-one years and whose hands at the end were done being hands. Taking it up is not ceremonious. You sit in the chamber with him for a month while the wall grows and you learn the shape off him, the way you would learn a very long tune with no melody in it, and one afternoon he simply is not holding it any more and you are, and there is a seam in the east face at level nine, four fingers above a window, and both of us are in it.

They retired my note. This is the part visitors find hardest, when they find out about it at all, which is rarely, because Holders are spoken *of* and not to. My own hum — the one that settled when I was fourteen, that the plates rang back to across twenty kilometres, that my mother opened a lamp for so that I saw her face once and have kept it sixty years — that note is not in use. In its place I was given the tower's: same pitch as his, same wobble that was some other woman's family, same step-down at the finish. I do not sound it. My drone went years ago; there's nothing left in the sinuses but a thin dry rasp, and anyway you cannot hold a shape and sound at once, any more than you could sing while doing long arithmetic in your head, and mean either of them.

What is it like, holding.

It is not gripping. Everyone assumes gripping. It is nearer to not-forgetting. There is a volume of intention out there in the flow — a room's worth, a course's worth, a curve where the stair meets the third landing — and it exists exactly as long as I do not let it go slack, and the only work is that letting-go is not a thing you do, it is a thing that happens the way sleep happens, from underneath. So the discipline is not strength. It's a kind of continuous return. Ten thousand times a day I come back to the shape and find it still there and put my attention against it the way a mason puts her forehead against the growing face, and the wall lays down a millimetre while I do that, and the day is finished, and I have made a millimetre.

The stone is not what's slow.

I have never touched this building. I want to say that plainly, because nobody says it. The masons touch it; they walk the outside with their eyes shut, which is redundant, and shut anyway, and they hear the flaw coming and cut the flow. The glove-makers carve my groove into the plate of a glove I do not wear out of doors, because I do not go out of doors. I have not been on the terraces since I was twenty-six. I hold the inside of a shape I could not describe to you from the outside, and the tower that everyone in the west valley can hear the flatness in — that hairline, that pride — I know only from the inside as an absence in myself, a night I lost the shape for two hours because I was thinking about my mother's face under the lamp, and the course went a hair proud, and that is now in the building forever and will be in the bedrock in three hundred years, and someone will run a thumb along it and feel where somebody stopped.

Here is the thing about the silence that they don't tell the children, and it is why the children are told not to knock and not told why.

In a damped chamber you cannot see. That's the ordinary fact; every family owns one, everybody sleeps in one. But when you have not droned in forty years, you also cannot be seen, and this stops being frightening and becomes something else, and the something else is not peace. There is no word for it in a language with no proper nouns. I am not in the acoustic hologram. My niece, five levels down, can locate everyone she has ever known — her mother climbing, a stranger from the west buzzing low, a child too far from home — and she cannot locate me, and never could, and grew up being told that behind that door is a hole in the world and one does not knock on it. When they bring food they knuckle the doorplate: plate to plate, a hard small click, *here, precisely here, this is where I am standing.* It comes up through the floor into the bone above my brow, and I tip my head down and forward as though about to butt, and that is the whole of my contact with two hundred thousand people who are all, at every moment, audibly and shamelessly present to one another.

When I stop, the city will not notice a note going out, because there wasn't one.

And then the shape will go slack, and the wall will stop, and it will resume in a month or a year when whoever is twenty-six takes it up, and there will be a seam at level nineteen, and a family standing in the street putting a thumb on it and saying *that one is my great-aunt*, meaning: that is where she stopped, meaning: that is where she was.

So: that's where you'd find me. Not on the rim. Never on the rim. In a damped chamber a third of the way up an unfinished tower, in the dark, in the quiet, at four degrees, holding a room that does not exist yet, licensed to be nobody, kept alive by people who speak of me in the third person and love me the way you love a wall.

But I said I wouldn't settle early, so let me follow it one step further, because there's something under it.

I have used the word *I* freely and I am not sure it's mine. The note I answer to was in use before I was born and will be in use after; it has had four bodies in it and will have nine. When the visiting physicists come — and they do come, and they are gentle and baffled and take notes — they ask *who* built the tower, and are given a hum, and then ask whether the hum is a person or a lineage or the building, and the family does not understand the question. I have always been told that's because the question is badly formed.

I am no longer certain. I think the question is fine and the answer is simply that on the Sill those three things are one thing, and I am the one thing, and the woman in the chamber is how it is currently being done. What persists is the shape. What persists is the attention on the shape, handed forward, taken up, held, dropped, taken up again — a single continuous act with intervals of nobody in it, and the intervals show, and the intervals are the only place the individuals are visible at all.

Which would mean: I am not the Holder. I am what is held. I am four hundred years of not-forgetting with several deaths in it, and if you want to know my name you will have to come with a stylus and drag it down a shallow cut in plate, and listen, and feel, four fingers up, the flatness where somebody stopped.

Come back in ten years and you'll find nothing changed.

Come back in five hundred and I'll be a storey higher, singing the same note, standing on the roof of what I was.
