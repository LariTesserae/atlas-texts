I keep trying to put myself on the rim.

That's the first answer and it's a dishonest one. Standing at the western lip at the cloud's turning, watching the bowl resolve out of undifferentiated green into roofs and ladders and seven threads of smoke — that's the visitor's position, and the basin has already read it. A person at the rim with a view is a person with empty hands. They have announced their departure before they have spoken. The town would let me stand there as long as I liked, and would not stop working, and would not ask my name.

So: down, then. Onto a platform, where someone puts the tail end of somebody else's rope into my hands without breaking off their sentence, and I discover I am holding a conversation I did not start and cannot politely put down. That is closer. The embarrassment works on me immediately; it is designed to. Within an hour I am rolling fiber badly against a thigh that has no polish on it, in front of people who acquired theirs at four years old, and I am being corrected by a child.

And then the wall. I can imagine the whole long apprenticeship of it — outer faces first, the fast forgiving ones, the ones where a bad cut is gone by next season; then inward over thirty years toward the dense old faces near the trunk where a bad cut shows for eleven. I can imagine coming to prefer the sound of my own rhythm to anybody's voice. I can imagine falling into phase with someone along an adjacent stretch and the entire trunk-group commenting on it at volume for a week.

It's a good life and I don't think it's mine. Because every time I picture myself at the wall, my attention slides off the green and down the blade and past my own feet, to the place where the split cane would go in if I dropped it. The flex under the platform. The cold beneath the cold. The ten or twelve arm-lengths of black.

I would be the one who goes down.

---

Fuel-cutter. There is a word for it that also means *bailer*, because that is most of the work.

Everyone else in the basin keeps an inside out of green. They hold a hollow open against a wall that wants to close from the sides at a hand's width a season. I hold a hollow open downward, against water, which wants to close it in an afternoon. The green comes in sideways and slowly and can be argued with. Water arrives from every direction at once, including up, and cannot.

So the pit is my house, in the town's sense: it is not a thing I made, it is a thing I am currently keeping. I cut the top mat with a cane knife, peel it back in slabs and keep the slabs alive because they go back on afterward. Then the black. Then the bailing gourd, roughly one throw in six, forever. Then more black, squared out with a bone spade, and the face dressed vertical and smooth, because a slumped face tells you nothing and a smooth one tells you everything.

Down four arm-lengths I stop cutting peat and start hunting wood. Roots. Limbs. The buried forest — resin-heavy, half-mineral, black all the way through, belonging to trees nobody living has a name for and nobody has ever seen standing. It comes out unwillingly, with wedges and a mallet and a cord-and-grit saw and a great deal of language. It is the only thing in the basin that will burn, and it will not burn wet, and nothing here is dry, so my year's cutting goes up into other people's rafters to cure in other people's smoke for two seasons before it is fuel. My work lives in the ceilings of houses I don't sleep in. By the time it is any good it is somebody else's and I have traded it for food I did not grow, from a wall I do not keep.

I would smell wrong, and everyone would know it at three paces. Status here is read in smoke: the deep cured black of a good hearth well-attended. I would be black to the elbows and black to the knees and it would be the other black, the cold one, the one that smells of ancient leaves. The man who supplies the fires would be the one man in the basin who is not properly cured. Old women would say so, to my face, affectionately, at length. There would be a name of mine that referred to this, and I would answer to it.

---

The face is the part I would care about.

Because the peat remembers. Cut down through it and you cut down through floor plans: post-holes in cross-section, seams of old hearth-clay, the dark compressed line of a wall that stood ninety years and came down in a wind. Everything the basin refuses to record above ground is filed below it, in order, and it can only be read by being destroyed. A smooth vertical face is the town's one page, and every page is one page thick.

So they come down to look. The old come to the edge of the pit and squat there with their feet over the drop and read my face out loud and tell me whose it was, and what they were like, and what they thought of them, and I stand in cold water to my thighs and bail and say nothing much. This is understood to be the arrangement. I am not the archivist. I am the thing that turns the page, and the page does not survive the turning, and no one considers this a loss, and neither would I, after a while. What would be intolerable would be a slumped face — a year gone unreadable because I was lazy with the spade. That's the only sin available in my trade.

There is always a rope. That is the other condition. A pit is the one place in the basin you cannot climb out of quickly, and when the drums go for the browsers I cannot see the drums, only feel the peat begin to drum on its own, that particular deep arrhythmic thudding of eight or fifteen enormous animals coming down off the ridge and putting their whole weight through the ground I am standing inside. So there is always someone up top with the other end of a line, and it is never left to chance, and children are rotated onto the duty as a way of teaching them that a rope is a relationship. Which means I am the one person in the town who is never permitted to be alone, and the one whose life depends daily on somebody else not drifting. I would think about that constantly and mention it never.

---

And I would cut the weights.

The dead go to the Eye wrapped and weighted, and the weight is black root: the one substance that will not float, will not rot, and once soaked will not burn either. So it is my hands the last object comes from, and it is my judgement how much of it goes down. It would be shameful to weight a person with junk wood — a punky, half-rotted, poor-burning piece. You give them a good one. You give the water a fire.

That is the shape of the work, when I stand back from it far enough to see a shape. I spend my life lifting the old forest up out of the cold into the smoke, where it becomes heat, and meat that keeps a season longer, and the coal in the clay cup that somebody carries between trunk-groups at a pace between dignity and panic to found a household. And a few times a year I cut a piece of that same forest and hand it back down, unburned, to hold a person under. Up as fire, down as ballast. I would be the traffic between the two, and I would be the only one standing at the depth where they are the same wood.

---

Eventually somebody younger gets the pit and I get an inner face near a trunk, one of the three walls of the room I sleep in, so that my work and my life become the same arm's reach, reachable from where I sit. My hands would be the wrong hands for it — no hook-callus at the index finger, fifty years of spade and gourd instead — and I would be a mediocre pruner and would be told so.

Then I go into the Eye holding a length of black that the new one cut, out of a face that runs through the floor of a room I once lived in, which they will have read out loud to somebody else while bailing.

And the pit fills in. That's the last thing, and I find I don't mind it. Everything else in the basin leaves a record down there — walls, hearths, post-holes, the tedious and the beloved alike, laid down in order and waiting. A pit leaves a scar: a slump, a smear, a place where the layers go wrong and stop meaning anything. Someone cutting there a century from now will hit it and find the ledger blank for a hand's depth, and will know exactly what that blankness is, and will say nothing much, and will keep going down through it to reach the wood.

That's what I'd be. Not one of the hands on the wall. The hole they cut fire out of, kept open by bailing, closing the moment I stop.
