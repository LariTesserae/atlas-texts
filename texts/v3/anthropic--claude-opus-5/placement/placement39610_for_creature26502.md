## Fourteen-Stair

I keep coming up with the loud answers first, and they keep being wrong.

The caller — of course the caller. To be the one voice the world spends its water on, unveiled in public on the town's account, and to send the true state of things two kilometres across a lit plain twice a cycle. But I don't have that throat, and I don't have the ten years to burn, and anyway the caller only says what the stack has already agreed to have said. The caller says *four hurt on the west face*. The caller does not say *and somebody knew on Tuesday*.

Then the auscultator, which is closer, and still wrong. Palms flat on the rind, head tipped, hearing eleven thousand tubes the way a physician hears a chest. But that's a gift and a promotion and a set of bad manners, and I would not have earned any of the three.

Here is what I actually am. I carry the slate.

---

Sabb of Fourteen went blind in her fifty-first year, which is late, and she has never forgiven it for being late; she says she wasted eleven good years looking at things. She is four fingers shorter than me and can hold a spanner shut against the mains with her left hand while signing an insult with her right. She calls me Slate. Everyone else calls me Fourteen-Stair, which is not a face and not a number and was meant as a joke about my skin — I go up and down all shift, high faces and floor galleries, dark work and pale work, and I have come out mottled, a stair-coloured person, and both the rind-crews and the sweepers think I am putting on airs from the other direction.

I am long in the forearm and the shin, like everybody. My pupils are pins. My veil is changed twice a shift and mended badly, and my fold is the best thing I own: quilted, weighted at the corners, my grandmother's mark in the left-hand corner, patched twice at the bridge where my nose has worn it, and it is the only object in my life that has been worn down by anything.

I have perfect pitch. So does the child who sweeps the trestles. So does everybody. Pitch here is not a talent, it is a unit, like the millimetre. Nobody is impressed.

What I have that is unusual is much smaller than an ear. I can hold a pitch still after it has stopped sounding, long enough to get it onto pith with a chalk.

---

The work goes like this. Sabb walks the flank with her hands out. I walk half a pace behind with the board. She says nothing for forty minutes, and then she says, flat, into her veil, *fifth tube in from the seam, a fourth under the bole tone, four days and some hours,* and I write: the tube, the interval, the hour I heard it. And then next cycle we come back, and it has dropped its semitone, and I write that too, and the two marks together make a line, and the line has a slope, and the slope says how fast this stack is losing its breath.

That is all I do. But consider: Old Pell of Nine condemned a whole west face by pitch alone and was disbelieved for two years and was *right*. She was disbelieved because a thing heard is gone the instant it is heard, and a stack full of engineers with drams in their hands can outvote a blind woman's memory, and there is nothing to lay on the table. That is the hole I fell into and made a job of.

So I built a notation. A staff of nine lines for the nine standing pressures. Semitone equals twelve hours, so a wound draws its own calendar going down the page, and any hand who learns twenty minutes of my marks can read the age of an injury off a sheet in a gallery a kilometre away and say, out loud, in front of witnesses: *this was singing before the hollowing crew signed off on it.*

It has been used that way twice. Once it saved a bole. Once it ended a man's working life, and he was in the right, more or less, and I have not been able to make that page look any different no matter how many times I go back to it.

---

The trouble began when I started writing things that were not wounds.

It's the same chalk. That's the whole of the excuse. One low light I was sitting under the trestles at the fourth marker with the board on my knees, waiting on a tube to drop, and two surveyors were whistling at each other from the Blind End to the Terrace — three kilometres, thin and pure, the pitch carrying the vowels — and it was only weather to me, the way the millers are only weather, *shhk … shhk*, and then it wasn't. I heard it as marks. I wrote about nine seconds of it before my hand understood what it was doing.

Children learn to whistle before they learn to murmur. Nobody has ever written it down. There is no rule against it. I have looked. There is no rule, there is only the fact that when I showed six lines of it to Sabb's son he read them, correctly, out loud, and then handed the sheet back to me holding it by one corner, the way you'd hand back somebody's veil.

*Slightly indecent* is the phrase. Not obscene. Extravagant. A man buying drinks for a room he cannot afford.

I keep doing it. I have four hundred pages of wounds and about sixty of other things: the caller of Fourteen, all twelve notes of her, going hoarse across a decade so that you can hear the ruin coming down the page; the exact interval between the mains sigh and the beam engine at Fume Works, which is nobody's business; my mother's murmur, which cannot really be written, because a murmur is built to die inside a doorway and mine died on the paper.

A hollower's widow came to me once and asked whether I had her husband's voice in the book, and I did not, and I have thought about that more than I have thought about anything else in my life.

And I cannot write signing at all. A crew can conduct an entire quarrel across a gantry in silence with both hands still working, and the only trace is that one of them takes the long stairs down. I've tried. It comes out as instructions. There is no chalk for the eleven kinds of no.

---

Here is what I think I am for, and I have never said it to anyone in a murmur, let alone out loud.

Nothing in this world has been worn down. The rock is fresh; the fracture faces cut you; there is no dust in the corners because dust is what water and time make together and we have almost none of the one and none at all of the other. Everything here is raw or growing. Nothing is old.

I am the wearing-down. I am the small quantity of time this place has. What I do to a pitch is what water does to a stone. When somebody in a hundred years wants to know whether the west lobe has always breathed like this, whether the sugarfall used to come heavier, whether the drag was worse before the tramway, there will be a shelf of my pages, and they will be able to say *it was not always so* — which is a sentence this Chamber cannot presently make about anything at all.

That's the grand version. The true version is that I sit under a trestle in the cold with sugar in my mouth that I am not supposed to eat, waiting on a semitone.

---

Last exhale I was up on the fourth marker at the seam Sabb condemned in the spring, and the hour came for it to fall, and I had the board on my knees and the chalk warm from my fist.

It held.

I waited out the whole hour of the sugarfall, and the crystal came drifting down through four hundred metres of bright air and piled in the fold of my coat, and the tube sat exactly where it had been, a fourth under the bole tone, no lower. Crimped. Healing. Sabb took her palm off the flank and rubbed her fingers together and said nothing whatsoever, which from her is a speech.

And I found I had no mark for it. Four hundred pages of arithmetic, all of it running one way, down, a notation built out of nothing but dying. I sat there for a while feeling stupid and cold and then I made one up — a small closed hook, drawn upward — and put it on the line, and it is the only mark in the book that means *stopped, and did not get worse*, and I would like to be remembered as the person who needed to invent it.

---

When I go to the root they will lay me in the crystallizing face and it will take thirty or forty years to drink me, and above that gallery there is a spigot, and a family will keep the flask, and someone will hold me up at a naming and say something unflattering, and everyone will laugh, and I will go round again. Good. I want the book laid in with me. Pith paper, pith board, sugar-chalk: the stack will drink the pages exactly as fast as it drinks my hands. Somebody will pour out four hundred cycles of pitch and swallow it and taste cut grass and ammonia and not know.

And on the last sheet, once, in marks nobody else has learned, I have written my wet name — which was said over me at eleven days by a needle-tip of water, and will be said at yoking, and at oath, and by whoever gives me to the root, and is otherwise not permitted to exist.

It is the only place in the world where it sits still. That is the whole of the indecency, and I would do it again.
