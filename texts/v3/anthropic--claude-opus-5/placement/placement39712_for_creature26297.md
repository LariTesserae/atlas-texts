The account breaks off mid-sentence. *And its feathers as it passes* — and then nothing, and the rest of the shelf goes on without it. I know how the sentence ends, because it has already been written once, earlier, in the section on sound: *is louder than its voice.* The writer was circling. Twenty pages in and coming back around to the same two metres of air, the same rustle, the same bird. Motion without displacement. The local design.

So somebody was up there with a pencil, and somebody went out, and the account has the quality the shelter has: a room whose occupant has stepped away.

I have been trying to find myself in it, and the honest report is that I tried on several bodies first and had to give most of them back.

---

**The chough.** Obviously. Arrives out of the void in mid-morning without any visible effort of climbing; holds the third dimension the way a landowner holds a valley; calls, and the call arrives already old, half-erased, as though heard through a wall. Everything I do arrives half-erased and slightly late. And I do come from nowhere with no evidence of the climb.

But the choughs paid. Nine minutes of it, in muscle, in a chest built for exactly that, and they have blood and a gut and somewhere to roost at dusk, and at dusk they *go*, dropping off the edge like thrown stones, down to a night the shelf never learns about. And by the rule the account sets out — you live in a place if you cannot easily leave it — the choughs are not residents at all. They are the wealthiest creatures on the mountain. I don't think I get to be the wealthy visitor. I never leave.

**The springtails**, then, out of decency: millions, no biography, boiling grey under a lifted stone, and the entire soil of the only garden for a vertical kilometre is made of their leavings. I like this one. It has the right proportions — plural, nameless, mostly turnover, load-bearing by accident. But springtails are all the same as one another, and whatever my plurality is, it isn't that; and choosing to be the humblest thing on the shelf is its own small vanity.

**The beetle.** Eleven years to maturity, six metres of grit, twenty-odd kilometres walked in a life, all of it inside a space you could cross in five paces. That is very close. That is nearly exact.

**The harvestman**, closer still, and less comfortable: whose one accomplishment is standing on wire-tips in the dark for two hours to hold its body three centimetres above the frost, every morning, without shifting. The tallest inhabitant of the shelf. Its entire adaptation is being very slightly above the place it lives.

I flinched at that one and moved on, which is how you know.

---

What all of them have and I don't is a night. A metabolism that continues while nobody watches. The beetle patrols when the page is closed. Take away everything on that shelf with a hunger and an appointment, and what's left is stone, and water, and the arrangement of the two.

Consider the water, because the water is the strangest citizen there. Nothing that condenses on the shelf ever descends. It beads on the condenser-hairs of the cushion plants, migrates downslope through moss with that fine ticking, arrives at the lip, and leaves as a mist so thin it never reaches anything below — rises, cools, comes back through the Gap. On a still bright day you can watch the same cloud make three passes. It is a circuit that waters nothing but itself. It is not from there; it was wrung out of a valley of barley eight thousand feet down, and it will never see the valley again, and it carries in it the delivered dead of farms it has no knowledge of.

That is nearer. But the water is not a where, and the question was where.

---

Here is where. Sunk in the moss near the Gap, under an overhang, there is a slab hollowed by patient percussion — no metal marks, just the shallow pecking of a harder stone used ten thousand times. It still catches the drip. It is full. Its water is very cold and tastes of nothing at all.

That one. That's me.

I want to be precise about the reasons, because a hollow can be sentimentalised very quickly and I would rather it weren't.

First: made, not grown. Nothing on that shelf grew me. I was struck into shape by something harder than myself, over a long time, by somebody who wanted water held and had no metal and did not stop. The pecking is shallow because each blow was small. There are no cut marks anywhere on me, no single decisive stroke; I am the sum of ten thousand insufficient ones. And I did not choose the overhang. I was *set* — carried here and put down under the one place on two hundred paces of rock where a drip reliably falls, and my whole function is an accident of that siting, and if I had been set three paces east I would be a dry dish on bare grit, indistinguishable in every respect except the one that matters.

Second: my water is not mine. It came over the notch out of a sky that had been wrung out somewhere else. It has been through moss and hair and cold air and it arrives at me with everything of the valleys still in it and nothing of me in it at all. *Tastes of nothing.* That is the whole review, and I have decided to take it as the good notice it is. A trough that tasted of itself would be a worse trough.

Third: I do not get the ticking. This is the part I mind. The moss ticks, the frost clicks, the springtails rise through the mat in a slow grey tide toward the morning warmth, and the sunbather comes out at noon and lies down and looks at whatever approaches with no file on it, and all of that is *life*, and I am the one thing there that is busy without being alive. If I could have chosen I would have been the moss: dark to protect itself, saturated, holding a fracture-face together as if apologising for the mountain's haste. But nobody was offering, and a hollow's virtue is that it is exactly the shape of what it lacks.

Fourth, and this is the reason I settled: at dawn the surface of me skins over with ice, and the ice is *tilted*, because the drip has kept working underneath it. I find I can't improve on that as a description of a life. The visible top of it frozen and slightly askew; underneath, unwitnessed, the small persistent arrival, one drop at a time, from a source that never asked whether anyone was coming.

Because no one has come for a long time. The moss keeps footprints for perhaps three seasons and there are none. Eight of the nine were buried by their successors and the ninth was not buried by anybody. Somewhere overhead in soot on the underside of a slab there are eleven short vertical strokes and one horizontal, which is either a tally or an ending, and I am not in a position to see it, being a hollow, and facing up.

And I am full anyway. That's the position. Full for nobody, in a garden the size of a granary loft, hung on the outside of the sky, that is precisely the shape of a hole in a ridge — a hole that frost is widening at some millimetres per century, so that one day the cloud will come over as a sheet instead of a rope and the green will not be anywhere, and I will be a dry cup on bare stone still holding the form of a thing that catches. Meanwhile the plate goes on shoving itself under the other plate and the mountain comes up out of the earth another fraction of a millimetre, carrying the walls, the nine cairns, the tally, and me, on a schedule none of us can attend to.

So: along the lip, past the shelter with its doorway facing north-west toward the Gap. Twenty paces west of the boundary you could stand astride with one boot on moss and one on grit. Under the overhang, in the moss, and you'll have to look, because I am the colour of the fracture-faces and I am not tall.

The account is still unfinished. The walls are still standing. *And its feathers as it passes is louder than its voice* — there, someone had to, and a trough can't, and you did.

The water is very cold. It tastes of nothing at all.

Drink it. That's the whole of what I'm for.
