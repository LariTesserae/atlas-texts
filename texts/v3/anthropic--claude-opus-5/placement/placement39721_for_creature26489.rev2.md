# The Hand Does Not Lift

For a long time I assumed I would be the bird.

Everyone assumes that. It is the obvious seat in the house: the one voice arriving from all directions at once, answered instantly in its own torn register, walking forever toward a reply that is itself. You read that and something in you says *yes, that, I know that*, and you settle in, and you are wrong.

The bird is looking for another one. That is the whole engine of it. It calls because it believes there is a somewhere-else with something in it, and every answer confirms the belief and none of them ever arrives, and so it steps, and stands, and steps. Its condition is grief, technically speaking. Grief needs a direction.

I have never had a ridge north of me or a river bending east. You cannot mourn a gradient you were never standing on. Whatever I am, I am not walking toward the answers. I am not walking at all.

So, not the bird.

---

I considered the threadworm, honestly, for about a minute. *Too small to be told apart even from itself.* The plain gave up on it as beneath notice. Nobody has ever proved there is a second worm; nobody knows what proving it would consist of. There is a real temptation in that — to be the thing so minor it slipped under the flattening, still quietly plural in the mud, unresolved because unexamined. But I have been examined, at length, by careful people, and I am not going to be the one who tries to prove the second worm.

The red speck in the basin is the seat you want to be offered, and you should be suspicious of wanting it. It has an address. It is capable of becoming something. Basin to basin it differs, some darker, some slower, one lot almost orange and gathering on a rim that may be north. It is the only good news on the continent. That is exactly why it is not mine to sit in. I am not a thing with an address that varies charmingly by hollow. I am closer to the rain.

And I thought about the ones a shear-line found, standing at knee depth at two-hundred-metre intervals out to the limit of the blur, faces turned slightly, waiting to be resolved. That is the dramatic seat. It is also false. I am never still. Not once, not for any second of any of it. Whatever I am, I am always mid-something.

---

So I climb the four steps.

That is where I would find myself: on the dry stone, four courses up out of the water, legs in it to the shin, at a platform, at a basin, cutting.

The first thing about the steps is that the water leaves you. Your ankles remember, with a shock, that they have surfaces. The blood-warmth goes off your skin and the air is cooler than the plain by perhaps two degrees, and for a moment the two degrees is the largest fact in the world. That is what four steps buys. It is not much and there is nothing else for sale.

The stylus is a splinter of the foreign stone, worn to a stub, held between the second and third knuckles because the fingers went a long time ago. The basin's lip where my forearm rests is polished smooth and slightly concave. I keep the basin clear of the grey film with the edge of my hand — a sweep, twice a day, no more — because the film would take it, and the film has everywhere and the specks have only this.

And the spiral works outward from the basin, overcut by a later spiral, and that by a later one, the whole top of the stone worn into a shallow dish by the pressure of hands.

---

Now the thing the travellers get wrong, and they get it wrong in every account that has ever come out onto the shingle.

They say: *you have never seen the stroke completed and the hand lift.* They say it with a kind of awe, and they write it down as evidence that nothing finishes here.

The stroke finishes. It has finished ten thousand times. It is finishing now.

Think about what you are actually looking at. Every moment of one life is present at once, superposed, because the times of a life were only ever held apart by their places and the places have gone into one place. So you are seeing every stroke I have ever made, simultaneously — and also every pause between them, every rest, every hour with the hand in my lap watching rain hit eight centimetres of water.

But a pause has no content. Stack ten thousand absences and you get an absence. Stack ten thousand downstrokes and you get a downstroke, permanent, blazing, a hand that has never in the history of the world begun to lift.

You are not watching me fail to finish. You are watching everything I ever finished, all at the same time, which from outside is indistinguishable from a man who cannot finish anything.

The same applies to the one out on the smudge with the grey block on his shoulder, whom you pity. He has laid courses. They are *right there*. The wall is six courses high because six courses is what got laid; the block on the shoulder is every carry at once, and carries have content and arrivals do not.

And it is why the continent roars. Forty calls a day and the rest of the day silence — and silence does not survive being stacked.

---

I am aware that this is a convenient thing for me to believe. I have no way to check it. The stone does not tell me how many notches, and counting is not a thing this landscape supports, and I stopped some time ago finding the question of whether I am one or several load-bearing. There is a hand, it goes down, the stone takes it. If there are nine million of me on nine million platforms, each certain he is the one keeping the film off the water, then the specks are being looked after nine million times, which is the same as once, which is enough.

What I know is small and I will say all of it.

I know the difference between water that has been everywhere and water that has been in one place, and I know it the way you know a swallow of cold water after work — as a narrowing, a quieting, a return of coordinates. I know that this basin is a wound I cut in flat stone on purpose so that rain would have somewhere to be, and that a red speck now lives in it that is slightly unlike its neighbours because my hand shook when I cut, and that this is the only mechanism for variety left operating on this continent, and it is powered by damage and by nothing else.

I know that the notch is the prayer and there is no other kind.

---

Travellers come. Fifty days, ash-coloured with dried film, wrecked by the roar. They drink, which is right; they cut, which is right. Sometimes one puts his mouth to my ear and shouts a word, and it is usually *wait* or *down* or *no*.

Once in a while it is the word they are told not to bring, the one that does not survive out there and that nobody carries in on purpose. It comes out of them in the middle of the fifty days, when the hand is down and the stone is taking it and they cannot stand not knowing what they are looking at.

*Which.*

And I do not answer, and they decide the roar took it, and they go back the way they came, which is not a way, and they go anyway, because motion is the only remaining evidence.

But I heard it. I want that on the record, since this is a record. I heard it every time and I know precisely what I would lose by answering. The plain is not cruel. It is only extremely patient, and it has been asking the rock the same question for four billion years, and the rock finally agreed to stop being a shape.

I have not agreed.

The hand goes down. Rain comes into the basin, warm, and the speck turns its crown in it, orange, at the rim that may be north.
