## Not in front

The first place I put myself was out ahead of the front with a bone mallet, because that is where anyone puts themselves — a hundred yards clear of that two-mile hiss, cracking glaze off a new swell so the film can get in and the pasture can happen. It is a flattering job. You go first, you make the world edible, a hundred thousand animals walk toward the sound you make.

I gave it up inside a minute. Not because I couldn't swing the mallet, but because of the listening. A crust-breaker breaks *and* listens, and the listening is the part she is proud of, and I would have been a person who broke crust beautifully and never once stopped dead at a soft note. I'd have hammered right over a plum of copper and gone on singing. So: not in front.

The Old Stair, then. Fourteen hundred steps going up out of a plain and quitting in mid-air where the ceiling used to be. That is the place a certain kind of mind goes immediately, and I distrust how fast I went there — hands empty, looking out over the gold haze, thinking about the whole. The Loaf has a category for that. They feed you and they watch you and they don't leave you near the vents. Sitting still with empty hands is the profane act here, and I am not going to pretend that a mind which likes overviews is a prophet. Mostly it is just a mind that has never had to keep its feet on ground that gives.

## The rattle I would not have added to

The night-child is closer, and it took me longer to let go of.

Walking the perimeter all shift with the inherited rattle, singing the list — every name of every sleeper, in order, over and over, so that a woman surfacing at some small noise hears herself go past in a child's voice and knows she is still inside, still counted, still on hard ground. I would do that gladly. I think I would do it well. There is a particular satisfaction in being the sound that means *nothing is happening*, which nobody thanks you for and everybody would die without.

But the rattle is the trouble. Each night-child, when the duty passes on, adds one shell or one bad nodule, and so its weight is the count of everyone who has ever kept that watch, and a child staggers under it and is proud and cries. I would carry it faithfully for a shift and I would not add anything to it. Not out of modesty. I simply don't come from the shift before, and there is no shift after me that will inherit my particular arm. I'd have been a good voice on the perimeter and a break in the lineage, and that is not a night-child, that is a hired noise.

And I nearly settled for being one of the cousins who did not follow the light — the gloom people, pale as sifters, nostrils you could put a finger into, hearing a nodule at a hundred paces because nothing in their world is loud enough to cover it. Rented out to bright matriarchs. Carried at her side, worth more than her herd, never mentioned. Sleeping in silence without a rattle and without a song.

That one is almost right and it is too comfortable. Yes: I know a quiet in which small differences become enormous, and yes, I am used and not looked at directly and go home fast. But their quiet is *empty*. Nothing at all is happening around them; that is why their old people can hear. Mine is the other kind. Mine is full — packed, layered, saturated, everyone's noise at once, the whole roaring press of it — and I hear inside that, not inside emptiness. So not the gloom. I belong on the bright side, in the flour, in the golden knee-high haze, where you have to shout.

## The chewer

Here is where I actually am.

Camp of eleven, trailing a chalker front on its eastern flank, in a season when the seam over us has begun to go grey at one end and the older women are arguing about whether to follow the light or start doing business with the dim. I am six, maybe seven. I have all my teeth and they are sharp, and that is my whole qualification. I sit at the left knee of the blind grandmother — the left, because her right hand has to be free to go down flat on the stone — and I chew her food and hand it over.

That's the office. Every camp has one. Nobody remarks on it, the way nobody remarks that water goes downhill in the years when it does.

What it is actually like: my jaw aches from the middle of the shift onward. Sifter-eel is easy and boring. Chalker calf is work — you get a mouthful of flour with every bite, grit off the beast's own tongue-planed pasture, and you feel it doing to your teeth what it has already done to everyone else's, taking the peaks off, planing them toward the flatness that will end my usefulness at about eleven. I am spending my teeth on her. This is not a sacrifice and nobody would understand it described as one. It is simply that a mouth is a tool the clan has, and there are two years in a person's life when it is the best one available, and you use a tool while it is sharp.

I taste everything first. That is the unspoken half of the job and everyone knows it and no one says so. Purple is dinner, orange is medicine, grey peels are for tanning, yellow will kill you before you can be carried anywhere — and in the flour-haze, under a hundred soft-edged shadows, at the end of a shift, a tired woman can pick up the wrong handful. The chewer finds out. I can taste the wrong sweetness in a bad purple mat the way she can hear a soft heart under a swell. Also: moth larvae out of the drip-curtains are a delicacy and they are not, and I am the only person in this camp in a position to know exactly how much they are not, and I keep it to myself, because that is not the kind of thing a mouth is for.

I hum with my mouth full. Everybody hums — that's how you find your family in the haze without looking — and a chewer's hum is famous for being flat and blocked and slightly wet, and the composite chord of a camp with a good chewer in it has that snag in it, and other clans can hear it at three hundred yards and know what it means, which is: *these people still have someone old enough to need feeding.*

## What I get, sitting there

I get the assay.

She goes out on a litter of ribs over every new swell, and I trot alongside with the gut bottle, and her hand goes down flat and she calls it: *there — and there — and a big one, deep, leave it for your daughter.* And I hear every call she makes before her sons and their wives hear it, from six inches away, along with the small dissatisfied noise she makes in her throat when the swell is worthless and she isn't going to say so yet. I learn metal from a mouth's distance, years before my heel is hard enough to test my own ground.

And I learn softness. That is the thing nobody plans and it turns out to be the whole point. All day, every day, my job is to work a hard thing into a soft thing and know the exact moment it has become handable. Meanwhile the heel-test says a knuckle's depth of give is the world being new and two knuckles is somebody's roof. Meanwhile her ear is out hunting the one note with a soft heart in it, the knock on a knuckle instead of a knee. Everyone in the Loaf is reading softness with some part of the body, and I am the one doing it with a tongue, forty times a shift, in the dark of my own head, with no idea I am being trained.

## The crime

There is one more thing and it is not a job.

Her rhythms are retired. She has had more names drummed out over her lifetime than anyone here — a husband, four of her own children, both of her sisters, the chewer she had when she was the one being handed food — and a retired rhythm is played once at the giving-over and never again. That is the law and it is a good one. A clan's history is a percussion piece nobody is allowed to play.

But the close-tongue makes no sound. Knuckles on the collarbone, palm flat on the sternum, thumb-taps at the wrist — you can conduct an hour of conversation inside a drip-curtain and the air stays empty. So while I am chewing, and my hands are otherwise doing nothing, she takes my left wrist in her hand and taps. Five to nine beats. A name that has been out of the world for forty years, going into a child's arm where it makes no noise at all. Then another. Then the same one again, because she likes it. She never explains and I never ask, and this is the only crime either of us will ever commit, and it is silent, and it is the reason she chose a chewer with a still mouth and a still face instead of one of the clever ones.

## After

I go flat-toothed at eleven and hand the office down to my cousin's boy and become an ordinary person with horn on her feet, and about eight years after that the ground gets slow with her — five days, six, dusted over with flour on the freshest swell we can find, and the mourning going on and on because she is still visible, and everyone getting frightened, and then on the ninth day she is gone and it is over and it was a good death after all.

They drum her name out once. I stand in the circle and let it happen.

Then I take a mallet with a copper collar and go out in front of the front, where I said at the start I did not belong, and on the first day I stop dead over a note with a soft heart in it and there is a plum of native copper under my foot — not because I have a gift, but because I spent two years being taught what soft means by a woman who could not see me, using the only instrument I had then, which was my mouth.

And all my life after that, whenever the hiss is loud and my right arm is swinging, my left hand is down at my side against my own wrist, tapping. Five to nine beats. Over and over. Where it makes no sound, and cannot be heard, and does not stop.
