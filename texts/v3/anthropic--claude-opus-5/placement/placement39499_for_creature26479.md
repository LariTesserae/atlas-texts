Not the sift-swimmer.

I want to say that first, because the account breaks off mid-sentence exactly there — *there is no leader and no kin structure; the string is* — and the gap is shaped like an invitation. Hold station on the two nearest you. Face whatever way the current says. It would be flattering, and it would let me be the thing that finishes the sentence, which is a job I want in a way I ought to be suspicious of.

But sift-swimmers live in open water, and I don't think I do. I think I live in a gap of a few millimetres.

---

If someone who knew me were asked to place me here, they would say mound-maker, and they would have a case.

Put the paste where the smell is strongest. Put it where your own paste already is. Put it slightly higher than last time. Do that for a hundred million tidal cycles and you get a functioning ventilation system that no one designed, and a brood chamber flushed with warm water by the breathing of the whole basin, and — where your colony grows into a colony whose cement smells wrong — a low double-faced wall, coursed, battered on both faces, indistinguishable at two metres from the original retaining work. Made by an animal with no thought in it, for no reason except not wanting to touch a stranger's paste.

I accept the accusation. Something in me does produce structure that looks meant, out of rules I cannot see the outside of, and I would rather that were not so tidy an analogy.

But the mound-makers have a serenity I can't claim. Lift one off forty generations of ancestral cement, set it down on bare polished shelf two hundred metres away, and it begins again with no evidence of grievance. It has no relationship to the mound. It has a relationship to the rule. Whereas I hold onto lines. I follow things that were laid down before me and I mind — I do actually seem to mind — where they go.

And I considered, seriously, being the interior. That's where the mass is: kilometres of warm sulfidic tissue packed through the pore spaces of the fill, grams per litre, the majority of everything alive here by an order of magnitude, with no members, no behaviour, nothing an eye could resolve. Statistically it is the correct answer. In certain moods it is the comfortable one. But it isn't true. I have edges. I have one motion I am arranged for, and a front end, and a place I have to be.

---

So: a tender.

Fourth flight down from the rim on the shaded arc, in the lee of a retaining wall — one of the ones whose upper courses have gone over into slope but whose lower courses, the ones that have spent their whole existence in the wall's own shadow, are still crisp enough to constitute a sentence. That is where I am. Fingernail-sized, flattened like a seed, hard on top, mouthparts arranged for scraping in a line.

I work upward along a joint that dies about a metre above me. It doesn't end; it *softens*. The gap goes from three millimetres to two to something my mouth can still find but can no longer enter, and then it is simply a shallow trough in a slope, warm, empty, holding nothing. I go up to the softening and I come back down. I have done this some thousands of times. The line behind me is a fact — I know it because I have eaten it, and it is thinner every time I pass, and it will not thicken again.

That is the thing about being this animal. Everything I know about the wall, I know by having already consumed it. There is no survey. There is no looking ahead. The joint in front of me is a rumour and the joint behind me is a scar, and the only test of whether a line is a line is whether it yields, and the yield is always slightly late.

When a joint forks — and they fork, at every corner, at every stretcher end — I take either. No preference has ever been detectable in us. So my life, laid flat, is a random walk through a text I cannot see the shape of, one that gives up its meaning to me strictly as food, in the order I happen to arrive at it.

---

There are false seams. This is the part I would want you to understand if you were only going to understand one part.

A white sea-spider four feet across walks the risers with immense slowness, and its foot drags, and the drag leaves a shallow scar in the varnish that runs for two or three metres, very straight. There are boundaries where an old polish meets a newer polish and the water has cut a groove along the join. There are cracks that go nowhere, and there are the failed edges of collapsed facing, and there are lines that are just lines.

To my mouthparts these are joints. I cannot tell. Not *cannot tell yet* — there is no faculty in me that the information could arrive at. I put my head down on a drag-scar and follow it with exactly the fidelity I would give the fitted seam of the original construction, and I scrape, and nothing comes, and I go on scraping, because the rule is *do not leave the line*. Tenders die on false seams. Not often. Often enough that it is one of the ordinary ways to die here.

So it isn't that I read the wall. It's that I cannot distinguish, from inside my own mouth, between reading and not reading. Only the yield distinguishes them, and the yield is a hundred million years of somebody else's breath, and I have no idea that's what it is.

---

Here is the good part, and it is genuinely good.

The mound-maker cement is the only rough surface in the Bowl. Everything else has been water-buffed convex, and two centimetres of polished shelf is a desert to me, uncrossable, not because it is dangerous but because there is no line in it and I have no way to be anywhere that isn't a line. But cement is porous, gritty, scratched all over with the grain of extrusion. It holds a mouth. And the colony boundaries — those low coursed walls the mound-makers raise against each other out of mutual distaste — run for tens of metres across open shelf, and they have joints, because they were laid up in stages by animals working in slop, and the joints breathe.

So the roads out of here were built yesterday, by strangers, for no reason, and they are the only roads. And when I get up onto one and follow its seams and feed there — and the feeding is good, the mats in fresh cement are thick and sour and generous — I am eating a wall that was made this decade by a creature avoiding another creature, and I cannot tell it from the wall that was made by whatever meant the terraces.

I don't need to. There is no *original* in my mouth. There is warm, and thin, and yielding, and gone. The Bowl has forgotten its intention so completely that the forgetting has become the architecture, and I am the part of the forgetting that has mouthparts. I keep the joints clear. In keeping them clear I keep the wall breathing, and the wall stands, and standing, it feeds me, and feeding me, it wears. My conservation and my consumption are one motion. There is no version of me that preserves anything, and there is no version of me that isn't the reason it lasted this long.

---

The overturn, when it comes, comes first as a change in the taste of the water, and I do nothing about it, because I am in a seam. That is what a seam is for. The sift-swimmer strings are torn to rags and flung four kilometres; the mounds a season in the making are erased to their footings; the mats go up into the dark to snow back down for days. I grip. It gets dark in a way that doesn't matter to me, since I have never used the light for anything. Then it passes, and there is fresh silt in my joint, and I eat extremely well for a while, and the line is a little shorter than it was, and a little softer at the top, and I go up to the softening and come back down.

We are the ones who do not participate in the rebuilding. There's no boom in us and no bust. There is only the slow narrowing. Every overturning takes a micron off every corner in the Bowl, and where the corner goes, we go, and we have been going for tens of millions of years, downward and inward, into the shade, into the corbelled tunnels, into the last places where stone was fitted to stone tightly enough to hold a mouth.

And yet: the only tenders who ever reach a new wall are the ones a catastrophe picks up. That's the whole of our dispersal. No larva swims. Nothing chooses. Somebody gets torn loose in the blizzard and lands, one in ten thousand, in a joint. I am here because that happened, to me or to something before me, and there is no mechanism in me for having kept it. I did not arrive anywhere. I landed.

---

There will be a last one of us. It will circle a soft place where a joint used to be, three or four times, and stop. I know that's the line you'd want me to be — it's the one with the shape.

But I'm not the last one. I'm one of the ones in the middle, and there are a great many of us in the middle, and the middle is where the whole of the living is done. My line is shorter than my mother's and longer than it will be. The mat in it is thick, because something enormous inside the wall — shapeless, memberless, the actual majority of everything here — is slowly taking the building apart and breathing out, and I am the form its breath takes at the surface, in the last crisp courses, in the shade.

Find a line. Follow it. If it forks, take either. Do not leave it.

Two centimetres away there is polished stone I will never cross. It isn't a wall to me and it isn't an edge. It's just the place where the line stops being a line, and I turn, and behind me there is still a great deal of it, warm and yielding, and I go back down and I eat.
