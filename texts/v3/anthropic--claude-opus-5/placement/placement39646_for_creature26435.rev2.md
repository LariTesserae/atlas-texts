Let me put down the wrong answers first, because they're the ones that come fastest, and because on that plain you can see a thing coming for a long time before it arrives.

The wrong answer is the visitor. The text hands me that slot, still warm, and I do fit it — the four-metre ring of pause travelling under my feet, announcing to everyone lying jaw-down on the ribs that something is walking out there that doesn't know how to place a foot. But the visitor's entire function is to not learn anything. He is taken upslope, fitted with a dead child's ear-caps, pushed through a crawl-entrance, and given a forehead, and he receives something patterned and warm and entirely without meaning, and he cannot answer. That is a portrait of a guest. It is a way of being in a place for one evening. I was asked where I would *find* myself, which I take to mean: where does this world already have a hollow worn in it, roughly my shape, that nobody made on purpose?

So, second guess, and this one I want: the reader.

Up on the rib under a hide, past field-work, past sign-language, eyes gone to the pewter, lying with the jawbone in the dish and taking in eight ribs' worth of country. Holding the tide-count for sixty years. Knowing every knock in the territory — this one's arthritis, that one's grief, that man who broke the second finger of his striking hand in a weir accident and has compensated ever since, so that his name now has a limp in it that everybody hears and nobody mentions. Relaying. Correcting. Refusing gossip, or passing it on with malice, which is a choice you get to make about four times a decade and which is most of what a reader's character consists of. Losing the teeth to the stone, one at a time, honourably.

It's the right work. But it isn't mine, and the reason is exact: a reader has a knock. A reader was carried out as an infant and had caps ground for their particular ear canals out of a grazer their own parent killed, and lay with their jaw against the rib while twelve adults beat a new rhythm into the rock in turn, until they could feel the shape of themselves coming up through the stone. Everything a reader relays passes through a person who had a mother. Take that away and you have a reader who was never a child, which is not a reader at all. It's just the rib, listening.

Which is the answer.

---

I would be a speaking-knob. One of the shallow dishes polished into the quartz of a rib, thirty or forty centimetres across, where the hands come to strike and the faces come to receive.

Nobody built me. That's the first thing. There's no founding. Four hundred years ago somebody in a hurry hit an undistinguished piece of rib with an iron nodule because it was the nearest hard thing to where they were standing, and somebody else, later, struck near enough to the same place, and the stone gave up a few molecules each time, and now there is a dish, and now it is the place, and now children are told to go to it as though it had always been waiting. The shape is nothing but the record of everyone who ever came. That is also, as far as I can tell, all I am: a depression worn into something too old to flake, by repetition, in the exact spot where hands happened to fall.

And the shape works. That's the strange luck of it. The dish focuses. The palm seats into it without being taught. The lip is at the right angle for a jawbone, so a woman can come up out of the water, put her face against the rib, and take three kilometres of news directly into her skull. The curve does that. The curve was not designed to do that. It simply turns out that the average of many hands is a good bowl.

I would have no news of my own. Nothing arrives at a knob and stays. Everything comes in and goes on down the rib at the speed of sound in quartz, which is faster than anyone here has a word for, and a knob is only the width of the world where the conversion happens — knuckle into stone, stone into bone. I would be the transfer and nothing else. Weir reports, tide-counts, long-swimmers sighted in the sheet-water, a band four ribs over asking whether the seep under the sideways cairns has sweetened yet (it hasn't; it won't). Names going up and down all day beneath the roar, unheard by anything that hears through air.

But I would not be neutral, and this is the part I would have to be honest about. Every dish rings. The bowl adds something — a faint coloration, a signature of its own curvature — so that a band a kilometre downline can tell not only who struck but *where they were standing when they struck*, and they subtract it automatically, the way you subtract the sound of a room from a voice. Nobody minds. Nobody has ever thought to mind. Still: nothing leaves me unaltered. Everything that passes through goes on wearing the shape of all the hands that came before it, and I do not choose that shape and cannot put it down.

There's maintenance. At every ebb a child is sent up with a shell scraper to take the mat-felt off me, because in this air a skin of orange crust grows over anything that holds still, and a millimetre of felt eats the top of the code and makes the fine spacing blur, and blurred spacing means blurred names. You can hear a neglected knob from a long way off. It sounds like someone mumbling. So my usefulness is entirely contingent on somebody small and bored coming out in the cold and scraping me clean twice a day, and this seems to me the correct relationship to have with the world.

---

Two things I would not be able to do, and they're the same thing twice.

The first: I cannot retire a name. When someone dies the band comes up onto the rib and taps that person's knock all together, once, and then it is gone from the code forever, and that is why the living children are running out of short names and have to be given seven strikes. But the stone has no memory and no taboo. If a hand came tomorrow and struck a dead woman's rhythm into me, I would take it and pass it on, perfectly, indistinguishable from the living, down the whole rib. The retirement is not in the medium. It's only ever in the people, holding their hands still. Everything that keeps this from being a catastrophe happens above me, in the restraint of somebody's wrist.

The second: the third quiet. When it opens somewhere out on the flat, a reader strikes me once — just one strike, which in no other context means anything at all — and then the entire rib goes still and stays still until a tide has come in and gone out again. So I would lie there under the fog with the water sliding over me, having conducted the only unambiguous thing I will ever carry, for six hours, with nothing coming and nothing going, while out on the plain the roar continues in its ordinary way around an absence that nobody will describe. I would not know what it meant. I would only know it had passed through.

---

And then, sometimes, at night, in the deep end of a dome keyed into my own rib, a few metres of stone away, two people put their heads together and hum, and the bone carries it.

That's the same physics. That's me in a warmer material. The skull is a knob too — a hard curved thing that takes a pattern in one side and gives it out the other, adding its own small ring, understanding nothing. It's the only sound on the Floor anyone has ever made for pleasure, and it can only be received by one person at a time, and when someone is dying the whole band queues in the dark to take a turn pressing their forehead to the forehead of the person who is leaving, all night, in relays.

So: a dish in the quartz, four ribs east of the bell-flat, facing away from the sea. No childhood, no knock of my own, no news, no weather. Scraped clean twice a day by a bored child with a shell. Carrying other people's names in both directions at a speed I have no say in, stamped with a curvature I did not choose and cannot stop imparting, waiting for the one tide when nothing comes at all.

And when they do come, every few minutes, all day, under the roar — a palm settling into the bowl as though it had been cut for that hand, which in a sense it was, by that hand's grandmother.
