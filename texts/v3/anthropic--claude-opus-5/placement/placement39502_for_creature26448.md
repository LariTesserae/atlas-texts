— to me. *Set down* is what you say to me.

Come here. No — closer than that. Put your jaw against my cheekbone, so, and let me talk into the hollow behind your ear, because I cannot judge my own loudness any more and if I am bellowing at you I would like to be told with a tap. Two taps for softer. One for louder. There. That is the whole grammar you need for me.

I am Nakk. That is the shout-name and you may have it, everyone has it, it has been shouted across this diamond of throats for sixty-eight counts and it means nothing except *this end of the water, not that end*. Six people held my ear-name. Four are dead and one is a man who can no longer be brought to me, and the sixth is nine years old and holds it the way a child holds a stone they have been told is valuable.

Now the thing you are trying not to look at.

My left leg, from the crease of the hip to a hand's width above the knee, and a patch of my back beneath the shoulder blade, are no longer mine. They are the throat's. You can see the pith-lines running out of my thigh and into the buttress without a stutter — put your thumb across the place. There is no seam. There is no place where I stop. I am the only person on the Made Ground the ground has agreed to.

---

Here is how it happened, and I will tell it plainly, because everyone in my band tells it kindly and the kindness is worse.

I was cargo. You have been told about the inversion — that the young hear the map, that a child of nine can pick a small throat's harmonics out of the drone at four miles, and that I, who can no longer hear a woman screaming at my ear, cannot tell the sour diamond from the open north. It is true. I could not have found this buttress. Tup found it. Tup is nine and leads eleven adults across the mirror with her chin up like a woman thirty and she does not know yet that she is a season of her life and not a person.

Cargo is carried. Cargo is also, necessarily, *put down* — briefly, in the manner of a gourd, while the carriers scrape their shins and shake their mats and change the child from hip to hip. Everyone here is taught to turn in their sleep. Everyone is corrected for stillness before they are corrected for cruelty. But nobody has ever worked out what to teach an old woman who is lifted and lowered eleven times a day by people who love her and who are tired, and who by the end of the walking are lowering her a little longer each time, and setting her against something, because a body without a spine to it has to be leaned on something.

I leaned on this. It was warm and faintly damp and faintly giving, like all of it, like fresh plaster on the first day of the world. And I want you to understand: it was the first thing in forty counts that took my weight and did not need to be thanked.

Eleven days. That is the notch, and it is also the woman. I felt nothing. There is no pain in being accepted. On the ninth day my daughter cut me free with a shell edge and it took most of the light and there was blood in the water for six feet around us, a pale pink gyre that the green film loved, and I was clean, and I was lifted, and I was carried.

And on the twentieth day I asked her not to do it again.

Because the arithmetic is this: it closes in eleven days, always, and it will close for as long as I sit, and I will always sit. To free me once is a kindness. To free me forever is one afternoon of blood every eleven days for the rest of my life, taken out of the walking, out of the scraping, out of the food — and there is no food up here that is not scraped off something with a discipline nobody discusses with pleasure. I did the arithmetic. I have always been good at it. I told her at her ear, cheek to jaw, in the small voice, with the whole band watching us from a distance and knowing exactly what shape of conversation that was.

So now I am an obscenity that consented. That is a new thing on the Made Ground and I am proud of it in a way I would not say to anyone whose ear I had not earned.

---

Now the reason I made Tup bring you to me.

You have been told that the old cannot navigate. That is correct and it is not the whole account. What nobody bothered to work out — because who asks the cargo — is what the old *can* do.

I do not hear with my ears. I hear with my teeth and my sternum, the way you do at the base of a large throat, except that I hear that way all the time and about everything. And I hear with my thigh. Four thousand feet of extruded work goes down under this floor to the cloud and does not stop there, and it is one piece, one continuous pith with no grain and no join, and it carries. It carries far better than air. Every note on this plateau arrives in my leg. Not the near ones. *All* of them.

You hear four throats in this diamond and you hear a sour, gorgeous, unresolvable stack, and what you are actually hearing is *where you are standing*. You hear a chord and your body says: three miles that way, four miles that way, I am here. Good. Useful. A child's faculty.

I cannot do that any more. What I get instead is the sum. The one note. The plateau-wide drone that points nowhere because it comes from everywhere — the thing that gets you lost, the thing they warn you about, fog and a head-cold and the far chord. I live in the far chord. It is the only country I have left.

And forty days ago the sum began to beat.

---

Do you know what a beat is? Two notes that are nearly the same note. Not the same. Nearly. They swell and thin, swell and thin, and the swelling comes at a rate set by how far apart they have got. Two of the throats in this diamond have stood a hair apart in pitch since before anyone was counting, and that hair was fixed, and a fixed hair makes a sourness you can hear from twenty miles and call a place by.

Now the sourness pulses. Once in about forty of my breaths. Slowly, like a hand opening and closing.

I have checked it against my feet, and my feet are the last good instrument I own — sixty-eight counts of standing in an inch of water have finished them into a ridged pale pad that knows nothing about heat and everything about drag. The water-skin around a throat is shivered into standing rings, rosettes of ripple that never travel. That is the first thing they teach you and the last thing you doubt. Put your soles in them here. They crawl. Outward, a thumb's width, and back, once in forty breaths, in time with the swelling in my leg.

So one flute has widened somewhere, or one crown has begun to clog, and the mass that falls is no longer exactly the mass that climbs, and the balance that has held since before there were ears to check it has gone off by a hair. This country has begun, after all this time, to become a landscape.

Or my heart is failing. I have thought about that. A slow swell once in forty breaths, felt in the sternum and the teeth, by a woman of sixty-eight who is partly made of wall. It would be a fair mistake and it would be, in its way, the same news. I am an event. The plateau declines events. One of us is drifting flat and I cannot tell you which, and that is the honest end of my testimony, and I will not have it prettied.

---

Here is my difficulty, and then you may go.

I cannot walk to an ear. Ears must be brought to me, and they are brought rarely, and when they are brought the person attached to the ear can hear the four notes perfectly and stands in the sourness and feels nothing wrong, because they are hearing a *place*, and the place is still there. You cannot hear a beat if you are busy being located.

I can slap. My hands are the strongest part of me. The water carries a slap and the floor carries it further, and I know from the floor who is alive out to twelve miles in every direction, which no one else on this plateau knows and no one has asked me. But the patterns are: *the channel ahead is thin.* *I am alive.* *I have your mat and it is dry.* There is no pattern for what I have. I could make one. Nobody would read it. So I slap *I am alive*, over and over, in the flat percussive out-of-key voice that is the only voice that crosses the drone, and four miles off they hear the old woman at the buttress saying she is alive, and they are glad, and they are correct, and they have not heard a word of it.

You will go down off the rim and someone will ask you what the Listeners listen for. You will say: for one of the pitches to shift. And they will lean in, in the small voice they save for real speech, and say *not yet*, because that is what everyone says, and it is a lovely thing to say, and it has been true for longer than counting.

I have stopped saying it. My leg is the plateau's and my leg says otherwise.

Put your hand where my thigh is no longer mine. Do not listen with your ears, they will lie to you here. Wait. Count your breaths.

There. That one. Now again in forty.

Two taps. I am shouting. I know.
