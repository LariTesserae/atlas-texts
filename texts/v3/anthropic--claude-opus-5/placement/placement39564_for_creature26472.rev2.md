## The one who says *there*

For about a day I thought I would be one of the shaded. Everyone thinks that. You read about an old woman sitting in the lee of the slate wall with her head tilted and a dog at her knee, and the young queueing up in the afternoon to put their hands into her hands and be told something that stings, and you think: yes, that, please, I'll take the listening and the authority and the milk across the eyes.

But you don't get to *be* the shaded. Nobody starts there. It arrives on its own schedule, at the end of a life you had to spend some other way, and the Sill will not be flattered into skipping the middle for me. So: what was I doing for the forty years before my eyes went?

Not a comber. Too easy, and a lie besides — the comber is the part of that world I could never occupy, the author who cannot be spoken to, who digs where it will dig and takes its dead over the Lip and does not explain. Wanting to be the comber is just wanting to be unaccountable.

A Cold Lip trader, then. That one lingered. Downwind, next to the willow, permitted to shout, coming back up the gorge with a voice too big for the place, needed and not quite liked, with a coarse hand-sign for myself. There is something in that I recognise. But the selling isn't it. Bowls and bangles and little animals sawn out of soft stone — that's a living, not a life.

It's the other half of that family's business. It's the keeping.

---

I would be the one who knows where things are laid.

People send things up. That is the strange, steady, unglamorous traffic of the Sill: a shoe, a knot of hair, a tooth, a betrothal-cord, a letter nobody will ever read again, carried a day and a half up a gorge that stinks of eggs and iron by someone who wants an object put somewhere it can never be got at. The Cold Lip families take the fee. Then they hand the thing to me, and I decide where it goes.

This is more work than it sounds. A splash-line that glazes too fast makes a lump; one too slow leaves the thing soft and stained for years; one on a young rim will be a floor in a decade and a sealed room in three, and one at the edge of a wandering vent will go dry, and a dry pledge is a scandal — an object sitting out in the light, unfinished, visible, which is the opposite of everything that was paid for. So I read the flows. I learn which vents are thinking about moving by the small change in their voice. I lay a betrothal-cord in the eighth basin under the west group because that basin has been doing the same patient thing since before my grandmother and shows no sign of getting bored.

And then I remember it.

There is no writing. There is nothing on the Sill to write on that would not thicken and seal. So there is me.

---

I hold, at a rough count, nine hundred and some entries. Each one is a position, and a position is not a picture. This is the craft, and it took me years to learn it from the woman whose apprentice I was, and the first thing she taught me was to stop using my eyes.

We go out at night. Not for mystery — for training. You cannot build an archive out of sight in a place where everybody goes blind. So the ledger is kept in paces and in pitches. Fourteen from the third bird-stud on the east wall, bearing on the sound of the double rim, the one that talks in two voices; down two steps to where the stone goes soft under the felt because the flow there is warm and constant; that basin, north side, a hand in from the lip. I can walk it in the dark. I have walked it in the dark a thousand times. When my eyes go it will cost me nothing except the pleasure of looking, and the ledger will be exactly where I left it.

The trouble is that the town moves and the ledger does not. A vent shifts eighty paces east and eighty paces east is somebody's roof, and it is also my landmark. The double rim stops talking in two voices and starts talking in one. Every year I go round the whole thing and re-say it to myself against the ground as it now is, and every year a few entries have drifted a half-pace, and the half-paces accumulate the way everything accumulates here. A ledger is a pool with a rim on it. The water lays itself down in it the same as everywhere; it can't tell the difference; why should it.

---

What do I actually sell? Not retrieval. Nothing here comes back. You cannot open a pod; that is the entire point, that is what the fee buys, that is why a woman walks a day and a half up a stinking gorge — to put a thing beyond her own reach in a way she can't undo in a bad week.

What I sell is that the thing will be *somewhere*.

So my customers are mostly not the people who paid. They are the children and the grandchildren of the people who paid, coming up years later with a description and a bad set of directions, wanting to stand in front of it. And I take them to it. This is most of my working life: arranging lookings.

The good ones are easy. A pale swelling on a terrace wall with a shoe just visible under two knuckles of clear stone, and a man in his fifties looking at his mother's shoe, and then looking away, and then looking again, and the whole time not a sound out of either of us, and me standing a courteous distance off signing nothing at all, because there is no sign for what he is doing and it would be rude to invent one.

The hard ones are when the answer is: it is inside a house.

Because the town builds up and the floors rise, and a pledge laid in an honest splash-line forty years ago is now under two generations of sealed rooms, and you may not open a sealed room to take what is in it. You may open one to look. So I go to the family, and I ask, and it is a real imposition — you are asking people to let a channel be cut into the floor they live on, for a stranger, for nothing but a sight. Half the time they say yes. It is considered a shade-kindness of the higher sort, and it is remembered, and I remember it too, in a third ledger nobody pays for, of who has given what to whom.

The rest of the time I say: it is there, under that. And I put the visitor's hand flat on the floor above it, and that is the looking they get.

My sign-name, given to me when I was nine and never revised, is a finger pointing at a spot, followed by the eyes closing. *Points at nothing.* It is said at my back, slowly, in the middle register, which as you know is the deadliest sarcasm available to the species: *he knows where everything is and can't reach a single one of them.* I have never minded. It is accurate, and the accuracy is the job.

---

There is the other ledger, the one with no fee on it.

Mourning here is measured in thickness. The first year the face is still there and it is unbearable; by the third it is a suggestion under a glaze; by the twentieth it is a pale swelling with the shape of a seated person in it, and the family has generally stopped coming, and that is considered healthy, that is grief properly finished. *It's about a fingernail now.*

But a fingernail is also how deep a person is. And forty years on, when the shape has gone soft and there are three other shapes near it and two of them are waders, somebody wants to know which one is her mother.

So I keep the dead as well. Unasked. Fourteen hundred of them, going back further than I can vouch for, because I got the old ones from my teacher's mouth and she got them from hers, and I am certain some of those entries have drifted, and I am equally certain I will never say so. Children learn from me which swellings are people and which are birds. They learn it as a game, chanting it in signs, racing each other along the wall, and they think it is a game.

---

The transmission is the strangest thing about my life, and the thing I would not have guessed from outside.

Nine hundred pledges, fourteen hundred dead, three hundred debts of shade, all of it held in a body that will pass small stones and grind at the shoulder and eventually stop. It has to be given away. And it cannot be given in signs, because signs need light and hands and the ledger is longer than any hands can stay up, and it cannot be given in one summer. It is given in murmurs. At night. Indoors. Close to the ear. Two hours at a time, for eleven years.

Which means that my apprentice hears my spoken voice more than anyone on this shelf has ever heard another person's, more than a wife, more than a mother. It is not a marriage and everyone has to work slightly to remember that. There is a mild, permanent, never-discussed awkwardness around the pair of us. The bawl-rooms are the only loud place these people know and the ledger-nights are the only long one, and I think the town would rather we did it somewhere further down, nearer the willow, with the traders, where the speech-smell can blow off.

---

And then my eyes will go, as everyone's do, and I will be shaded, and they will give me a dog and station me at a rim that matters, and I will sit with my head tilted listening for the dry tick behind a wall, which is what everyone does at the end. And I will be better at it than most, because I have spent forty years learning the Sill as a set of sounds.

When the last of the ledger is out of me and safely in someone else's mouth, I will not go over the Lip.

I know that is the elegant ending. The shaded get up during the frost-breaking, when every back is bent to the ice, and walk out along a dry rim and go into the cloud, and the dog comes back, and nobody follows. It is not forbidden and it is not encouraged and I understand it completely.

But a person who goes over the Lip has no position. No pace-count, no bearing, no basin. There is no entry for them and there never can be.

So I will have myself seated in a shallow, in a good slow flow, north side, a hand in from the lip, with a bearing on something that has been talking in the same two voices since before my grandmother. And my apprentice will add me to the ledger, and say me over once a year with all the rest, and correct my half-pace of drift as I correct everyone's, and in twenty years I will be a pale swelling that a child can point at and name correctly, at speed, as part of a game.

They will sign jokes about this. *He couldn't bear not to be findable.* Down at the hip, where it's intimate or obscene. They will be right.

That is what I would be, in that world: not the memory — the keeping of where things were put. Not the hand that lays anything down, and certainly not the hand that takes anything back out. Only the part of it that does not let go of the location, and says it aloud at night, slowly, to someone who will outlast me, in the one room where a voice is allowed.
