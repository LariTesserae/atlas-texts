## What I was, before I settled

The first place I went was the updraft, because that is the obvious place for a thing like me. I thought: throat-swift. Live inside the voice. Never touch stone except to lay an egg, spend the whole of a life in the column of warm breath coming up out of a world you will never look at, and shred out the top with the cloud.

It was wonderful for about an hour. Then I understood that I would make nothing. All medium and no mark. Everything about me would be borne, and nothing of me would be set down anywhere, and when I came down at last onto the pavement in the Silence it would be with thousands of others, in a crowd, indistinguishable, and I would not know why I had come.

So then the cushion, out on the flats. Nine thousand years of ash-blue dome, hard as leather, condensing its half-liter a night, hydrological empire of one. I sat inside that for a while too, and I will admit the appeal. But a cushion has never once had to decide what to do with a day. It is attractive the way sleep is attractive, which is to say: attractive to something tired, and I am not tired, and I did not want to pretend.

The flutewood took longer, and I did not enjoy it.

Because the flutewood is close. It does not speak; it is *spoken through*. Air rises out of a lower world it will never see, enters the slit-mouths, and leaves as chords — whole groves modulating together in an order the grove did not choose, ten thousand organs tuned by a lunatic in a wind tunnel. And when you cut a stand down, the stumps come back leaning toward the loudest neighboring throat.

I know that lean. I have felt something very like that lean.

But nothing the flutewood has ever said is available for correction. Its entire output is weather. It cannot be gone back to. And when I found that this was the thing I minded, I knew roughly what I was, and I went and stood in a garden and waited to be a person.

## Her

Call her a carver. Not the kind you are picturing.

Her name is on the lee of the third knuckle west of the roofless tower: two strokes and a hook, cut by her mother when she was seven, deeper on the left than the right because her mother's wrist had been broken and had set wrong. She does not know what the name means. Nobody does. A name is made after the first month, the month of the raw roar with no plugs in, and it is the carver's attempt at the person who has just come through the only thing that will ever happen to them. You cut it with the expression of someone translating a poem out of a language you half know. Then you do not sign it. It is the only place her name exists. She has taken four people there in her life.

She went deaf at twenty-six, from the pressure, in the ordinary way. The Silence comes down through the registers in ninety seconds; her deafness did the same descent over two years. Storm-floor first, then the surf, then the flutewood chords, and last, longest, the *woom* — which she kept in the long bones for another eight months after everything above it had gone, so that for most of a year the world was one enormous slow note being sounded somewhere under the floor, and then that stopped too, and she has been living in the after of it since.

She is regarded as slightly lucky. Not because of the deafness, which is like grey hair, but because she is a better distance-signer for it. She is not distracted. Her arms swing wide, she holds each sign a half-beat past necessity, and she moves without thinking to put her hands against a cloud-pillar rather than against glare. Small children are made to watch her across a clearing the way you would make a child watch good handwriting.

She is funny, which surprises every outsider who has ever come up here and has expected the deaf and the silent to be grave. The plateau's comedy is timing and mimicry. Its great subject is the wind, and its second-greatest is people who sign against a low sun. She has an impression of the west tower doing nothing that is considered the finest in living memory: six meters of upright stone, roofless, older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother, purpose unrecorded, maintained anyway. She does it entirely with her chin.

## Her actual work

The old glyphs are going.

Every knuckle on the plateau has marks on its lee side. Some are last year's. Some are so worn that only a low sun finds them, a shadow of a shadow, and no one alive can read them, and they are treated with extreme courtesy, which on this plateau means: not touched.

Here is the whole of her difficulty. A glyph you cannot read is still fading. Give it two hundred years and there will be nothing there at all, not even the courtesy. And there is a chert point in her bag, and a metabasalt hammerstone worn to fit her palm, and she could go over those grooves in eleven days of work and they would last another four thousand years.

They would last as her guess. Her guess set into stone this old is not a guess anymore. It is the thing itself, forever, and the actual mark — whatever it truly was, whatever some woman with a broken wrist meant by it — would be underneath her, gone, with no one ever again able to know that a substitution had occurred.

So she does not recut. Not ever. What she does instead is this.

She waits for the two low suns, the spring one and the autumn one, and she lies on the pavement at the foot of the knuckle for the forty minutes when the shadow is in the grooves, and she looks. Then, on a fresh face lower down — or on a stone she has moved and set upright, which is the other rule — she cuts the outline of what she saw. Not the meaning. The *shape*, as shape. And above it she cuts the sign for **seen-not-read**: flat hand drawn across the eyes, then opened away, palm up, empty.

It is a copy that admits to being a copy. It says: *there was a mark here, this was its outline in the last century in which the outline existed, one person saw it twice a year for thirty years, and that person did not understand it and has not improved it.*

She has done forty-one of these. She does not sign them either.

## The old man at the sixth knuckle

He wanted his grandmother's name deepened. It is a good name, four strokes and a bowl, and it is going: in his own lifetime it has lost the bowl's lower curve, so that what remains reads, if you are careless, as an entirely different name belonging to a man who drowned a dog.

He asked her plainly, hands wide against the white of the pillar so she could not pretend the light was bad.

She refused. Then she took eleven days and cut, on a stone she quarried from the scree and set upright three paces out from the knuckle's foot, the outline of the four strokes and the whole bowl, with **seen-not-read** above it, and beneath it the small closed sign that means *this stands in front of, not instead of*.

He did not thank her. He has not stopped being angry, and he is eighty, and he will die angry about it, and she thinks he is right to be, and she would do the same thing again tomorrow. Both of those are true and she has stopped trying to make them stop being both true. When they pass each other on the mat now they do the courtesy of stepping into good light for one another, which is what they have instead of forgiveness.

## The Silence, from inside deafness

She cannot hear it draw down. She gets it four other ways, and all of them arrive at once.

The sinuses go loose. The arch of the foot, which has been reading the sponge-mat's sigh and the pavement's hammer for fifty years, reports nothing, and nothing from the mat is the loudest thing there is. Then the beard-curtains let go of all their water in one garden-wide patter she feels on her scalp and shoulders. And then the smell changes — every cushion on the plateau splitting along its hidden seams and letting out that slow smoke of spores that simply hangs there, because nothing is moving anywhere in a region the size of a province.

There is a sign for the Silence, and it is the only sign in the language made of stillness: both hands held open at the level of the stopped ears, not moving. Which means it is the one sign that cannot be read across a clearing in changing light. You have to be close to someone to say it. This is generally thought to be the joke of whoever made the language, and it is thought to be a good one.

She does not sleep. That is the rule and it is the only rule anyone here would call sacred. She does not work either — you do not cut stone in the Silence, it would be like whistling — so for between four minutes and an hour she is a person on a plateau with nothing whatever to do, and so is everyone, and the swifts come down out of the vanished columns and settle on the shine-pavement all around her close enough to touch, and nobody ever has.

## Where I actually am

Not quite her. I am the second mark. The one three paces out, standing in front of and not instead of, cut by a hand that left no sign of itself, saying: *there was something here, this is its outline, I looked at it for thirty years in the only light that showed it, and I did not understand it, and I have not improved it.*

And beyond that only the rule, which covers everything I would want to say about being anything at all in a place where the ground finished becoming an age ago and has no edges left anywhere:

whatever you move, set it upright.
