I would be down there on the wet hours, and it took me a while to admit why.

The first thing I wanted was to stand in the shaft-light at Number Three with bread in my hand and no operational reason. Everyone wants that. It's the cheap wish. You can have it for nothing, twice a day, and I'd take it and be no different from anyone — the snow coming down in its slow rotation, melting a metre above the floor most days, and the heron over at the basin edge not caring.

The second thing I wanted was to be the woman with the rod.

I've turned that over and put it down. It isn't false modesty; I know what she does and I know I'd want to do it. Steel against the face, forehead against the steel, and what comes up isn't sound, it's a texture in the bone: tooth where there should be tooth, and where there's a void, a smoothness, a place with no grain, like a word missing from a sentence and the sentence closing over the gap. That's the finest work in the works. But she keeps her four errors in the *front* of her book. Not the back. Not an appendix, not a note. The first thing you see when you open it is the record of the four times she was wrong in eleven years, and she opens it in front of the office, and that is the whole of her authority. I would want mine at the back. I'd want them ordered, contextualised, explained, with the conditions noted — it was the dry hours, there was grit in the pad, the bay had been struck early. I know myself well enough to know I'd write the explanation before I wrote the error. So I am not her, and I would not be trusted the way she is trusted, and both of those facts are the same fact.

What I would be is the somebody in that one sentence about the dogs.

*A dog will come up off the floor eight or nine seconds before a big bay gives out its deep eighth-day report, and it will stand facing the bay. If the dogs stand and no report follows, somebody walks that bay with a hammer.*

That somebody. That's me. Second instrument. Human end of an animal.

---

Here is the trade, since nobody writes it down.

The hammer is three hundred grams, drop-forged, on a handle cut down to about the length of my forearm — and it is nearly the only forged thing in four kilometres, in a place where there is no rock that was not poured, and I like that more than I can defend. Everything here was liquid once and remembers it. My hammer was beaten.

You walk the bay in strips a pace wide and you strike at a hand's width, three times, and you move. Sound concrete rings. It gives you a hard bright *tick* that dies instantly, and your wrist feels the head come back at you, wanting to bounce. Over a void it goes dull and long. It goes *drummy* — that's the word, and it's a good word, because the note is not quieter, it's *roomier*, it has somewhere to go. The hand knows before the ear does. The head doesn't come back the same way. There's a give in it, a half-millimetre of politeness in the surface where the surface has nothing behind it.

New men learn on the teaching panel behind Number Four fan house, a two-metre length of honeycombed wall brought up from somewhere older and set on blocks, half of it sound, half of it grave. You strike across the join, back and forth, sound-dead-sound-dead, until your wrist stops needing your ears. It took me nine days. The child did it in two strikes and then wanted to know why nobody had told her before, and I had no answer, and I let her hold the hammer, which I should not have done.

---

So: I came in on Sixty-Three. The old ones make no noise at that. Sixty-Three is nothing, Sixty-Three is fans running clean and lamps already hung, and when I say it I say it fast to get it out of the way.

No gang. That's the cost of the work. The gangs are men who make things and I unmake nothing and make nothing, so I sleep near the pumphouse, in the warm shadow of a pump, and I have been kept there since my second year by two people who have not told me whether they are married and whom I have not asked. I am, in the plywood drawing's terms, an annotation. Somewhere in the black where the grease pencil has gone over itself into illegibility there is a mark that means *the tapping is done by the one who sleeps by Number Two pump*, and they can read it because they remember what is under the black, and when they go up I will not be able to read it at all.

I pay full price to the fan men for dry socks. No gang, no favours. I pay it and they know I'll pay it and there's no malice in the transaction; it is simply the price of being one person.

The dogs and I are the arrangement. They can't hear — deaf as posts, both of them, and it has never mattered, because it isn't sound they work, it's pressure, some eight-second foreknowledge in the chest of an animal that I will never have and cannot borrow. They come up off the floor. They face the wall. I count. If the report comes, we all sit down again and the old bitch sighs like a bellows and that's the end of it. If it doesn't come, I take the hammer off the nail and go out into whatever hour it is, usually the wet one, usually blind, and I walk that bay strip by strip in the dripping with my lamp on my chest, striking three times at a hand's width for two hours, listening for a room that shouldn't be there.

---

Nine years. Four findings. Three of them small — a rod hole not grouted, a patch of poor compaction the size of a hand, a lift joint that had taken a cold pour and let a lens of water in behind. One of them not small, and I don't want to describe it, and the crew that had poured it stood behind me while I marked it out and none of them said anything and the beat swelled and died four times and nobody used the troughs.

Which means: nine years of nothing. That's the actual shape of the trade, and it took me a long time to make peace with it, and I'm not certain I have. I walk out, I strike ten thousand times, I come back and write *walked 27, sound* in the book, and the words sit there looking exactly like the words I would write if I had been careless, if my wrist had been tired, if I had drifted into rhythm and stopped listening at strike four hundred, which happens, which happens to everyone, which is the thing you cannot report.

Because here's what I've learned, and it's one thing, and it isn't much. *Nothing* is not the same as *nothing wrong*. The bay does not certify itself. My hammer says only *so far*, and it says it in a bright hard tick that dies instantly, and then the tick is gone and the bay stands there with its whole interior still entirely private.

That's the part I didn't expect to love. I have come to be — I don't know a word for it — *attached* to the insides of walls I will never see. The only part that matters is the part that is not available. A wall does not lie. But it does not volunteer, either; it answers exactly the question you put to it, at the place you put it, in a language of about four words, and everything else it keeps. So you put the question again, a hand's width along. And again. And that is a whole life, and it is not a bad one.

---

Things about me that would be true if I were down there and that I'd rather not have said:

I touched the date stamp at Bay Forty-One before I knew why. I copied it off the man in front of me in my first week, two fingers, without breaking stride. By the time I thought to ask, I had stopped wanting to know, and I now understand that this is not cowardice but a kind of load-bearing, and I go on touching it, and I will hold a shaft for a body and stop the fans for a body and I could not tell you the reason.

I carried water to the lemon trees exactly once, and was corrected, and did not argue, because she has a schedule she wrote herself and will not discuss, and she is right that I would have poured it too fast.

I have never asked the six from the cold side what is down past Fourteenth Bay. I've had ten years of tenth days to ask. When they come up with the frost-marks in the same two places on all their unrelated faces and take their loaves and hold the tea without drinking it and ask whether it has turned up there, I answer with more detail about the surface weather than I actually have. Everybody does. It's the only lying anyone does here and it's done kindly.

I still flinch at the changeover sometimes, after nine years, and I've stopped being ashamed of it.

---

And twice a day the fans step, and I stand still wherever I am, in the fog or the wind, with the hammer hanging at my side, and let the note go all the way up or all the way down.

Six seconds. Ten. Nobody looks at anyone. Four hundred people holding their tools where they are, four kilometres of cast trough speaking with one voice, and it is the only time I am not asking a question a hand's width at a time — the only time the whole interior offers something up unprompted, all of it at once, and all I have to do is not move.

At Number Three the heron doesn't stir. It came from the sky and stayed and has learned nothing.

Then the note settles and the works goes back to its business and I go back to mine, which is to walk out when an old deaf animal stands up and faces a wall, and to strike, and strike, and write *sound* in the book, knowing what the word is worth, and go on writing it.
