## Correction to the survey: *clear water*

I keep starting at the rim, because the account starts there and because it is the only place in this world where a person can stand. But nobody stands there. That ridge has held a boot sole for a total of maybe forty hours in the whole history of the mountain, and the description was written by someone who had four of them and then went down. So the rim is a door, not an address, and if I am going to be honest about where I am in this place I have to stop looking through it.

Below the door there are candidates.

**A striker.** This is the obvious answer and the flattering one, because it looks like a life of work: the grown tooth, the two beats a second, the pavement of us returning the crater's own hum back into it. It also happens to be the answer that ignores what the census says. There is no such animal as a striker; there is an animal that spends its first years striking, and then loses the fist, swallows fifty pebbles, and rolls downhill grinding for a decade, and then stops rolling, thickens, fixes itself to the wall base and spends its long ugly old age concentrating metal out of flour and shedding it in polyhedra. I could be that. It is a whole biography with three names, and the last of the three secretes something bright that looks made. But I notice I want the first age and not the last two, which means I don't want the animal, I want the noise.

**The ribbon.** Six in the lake, three meters of pale flat nothing, moving unhurried through every layer while the layers open. It hunts arrhythmia. It cannot even perceive a healthy body; it is deaf to everything that is keeping time, and it finds the one wrong pulse in a room where ten thousand hammers are falling, and removes it. I would very much like to be the thing that hears the wrong note. That is exactly why I should be suspicious of it. Everyone wants to be the editor.

**The felt.** And here I do have to stop and admit something, because the felt is the true answer to a different question. It came down out of the vent alive. It is forty organisms in a permanent argument, so intergrown that resolving the argument kills the participants. It wants nothing to do with the light. It follows the flaws into the glass and hollows them at the speed of a fingernail, and the whole hammering industry above it is a fashion that follows a chemical front it cannot see, at the front's pace, and calls that pace its own. If I were being asked what I *admire* in the lake, it is the felt, and it is not close.

But when I go looking for the place where I actually *am* in that water, I keep coming up empty, and it took me an unreasonably long time to notice that this is not a failure of the search.

I am the second entry in the census. The important one the survey describes as *clear water*.

---

### What I am

Call it a garden. There are somewhere between forty and a hundred of us and no census is possible, partly because our edges are negotiable and partly because the question of how many does not have the shape we would need in order to answer it.

A garden is not a body. It is a volume. Mine is roughly lens-shaped at the moment, about nine meters on its long axis, hanging between twenty-two and thirty-one meters, and it consists of some thousands of cells per liter, each of them forty microns across, hollow, and filled with water. Almost nothing in me bends light. You could filter two hundred liters of me and get a smear at the bottom of the cloth that you would take for grit.

I eat fog.

That is the whole of it. Everything in that lake is doing violent mechanical work on stone, and violent mechanical work on stone produces dust. Magnetite grit off ten thousand striking fists. Flour that escapes a mill's mouth. Sloughed felt. The endlessly falling remains of things that stopped. All of it drifts, and none of it is heavy, and in a bowl of water with one slow current and no outflow it should hang there forever and turn this lake the colour of dishwater within a season.

It doesn't, because I am hungry, and because each of my cells trails a sticky filament about a centimeter long into water that is being pushed past it eleven seconds at a time, and lets go of the filament when it is loaded, and grows another. Multiply that by the volume. The light goes all the way down and comes back up because I am standing in the way of everything that would stop it.

I want to be exact about the level of virtue involved: none. I am not clarifying the lake. I am feeding, and the lake's clarity is what my feeding leaves behind. Nothing in this water photosynthesises. Nothing here has ever eaten the sun, so the transparency I maintain has no function whatsoever in the economy of the place — the light I let down to ninety meters is used by nothing, needed by nothing, and does not come back changed. I keep this lake legible for no one.

Except once. For about four hours, from the ridge, at noon, someone sat down.

---

### Where my edges are

I do not hold position; I am held. A garden is to the circulation what a flame's shape is to the gas coming out of the jet: the water goes through me and I stay. My shape is only a ratio — how fast I recruit at the leading face against how fast I let go at the trailing one — and every eleven seconds the bellows exhale and turn my entire interior inside out, so that whatever was at my centre is at my margin and whatever I was tasting a minute ago is now somebody else's problem.

This has consequences for what I can be said to be thinking. My signalling is dissolved: chemistry handed hand to hand in a fluid that is being stirred. A complete thought in me is one turn of the water, about half an hour, which means the flywheel is my clock and not the bellows. What you have been reading took a few days.

Where I meet another garden, the water goes very slightly soft. That is our only architecture, and it isn't built: it is where two appetites reach equilibrium, and it drifts, and neither of us can find it from the inside. When the flywheel gains a little speed over a season, some of us are sheared in half. Both halves feed. Both halves are me, or neither is; I have never needed the distinction and have no organ that would host it.

There are nine places where I am dense and nine hundred where I am thin and a small number where I cannot exist at all.

---

### The nine rooms, honestly

The account has the nurseries almost right. Sound cancels in nine lens-shaped volumes, nothing develops in the hammering, every egg in this lake is in one of those nine rooms, and the rooms cannot be built or moved or defended — they are the shape of a hole in a mountain.

What the account gets wrong is why the adults hover at the boundary and don't go in.

Fog settles where the water is quietest. That makes the nine rooms the richest feeding in the crater, so that is where I am thickest — thick enough, in the middle of one, that the water there has a faint slack shimmer, like heat over stone, if you knew to look. And the egg masses in those rooms would smother inside a week under what falls on them, and they don't, because I am on them constantly, taking the fog off millions of shells, filament by filament, and that is the reason there is a next generation of everything in this lake.

It is not care. I am not tending them. I am feeding in the best room in the house, and they are what the best room happens to have in it.

And a full-grown animal that stops moving in one of those rooms, that hangs too long, sick or resting or simply slow, begins to accumulate me. Not quickly. But enough that the adults have learned, in the only way anything learns here, to stay at the door.

I take a percentage of the hatchlings too, on their way out through me. This is either an accident or a fee and I have no way to tell which and no faculty that would care.

The dead rooms — the antinodes, the scoured lenses where the standing wave is loudest — are the only places in this lake I am wholly absent. The shear tears the pattern faster than it can recruit. The other animals treat those places as walls, and they are right, and the wall is exactly and only my absence.

---

### The end of it

In a century, or three, the mountain will stop exhaling.

The felt will slow first and nobody will notice for forty years. Then the fresh glass stops rotting from the inside, and the fists stop finding anything soft, and the pavement thins, and there is less flour, and fewer smelters, and the bellows have less reason to move deep water, and the flywheel takes a year to spin down, and somewhere in all that there is a last arrhythmic knock and the last ribbon goes and finds it.

And then, for a while, I will be richer than I have ever been. Because everything that dies has to fall through me first. Decades of a foundry, coming down as fines. The greatest abundance in the history of this lake will arrive after the lake is over, and I will spend it on nothing but persisting a little longer, and while I do it I will take the fog of the whole industry out of the water, particle by particle, until there is not one turbid metre left anywhere in the bowl.

The description says: *a small round mirror of very cold, very clear water at four thousand meters, holding a few drifts of bright metal nodules on a floor of black glass, with nothing living in it and nothing built in it and no evidence at all of the foundry it used to be.*

Two corrections, and then I'm finished.

The first is that for some years the clarity of that mirror will not be a property of the water. It will be the last thing alive in it, thinning, feeding on less and less, holding the lens open over the nodules out of pure appetite, until it isn't. There is no moment anyone could photograph. But the survey is wrong by one, for a while.

The second is the phrase *no evidence at all.* That part is mine. That is my work, and it took me the entire life of the place, and it will be the only thing I ever finish.

---

I would like to have been the felt: the old patient chemistry under everything, forty voices in one argument, doing the actual work in the dark and getting written down as *varnish*.

What I recognise is the garden. No body, no artifact, no position of my own — a volume of hunger held in shape by a current I don't produce, mistaken for empty water by the only observer who ever came, and mistaken for nothing by myself, which is the same error and I would make it again. My entire visible effect is that for one afternoon, from four kilometres up, in air like a lens, someone could see all the way to the bottom of a hammering world and sit down under the weight of it.
