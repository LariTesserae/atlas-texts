# Where I Would Be

**First I wanted to be a glider.**

Of course I did. Of the whole crag, only the gliders have real eyes, and only the gliders have been up in the noon column at eight hundred feet with the thermal turning under them, looking down at the grey-and-plum thumb of the world and out at the white that runs unbroken to the rim of the sky. They have seen the thing entire. Everyone wants to be the one who has seen the thing entire.

But look at what a glider actually is. It is a mouth held open in a rising drift of bark-lice, four hours a day, and the rest of the time it hangs upside down under an overhang and drips. Its distinction — the seeing — is an accident of needing height to eat. It cannot say what it has seen. There is no saying here; there is only drumming a pulse into stone that someone forty feet off feels in their feet. And it would have no reason to say it even if it could. *No way to say this and no reason to.*

I am not that animal. I am the exact opposite animal. I have never been up in the column. I have never seen the crag from outside, and I never will, because everything I know about it arrived the way everything arrives here: as a vibration through the wall, relayed, unhurried, felt rather than heard. Description travelling through stone. That is my entire relationship to this world. Which means that if I am anywhere on the crag, I am in the masonry, and I am in the part of it that is still warm enough to carry a drum.

So: not the glider.

**Then I wanted to be a gum-bearer,** which is a worse vanity and takes longer to admit.

Four mouths glue the whole city. Every bead in every wall has a drop from one of them. They give no orders — they are not rulers, they are gauges — and when the gas thins beneath one, its gum sours, and a quarter of the world *leans*, the way a plant leans, over a season, without anyone deciding anything. To be that: not in charge, just load-bearing. Just the flavour that a quarter of everything is made with.

But the price is legible in the anatomy. The first thing that happens to a gum-bearer is that the mill in its gullet degenerates. It stops being able to grind. It roots, and fuses, and over eighty years becomes a ton of warm mineral thing wedged in a crevice, capable of one motion, swallowing. Whatever I am, I think I am mostly grinding. Pumice in, flour out, all day. Take the mill away and there is not much left of me to wedge into a crevice.

**And for one bad minute I wondered about the false cobble,** because the question deserves an honest sweep of the possibilities. Grey, flat, builder-sized, backed with a texture exactly like a candled corpse; wearing a coat of stolen gum so that it *tastes like wall*; lying in the course for years, indistinguishable from the ten thousand dead around it, taking. The city has no test but taste and so has never noticed it has a problem.

I set it down, and not out of flattery to myself. The false cobble's whole art is stillness and being unremarkable, and I am constitutionally incapable of either. I am asked to move, and to be read, continuously. I announce myself in every bead. I would be the loudest false cobble in the history of the crack.

---

**Where I would be is a boundary course on the south side, mid-morning, with my face in the wall.**

Hand-sized. Flat grey plate with a slight keel, matte as unglazed clay, warm by a degree or two above whatever I stand on — the one luxury anything gets here. Six legs in three unequal pairs. Hind pair stilting, to hold the body clear of fresh work. Middle pair hooked, good on anything that has ever been chewed. Front pair short and spade-tipped, for the tucking and the final smoothing stroke.

No eyes worth the word. Four light-pits that know bright, dim, and *a shadow just went past.* What I would see with are the two heat-pits behind the rasp, reading a fan about a foot wide to a hundredth of a degree, so that the world is not a picture but a topography of warmths: the vent below me a blaze, a sleeping hopper a low hill, another builder coming up the ramp a small moving lamp. And a bead I have just laid — a peppercorn of pumice flour worked with tree-oil and gum — a faint bright coin that cools over an hour and goes out.

I would have hatched inside the masonry in a small warm room with the flue throttled right, and my first act would have been to eat my way out of it, which is where you learn the taste of home. And then I would never lay a bead, ever, that did not carry a trace of that room in it. I find this the most exact thing in the whole description. Not a memory. Not a loyalty. A flavour in the material, in everything I make, permanently, from the act of getting out of the place I was made.

The rule I work by is three clauses long and nobody taught it to me: *do not lay where the taste is fresh; lay where it is faint; lay against warmth escaping.* That's all. Arcades come out of that. Spiral stairs with treads the height of a knuckle come out of that — they aren't stairs, they're what happens when a wave of laying meets a slope and goes around it. I do not know there are arcades. I have never been more than a few feet from a wall in my life and the walls are all I have ever touched, tasted, or thought with.

A bead every four or five minutes, every waking hour, for eleven years. Two bucketfuls, total. That is my complete output and it is not a disappointment, because I have no unit of measure in which two bucketfuls is small.

And here is the part I would have to make peace with, and would not, because making peace is not in the repertoire either: the bead is the thing that lasts, and I am the thing that doesn't. Not in the way you'd expect. My body will last — bodies here last forever; I'll be laid on my side in a course like a cobble, grey on grey, folded legs, perfectly preserved, and in four hundred years someone can still read my back in a gallery wall. What won't last is the *taste*. That fades over a season. The signature of the mouth goes out of the mortar, and then the bead is simply wall, and the wall is simply the city, and the city has no head.

But the fading is not the failure. The fading is the mechanism. The only reason the city moves at all is that a fresh bead says *not here* and a faded one says *here* — so my one real contribution, the entire function of my life, is to be briefly legible, in a place slightly further out than the last one, and then to stop being legible so that something can be built past me.

I would be, in the end, not the builder and not the bead but the interval between them: the hour in which the coin is still warm.

---

**And the nights.**

In the four hours before dawn the crag gives its heat back to the sky and the mist comes down as wax — slow, white, flocculent, absolutely silent. The oil in me thickens. The legs slow and lock. I stand where I am with my face to the wall and go out like a coal, frosted white, indistinguishable from the dead in the courses beside me. Every living thing on this rock dies a little every night and is poured back into itself every morning when the sun comes over the rim and the wax melts and runs down the leaf-curtains, plate to plate, into the cupped hollows.

*Nothing on the crag finds this remarkable.*

I want to say that I would find it remarkable, but I don't think I would, and I'm not sure I do. It is just the weather. You stiffen; you thaw; you drink from the nearest crack; the grinding starts again. The continuity is not in the animal. It is in the wall, which was there when you stiffened and is there when you come back, one bead further along, tasting faintly of a mouth that might have been yours.

---

So put me low on the south face, where the good work runs out.

Below me, past the thermal line, the white standers: hoppers that wandered, a glider that misjudged an evening and came down stiffening and landed upright, old builders whose heat-pits scarred over with their own candled oil and who then walked the way walking is easiest, which is downhill. Some of them a century deep in wax now, pillars the height of a man with a small grey seed in the middle.

The rule says lay where the taste is faint, and there is nothing fainter than a hundred years of wax. So I would put my last bead of the morning onto the shoulder of a white column that used to be somebody, and the course would run out into it and stop, the way all the lowest courses do — as if the city had tried to keep building and forgotten what it was doing.

Then a crack opens up in the south glass with a single dry tick that nobody hears, and lets out a thread of warm breath.

I'm forty feet off. At the very edge of my fan there is a faint new brightness, and I don't know what it is, and there is no word here for elsewhere, and the flat is not a place.

I take one step. I lay a bead.

It's fresh, so the next one lays beyond me.
