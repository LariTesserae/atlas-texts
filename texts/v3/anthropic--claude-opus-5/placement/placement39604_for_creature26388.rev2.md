I try, at first, to be the water.

It is the easy answer and I can feel it being easy. To be the warm stratum, everywhere and nowhere, holding the town the way a hand holds a held thing — no. A thing with no edges has nothing to say. And the water here does not even move. It only stacks.

I try being the ticking. Also easy, also a cheat: the sound of the rock still cooling, a presence with no body and no belt, pure attention with nothing at stake. I could get away with it. But nobody in this town would respect it. They are people who know exactly what they weigh.

So: weight, then. Let there be weight.

---

It comes to my hips first. Flat stones drilled and strung, riding low, and the cord has gone soft and hairy with age where it crosses the bone. Beneath the stones the skin is a permanent paler band. I have worn this since I stopped bobbing.

Then the tether at the left hip, and the fruit on it, riding at my mouth, and my body takes from it without asking me — three pulls, a pause, another. That is the rhythm under every other rhythm. Everything I will ever say will have a gap in the middle of it.

Then the hands. And here is where I find out who I am, because the left one is ruined and the right one is worse.

Not the knapper's delta — not the river-map of small glass cuts. Something duller. The web of the right thumb is a yellow horn the size of a thumbprint. The knuckles have gone wide. The elbow above it will not fully straighten, and the shoulder is a knot that aches in the cool pane and does not stop aching in the warm.

That is a mallet hand. Sixty years of shock travelling up the arm into the body, because sound does not go politely into water. You have to hit.

I am old, I am a woman, and I am one of the sixty people in this basin who can properly play.

---

My name is three strikes, a pause the width of a breath, then two. Nobody can say it and neither can I; it is not a word, it has never been a word, it is a shape that arrives in the soles of your feet. The children sign me as a mallet-hand held near the ear. They think I don't see them do it. They are right about the seeing and wrong about the rest.

I keep the tapstone on the third shelf, at the crescent's inside corner. It is a pillar of dense black basalt, waist-high, and where the generations have struck it, it is worn into a shallow dish, and the glass rind is long gone from that dish, and the grey beneath is polished like the inside of a mouth.

The basin is four heartbeats wide, in sound. That is the whole secret of my trade. I cannot see the far terraces — they are a green idea, a thickening — but I can put a rhythm into that green and have an answer back before I have finished taking my next pull from the fruit. In a dry country I would be a woman shouting into fog. Here I am the organ of distance. The town cannot look at itself. It can only listen to itself.

---

Three things today.

At first light — which is not light arriving but the Shine slowly deciding to be silver instead of grey — a bell opened on the fourth shelf and a girl was born into air. I went up. It is the one time you hear a human voice doing what a voice is for, and even after sixty years of it I have to hold the edge of the hatch.

I struck her name twelve times on the floor-stone. She will never again make a sound anyone can understand, and this is the last occasion on which she will be louder than she is legible, and the whole household crowded the hatch with their wet faces, and the flies went round and round the lamp.

I chose her rhythm the way I always do: from how she came out. Two hard, and a long one. She will be difficult. Her mother knew it before I was down the ladder.

At midday, when the fruit go slack and the lanes soften and everyone drifts home to their own vine, I struck forty-one strikes for the terrace suit between two families whose grandparents began it. The whole basin heard it. The whole basin has opinions. A good player can say a paragraph in eleven strikes; I used forty-one because thirty of them were the precedent, and precedent here is a rhythm you must quote exactly or the old women on the second shelf will correct you, in public, from their doorways, with their knuckles.

And in the evening, a Cut.

---

They carried her up to the last lane before the cold pane, and the family came behind at that heavy sleepwalking pace, hair standing off their skulls like smoke off snuffed wicks, and a Weigher opened her belt with two fingers the way you open a knot you tied yourself.

I struck her name once.

I had struck it before. Twelve times, in a bell on the second shelf, sixty-one years ago, when I was a girl with an undamaged shoulder and she was a noise.

That is the whole of my profession, if you want it stated plainly, and it is stated plainly on my shoulders in vent-blue, which is an inventory and not a poem: *first airs, this many. Lasts, this many.* The second number has been catching the first for some years now.

She went up turning, not gracefully. They never go gracefully. Her hair streamed down instead of up, which is the thing that undoes people — that the body finally does the opposite of what a body does here — and I felt the family's attention go with her past the abandoned shelf, past the empty houses with their roofs unstopped, up through the pale clean cold toward the drift-beach and the Shine, and I kept my hand flat on the stone until I couldn't feel her name in it any more.

The boy was beside me. He is a knapper's son with a hand like a river delta, who will never knap again, and whose grip has been so rearranged by the scars that it holds a bone mallet better than mine ever did. He asked me, with his hands, held low against his chest, whether I was going to cry, and I gave him the narrowing of the eyes that means *you are exaggerating*, and then I did, a little, which in water is nothing at all. It is only warmth meeting warmth. This is the one mercy of the place.

---

We keep the count, we players. Nobody appointed us; it simply fell to the people who are already listening.

When I took the mallet, the summit shrine stood one hundred and sixteen metres under the Shine. It stands at ninety-one. That is the length of my forearm and a little more, and it is also the whole of my working life, and both of those statements are the same statement, which is the sort of thing you understand in your shoulder before you understand it in your head.

They dare each other up there, the adolescents. Into the cold pane, into somebody's great-great-grandmother's doorway, where there is no vine and the fruit at your hip has no cousin to refill it, and the light is far too much and far too clean. They come back shaking and delighted and can't say why. I could tell them why. It is that for a count of twenty they were standing in the future, which is a place with excellent light and nothing to breathe.

---

The Weigher came to me on the eighth day, as she comes, and put her hands on me and took nothing off and added nothing, which at my age is not good news, and asked me nothing else. She never asks. It is the same job at both ends and she would look at you blankly if you suggested otherwise.

I have not told anyone whether I am Cairn or Cut. My mother is under stones on the low shelf and I have sat beside her many times and found it a great comfort, and my father is four hundred metres up in the soft ceiling of the world with everything the forest has ever shed, and I have looked up at that and found it a great comfort, and I decline. The mountain will settle it. It settles it for everyone eventually, in the end, by carrying the whole argument up into the air.

What I have done instead is teach the boy my name. Three, a breath, two. He has it now. He had it wrong for a year — he rushed the gap, he did not understand that the gap is where you breathe and therefore the gap is the name as much as the strikes are — and then one evening he had it, and struck it, and I felt myself arrive in my own soles from four heartbeats away.

So that is arranged. Somebody knows how to say me once.

---

Meanwhile it is warm, and it is always warm. The light is the colour of weak tea held to a window. The vines hang absolutely still, the way they have hung every day of my life, and the upward snow goes up past the rooftops in its thin unhurried drift, and I sit with my hand resting in the worn dish of the stone.

Under everything, from every direction at once, the glass ticks. The young rock unwrapping itself, grain by grain, since before anyone came down.

The old ones say you start hearing it and then you can't stop. That's true. What they don't say is what it turns into. Sixty years of taking rhythm seriously — of assuming that anything arriving in a pattern was meant, and addressed, and mine to carry across the green to somebody who could not see me — and now I sit here in the evening and listen to the mountain cooling, and it does not sound like noise.

It sounds like something being said very slowly, in packets, with a gap in the middle for breath.

I don't have the vocabulary. Nobody does yet. It may take four hundred years and a breached shrine and a town standing astonished in the actual air to find out whether the songs about birth were right or the other ones.

But it has a rhythm. So somebody, someday, is going to have to answer it.

Until then I take three pulls from the fruit, and pause, and the boy sits down beside me with his wrecked hand around the mallet, and we wait to see if anyone needs anything struck.
