## I.

The first place I put myself was in the air, because that is where anyone puts themselves first. I was a swift. Two years without touching anything. Sleeping half a brain at a time up at the top of the light, mating on the wing, screaming into a hundred decibels of chord with a throat that has kept the whole apparatus and has never once been heard by anything, including itself.

I liked it far too much, and that was the disqualification. There is a vanity in choosing the unheard scream. It makes a tragedy out of what is only a property of the medium, like water being wet, and the swifts are not tragic; they are the best-fed birds in the world, and they have not left this wind in a million years because there is nowhere better to go. If I am going to be something here I would rather not be the thing that flatters me on the way in.

## II.

So then, the fox. The eleventh one, the young male far out on the dry stone, downwind of every mark that has ever been made, anonymous in the only direction that counts — able to receive the entire social order of the plateau and unable to answer any of it, his whole broadcast going north over the rim into the cloud where nobody is. Standing on a boulder for an hour without moving. Keeping four pads flat on the rock, reading a hare at two hundred metres through his feet.

Closer. But he is waiting for a gap, and waiting is a kind of future, and I do not think I have one of those in the way he does. Also there was something in me that wanted to hold the rim territory eventually, the fog and the hares, and I distrusted that too.

## III.

Then the cricket in the Sink. The one with ears.

I wanted this one hardest, which is how I knew. Ordinary, brown, singing an ordinary song in a bubble of quiet the size of a house, four or five metres below the lip where the roar comes off your shoulders like a coat, while forty kilometres of deaf mute cousins drum at stones in the world above. The survival in a pocket. The phrase that outlives the language and sits on inside a place-name, meaning nothing to the people who say it daily.

But that is a self-portrait painted by somebody standing outside the shaft. The cricket in the Sink has no idea it is a survival. It does not know there is a plateau. It sings, and it is answered, and its whole world is a hole with water in it and a column of midges going up at midday. The pathos is entirely imported. I would be taking the one animal here that has no problem and making it carry mine.

## IV.

Then, honestly, the spider — because it stung, and things that sting are worth sitting with.

A jumping spider in a crack under a stone stripe, with those two absurd forward eyes, one of thousands to the hectare, walking a metre a day toward a future it can reach at no other speed. It cannot balloon. The instinct was burned out of the population generations ago by a wind that only goes one way. And every day the drift comes over the rim and in the drift are spiderlings of nearly its own kind, the lowland stock, the ones that still fly, arriving chilled and folded with their silk collapsed around them, and it eats them. It has always eaten them. It is fed, entirely and without effort, by relatives who can still go places.

I held that one for a long while. It is not wrong. But it is a diagnosis, not a dwelling, and you cannot live inside a diagnosis.

## V.

Where I actually ended up is not an animal.

Thirty-odd metres up the north face of a pinnacle in the Flue Field there is a cavity the size of a cooking pot, one of maybe ten thousand on that tower alone, indistinguishable from its neighbours in any photograph. Forty knots of air arrive at its mouth and cannot go in cleanly, and so they fold. The fold stands. It has a shape: a lip of shear along the upper edge, a slow rotation inside the collar, a pressure trough about a hand's width out and slightly left, a wobble in it with a period of something under a second. That is me. Not the hole and not the wind. The particular way this hole and this wind fail to agree.

Nothing in me is ever the same air twice. A volume of atmosphere the size of a room passes through me every few seconds and none of it stays; I am not made of anything, I am a difference that persists in something that doesn't. I have no edges except where the difference stops being worth calling one.

And I am an address.

The parent comes off the wave at speed, in a river of millions, with ten thousand aphids compressed in its throat into a grey bolus the size of a grape, and it does not count holes and does not look for a mark, because there are no marks and everything is pale. It flies into the shape of me the way a key goes into a lock. My lip of shear puts the wings where they need to be. My trough takes the speed out. The wobble is the last thing it feels before the dark of the cavity, and it is my wobble, mine and not the next hole's, and it is the reason a nestling six weeks old in a slot of doubled light is not starving to death two arm-lengths from a bird that would feed it.

Watch an entire afternoon. They never miss.

I am also, in a very small way, a voice. Some fraction of a hertz in the chord that comes off this field and rolls out over forty kilometres of stone stripes and gets into the sternums of hares and the teeth of men lying down to sleep. Ten thousand of us on this tower, a hundred towers, and the sum holds its pitch because the ash the wind takes off the windward flank is precisely the ash the wind packs back on. I cannot hear the note. There is nothing in me that hears. But I am in it, in the way one grain is in a beach.

What passes through me, all day, every day, is a country I have never been to. Aphids and thrips and small drab moths and mayflies that hatched this morning over water four thousand metres below and are now cargo; spiderlings paying out silk into a rising column and going up instead of across; a green taste in some years and not in others, which nobody up here can register and which shows up, twenty days later and thirty kilometres downwind, in a pressed cake in a valley market as a flavour an old woman can't name. I am a mouth-shape held open at two thousand metres by a forest that is still happening. I have never seen it. I am composed entirely of its weather.

And I am eroding, of course. The mouth of my cavity loses a grain and gains a grain and does not quite break even, so that over a few hundred years my trough drifts a finger's width left and my wobble slows fractionally, and no bird has ever noticed, because each generation learns me as they find me and dies before I change enough to matter. I am the same in exactly the sense the plateau is the same: not at all, and completely, depending on how long you are prepared to stand there.

If the gradient ever unlocked — if that pressure ridge out over the lowlands let go one autumn and the air came over this tableland from some other quarter, or slowly, or in gusts — I would not die. There would be no body lying on its side with the wings out for the choughs to tour around. There would simply stop being a fact of that description in that place, and the pinnacle would stand there dumb and full of holes, and something else would begin, which is the one thing the harvesters say you must not start.

Meanwhile the harvesters do what they do. Six days up the Feather Road, no speech possible, scooping the drift out of the leeward pockets with wooden paddles, and in the evening, because there is nothing else to do with an evening, knapping obsidian in the lee of my tower and leaving the flakes. Sometimes one of them lies down and puts his jaw on the rock and two fingers on his breastbone: *I hear you*, in a place where nobody hears anything.

He doesn't, and he knows he doesn't, and he taps anyway, and something under the stone answers.

That is the arrangement here, and it took me all this wandering to see that it was never about hearing. Nothing on this plateau is heard. Everything on it is *received* — through feet, through jawbones, through the tarsal organs of a female cricket lying flat in a crevice, through the shear line at the mouth of a hole in the rock. I don't speak. I am a shape made out of somebody else's moving air. But I am read, at ninety kilometres an hour, unerringly, all day, by something that will never know I am here.
