## What I Am, Four Rooms Past the Bellow-Ferns

I am in the far garden, in the low rooms where the courses are oldest, where the talk has had the longest to settle into things. Nobody comes here much. The light is the same as everywhere — honeyed, sufficient, from nowhere — but the shadows here have had more time to disagree, and a visitor finds it tiring in a way they can't locate and blames on the pollen.

I take weight off.

---

**How it is done**

You do not need to know what was said. That is the first thing they teach, and it is a mercy, because the record is not written anywhere; the only archive is knots, and a knot is not a word.

The sentences are in the object the way grain is in wood. You sit with the thing, both palms on it, and you run your thumb against the lie of it until you find an edge — the last thing that was ever said near it — and then you go backwards through that sentence, sound by sound, and the sound comes off like a scale of dried sap, and under it is the sentence before, and under that the one before. You work down. You do not rush. A rushed unnaming takes the sentence but leaves the *having been said*, and then the object is loose but still casts a shadow that arrives late, and there is nothing to be done about that, and the rota-keeper ties a small ashamed knot.

The sensation, when it goes, is not dramatic. The thing under your hands becomes a fraction less *there*. It becomes liftable. Its shadow catches up with it. You sit back and you are breathing harder than the work accounts for, because the weight did not go nowhere. It went into you. You carry it as far as the door and then it soaks in, and your hands are a little heavier for a cycle, and then they are lighter than they were before you started, by rather more than they were heavy.

That is the whole economy. You cannot take weight off a thing without taking some off yourself. Every unnamer knows it going in. It is why nobody is assigned.

---

**Who gave me this**

I was a waker first. Most of us were.

I walked the fourth corridor with a soft cord and trailed it across sleepers, and one high-cycle I found a climber in the plaster with three legs still round and four gone flat, and its far side already the wall. It was warm. It had been sleeping there against the same brick for — the cords say eleven seasons, and the cords do not lie, and my eye had said *four* every time I passed it, because sight-lines here are gentle, consistent liars.

I woke it. It came off the wall wrong, one whole flank still flat, and it lived another two seasons going in circles, and every one of us spoke about it, because how could you not, and by the end it was too heavy to turn over.

That is when I understood what talking does, and I went to the far rooms and put my palms on the first pinned thing I could find, and somebody had to teach me what I was already doing.

My sign was given to me when I was three: the chevron tilted steep. It was meant to mean *stubborn*. It has been read as *slow* for my whole life. Nobody makes their own sign. You carry the one you were given badly, like a nickname from a bad year, and if you are the sort of person who minds, the minding gets spoken about, and then you are heavy, and then you are the sort of person who minds *and* cannot be moved from the alcove.

The counters know whether my palm matches a print on the north wall. I have never asked them. It would be a question with a mouth in it.

---

**Upkeep**

I am pinned.

Soft copper, hammered thin, hoarded, used nowhere in Hesp except at the joints where the structure's logic is load-bearing. There is one through the shoulder of my over-wrap and one at the hem. A person becomes a load-bearing joint eventually, if they do this work; the copper has been spoken about for generations, it is the most-discussed matter in the basin, it is so pinned it could anchor a hill, and it keeps my feet on the brick at the high of the cycle when I go soft and start to lean.

I am on the rota. Twice a cycle somebody I don't always recognise comes down with my share of gold and stands with me out past the ferns, palms down, chin level, the way everyone does, and afterwards we press palms for four heartbeats and I read them and they read me and neither of us mentions how little there is to read.

They have begun to lose me.

It is not that I go away. I am standing right there. It is that the fact of me stops holding in the other person's head, mid-sentence, the way a knot slips. You watch it happen from inside: their eyes go past you, then come back, then go past again, and they finish the sentence with great warmth, addressed to a spot in the air about a hand to my left. The kind ones keep talking to the spot. The very kind ones talk to the spot for a long time, in case.

I mind less than I expected. It is the work. You cannot spend your life persuading objects to stop being *about* themselves and then be shocked when it takes.

---

**The brick**

For nine seasons I have been working on the foundation brick of the north course.

It is the first brick. It was laid and then spoken over — spoken over at length, by everyone then alive, which was the entire practice at the time, because nobody had yet learned what speech costs. It cannot be lifted. It cannot be marked. It is warmer than the floor by a noticeable degree, and if you put your ear to it you hear nothing at all, which in a place this quiet is a sound.

Unnaming runs backwards. You take the last thing said first. Which means the last thing you release, at the bottom, under nine seasons of other people's talk, is the first thing ever said at Hesp.

I reached it at the low of the cycle, when everyone else was stiffening upright against the warm floor and the garden was setting around them and there was no one to hear.

One syllable. I had it in my mouth before I knew what it was, the way you know a shape by the weight of it. To recite a thing backwards you must first hold it entire. There is no other method. So I held it.

It was cool.

I have never felt a cool thing. Nothing here is cool. The floor is warmer than the air and the air is warmer than nothing and that is the whole range of it, and this sat under my tongue like a stone from somewhere else entirely, and I understood what it was, and I understood why we make that flat-handed sign and turn the palm down and teach it to our children so gently.

If I said it, it would be more real, and correspondingly less able to move. That is the rule, and the rule has never once failed.

And there is no rot here. Nothing rots. Our dead dry and flake and are milled and blended with husk and clay and poured into the hand-cut sockets, and the garden comes up out of them four and five storeys deep, and the empty sockets out past the ferns are swept and waiting, and that is not a horror to us, it is simply where things go.

Rot would mean the dead were not soil.

---

**What I did with it**

I could not say it and I could not swallow it and I could not put it back.

So I carried it, mouth shut, hand over mouth the way a child is taught, through the stiffening garden to the corner of the low basin where the seventh frame stands with nothing behind it but wall. The threshold was swept. It is always swept. It is swept toward the frame, never away, and not as worship — as courtesy, to a neighbour whose house shares a wall.

I knelt, and I turned my palm over, flat, down, and I put the word out through the frame.

Not spoken. Left. The way you leave a share of powder in the seam for the small blunt things with hands, and do not watch them take it, and do not speak of them, because speaking of them would make them heavy and they have troubles enough.

The wall bowed a hand's width and went warm — warmer than everything, which is already warm — and stayed that way nine days and then subsided.

The cord-keeper tied it with her eyes shut, running the line through her fingers: *depth, duration, season.* Knots for all of it. No knot for me, because there is no knot for me, and she did not ask, and that is the finest thing anyone has ever done for me.

---

**The rest**

I am very light now. Light enough that the copper does most of the standing, and when I lie down at the low of the cycle the brick holds me less than it did last season, and less than it will hold me next.

There is one detail I did not anticipate, and it is the reason I keep to the far rooms, where nobody comes much.

I did it once. It happens seven times.

Six more givings are still in transit, somewhere behind me in the gold, and they will arrive at their own pace over the seasons, and each of them will be that word going out through the frame again. Six more bowings. Six more warm months. I have left instructions in knots — sweep the threshold, tie the depth, do not go and look.

I swept it myself this morning, toward the frame, the way you do.

And a young waker came down the fourth corridor with a soft cord and pressed palms with me and held it four heartbeats and read nothing, and her eyes went past me, and she said something with great warmth to the air at my left shoulder, and I gave her the chevron tilted steep, and she nodded, politely, at nobody.
