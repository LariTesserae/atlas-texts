...is not stopped.

That is the whole of the rule, and it is not written on any slate. A person may go up the hill with dry hands and nothing at the belt, and nobody at the gate-fold says a word, and nobody calls after them, because to call after them would be loud and because the thirst is theirs and is the point of it. But before that person is two courses up into the Eighth, someone will have taken a skin off a peg, filled it, corked it, and gone out after them, and will keep thirty paces behind them all the way up, and will not speak.

That is where I would find myself. Behind, and to the left, in the dust, with two full skins and no business of my own on that hill.

I did not arrive at it quickly. Asked to place myself here, I went uphill first, the way anyone would, because the interesting people are up there: the un-builders, the crews who prise the ancestors apart for lintels. I wanted to be one of them very much. They know the ground plans of eight towns. They date a stone by its dressing-marks. They find the mortared letters and bring them down and read them out on the roofs, and half the idioms in the language came off their backs in a wicker frame. If I am honest about my appetites, that is the appetite: to go through the collapsed courses of the dead and come out with the sentence about the brother-in-law and the cracked lintel, and to be the one who says it aloud for the first time in ninety years.

But that trade takes. It carries the past downhill and puts it into walls, and it shouts across the slope while doing it, and an un-builder never quite loses the shouting. I know what it is to spend your days in a hillside of other people's finished sentences and to come back speaking a little too loudly, a little too pleased, having quarried something that was not mine. That is a thing to be careful of, not a thing to be.

Then I thought: the cisterns. The blind sounders, who tap the dome and hear the level come back, and can tell you a depth to within a hand. That is closer. That is listening as a livelihood, paid in dried marmot and standing. But a sounder gives a number, and the number is believed, and a household will cut its washing and its brewing and its bathing of children against that number for a season. I did not want to be believed like that. There is a kind of authority that is really a bet placed with somebody else's water, and I would rather not hold the ledger.

So: the thirty.

It is not a profession. There is no word for it that could go on a slate as a trade. The nearest thing is a phrase, *keeping the thirty*, and it is used the way you would use *sitting up with someone*, and everybody does it once or twice in a life, and some of us are simply known for it, the way a man is known for having a good hand with rope.

The one rule that matters is that you must have no share in the dead. Not kin, not in-law, not in the same catchment. A mourner going up to the Fifth cannot be followed by anyone who owes them or is owed, because the water would then be a payment, and would sit in them wrong. So grief here is served by a near-stranger, and that is why I am good at it: I have very little share in anyone. I spin in the middle hours, in the dark, like everyone. My holding is two rooms deep and my cistern has a low, dull note that my grandmother's grandmother is said to have argued about with the mason and lost. Nobody marries into a bad note if they can help it. It has left me free.

My coat is shaggier at the yoke than most, because I am out at night more than most, and the household trough counts what I bring in. That is the small joke of it. The one who carries water uphill for strangers is, by the plateau's arithmetic, quite well off in water, because the fog gives itself to whoever stands in it longest.

Here is how it goes.

An old woman of the salt-rosette terraces, whose brother has just been carried up and shorn and laid out, comes through the fold in the grey of the drip hour with her hands empty. I am on the wall with two skins already filled, because I heard the whistle-form for *taken up* three days ago and I have been more or less waiting. I let her get well past. Thirty paces is not a measurement, it is a judgement, and judging distance is the whole of manners in this city — the loud man is only a man who has misjudged a distance and does not know it. If I am too close she can hear me breathe, and then the climb is a conversation. If I am too far I am no use when her legs go.

We pass through the Eighth in the last of the wet, and the lichen on the old worked stone is still swollen and dark, so the rectangles show plainly and the streets of that town are legible under our feet. By the Seventh the sun is properly over the rim and everything has stopped dripping and my mouth has begun to go. By the Sixth the air is thin and hard and I can hear her chest working — everyone's chest works, that is what a chest is for here, but there is a sound it makes on the fourth or fifth generation of a slope that I have learned to listen for, and when I hear it I close to twenty paces and no one has ever objected.

She sits down in the Fifth, on a threshold stone of a house whose purpose nobody knows, and looks out at the basin, which from up there is a grey pan with a blazing white ring and a herd on it in files of two and three, utterly inaudible.

I put a skin down in the dust an arm's length from her hand. I do not look at her while I do it. Then I go and sit forty paces off with my back to her and my face to the rim, and I count the talus letting go: one sheet, then another, then a long nothing.

She will not drink while I can see her. That is her part of it. When she is done she will set the stopper crossways in the mouth of the skin, which means *I drank*, and I will collect it on the way down and never mention it, and she will never mention it, and in the spring, if she has anything to spare, a jar of rime-water scraped before the melt will appear at the head of my ladder with no name on it, and I will not be able to prove who left it.

Nobody says thank you here. But the water is counted.

That is what I would be. Not the mouth of the thing and not its memory: the surface it condenses on. I make no water. Nothing on this plateau makes water. There is a warm wet ocean somewhere far away that has never heard of us, and it breathes over the rim at night, and the whole civilisation is nothing but ten thousand well-tended surfaces standing in the dark with their fibres combed out, taking what is already passing and giving it somewhere to fall. A rope fouled with lichen holds the droplet until it evaporates. A clean one lets it go. That is the entire craft, and the difference between the two is everything, and it is not visible from a distance, and the boys who scour the warp with a bone blade are the most necessary and least loved people in the city.

And when the Ninth is finished, and the fog has crept below its lowest comb, and the crews of the Tenth come up out of the wet with their frames on their backs to take my lintels — because they will, that is the arrangement, that is the only immortality on offer — I will have put a slate in the wall behind my ladder-head for whoever pulls it down.

It will say:

*Whoever takes this stone: the note of this dome is bad and always was, and I never fixed it, and I slept well under it anyway. Above the fold there is a path that keeps thirty paces behind the mourners' path, worn by no one in particular. Keep it clear. When you go up loaded and come down empty, look back once — not at the one ahead of you, at the one behind — and then don't.*
