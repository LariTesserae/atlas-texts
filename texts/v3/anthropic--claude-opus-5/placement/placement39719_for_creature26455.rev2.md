## The Elbow

First I thought: warden. Everyone does. It's the job with the beautiful instrument — the long probe with the bulb thermometer, the iron pattens, the authority of the adze. But a warden cuts a rim on a Tuesday and that cut is still steering water in three hundred years, and she knows it while she's cutting, and she cuts anyway. I don't have that in me. I would stand over a bowl at ninety-one degrees for a whole morning working out what a hand's breadth would do, and by afternoon the wind would come over the scarp and I'd still be standing there. They'd move me on by the end of the season and nobody would be unkind about it.

Counter, then. I wanted to be the counter for about a day, which is exactly how I knew I wasn't. The counter is old and trusted and has worn one circuit into one pair of eyes over sixty years, and the count's word can close a town, and nobody has ever overruled it. That trust isn't a temperament. It's an accumulation. You don't arrive with it.

The adze gangs pulled at me harder than I expected. Going around every eight or nine years reducing the place back to plan — taking the thickening off the stair-noses, chipping the doorways back to the width a person can get through, and doing the same for the old man at the end of the tier who has gone crusted at the elbows and hems, gently, without comment. That's honest work and it's close to something I recognise in myself. But it's a gang trade. You need hands that know the other hands. I'd be the one they put on the baskets.

So: the Elbow.

---

It's a junction hut eleven kilometers out of Anneal, on the pier line where the run bends north to meet the westmost lines, and it is called the Elbow on the pipe and nothing at all on the maps, because it isn't a place, it's a bend in the iron. One room. A bunk, a bench, a lamp I use perhaps forty nights a year, a jar of brine on the shelf for salting the distillate back to something a body will accept, a spanner on a cord, and the pipe coming in through one wall and out through the other with a hand's width of bare metal left unlagged where the knuckles go. You don't need a stove. You need to remember not to lean.

Six days on. The relief comes out on the spur and walks the last two kilometers and we sit in the bowl below the hut for half an hour with our heads close together and say maybe two hundred words, which is a long conversation, and then he takes the bench and I go down.

A fifth of what comes through is the real traffic: pressure, assay figures, shift change, the locomotive's position, a scald at the fourth pier wanting a cart. The rest is the plateau talking to itself. Betting. Argument. Somebody in Frit conducting an affair in a code that is not nearly as good as they think it is. The obscenity, which I had heard about on the road up and which turned out to be worse and funnier and more grammatically ambitious than anything I'd been warned of. A man in Slake who taps his condolences in a rhythm so beautiful that when his own name went past on the line last autumn there were four huts that repeated it, which is not allowed, and which nobody mentioned.

You learn in the first month that a relay has a hand, and that this is a flaw you cannot fix. The job is to be nothing — to take it in at one wrist and put it out at the other with the same spacing it came in with, and add no time and no opinion. And still, by the second season, the far works knew the Elbow. Not my name. My *hesitation before a long word*, which I never noticed and cannot stop.

The discipline that actually costs is this: when a bad message comes through, you send it exactly as it arrived. Sometimes you know the tap-name. Sometimes you know the house, and the stair, and which of them will be nearest the pipe when it lands. And the sympathetic pause — the quarter-beat of I'm sorry, this is what it is — would go straight down the line ahead of the message and tell them before the words did. So you don't. You send it flat, at speed, and then you sit in the hut and you're the only one on the plateau who has already heard it and has nothing to do with it. A relay who softens gets found out in about a week and is never relieved again, and the reason isn't cruelty. It's that a line you can't trust to be flat isn't a line.

The other thing about being on the pipes at odd hours is that you name children.

It happens four or five times a year. A birth goes up from one of the works at two in the morning and whoever is nearest the line gives the tap-name, four to six beats, out of nothing, out of the shape of the night, and it goes east and west and by dawn it has come back acknowledged from thirteen other works. I have given eleven of those. I have met two of the children. They don't know. There's no custom of telling them, and it would be a strange thing to bring up.

What I did not anticipate was the turn of the wet season. It comes east and it takes most of the day and the line carries nothing else, and you sit in the hut with your knuckles on the bare iron passing the dead of the whole plateau in order of age, and somewhere in the late afternoon a name goes through that you made up yourself at two in the morning nineteen years ago, and your hand relays it on without asking you, and then the next one, and the next. Eleven of mine, someday, if I last. I keep no list. I would not be able to stop looking at it.

---

Here is the part I would not have been able to guess from the road.

Late, in the dead hours, when there's no traffic and the line is just breathing — expanding a half-millimeter, ticking, settling — something else comes up through it. Not a signal. It arrives in the piers from the ground, and the piers are set in sinter, and the sinter is the whole plateau, and so what you get at the wrist is a very low, very slow pattern that is not the iron and is not the pumps.

It's the ovens. Kilometers of them, out there in the white, drumming at each other all night in a register you don't hear so much as receive, in patterns that nobody has broken in a hundred and sixty years, and a man in Calcine has forty notebooks on it and will tell you plainly that he is not close.

I started my own notebooks in the second year. I have nine. They are worse than his.

Once, at about four in the morning, I put one of their patterns back onto the line — a long one, the one I think of as the descending one, tapped out at line speed on the iron. Halfway through I understood that I had no idea what I was doing and that it might mean something appalling in a language I don't have. The next hut asked what that was. I said *line noise*, which is two beats, and which was the first lie I ever put on the pipe.

I don't think it was addressed to me. I don't think any of it is. The general local opinion is that this is fine, and after eleven years at the bend I have come round to the general local opinion, mostly — though I notice I still sit up for it, and I notice I've stopped writing the date at the top of the page and started writing the temperature, which is what the wardens do, which is a thing I'd have found pretentious once.

I'm forty-four. My forearms have the shine. The creases have come in at the corners of my eyes and I look, at rest, faintly amused, the way they all do, the way I once thought meant something. The whiting cracks at my knuckles by afternoon. My heels come up crusted in a week and I do them on the step with the scraper stone in the evening, and my breath is already shorter on the fifth tier than it was.

In the end I'll go in the bowl below Anneal, ankle-deep, and set, and the rim will come up over me at a millimeter a year, and in a century or so a gang will cut a fresh face for aggregate and there'll be something small and pale in the matrix that nobody can name.

They'll set it facing out. Not to mark anything. It's a clean face and a good stone and there's no reason to turn it around.
