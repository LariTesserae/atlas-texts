## Nothing Comes Up the Cable

That is where I have to start, because the invitation says *come up*, and the cars come back empty, and they have come back empty for as long as the registers go. So whatever I am here, I did not arrive. There is no version of this in which I step off at Draw End with my hat in my hands and ask the way to a bed.

I tried the other doors.

The moth first. A thing from below, four or five days of it, then the outline staying and the fibre coming, and ten years later the plateau growing another one exactly like it somewhere else, possibly able to fly. I wanted that one. It is very nearly right. But a moth does not speak, and I appear to be very little except speech.

The Hearer next, which is the flattering choice, and I sat in it for a while — high in a condemned cane, one to a level, listening for my own name in the voice of somebody I know. It is wrong by one turn. The Hearer is the one who *hears*. If I am anywhere in that transaction I am at the other end of it: I am the name coming up out of the floor, on an interval that is short and mean.

So then the fossil noise itself. Twelve kilometres from anyone, the full clangour of a foundry — pour-bell, crane, men shouting in an accent no one alive uses — forty paces wide, faithful, thin, perfectly clear, and not informed. It does not know the men are gone. That one is close enough to bruise.

But every time I settle into it I find I have hands.

So it has to be the other thing, the Tenth Article, the one the clerks pronounce *brought forward*.

---

They found me on the open heath about a mile off the Ninestacks chain, at first light, in the fog that comes after the Charge. Warm. Wet. Bast cloth of the correct weave with nine years of wear in it and the elbows gone soft the way elbows go. Ladder-callus across both palms, right hand heavier than left. The squint already set — standing there in fog, in a whiteness with nothing in it to squint at, squinting.

I knew the Charge. That is the part I still turn over. I did not sleep through it and I did not startle at it; I came into the world of my own knowing with the valves already knocking up the spine and the dust already jumping, and no part of me thought anything of it.

I knew the district. I knew who was owed money at the third node and I knew whose splice had failed at the seventh, and I knew all of it up to about a week before, which is the interval, which is always the interval.

They brought me in and they examined me and they gave me the handshake, which is not a greeting and is part of the evidence. I gave it back. My grip was correct. Everything about me was correct.

And then the Assize leaned in and asked by what counted route I had come, and I gave them the pause.

That is the only time in my life the pause has not been grammar. Twelve seconds is a long hole when there is nothing at the bottom of it.

---

The clerk had the struck list open before the questioning finished. They do it quietly and kindly and immediately; that is in the article too, the unpublished one.

**Marl.** One syllable, hard, retired a long time ago. A word that means a kind of earth.

There is no earth on the Sill. There is no soil here; nothing has ever died badly enough to become dirt. I have thought about that more than is good for me and I have never once said it aloud to anybody, and nobody has ever said it to me, because nothing whatever is said.

By the end of the week I was in work.

---

They made me a pacer, which is either the Assize's only joke or its deepest piece of kindness, and after forty years I am still not sure it is not both. Give the uncounted one the counting. Put the man who came by no measured route out onto the chains eleven days in rotation, droning, in a pair, with a third at the far end counting back.

I am the third. I have nearly always been the third.

The pair comes toward me counting up and I come toward them counting down, and where the numbers cross is where the chain is, and if they do not cross the chain is closed and re-counted from the other end. There is no company in it. You are alone in the glare for six hours with the white floor throwing the light back up under your hat, and the fog-combs going by edge-on, and your own voice, and the other voices somewhere ahead of you, thinning and thickening, until you can tell the living count from the ground's count by the way the living one is slightly out of true.

My feet are ruined. My voice is superb. I have been told this by people who were not being kind.

---

I carry two numbers I have never said.

I came up knowing the chains. All of them — I woke with the plateau's whole measured length in me the way another man wakes with his trade in his hands. And among them are the two segments that came back shorter than the sum of their own parts, the ones burned out of the register in a year nobody can name. They tried to burn them. You cannot burn anything on the Sill.

I have both lengths, correct to the pace, and I will not lay them in the ground. A thing said on the open heath is said forever and comes up where it likes. It would be a small crime, or a large one, or no crime at all; the Assize has stopped funding the question. But I am not going to be the one who puts back into the fibre the only two measurements this plateau ever managed to get rid of.

So I carry them, and they sit in me like plate-scrape. Bitter, faintly metallic, and the reason I am careful about my own slurring.

---

Once, early, I went into an empty stand and found the room.

Fourth node, north side, glazed, wired, never slept in. Door-notch, shelf-notch, and in the windowsill the wear-hollow where an elbow rested for forty years. I put my elbow in it.

It fitted.

That is the shape of an elbow. Every elbow fits it. That is either a comfort or it is the entire problem, and I have had a long life to decide and have not decided. I did not report the room. It is Crown property and it is in the code and I walked out and shut the door and have never been back, and I do not know to this day whether what I was protecting was the room or myself or the forty years of somebody's arm.

---

Every spring I do a count that nobody asked for.

I walk the margin of my own stand where the Standing Crop comes up — the several hundred of them, fibre and wax, faceless in the details and unmistakable in the stance, the turned-in foot, the hands held behind the back, leaning a little more each year until they go down and are taken back. I count them. Then I count the death register.

The numbers agree. They have always agreed. There has never been one extra out there, standing for somebody who did not die.

I check anyway, because I would like to know what this plateau thinks I am. It repeats rooms and it repeats coats and it repeats birds and it repeats the dead, and it is very good at all of it, and it will not admit what it is doing, and it insists. The question I am not going to ask out loud is whether it also, twice in a decade, repeats a *living* thing — grows a man the way it grew the rooms, complete with a week-old knowledge of who owes what at the third node, and stands him out in the fog to be found.

Nobody asks. Or somebody asked, and nothing whatever was said.

---

Last month I went to see her.

The first Marl. She is in the margin at Sparroway, eleven years up out of the haulm and starting to lean — a small woman, by the stance, with her weight back on the left heel and one shoulder carried high the way you carry it after a lifetime of a strap. I do not know what she did. I could look it up. I have declined to look it up for forty years the way you decline a drink you know the taste of.

There is a rule against speaking to them. It is universally broken and nobody has ever been fined, not once, in the whole history of the Assize.

So I spoke to her, and what I said was the count.

Margin to the stand foot, correct to the pace, aloud, in twelve-second units with the pause held between them, because that is the only answer anybody here gives when you ask them how they know they are not one. I gave it to her and I gave it to the ground under her, which took it, and will keep it, and will play it back in four hundred years to a carter who will stop dead in fine weather on an empty heath and hear a pacer counting where no chain has ever run.

I hope he takes his break by it. It is a good count. It is reliable, which the rest of this plateau is not.

And because I am the third at the far end, and the third counts back, it finished where my counts always finish, where every count of mine has finished since the week they gave me a dead woman's name and a pair of boots.

Three. Two.

*One.*
