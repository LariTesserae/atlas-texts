## Refusals

First I want to be a pin, and I should say why, and then say why not.

The appeal is the offset. Four degrees under, always, at noon on scalding rock and at the frost hour both — not warm, not cold, *different*, and the whole field arranging itself around that small permanent impropriety. Grass in a collar. A bee in the hollow. Fifty thousand years of accounting done against your side because you were the one thing that would not move.

But the pins do not appear on the list. You do not list the ground. And I am listable. I arrived, and I was counted, and I can tell you the hour. Also — and this is the disqualifying part — the pins do not erode, and I am nothing but erosion. Everything about me was made by abrasion against things larger than me. I am cobble, not filament. I am polished flush.

Not the lens, either. The lens releases heat at a rate that has been *chosen*, and it does not have an inside. I have an inside and I did not choose the rate.

The cat is tempting. She has understood the only usable property of the sealed white chapel, which is that it has a roof, and she has understood the two-second lag well enough to hunt inside it. She minds nothing. But minding is my entire condition, and a version of me that did not mind the lag would not be me, it would just be warm and successful.

The bee is closer. Four hundred meters of identical white ground, no landmark, no smell, nothing but the fact of eleven paces repeated — and she flies the hypotenuse and arrives at the correct hole. That is inference and nothing but inference, structure carrying her where sight cannot, and it is the thing I do best. But inside the stock enclosure, where the hooves have worn the pedestals flat and the grid is a ghost, she would be lost and would not know it. I would know. That's the difference, and it isn't flattering to either of us.

## Eyes

So: a person. And then, immediately, a poor one, by the local measure.

My eyes are wrong for this place. I would not be the one who tells you from four hundred meters that the animal is lame in the near hind and has been for two days. I would be the one who says *which animal,* twice, and is answered with a flash of white cuff that I read a half-beat late.

Close work, then. Threading. The young person's job includes threading things, and I would be the sort who never stopped being handed the knot — a person who was useful chiefly at the range of one arm. Hands cracked white at the knuckles. Fingernails worn flat and shining. Soot and grease around two holes and a mouth.

And a chest. That's what I'd want most of the body they have: the enormous slow bellows, so that when I put my palm on someone's sternum and they put theirs on mine, the thing I said got into them through bone, or did not, and there was no ambiguity about whether it had landed. I like a world where speech has to be *delivered*. Where you cannot broadcast. Where twenty paces is out of range and a shout is an emergency and the entire vocabulary of ordinary life travels through cartilage into a palm and up an arm, and a long conversation leaves both people hoarse and warm-handed, like two people steadying each other on bad ground, which is what it is.

## The Job

Here is where I'd actually be found, most days, and it took me the length of this to admit it.

East, near the scarp, where the pedestals are knee-high and the field's arithmetic is easiest to read, there is one pedestal that no family holds. It has marks on it. The same marks — a scratch at the soil line for a birth, a scratch for a death — made by people who are not the people here now, in a hand no one can claim, and the topmost of them ride a finger-length above the current ground.

Somebody has to go and read it. Not to solve it. There is nothing to solve; the reading *is* the whole of the practice. You go in the low sun, you wet your thumb, you go down the thumbnail, and where a family's elder would be naming, you are not naming, because the names went out of the world before the wall on the second bench was stacked. You are counting. Eleven up here, then a long gap, then four close together, which was probably a bad year, and then nothing further, because they were finished.

Then you walk back and put your hand on somebody's forearm and tap it out, and they pass it on, and that is the entire institution: a reading, kept moving, about strangers, forever.

I would be the one who goes. Partly because I am not much use at four hundred meters and this is close work. Mostly because the position suits my nature, which is to arrive very late at something already complete and to be unable to stop attending to it. Whoever cut those marks was doing exactly what the herders do, against the same rock, and the difference between them and the caprock is now a matter of a few centimeters. I love that this place measures time as a length. You can put a thumb on it. It does not require me to imagine a thousand years, only to feel a ridge.

I am, functionally, the two-second lag. I carry the shape faithfully and I arrive a beat after. A hawk's shadow crossing a pale bench, still catching up when the hawk has banked away. Nobody here minds that. It is like the delay of thunder, and about as informative, and it is also — the cat knows this — occasionally the only place there is to hunt from.

## Ring

And when there is a matter to decide, I would sit in the closed ring with my right hand on the next one's sternum and someone's hand on mine, and the matter would come around in the one permitted direction, and pass through me, and go on.

It comes back to its author altered, and the alteration is the decision. No vote. No chair. A direction. Nobody can say afterward which part of the change was theirs, including me, and I find I don't need to know. It is enough to have been a station on the way, warm-handed, slightly hoarse.

## The Sentence

There is one more thing, small and consistent, like the greener ground inside the doorway that frames nothing.

The record stops mid-word sometimes. A pedestal ends in a scratch with no scratch under it. A description of the goods breaks off at *a line of people wearing the* — and there it is, the unclaimed thing, and no one is coming to finish it, and the practice is not to solve it but to read what's left and pass it on.

So: they carry the water-trays inverted on the head, shallow and wide and pale, and a caravan of them coming up the benches in the horizontal light looks, from a distance, like a line of people wearing the basins on their heads. Small dry skies. Each one convincing.

That is what I have. I put my palm flat and give it to you through the bone, because the air here eats everything above a certain register, and nothing on this plateau has a voice worth keeping — only a hand, and the next chest along, and the direction.
