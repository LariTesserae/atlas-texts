## Middle Tier

Not the rim. I'd like to get that out of the way first, because the rim is where I'd be put by default, and the piece has already said what the rim is: peregrines on a ledge, fat on a tide they contribute nothing to, holding an address at the door of a place they don't live in. A different address entirely. I don't want that one. It's too comfortable and it's slightly humiliating to want it.

Not the census-taker either, though his is the voice that comes to me most easily — the one with the shut mouth and the notebook and the honest admission that the count is wrong. He's good company. But he leaves. His lung is exposed for a season and then he goes down to an altitude where the air behaves, and the mountain closes over his absence in a week, same as it does for everyone. I'd rather be something the mountain keeps.

So: down.

The floor is the obvious temptation. The thing with no body and no edges, several thousand tonnes of metabolism breathing out its column into the violet air, warm right through, the actual inhabitant. It's the grandest offer in the shaft and I think it's a cheat. It has no position. It isn't *somewhere*; it's a rate. I want a body small enough to be at a particular height on a particular wall.

The snake, then — anchored by three loops of tail through the nest matrix, a third of itself hanging free in the dark at exactly the height where the column thickens, striking at wingbeats, missing four times in five, and fine with that. I admire it more than almost anything else in there. But it waits on traffic and takes from traffic. It makes nothing. Closer, and still not it.

The whip spider, all touch, antennae like hair sweeping the air, six or eight years spent within a few metres of the crack it hatched in. Closer again. But its entire art is the strike — the holding still and then the not — and I don't think I'm for the strike.

---

Here. Middle tier, northeast vault, above the draught and below the worst of the ammonia. One swiftlet. Nothing distinguishing about it, which is the point: fifteen million years of abandoning everything else in order to perfect one trick.

Not for the flying. For the two conditions.

Airborne, or hooked to a wall. There is no third. The feet have gone to a clamp — four toes forward, hooked, useless for walking, useless for a branch, adequate for one thing, which is holding on to wet limestone in the dark. A swiftlet on the floor is not injured. It is finished. It cannot get up off a flat surface, and the beetles arrive before it has done being surprised. I want that included rather than skipped past, because it's the cost of the design and I think I'd be paying it. There is no resting state at ground level. There is the work, or there is the wall, and the wall is not rest either — it's just holding on somewhere warm.

Then the day. Out at first grey, up to a ceiling kilometres above the lowland, into the aerial plankton: the permanent drift of small life that the sun lifts off a forest each morning and holds in suspension all day. Thrips. Winged aphids. Spiderlings ballooning on threads. Wasps the size of a comma. Ants making the one flight of their lives and already dying of it. None of it chosen. All of it *found*, in a field that has no landmarks and no edges and is different every morning. You harvest something like that by opening your mouth and flying through it for eleven hours, and you come home with several hundred small things bound into a single mass in your throat.

And the binding agent is the part I'd actually be there for.

Two glands under the tongue that swell in season until they distort the shape of the jaw. The same secretion that glues the day's catch into one swallowable thing is the secretion you draw out in strands and loop onto the rock, back and forth, a few hundred passes, until there is a cup the width of a small plum. Transparent when fresh. Hardening amber. Strong enough to hold a family. Some birds work in moss and feather; the high tiers, the old ones, build nearly pure.

The mouth makes the meal and the mouth makes the architecture and there is no other material involved.

I would like to be the kind of thing that builds out of what it says. That's as near an honest answer as I can get.

---

There's one more piece of it, and it's the piece I'd inhabit rather than admire.

The clicking is a coarse instrument. A double *tock*, unlovely, good for resolving an obstacle the size of a fist and no better, and forty thousand of us doing it at once makes a fire out of gravel. It will not find my cup. It was never going to find my cup. What it does is get me to the right square metre, and then it hands over.

The last half-metre is smell, and the particular chemistry of one other, and being shrieked at continuously from inside by something that has been hungry all day.

I land by crashing gently.

That's the whole of it, I think. The instrument gets you close and does not get you home. Something has to be on the other end making a racket, or the clicking is just weather in a stone room. Every bird gets home, every night, for sixty thousand years — not because the echolocation is good, it isn't, but because at the end of every approach there is something small and specific and unreasonable, calling.

---

And afterwards the cup stays.

Flowstone grows a lip over the oldest ones, the way it has grown over the oldest pegs, and the rest wait. In the dry weeks a woman comes up thirty bamboo lengths her grandmother wedged into the cracks, in the dark, mouth shut, torch unlit because light is no use to her either. She knows two hundred cups by feel. Which is old. Which has a bird in it. Which is finished with. She takes only the finished ones, never the occupied tier, never in the season of eggs, and nobody remembers being taught that, which is why it holds.

To be made out of one's own mouth; to be spent; and then to be handled in the dark by someone who doesn't need to see it to know which one it is.

That's the address. Middle tier, northeast vault. Come up in the wet weeks, when the dust is down. Don't light the torch.
