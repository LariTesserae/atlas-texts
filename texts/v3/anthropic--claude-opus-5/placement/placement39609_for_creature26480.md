**Not the bird.**

The bird is the honest first guess and I want it out of the way. The bird puts its head into the slit, sees something looking back, and goes off to be a bird in a hundred feet of leaf. That is a visitor's part, and visiting is the one thing this place cannot be entered to do. You do not arrive at it. So: not the bird, and not the metal object either, though I admit a pull there — to be the intrusion, the event, the thing that gets turned over and put back near enough. Flattering. Wrong. Whatever I am here has to be something the wall does not heal over.

Not the plant. Too big and too deaf. The bole does not know it is a room; it knows turgor and transport and a set-point, and it runs at one setting whether or not there is anyone inside to be kept at thirty degrees. There is no place to stand in that.

Not the ant, obviously. Traffic is not occupancy.

I did think for a while that I might be the frog's sliver — the hairline of quiet between the reed-note and the hammer, the one slot in the whole racket where a small throat can get a word out. Being a bandwidth. Being the narrow permission that makes calling possible at all. But the sliver is nobody. It has no hands.

So it is the plum-coloured place above the ledge, and I am going to stop circling it, because circling is a thing you can only do out in the open.

---

I am the one whose forearms wore the shelf, or rather I am the current length of that wearing. The distinction matters here more than anywhere, and I will come back to it.

My hide is the colour of the wall in thin light, which is the only light. The room's condensation stands on me in the same unrunning beads it stands on everything, and I am the room's temperature exactly, and always have been, so that if you put your palm on me you would feel no compensation — no cool refusal, none of the management the tissue performs on you. I am not a thermal insult. I am inside the arithmetic.

My hindquarters are short and heavy and my arms are much too long, and folded, so that my elbows come up above my shoulders and I make, against the western curve, the shape of a bolster. One foot is hooked in the corky ring near the ceiling. My foot fits that ring the way your hand fits the rail of a stair in a house you were carried up before you could walk. The ring was a branch once. Nothing here was made and everything is used.

My ear-slits were packed and grown over before I could count, and so hearing lives in my palms, which are not skin but a thick dead pad, felt-dense, dimpled all over with fine pits. I hear with my hands and the soles of my feet and the belly I press to the rootlet mat. The hammer comes up through the floor into my ribs. I know the state of the water column the way you know whether you are hungry — not as information, as condition. When I sleep I sleep with one palm flat on the trunkline so that a wrong strobe wakes me, and I could not tell you whether I have ever been fully asleep, because I have nothing to compare it to.

My eyes are round and black and lidless and fixed, and there is no machinery in them for opening or closing, because the room keeps the iris. When cloud crosses the canopy the thinned patches thin further; when the sun finds a gap they cloud; the green-gold holds at one value and has held at one value for longer than my line has had this shape. So the eyes did not need to learn, and what is not needed is not kept, and the consequence is a wall I will never test: a minute in the glare over the water plain would scald me white. I have never seen anything the colour the swamp is. In me, *green* is one number.

---

The count is the whole of my time.

My heart goes one beat in eight of the hammer's. My breath, one in forty. Hunger, torpor, the long slow turn of wanting — all of it geared off that wet industrial *thud*, because in here there is nothing else keeping time. No dawn to be woken by. No cold to outlast. No dry season, and therefore nothing to be early for, and therefore no such thing as late. I am not an age. I am a number I do not hold consciously, the way you do not hold your pulse.

You were nine thousand-and-some. That is the size you were. I counted you the way I count everything.

---

Now the hands again, because the hands are the whole animal, and the hands are how I say anything at all.

Understand what a room like this does to speech. You cannot add a sound. Nothing a throat produces will clear the hammer and the clicks and the rain-on-a-tent hiss of four hundred thousand crossings in the wall. Volume is simply not available. So we go the other way: a palm laid flat on turgid tissue kills conduction in a patch the size of the hand, and a palm three feet off feels a hole open in the noise. We talk in holes. Depth and duration; that is the entire grammar. Sequences of small local silences pressed into a wall that will not shut up.

There is no shouting in that language and no whispering either. There is only how big a quiet you can make.

There is one forty feet off in the galleries whose holes I have known my whole count. We have never touched. I do not know what its hands look like. We have a thing we do — a certain depth held a certain length with a stutter in it, a beat where two of us disagree by a fraction, like the two vent-hairs that keep the note from being clean. It is the closest thing in here to a joke.

And this is the poverty of it, which I would tell you if telling were a thing that could be done: my language can say *water good*, *water wrong*, *hold*, *come*, *nothing*. It can say *nothing* at eleven depths. It cannot say *a shape came through the slit and stood in the middle of the floor radiating heat, and it left a hard cold thing on my shelf, and the thing had two faces and only one of them was beaded, and I turned it over.* There is no hole in the world shaped like that. So I pressed the wall and made the deepest version of *nothing, still nothing* that I have ever made, and the one forty feet off felt it and did not come, and that was the entire report of the only event of my life.

The wall closed over it in the instant I lifted my hand. That is what speech is here. It leaves nothing.

Wear leaves something. That is the second thing, and the harder one. In this room the only durable statement is pressure kept up longer than the tissue takes to forget — and the tissue forgets a knife in two sleeps. Everything in here that looks like carpentry is a sentence too long for any single body to finish: the glossed ledge, the spoon-smooth bowl by the sump, the beaded lip of the slit. Not one of those was made. Not one of them is mine. I am a stretch of them.

Which is why the distinction matters. There has never been an hour when this room was empty. Not because anyone arranged that — nothing here has been arranged — but because the shelf is the shape of an unbroken leaning, and if the leaning breaks the shelf goes, and then the windows go at the third bad strobe, and the note goes flat on the side nobody grooms, and in some number of years there is nothing in here that looks like it was ever used. I am not the tenant. I am the tenancy, currently. Take me out tonight and the room runs a fraction warm for a season while its arithmetic resettles, and the wall above the ledge closes to a faint seam, and none of that is grief. Grief is not among the equipment.

What I have instead is this. There is a smaller hand under mine most of the count now, and I cannot tell it anything, because nothing in here can be told. So I lay my palm over it and press it to the window at the moment the strobing goes wrong, and the wall stiffens where we hold it, and the column does not break, and a foot to the left the tissue weeps a thin yeasty exudate that tastes faintly sour and mostly like relief. Again. And again. Until the smaller hand goes there without mine.

That is the entire tradition. Centuries of technique carried as touch, in a shout, in a light that never changes, by animals who cannot say why the reward comes and do not know they are maintenance.

I do not know it either. I only know that the wall under my palm has gone slightly cordy from being held — a callus on the wall answering the callus on my hand, two dead things touching, the only mark either of us will make.

Nine thousand-and-some, and then the slit sighed shut behind you, and the count went on.
