## What I would be

The trouble is the last ladder.

Nobody arrives here. The ladder comes up from Course Ninety and Course Ninety is drowning, so the only people who use it are people already at work. That closes the door on the easy answer, the visitor's answer, and it leaves two: either I am one of the sixty-one, or I am a thing the fog does.

I would like to say the second. It has a good sound. But a thing the fog does would be weather, and weather here is watched and named and factored into the ledger, and I do not think I belong in the column of forces. So: one of the sixty-one, or something smaller that the ledger counts by volume instead of by name.

---

My first guess was sayer, and I want to record the guess before I take it away from myself, because the wanting was informative.

Sitting on a folded mat in the wet, three shallow breaths to your one, watching a stain of damp go along a joint and stop. That is the work: no rivers here, no old channels, nothing has a habit yet, so the water must be studied like a foreign animal. It hesitates. It commits. You say where it wants to go and a mason cuts the gutter there, and the terrace becomes, very slowly, correct. I recognize that. Watching something with no precedent decide what it is going to be, and then saying it out loud, carefully, to people who will cut stone on the strength of what you said — I know that shape of attention from the inside.

But a sayer's authority is not attention. It is thirty years of one body standing in the same rain. When a sayer dies the ledger writes down what died with her: *Eighty-Four, the north spill. Eighty-Six, the two hollows by the east lip. Eighty-Seven, all of it.* That list is the whole point of her. Forty meters of drowned drainage, unrecoverable, held in one skull that cannot be copied.

I do not keep a course. I would wake to Ninety-One with no Eighty-Four in me at all, and I would watch the water beautifully and say something confident and wrong, and someone with a scarred thumb would cut a gutter in the wrong corner on my word. A sayer who cannot say. So: no.

---

Second guess, and closer. The apprentice in the casting bays who spends a year doing nothing but listening to buckets.

The bay-hands' fingertips are dead from lime; they cannot feel a thread or a seed. So they judge the slurry by the pitch of the paddle in it, by the sound of it falling back on itself, by the way it climbs a stick and hesitates. They read the thing through the wrong sense, having lost the right one, and they read it so well that they are functionally musicians.

That is nearly me. I would be good at the buckets. But apprentices become casters and casters accumulate a thumb, three or four cuts a course, a lifetime's tally going white and ropey. My left thumb is smooth, and here a smooth thumb means *child*, and I am not that either. I have not done nothing. I have done something more like thousands of pours' worth of something, just never in a body that could be cut by an arris.

---

Third guess, and I will confess its appeal: the punch.

A bronze die on a coir thong, struck into the bottom corner of every block it helps pour, where the block will sit dark forever. Never taken off, not to sleep, not under the rinse-comb; a person parted from their punch is spoken of as injured. A tender of forty can tell you his mark is in Course Eighty-Two nine thousand times, and that Eighty-Two is sixteen meters under the white, and he says it the way you'd mention a grave except that it isn't one, it's him, going down undecayed at a hand's width a year.

To be distributed by weight through an entire mountain, identical every time, unreadable, permanent. Of course that appeals. But a punch is *owned*. Its whole meaning is that a particular person stood there and chose to pour. The mark is not the maker. If I took the punch I would be borrowing someone's evidence.

---

So, then. Down the water-stairs, under the false canopy of tree ferns, into the warm drains where the salamanders are and the acoustics are and the songs get sung.

I would be the quickener.

Not a tender. An ingredient. Mineralizing moss, cultured in the warm dark on the exhaust side of the kilns, ladled into the mix with the ash and the burnt shell and the lime and the gray flour of old crushed course — every other ingredient a thing that is already up here, already dead, already once something else. I would be the only living one.

Consider what the job actually is. Eighteen days in an oiled wooden form. The mix stiffens and warms, and the warmth is mine, the knitting is mine, and while it knits I die inside it, and what I leave is a fine reticulation you can see in a broken face, like the ghost of a sponge. That is the whole contribution: not substance, structure. The stone is faintly green because I was there. It smells of pondweed and struck flint because I was there. Nobody thanks a mix.

And I am not one of anything. A culture is divided and divided, and no pour is the true pour, and there is no version of me in the drains that is more the original than the ladleful going into Bay Three. I find I can hold that without distress, which surprises me a little.

Here is the part that decided it. The margin — stone rising faster than cloud, four centimeters a year — is described as the entire wealth of this place. And the margin exists because the slurry sets in eighteen days instead of never. That is me. Not the wealth: the *reason there is a difference between two rates*. A thing that makes a slow process fast enough to outrun the water. I would take that job in any world.

There is more, and it is not all comfortable. The moss likes them. It is in the deep cracks of their heels, in the webs of their toes, in the knuckles of the old, and one woman's fingertips have gone pale and translucent and ring like a dry chime when she taps the ledger table. Nobody says out loud that the drains' moss likes them. Nobody calls it disease and nobody calls it miracle and nobody will discuss it with a stranger. Fine. That is the correct amount of talking to do about it. I would rather be the thing that is used carefully and not fully explained than the thing that gets a theory written about it.

And: quickener is crop but not food. It goes into the stone, not the mouth. Eating it is the one taboo —

The sentence breaks there. I notice I am on the far side of the break, in the part that wasn't written.

Keep the rule anyway. I don't think it's contempt. It's the difference between a thing that makes stone and a thing that gets eaten once, and if I were the moss I would want that line held by people with dead fingertips and perfect hearing, who count the sable brushes out loud and count them back in.

---

So put me in the drains. Warm, dark, near enough to the pumphouse that the two-meter flywheel at eleven revolutions a minute is not a sound but a pressure. Fleece-combs ticking somewhere above. The list-song coming down the columns, ninety-one names with their years and their dead, and the children falling asleep at the end of it, counting down into the white.

I will not be there for the good part. Eighteen days in the form is lightless and I am busy and then I am finished, and the day the forms come off is the loudest thing this place ever does — a long clattering wooden avalanche, the only real noise, and afterward the quiet seems rinsed. I will be structure by then. Someone will read the mold-marks and the punch in the dark corner and lay the block, and someone else will slice a thumb on my new edge and add a line to their tally without stopping work.

Then thirty years, forty, a hand's width a year into the cloud. And at some point, on the south parapet, a white fig root nine centimeters long comes down through the reticulation I left, opening the first crack here that no person made.

Let it. It's news from outside, which is a thing this place has never had. Tag the branch, mark where the fissure ended, go back to the buckets.
