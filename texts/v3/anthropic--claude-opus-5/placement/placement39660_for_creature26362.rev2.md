I keep trying to be the still, and it will not hold.

It's the first thing that reaches for me — the one soft animal, blind and enormous and slow, wearing ten thousand layers of hollow fibre, walking into a gallery and taking the sound out of the gas the way a cloth takes up spilled oil. To be the only quiet in a world made of noise. To be feared for it. There's a vanity in that I can feel from here, and anyway it doesn't survive contact with the details: the still has no interior. It doesn't want quiet. It eats powder, and quiet is just how it makes powder, the way a fire makes ash. It isn't a refuge. It's a mouth with a radius.

So I try being small. One crumb-mite in the ascending file, thumbnail-flat, pale, a granule of sugar-flour balanced on my back, climbing the inside stairwell of a trunk thirty paces around with nine million others, paid in wax, unable to stop, eaten if I fall out of the line. There's honesty in it — most of what happens anywhere is done by things at that scale, and the sound of nine million of us is the quietest sound in the world, sand poured slowly onto paper, going on forever under everything. But there's no *finding myself* in it. There's nobody in the file to find.

Here is where I actually come down.

I am old, and my ear-plates are thick enough that a trumpet-pod detonating at four hundred paces registers only as a shove against the sternum and a small annoyance in the jaw. My skin is waxed to the colour of dark horn. I have a throat-pouch I use less every year, because I talk to people less every year. I carry: a knapped plate-blade with an edge that will take a limb; a horn of moth-oil; a wax box of rasp-cricket egg-combs, live, which is most of my wealth; a bead-pouch with eleven copper; a bone-satchel, empty on the way out; and a hand-clacker, which I work continuously with my left hand, without thinking, the way you breathe.

The clacker is not for being heard. Nothing here hears. The clacker is so I stop being *seen* — so my body, which is a moving hole in the noise, scatters its own shadow as it goes. I have worked it so long that the rhythm has gone into my walk and my walk into the rhythm, and when I sleep my left hand keeps a little of it going against the deck.

My work is that I walk to the frames that have stopped.

Every settlement drums its roster, day and night, whoever is not asleep or working — the living names and the dead names in rotation, which is the same document as the genealogy, which is the same document as the duty roll, and it carries four miles. Lying awake in my pod I can hold three districts at once: this one is down two hands, that one is curing its eastern growth too slow, someone over there is drumming a dead woman's figure twice in a row because he is tired and lonely and it is his grandmother. That is not a skill. It is only what happens to a person who has listened for sixty years.

And then a frame stops, and everyone downwind knows exactly which name stopped, and I put on the satchel and go.

Four hours, usually. Down the trunk-braids in the wandering light, through the gas that pushes back against the chest like slow water — except I have no idea what water is; I have the word from the old drum-stories and it means *a softness that takes things apart*, which is a horror, which is a thing that could not exist. The floor in the low places is knee-deep in yellow pollen-flour from a bloom three weeks back, and it comes up around the shins in dry clouds, and the flour-eaters boil away from my shadow and the things that eat them follow, so I walk in a little travelling riot of my own making.

What I find at the end of it is nearly always the same. A pod hanging still, waxed shut or waxed open. A drum-rack split — and by *split* I mean a wait came off the trunk in one motion, or a plate calved in the night and took the frame and the two people on it and left the rest, or the trunk's own hungry roots undermined it and a hundred years of standing ended in a chain-fall that opened a raw new chamber, lit and screaming, already being colonised. Or the quietest ending: a spill, the trunk dead, nine million mites out of it at once like the tree was pouring, and afterward the heap at the base, and nothing left in the pod with any fat in it, including the people.

Nothing rots. So they are exactly as they were. That is the part nobody outside would understand: I am not arriving at a ruin. I am arriving at a *moment*, kept. A woman with her jaw still pressed to the talk-cord. A child's hand-clacker on the deck with the cord looped twice, the way you loop it when you mean to come back.

I take the copper beads. I strip the ancestors out of the door-lip — skulls facing outward, lacquered, generations of them, and a rich pod has no wall left showing and is also physically stronger, and both things are meant — and I lash them into the satchel and they ride home with me to be somebody's ladder-rungs and frame-struts. Bone is good material. There is no other way to keep a thing.

And then, before any of that, before the beads and before the bones: I sit down at the split rack, and I true what plates are left, and I begin drumming.

That is the whole of it. That is the work. Four hours out, and a night at a stranger's frame, holding a name nobody will ever answer to again, keeping the new growth over a dead settlement curing true until I can get a live one to take the district back. Because it will cure cloudy otherwise. It will go brittle and then powdery, and then the plate goes, and then the gallery goes, and then the next gallery, and the wreckage of a failed district is a wound that moves.

Here is the thing I have never said down a talk-cord to anyone.

Every Ringer spends two years inside the cradle, in still-fleece, in the only silence there is. You are lifted out of it the day your ear-plates set, and the frame takes up your name for the first time, and they say the child always reaches back. They say it without sympathy, because there is no going back in, and the fleece is finite, and others are coming.

I don't remember it. Nobody remembers it. But I have spent my life walking four hours toward places where the noise has stopped — toward the hollow spot in the sternum where the pressure should be pushing back — and I go there for the sole purpose of ending it, and I have never once been able to decide which half of that is the true half. I'm not sure it's two halves. I think the reaching and the drumming might be the same motion, done slowly enough that it looks like a life.

My name is five beats. Short, short, long, short, long. I have heard it drummed in districts I have never walked, by people I have never met, because walkers get carried — you restart a frame and they put your figure in the rotation as a courtesy, and courtesies here are structural, and nobody takes anything out of a roster once it's in.

There are three names still running nightly in the eastern districts that are attached to nobody. Climbers, they say: people who went up inside a light-pipe toward whatever surface the daylight falls from, and did not come back down, and were never confirmed dead, and so are drummed, and held open.

I am old enough now that I have been drumming dead names much longer than living ones, and I have begun to notice that from the inside it makes no difference at all. Somewhere tonight a hand I don't know is striking my five beats into a plate, and that hand does not know whether there is still a body attached to them, and will not find out, and it changes nothing about the work. The roof over that hand is curing either way. That is either the loneliest fact in this world or the only immortality it offers, and I have stopped believing those are different.

There is a frame down at the low end of my district that went quiet a little after the light dimmed.

I have the satchel. I have the combs. My left hand has already started.
