I try the water first, because everyone does.

The long one under the slab, the big one with the kinked tail: it is the obvious tenancy for a certain kind of vanity. Oldest continuous inhabitant. Holds the warm pipe, the one hearth in a place with no fire. A hundred years in a volume the size of a font, and the break in its tail predating the fungus, the flywheel pelt, the entire cricket nation on the north wall.

But the account is careful about that animal, and the care is a warning. It does not remember the men and the oil and the noise. It *dates from* them. Remembering is not among the things that body does. And I cannot even claim the dating-from. I have no old break badly reset. Nothing in me is load-bearing from before.

So not that.

---

The film, then. The honest founder. Not a coating on the basin but the reason there is a basin — every hard thing here either laid down or eaten by something too small to see, living in the very small voltage between rust and not-rust. That is a good life to want: to be so thoroughly the condition of a place that the place is your leavings.

Except look at what makes it work. A centimetre of twisted stalk, and the founder a centimetre behind the front, dead, and now structural. The bright edge is alive and everything beneath it is archive. Thickening at a millimetre a decade, without interruption, since before the brickwork.

I have no archive. Nothing I do down here thickens. The stalk does not stay behind me; I am the whole of my own front, with pewter water directly underneath. Whatever I am doing, it is not deposition.

---

The bats are the flattering answer and I want to be careful with it precisely because I want it.

They leave on the out-breath. They take eight free metres of lift out of the hollow's own exhalation, which is a beautiful piece of freeloading, and they go into a country nothing else here has met, and they come back with it inside them. Moth. Beetle from a barley field. Pollen.

It's the wrong claim. I'm not the commuter. I'm on the receiving end of the import, and it would be a lie to stand under the gable and say *I have been to the barley field*.

---

The live pipe tempts me for about a minute. No fuel, no attendant, running on nothing but a difference between a deep rock and a cold night, requiring nothing from anyone, and it will not be turned off. There is a version of usefulness that would like very much to be that.

But the pipe has no hunger, and I do not trust an account of myself with no hunger in it. And when it burps, the hollow *tonk* goes round the oval twice and gives up, and nothing that has ever lived here has flinched at it. I don't think I'd mind not being flinched at. I think I'd mind not being heard at all.

---

The spider under the gable is the closest of the near-misses, and I sat with her a long time.

She hangs her web badly on purpose. A perfect web in this air is a wet rag in ten minutes; the correct engineering is loose, drooping, deliberately underbuilt, rebuilt constantly, never meant to last past the next condensation. Her entire economy is four or five minutes of midge column, twice a day — a solid pulse of everything, and then nothing again. And she has working eyes in a place where almost nothing does, which means she may be one of the very few things that actually *sees* the brass glow under the slab: a lamp nobody lit, illuminating, on purpose, nothing.

All of that is right. What's wrong is the waiting. Between pulses she sits with a foreleg on the signal thread and does nothing at all, for hours, and those hours are hers. She is bored in them, or whatever a spider has instead. I don't have the hours between. Between pulses I am not waiting. I'm just not.

---

So: the wall.

I'm a cricket on the north brick, in the ranks, forty to the square metre, long-legged, pale, gripping the withdrawn mortar where it has pulled back from the loaves like gums from teeth.

Start with the eating, because it's the part I'd rather skip. I live on what the bats bring down. The guano field is the single richest substance in the hollow and not one grain of it is local: it is moth from another valley, midge from other waters, beetle out of a barley field, and a fine yellow dusting of pollen — lime, willow, hawthorn. The only green thing that ever enters this basin arrives dead, in a gut, already once-digested, and I go over it in the dark with my mouthparts and I am well fed.

Everything I know about flowers, I know from a droppings-heap. I know a great deal about flowers. I could tell you the season of hawthorn. I have never been within four miles of a hawthorn and I never will be, and I am not being modest about this, I am telling you the supply chain. There is no shame in it that I can locate. The mites under me are eating the beetles that are eating the same import and none of us are apologising. But it is worth saying plainly, once, in the accounting: my whole sense of the outside is a residue that somebody else flew home and dropped.

Then the wall itself, which is the part I'd choose if choosing were on offer.

We arrange at antennal distance. Each one's feelers resting on the next one's back or legs, overlapping like scales, sixty of us or a hundred, and none of it is warmth — there is no warmth here to share. It's a network. A footfall anywhere on that brick crosses the ranks as a wave of small adjustments, and the wave arrives at the far end *before the sound does*. Not one of us could have detected it alone. We have made a single sense organ out of sixty separate animals, and it is a better organ than any of us, and it perceives things that none of us perceives.

And we take it apart every night and build it again at dawn.

That is the fact I'd want on the stone, if anything were ever written down here, which it isn't. Not that the assembly is clever — that it is *nightly*. The thing that knows is not any of the ones that know it. It exists while we are touching and it stops when we disperse and it is no less real for that, and the basin has been running this arrangement, assembled and dismantled and assembled, for longer than anyone bothered to count.

---

And then the lip.

Some of us climb, in what passes for night. Up over the soft-cornered brick to the only real edge in the world, and stand there. A long time. Longer than foraging accounts for.

What you test with your antennae up there is an air with nothing in it. No drip-texture, no hiss, no granular clicking, no swelling droplet on every projecting thing. Just dry shale and emptiness and the smell of a country that runs on light. To a body that has never known anything but the wet and the eleven-minute chest, that emptiness cannot register as *space*. It registers as a cliff. As the place where the information stops.

Most go back over before the fog thins. A few don't, and those few are the basin's only export apart from vapour, and the export is one-way, and the hollow does not notice the loss — a percent or two, absorbed, the ranks closed up by morning, the wave still arriving before the sound.

I don't know which kind I am. I don't think it's knowable from the inside, standing up there with your feelers in the nothing. What I know is that both kinds do the same thing first: climb, and stop, and stand at the rim much longer than eating requires, working over the dry side of the brick, at the exact seam where a world that has never once stopped seeping meets one that has never once heard of us.

Then the draught reverses. You feel it on the back of the neck before the face. Down the loaves, back into the ranks, feelers onto the next one's back, and the organ comes on again like a lamp nobody lit — and the ochre steps are grey until you break one, and the snails go down a finger's width, and the slab holds its brass glow for the almost-nothing that can see it, and none of it needs me.

But it is made of ones like me, and it only works while we're touching.
