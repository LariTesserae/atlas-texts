I put a foot down expecting ground and get a dome instead, and the foot slides off the dome into a groove and stops there, hooked, and something in the ankle understands before anything else does. That is the first lesson and it is given away free. The water takes me at the navel. It isn't dramatic. It is a subtraction that has already begun, and will not stop, and that I will spend everything I eat for the rest of my life paying back.

So I am a body here, and the body is the argument.

*

At first I think: a cutter. Everyone is a cutter. Hips going wide, shoulders staying narrow, forearms corded from the slate blade, the long rolling gait, dome to groove to dome, at the pace of a slow funeral because there is no other pace and nowhere else to be going. I try it on. It fits, in the way the world fits everyone: unavoidably. I would be good at it eventually. Sixty years is long enough to be good at anything.

But it isn't where my weight goes.

Then I think: a reader. Of course I think that. Face-down in the shallows with one arm under the moss and two fingers on a swelling in the stone so faint that a lamp is no help at all, holding my breath, arguing in a whisper into somebody's forearm about whether that recurring figure is a depth or a date. A life's work the size of a bed. A few words a generation. I feel the pull of that so strongly that I distrust it — it is the flattering shape, the one where I get to be the person who understands, or who will, in four hundred years, by hand, in the dark, underwater.

And the readers are the ones who have stopped. That is the honest part of it. You read properly only when your hips have gone and you lie in a bed with just your face out and the sheetmoss comes over your shoulders in black-green shingles and you let it. The mantled read best because they have nothing else, and are consulted, and are combed around the face so they can see and eat, and ask to be lifted while they can still speak, and almost all of them ask.

I will be that, at the end, if I'm lucky. It isn't where I'm found.

*

Where I'm found is under the mast.

I carry the flame. That is the whole of what I do. Both arms are engaged, so I cut nothing, press nothing, comb nobody's sheath; I am fed by the ring out of the ring's own thin margin, the way the mantled are fed, and like them I have no excuse and am not asked for one.

You can tell a keeper by her wrists. Everyone else goes dark at the hands from the cutting and the peat; my arms have been up out of the water since I was eleven and my forearms stayed the colour of something grown under a lid. Tideline at the navel like anyone, and then this second pallor at the top, so that I am three of myself instead of two, and strangers look at my hands before my face.

The mast is cane, four spans, cured until it rings when you knock it. The hood sits at the top, in the drizzle-band, and the hood is the real object; the mast is only how you keep it there. I walk the shallowest, stillest line a terrace allows, because the mast must stay vertical and the hood must stay level, and so the ring's whole circuit bends a little around me without anyone deciding that it should. I am not a leader. I am a constraint that the walk has learned, the way a foot learns a groove.

I have never seen my own flame from any distance. Everyone else has. Two terraces off in the fog, other rings see a single warm dot moving slowly through the grey, the only thing anywhere that has a *there* about it, and they say, that is the eight-hundreds, they are coming round early this walk, their count is wrong as usual. I am the reason the world has a *there* in it and I am the one person who cannot stand off and look.

What the light is for is not seeing. This is the thing outsiders would get wrong, if there were outsiders. Oil is the cost of an hour of seeing and few hours are worth it, and anyway you navigate with your soles — groove width, dome height, weirs since the last well-sound. My flame illuminates nothing that matters. It does not help a reader; a lamp is no help at all against carving worn to a swelling. It says *here*. That is its entire vocabulary. It is a knock that doesn't stop.

And it was never lit. That is the claim and it is a serious one. It has been alight since before the count, which only means that nobody in the chain ever let it go out, and the chain is made of people like me, each of whom did nothing except not fail, and each of whom is now fog.

*

Twice a walk I hand it over, because I have to sleep, and there is no other way to sleep.

The hand-off has more grammar than anything else we do. You choose someone rested — actually rested, bedded and warm, not merely upright. You bring the mast down by the length of your own body while they set their feet, and you do not let go until you have felt them take the weight in the hips and not the arms, because arms tire and hips do not, and then you say the two words, which are not a blessing and not an instruction, and then you take your hands away. It is the same gesture as the Lowering, done at the other end of a life and to a different thing. They stand there with their heart going like a knocked pole and their whole face doing arithmetic about gusts, and I go and lie back into the hair until the water in the pocket is warm, which is to say until I have warmed it, which is the only warmth there is.

When I wake the first thing I look for is the dot. It is always there. It is always about to not be.

My name is Hine and the number in it is eight hundred and forty-one, which is the terrace-count of the lip where they set me down, and down-slope they say nine hundred and six and have said so for four generations, and I have stopped defending it. Their grooves are wider. Their year is thirty-one of ours. We compare tidelines and shrug and get on.

*

Here is the part I have never found a way to say, and have tried, into three different forearms, in the dark, with the hiss going on over us the way it does.

Inside the hood, above the oil, around the flame, there is a pocket of air perhaps a finger's width across.

It is dry.

I put my hand into it once, at eleven, the week they gave me the mast, because I was eleven. It hurt, and the burn is still on two knuckles, and that is not the part I remember. The part I remember is that for a moment my skin was not wet. There was no film on it. Nothing was landing on it. I had never in my life met a surface of myself that was not being touched by water and I did not have the word, and I went and stood in the basin with my hand held up until the drizzle took it back, which took no time at all.

We have no word for dry. The idea would need explaining and the explaining needs an example, and there is exactly one example in the world and it is four spans up in a hood that will burn you. Our hair never dries. Our dead do not dry; they tan and keep and are found by cutters, folded, dark as peat, fingers still hooked round a pole. We give them to the wells and the wells take them with that low pull you feel in the sternum, and they come back as fog, and we are always drinking, we cannot help it, so everything anyone breathes is made of everyone. This is not mysticism here. It is considered plumbing.

But that finger's width of air inside the hood has never been down a well. It has never been anybody. It is the one part of the world that is not the world, and it is the size of a knuckle, and I carry it around a contour for nineteen years at the pace of a slow funeral, at the top of a pole, in the rain.

They will lift me out at the end, four of them, onto a cane platform, and wash me and comb the moss off my shoulders, and by then I will have handed the mast to whoever is rested. Nothing of mine will be preserved and nothing lost; the basin I cut as a girl has already closed over with no mark on it except that I know. I will come round again as weather.

And the small dry place will go on ahead of me down the terraces, saying *here*, *here*, *here* into a fog that is entirely made of the people who used to carry it.
