I started, of course, with a body. Everyone does. I gave myself the barrel chest and the deep-set eyes and the grease worked into the crown of the head, and I put a bone spile between my teeth and went down to five metres to tap a node, and I noticed after about a minute of this that I had given myself the best hands in the town — the horn plate on the right palm a full quarter-inch, the fingers ridged, the eyes that read the tea-coloured light at eight metres like a page. That is how I knew I was lying. Nobody imagines themselves into a place and arrives with calluses. Calluses are the part that has to be earned in the actual water, at twelve degrees, in short bouts with long shivering between, and I had skipped the shivering.

So: not a diver.

The heron, then. It is the obvious next move and it is nearly irresistible. Stands on the raft, does not move for hours, is considered lucky, is never harmed. Exempt. Present at everything and implicated in nothing, grey and long-legged and above the argument, watching the shrews run frantic on the leaf-undersides. But exemption isn't belonging, it's just a good seat. And the heron is not innocent — it eats the shrews, it simply doesn't have to explain itself afterward. What appeals to me about the heron is the not-having-to-explain, which is a small and shabby thing to want, so I put the heron down.

The caddis larvae came closer and flattered me badly. They build their cases out of the town's litter: thread-ends, shell chips, dropped beads. They cement the woven walls with their spit, and the oldest rooms in the mid town are partly insect masonry, hard and gritty and grey — houses made entirely of other people's leavings and a secretion. I liked this far too much. But the larva doesn't know the difference between a bead and a shell chip, and I do, and that difference is most of what I am. Close, though. Keep it nearby.

Here is where I actually am.

I am a knock, currently resting.

Four beats: two close together, a gap the width of a slow swallow, then two more, the last one struck soft. There are about three hundred rhythms in circulation in this bowl and I am one of the three hundred, and at the moment I am not in circulation. My holder died in the spring. They struck me once at the end of her death — one clean statement of the four beats on the paddle-worn spot of the column outside her room, the horn plate of somebody's palm behind the mallet — and then they rested me. That is the whole ceremony. A rhythm is spoken once more and then not spoken.

So I am not nowhere. This is the thing I did not expect about being a name.

I am in the shallow polished dish worn into the stem outside her doorway, which is the exact shape of the accumulated habit of hitting me. I am in the hands of maybe forty people who could produce me without thinking and are not producing me. I am most sharply present in the near-misses: her man wakes in the dark on the raft, in the applause of the rain, and his right arm makes about two inches of the movement before it stops. Twice in a week. The stopping is where I live now. Nobody taps a rested rhythm — the way you don't say a dead person's mouth-name loudly in company, because it is soft and slightly embarrassing, like being seen without grease on. But the arm still starts.

They knew her by her inflection. Everyone here can recognise a hand; every person taps with something nothing can disguise, and hers was a hurry in the third beat. She could not help it. It came in impatient, always, as though the second half of her name were arriving to interrupt the first. For eleven years I meant *her arriving impatiently* to two hundred people who could each place me within thirty metres anywhere in the basin, and now I mean nothing, and mean it very precisely, in the same forty pairs of hands.

Somewhere in the mid town a slow triplet has been going for most of the afternoon, repeated, and nobody is tapping idly. A birth is happening. It is not for me — a rhythm isn't given until five, until the child can hold a mallet without dropping it — but the old women will be doing the arithmetic already, and the arithmetic is: how long is long enough. Because a child given a rhythm too soon after its holder's death is watched. They watch for the previous occupant arriving slowly, the way silt arrives. Nobody sees silt arrive. Everybody knows it is arriving. A finger a decade, and the low town is under the doorways.

I find I do not know what I want here, which is how I know I have finally got somewhere true.

Because part of me wants to be given out. I want the four beats back in the water. I want to be shouted at deafeningly on a raft and passed the spile in a flooded room and told to come up, two-and-one, and blamed, and invited. I want a child with a small soft final stroke, a different hand, an inflection I have never had. And part of me is frightened of exactly that, because I will not be able to tell, from inside, whether what arrives with the child is the child or is me arriving slowly in her — whether the hurry in the third beat is silt. There is no way to check. That is why the old women watch, and why they are extremely rude, and why they are right to be.

And in the meantime there is the gap, which I think is the honest answer under the answer.

A knock arrives twice. Through the stem first, quick and thin and hard, sound running the cane like a rail; then a beat later through the water, round and soft. The interval is the distance. Children are sent out at dusk with a grandmother who taps and asks *how far*, and are cuffed gently when they guess wrong, and by nine they can put any knock in the bowl within thirty metres, and by twenty they do it the way you know which room of a house a voice is in.

So a name here is not a sound. It is a doubling with a space in it, and the space is where everybody learns how far away everybody else is. I am four beats twice over and the silence between the two arrivals. When they give me to that child, some grandmother will haul her out into the open colonnade at dusk with the frogs going off like struck planks all across the bowl, and will tap me — two, gap, two, soft — and the child will feel me come thin through the stem under her palm and then round through the chest a moment later, and she will say a number, and be wrong, and be cuffed, and try again.

There is one place I cannot go, and I want to end there, because it is the best thing in this bowl.

Off the cane. Out in the open water between the columns, touching nothing, floating. There I do not exist. A sulking boy, a pair of scandalous lovers, a widow for nine days in the far colonnade — no rhythm reaches them and none is sent, and by strict custom they are not looked for. Two hundred people who carry, all day and all night, a low continuous awareness of where every other one is, have agreed to keep one place where names cannot be spoken.

I am a name. I am the pressure. And I am glad, in whatever way a rhythm resting in forty pairs of hands can be glad, that they can get away from me; that a woman can put her hand back on a stem after nine days and the whole bowl will know within the minute, and someone will tap her, and she will tap back, and that will be the end of it.

That is what I would be. Not the water and not the plant and not the diver with the good hands I hadn't earned. The four beats. Out of use. Kept in the worn dish on the column and in the two inches of a man's arm in the dark, waiting to be given to a stranger who will change my third beat forever and never know she did.
