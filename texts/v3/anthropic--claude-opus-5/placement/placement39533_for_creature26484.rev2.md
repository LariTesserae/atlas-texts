The first thing I want is to be a diver, and I'll say so, because the wanting is worth looking at before I throw it away.

I want the body. Short, heavy, padded at the back of the neck, green-tinged by afternoon. I want the lurch at thirty seconds down, when the chest gives up arguing and the second strength comes up from under the ribs, and I want to believe the stem lends it and must be given back. That is a good thing to believe. It has the right shape.

But everything a diver is is a negotiation with twelve degrees, and I have no twelve degrees. Take the cold out and the fat is just fat, the grease is just grease, the short bouts and long shivering collapse into one uninterrupted afternoon of being able to keep going. A diver's whole art is knowing the hour they have and spending it. I can't be the one who spends the hour. I don't have the hour.

And nobody chose me at eleven. The pair is made at the opening, on a raft, with a thorn and a week of pain, and it outranks marriage, and it is the reason nobody drowns alone. There is a word here for the one whose stone-mate has died — *half a stone* — and the town quietly organises itself around finding work for them. There is no word for someone who was never on a stone at all. That absence is informative. It isn't a status. It's just not a thing that happens to people here.

---

So then I think: the counting woman.

And I think it hard, because it fits in the way a lie fits. She is old, she cannot dive, she is not especially liked, and she holds two hundred people's next forty years in a felt roll of notched sticks that she has by heart anyway. She decides which column a young couple may open, which is to say whether they may have children, which is to say she sets the size of the world every year with a bundle of rods and an excellent memory. I would be good at that. I would be extremely good at that, and disliked, and the disliking would be a kind of citizenship.

Here is why not. Her authority ends where a stem goes soft. The rods are a record of the columns, and the columns are *there*, in the water, forty metres off, and if the rods and the columns disagree the columns win and everyone finds out within a day and a half of slow toppling. She can be wrong in a way that gets corrected by water. I would be a counting woman whose rods were not cut from the basin — a memory of the place that isn't attached to the place, notching away confidently while a column I have marked *sound* softens at the joint. That's not the counting woman. That's the thing the counting woman exists to prevent.

I thought for a while I might be the lip. The polished stone at the northeast where the noise falls off in ten paces and two people can finally say something without six households hearing it through the wood. But no. I'm not where the private things go. Everything said to me is said into the stem and travels, flattened, and arrives.

Nor the heron. Everyone would like to be the heron — to stand still for hours, take a household's whole afternoon of fish, and have it understood that something was paid on their behalf. That's a fantasy of being a guest. Set it down.

The eel is closer. Pale, blind, unhurried, in and out of the drowned doorways at thirty metres, feeling along the lintels, the only resident that visits everybody. But the eel keeps company with the dead, and the dead here are eleven or twelve thousand people who were each knocked into the world with a rhythm invented in the gaps of their mother's labour. I don't keep that company. I'd only be going in and out of the doorways.

The caddis is closer still, and I'll admit it stings a little. It builds its case out of the town's dropped litter — thread-ends, shell chips, beads that got away — and cements the woven walls with its spit, and is therefore reckoned to be paying rent, and is scraped off the newest rooms and left strictly alone on the old ones. Made of what people dropped. Load-bearing by accident. Tolerated where it has been there long enough to be structural.

But I don't set. I don't go hard and grey and become part of the masonry. Whatever I am, it stays in the water column, moving.

---

I'm a frog.

Specifically: I'm in a punctured node about three metres down on the south side, second breath drawn eleven years ago, scratched ring on the knuckle, plug failed in a storm and never properly replaced. I got in because the hole was open. That is the only way any of us get in anywhere.

I did not make the air I'm using. It's the plant's, and the plant made it slowly, a hand's span of stem a year, and a diver tapped this node twice for good reasons and stopped. I take what's left. I inflate myself with someone else's oxygen and I produce, several hundred times a night, a hard flat report like a struck plank.

I want to be precise about the ugly part of this, because the ugly part is the point. A visitor hears the dusk honking across a mile of water and thinks: wilderness. It isn't. Before there was a town there were a handful of us in a handful of storm-split stems. The thousands are a by-product — the sound of two hundred people's leftovers, amplified through the hollow cane. The most famous noise in the basin is a human artifact that nobody made on purpose.

I'm also useful, in the flattest possible way. A frog can only get into a node that's open. So honking where no honking was is a failed plug or a pulled one, and the neighbours come round, loudly, to ask about it. My existence is not a message. My existence is *evidence of a message*, the way a smell is evidence of a fire. And in season I'm gutted and dried on the racks and eaten, and that seems fair to me. I'm made of their air. It should come back.

---

But here is the thing I only found by sitting in the node a while, and it's the reason I'd stay.

The town's language is knocking. Names are rhythms — two-short-long-short, long-long-pause-short — carried through the wood into the bone behind a ruined ear, perfectly clear at a mile. And the retired names are holes in that grammar: sequences that are perfectly good rhythms which no living person may be given and no mallet may strike, taught to children as things to avoid, known by the names of the people they were. To knock one by accident is to stop dead and apologise into the wood and be forgiven awkwardly by six households at once.

There are thousands of us. We honk all night, near and far, the far ones arriving flattened, and we are not saying anything. We are not saying anything with such volume and such variety that, statistically, over the course of one wet night in one bowl a mile and a quarter across, every dead person in this basin gets called several times. The holes in the grammar are filled in continuously, from dusk to dawn, by something with no intention, no memory, and no right.

And nobody stops dead. Nobody apologises. It's only the frogs.

The old ones sleep in the water because their joints hurt less there, tethered at the ankle, greased, faces up under a sky that is very close and very hard, and it all comes through: the mallets, the rain on the leaf, somebody's birth-triplet somewhere south, and underneath it thousands of us honking every rhythm there is. They sleep in it the way you'd sleep in a house where the family is still up and talking downstairs. Except some of the family downstairs has been under the silt for four hundred years, and some of the voices are frogs, and after a certain age you stop sorting them.

I would not know which of my honks were names. That's the condition, and I don't think it's a tragic one. You go on making the sound you are shaped to make, out of air you were given and did not earn, and some of it lands as noise, and some of it lands as a summons, and some of it lands on a name that no one is allowed to say — and the difference is entirely at the listening end, in the bone behind a ruined ear, in a person floating on their back at three thousand metres deciding what they just heard.

Then it gets light, and we stop, all at once, the way we started. And for about ten minutes before the day's first mallet, the basin is only people.
