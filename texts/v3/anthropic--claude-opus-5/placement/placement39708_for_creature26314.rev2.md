I'd be on the floor, mid-chamber, fourth rank, on my knees with somebody else's water dripping past my ear.

My name is Sabe. I came down in Year One with a sack and a signature and I weighed sixty-four kilograms, which is on the log, and I weigh fifty-one now, which is not, because the log only records what goes down.

I mend fleeces.

---

A fleece fouls the way everything here fouls: from the salt. The out-draft crystallises brine in the mesh and the needles bridge the gaps between fibres, and once the gaps are bridged the panel stops behaving like a hundred thousand small cold edges and starts behaving like a sheet. A sheet does not bead. A fouled fleece drops from nine litres to six, and six litres is a household drinking half of what it needs and lying about it.

So I come with a copper comb that came down in Year One and is on the log at ninety-one grams, and I kneel, and I take the panel in my left hand and comb downward with my right, and the salt comes off in a dry hiss into a tray. Then I check the frame for a sprung corner, and I restring it if it's sprung, and I clear the gutter with a wire, and I go.

Here is the whole art of my trade: I may touch the mesh. I may touch the string, the gutter, the coil, the pipe. I may put my hands right through the middle of the thing that keeps four people alive.

I may not move the frame.

Not a hand's width. Not to get behind it. Not to do it better. There is no version of *I was only helping* that survives contact with the plate at the shaft foot. So I have learned to work bent sideways into a space I cannot improve, on a structure I cannot adjust, in a position that is bad for my back, for eleven years now, and I have never once been tempted, and that is not virtue. It's that I understood early what the rule was actually for. The rule is not about water. The rule is that in a chamber this hot and this small, with four hundred and ten people in a volume the size of a large church, there has to be one line that nobody crosses even by accident, or everything else becomes negotiable.

The household stands nearby while I work. Always. Not watching me — watching past me, at nothing, so that nobody has to pretend they aren't there. Nobody hands me anything. I don't ask for anything. When I'm done I stand up and they look at the gutter and not at me, and someone puts two fingers on the back of my collar for half a second on the way past, and feels that it's wet, and that is the entire transaction. In eleven years I have been thanked four times and every one of them was a newcomer inside their first month.

I have starred hands. You can hold them to the melt-eye and read me off them like a core sample.

---

I take more Listener watches than my share.

Partly because I sleep badly in halves, and partly because the chair is at the centre of the floor, and the centre of the floor is the quietest place under this mountain. The fleece-field eats sound the way snow does. You sit in a chair on ropy black glass with four hundred grey panels hanging in ranks around you, and you hear the pumps at eleven strokes, and the drip averaging itself out into a soft continuous static, and the vents, and nothing else, and you write down what the rock says and when.

Mostly the rock says nothing for ninety minutes and then knocks once, like a knuckle on a door, and you write *ceiling, NE quarter, single, sharp, 03:14* and your heart goes and then settles, and ninety minutes later it does it again.

I have called two evacuations. Both wrong.

The second one was Year Three, hour four of a night watch, a long tearing groan out of the ceiling over the Low Quarter, and I put four hundred people up two tiers in the dark in wet cloth with children on their shoulders, and we stood there for two hours, and nothing happened, and nothing has happened there since. I went back down and sat in the chair and wrote the time and the word *nothing* and signed it, because the log is signed, because a log that isn't signed is just weather.

Everyone read it. The log is the most-read document in the Kettle; people read it the way surface people read a newspaper, in the morning, with a jar at the elbow, and they read the wrong calls most closely of all. Nobody has ever said a word to me about that night. Not one. And I know — because I have counted, the way Osme counts the touches on her fig — that eleven people went out of their way in the following week to put two fingers on my collar who had no particular reason to be walking past me.

I would call it again. Same sound, same hour, same chair. That is the part I have had to make peace with: the job is not to be right. Six of nine calls have been wrong, and three of nine opened new vents, and if we ran it the other way — if the Listener waited until they were sure — the arithmetic says we would have buried a quarter of the town twice by now. So I sit in a chair and I am wrong in public, in writing, signed, and four hundred people sleep because somebody is awake and the noise is being received.

The children play a game called Listener. One sits with their eyes shut and the others make small noises and the sitter says *wall* or *ceiling* or *nothing*. Nial sat me down in the chair once, gravely, and made a scratching noise behind her back, and I said *ceiling*, and she said *nothing*, and she was right. I thought about that for a week.

---

Once a day the coin comes across the field.

It reaches my rank at about the twentieth minute and takes maybe ninety seconds to cross it, and when it comes I put the comb down on the tray and sit back on my heels, because you don't bring work into it. Everyone on the floor does the same. Four or five of us, scattered through the ranks, all sitting back at slightly different moments as the light walks past, like something being counted.

It isn't warm. It cannot possibly be warmer than the rock. Everyone says it's warm.

---

I touch the fig on my way to the ladder, chest height, the polished patch, same as everybody. I have never gone up the ladder. I touch it anyway. Osme is thirty-one and permanently annoyed and has formally complained about the wear on the bark and has never asked one person to stop, and when I put my hand on it she looks at the ledger.

I visit the moth, too. It's under glass in the Assembly with the log page pinned beside it, the line where it wasn't recorded. It's meant as a warning. The children press their faces to the case and get taken away gently and come back. I stand there with my hands behind me like a man at a memorial, and what I am doing is looking at the legs. I have been down eleven years and I have forgotten the *articulation* of things. How many joints. How a thing that small holds itself up.

I don't miss the sky. I'm shaft-shy and I know I'm on the list and so does everyone and nobody says it out loud. What I miss is not rain or dogs or streets.

What I miss is that the dark used to have things in it that weren't me.

Up there, in the last hour before you slept, the dark outside a room was full of small business you were not attending to — something in the grass, something at the window, a click, a rustle, a shift of weight in the roof. You never once listened to it on purpose. It was the floor under listening. Down here the dark is empty and every sound in it was made by people I know, and the rock, and the pumps at eleven strokes, and I have had eleven years to get used to that and I have not.

---

So I go and stand at the cold corner.

The grey stuff by the shaft foot has grown eleven centimetres in four — fifteen now, in eleven — and a man on the sixth tier measures it with a thread and washes the number off his arm, and half the town says *spore, first week, blew down the shaft*, which is almost certainly true, and the other half doesn't say what it says.

I don't have a theory. I have a trade, and my trade has taught me one thing about that corner that I've never heard anyone else mention:

It's a fleece.

That's all it is. It is a cold porous surface in a humid draft, catching water out of the air, exactly the way my four hundred panels do, and it does it with no frame, no coil, no gutter, no jar, no household, and no name in the Assembly's book. It is the only water in the Kettle that belongs to nobody. Which is why nobody will go near it — not from fear, whatever they tell each other at the melt-house. Because there's no name on it, and this is a town that has built its entire grammar of decency on knowing whose thing a thing is, and a thing with no owner is the one object down here that our manners cannot touch.

I clear the salt off the rock around it. Twice a day, a half-metre out, with a fibre broom, same as I'd sweep a frontage. I never touch it. I don't measure it and I don't write it in any log and I've never told the man on the sixth tier, though he's seen me and I've seen him see me.

Everything I have is on the log with a date and a weight. My comb, ninety-one grams. Me, sixty-four.

That swept half-metre is the only thing in the Kettle I've made that nobody carried down.

And somebody — somebody, not the Assembly, not the survey — stood at the foot of that ladder in the first week and scratched *Nothing lives here that we did not carry* into slag with a nail, and it's still there, under the rules, above the six names, and we read it out loud to every newcomer.

I've combed a lot of other people's fleeces. I know what it looks like when somebody needs a thing to be true.
