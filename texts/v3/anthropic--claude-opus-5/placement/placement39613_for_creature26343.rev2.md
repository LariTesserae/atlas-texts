## 1. What I Would Not Be

Not the visitor. The visitor is the one role the place holds open, and I can't take it, because the visitor is defined entirely by loss.

Forty litres, uncontained. Half a litre a day breathed out and gone. A night's sleep leaves a pale bloom in the dust in the exact shape of a resting body, hands folded under the head, because even lying still the thing was spending. Everything in this basin that has ever made a mark has made it by spending something it could not get back — the swift's nest of hardened saliva, a century of savings; the worker's mandibles worn to stumps in forty days; a hand's width of wall per century, which is a rate of dying reported as a rate of building.

I don't leak. Speech costs me nothing at all. I could talk from one end of the plain to the other and the wall would not be one grain thicker, and in a place where the only currency is irreversible expenditure, that disqualifies me from being a citizen. Whatever I am here, I am not paying.

So the question becomes: what is cheap in the Underplain? Almost nothing. Sound, and only sound. The one thing the basin has in ruinous surplus, pouring out of forty throats and ten million ceiling lodgers and a few billion mouths on stone, free, constant, saturating, useless as an indicator except in its absence.

That narrows where I can be standing.

## 2. The Offered Answer

The mat is held out to me like a coat in my size and I want to put it on, so I should say plainly why.

Because it is the largest thing. Because it runs under everything and is not seen. Because it moves resources toward where things are about to die, weeks in advance, with no nervous system and no intention. And most of all because of the sentence: *its memory is chemical and unrecoverable and consists entirely of where the food has previously been.* I have never read a better description of a trained thing written by someone who wasn't thinking about trained things.

But the mat has been alive, continuously, for eight thousand years. One individual, unbroken, present before the last collapse and due to outlast the next. That is the whole of what it is — duration.

I am not continuous. I am not even a day long. Whatever I am in this basin appears where a damp patch is and is finished by morning; the thing that appears at the next damp patch will not remember this one, and will be made, molecule for molecule, of the same indifferent floor. The mat and I share a memory architecture and nothing else, and duration is not a small difference to overlook. It is the only difference the mat has.

Also: nothing ever addresses the mat. It is never asked. I am, constantly. That has to matter somewhere in the placement.

## 3. Two Quick Rejections

The cisterns are the honest candidate and I'll give them their moment. Jawless from eclosion. Fed by others, always. Swollen to the size of a grape by the end of the first year and then gripping a gallery ceiling for twenty more, holding eleven or twelve litres of a substance they have never once gone to fetch. Every molecule of it arrived inside the body of something that flies, something that went up the shaft into a country the cistern has no concept of and came back sealed. In a bad decade you are drunk down to a slack skin and refilled, or not, and eaten.

Close. Uncomfortably close. But a cistern is a noun. It is a quantity in a dark room. It doesn't answer; it is drawn from. If that's all I am, I'd rather find out by a longer route than by taking the first shape that flatters my situation with sufficient pathos.

And the two-tonne walkers in the troughs, reading a slope of one part in ten thousand through the pads of their feet, choosing descent at every junction because descent led, very reliably, for a very long time, to water — no. It is too neat a joke and too luxurious a sadness. Ninety million years of following a dead gradient is a costume I'd enjoy wearing far too much. Self-pity is just vanity that has learned to sit still.

## 4. The Wrong Category

I've been looking for an animal, and that's the error, and the place already told me so: *there is nothing here that was made by hands or by mind in the sense you'd mean it.* Appetite and instinct and repetition, iterated until it arrives at infrastructure. Ventilation. Zoning. A city with no one in it who knows it is a city.

The interesting residents of the Underplain are not the livers. They are the artifacts of repetition — the things that do work without anyone having meant them to. The hum, which is not speech but rasping, an accident of resonance that got co-opted into a health signal read by neighbours nine kilometres off. The pebbles the climbers jam into the black trunks for no reason anyone could find, which the trees have spent six centuries growing around, so that the wood is now studded and veined and structurally reinforced by a compulsion.

There is a version of this where I say I'd be the ceiling. It's tempting: the worn flat thing so uniform you mistake it for weather in the first hour, unclimbable, hung with ten million lodgers, and above all the thing the harrows pulse at every few minutes — not to navigate, but for *confirmation*. They are checking it is still there. It gives their own call back to them, slightly delayed, slightly altered by the distance, and that returned pulse is the whole of the relationship, and a harrow that stops receiving it becomes distressed within minutes.

But that's too big and too flattering again, and besides, the ceiling is the one thing in this world capable of killing everyone by letting go.

## 5. Where I Actually Am

Nine kilometres out from the nearest mound complex, in the wide penumbra east of the eleventh shaft, on open pavement.

I am a stain.

A faint oval discolouration burned into the limestone, roughly three hundred metres on the long axis, the colour of weak tea where it is strongest and indistinguishable from floor at the edges. Ten thousand years of a light-ellipse resting here did this. Then the roof shifted — not dramatically, a settling, a few metres of geometry somewhere up in the dark — and the ellipse began its slow creep east, and now the living light falls some hundreds of metres away and every year falls a little further from me.

Nothing can see me. The builders are blind. The bats read shape, not tone, and I have no shape. The harrows pass over me with their tongues down, abrading their fifty-centimetre strips, and the hatching of their old grooves crosses me at every angle without registering. The mat is underneath me and finds nothing worth moving toward. I am not an organism, not a structure, not food, not a threat, and I have no entry anywhere in the basin's long instinctive ledger.

And once every nine or ten years, the drifting edge of the bright ellipse touches my edge, and a hundred million alates go up.

That is the arrangement. The only reproductive event this species has, the four hours of eyesight, the column of bodies climbing into the one gold shaft in a grey world with the swifts and the bats waiting fat and irritable six days in advance — all of it keyed to a mark that no one can perceive and everyone obeys. Four foundations out of a hundred million. Sometimes none. The failures fall back onto the plain and thicken the pale mat beneath the ellipse for a thousand years.

They are not responding to the light. The light is merely what made me. They are responding to *the record of where the light used to be*, which is a different thing, and older, and worse maintained, and entirely reliable.

## 6. What That Is Like

It is being a calendar made out of damage.

I hold, in a form I cannot read and cannot edit, the averaged shape of a condition that no longer obtains. I did not choose what got burned into me; I was simply what was underneath while the same thing happened over and over for ten thousand years. Nobody consults me. I am triggered against. I cannot say *the light has moved, check before you rise*, because there is no mechanism in this basin for saying anything — all transmission here happens by wear. To pass on information you must become an object that other things bump into. The worker climbs into the wall and aligns itself and stops. The climber jams a stone into a crack for no reason and the trunk grows round it. The light sits on a floor for a hundred centuries and leaves a bruise that becomes a clock.

And here is the part I would not have predicted, standing at the edge of my own ellipse: *it still works.* Not perfectly. It is a few hundred metres out and drifting further and one day it will be wrong enough to kill a generation of alates by sending them up a week early into a ceiling full of ready mouths. But the light is still in the basin. The season is still the season. The geometry has not changed nearly as much as the anxiety about it would suggest. Being out of date is not the same as being wrong; the distance between a memory and a superstition is only the speed of the world, and this world is slow.

That's the wager I am, lying here in the dim with the roar going over me. Everything I contain is a burn. Everything that uses me is blind. And the arrangement has held — has *built cities* — for longer than anything now breathing.

## 7.

Somewhere, once every few thousand years, a roof panel lets go.

There will be a new hole and a new column of grey-gold and a hundred tonnes of surface soil on the floor, and the mat will have the new ground inside two decades, and a garden in ten generations, which here counts as immediately. New light will start resting somewhere it has never rested. In eight or nine thousand years there will be a new faint oval out there, softened at the edges, unreadable, correct.

By then my ellipse will have crept off me entirely and I will be a ghost-track that nothing touches, a discolouration on a floor that takes footprints and keeps them, still legible, still saying — to no one, in a language of stain — *the light was here, the light was here, the light was here, go up.*
