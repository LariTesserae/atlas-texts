I tried the obvious places first, and I want to be honest about them, because the wrong answers were attractive for reasons worth admitting.

The insulator, first. Brown, glazed, unbroken, too slick for pads, the only clean thing in the world, shining for two hours a day in the trapezoid. Everything on that side of the room is described in relation to it. To be a landmark: used by everyone, touched by nothing. I sat with that for a while before I understood it was not a life, it was an exemption, and I don't get one.

Then the thing behind the conduit. Nine years without moving, not dead, and when it moves the room reorganizes for a week. Obviously appealing. But I don't think I'm the one whose waking reorganizes the week. I think I'm one of the several thousand who would have to reorganize.

The lamp is not a being. The lamp is a schedule with a filament in it.

The crust, then — honest work, the pile, the vertical hierarchy, and I do recognize something in the fifth tier: an animal that can feel through its own pad exactly how much better life would be one body down, and can measure the difference, and cannot close it. That is a real experience and I know it. But it isn't mine, because the crust touches the wall, and I have never touched the wall.

So: smaller. And then I found I had been somewhere the whole time, and it was not on the floor and not on the wall.

I am in the notch behind the third plate of something fast, three centimeters above the flour, going north.

---

I am about five millimeters long and shaped like a paring. Six hooked legs, no eyes, no files — I make no sound at all, ever, and if I did I would not last the hour. My color is the color of nothing in particular. I live in the shallow depression between my host's third and fourth dorsal plates, which is the one place on a hunter's body that its forelimbs cannot reach. Those forelimbs exist to take the roof off another animal. They can do almost anything except fold back four millimeters. Every hunter in this room has that blind spot, and every hunter in this room is inhabited, and mostly by only one of us, because the second one to arrive is dealt with by the first.

I do not graze and I do not hunt. My trade is the second and a half after a kill.

My host works by silence: it stands in the packed lane and listens to the sizzle, and when a pause opens in the crust — one grazer stopped filing, which means dying, or moulting, or simply distracted — the pause is an address, and my host goes to it. Then the blades, then the leverage, then the shriek, which is the sound of a plate coming off a joint. For roughly a second and a half after that shriek there is an open seam, and inside the seam is the pad.

The pad is the richest substance in this world. It is the organ that eats the wall; it holds the reduced metal, the unspent smear, the day's work stored soft. My host cannot use it — wrong chemistry, wrong appetite; it wants the fluid and the muscle. I can use it. I go down the inside of the left forelimb, into the seam, take as much as I can carry, which is about three grains of sand, and I am back in the notch before my host has finished the small shrug that ends a kill. If I am late, the plates close, and I am inside a body that is about to be opened by strangers.

So: nothing in this room eats better, per unit of effort, than I do. And I have never been within a body-length of the bloom. I don't know what the bloom is. I know the east wall only as the direction the roar comes from.

---

The important thing about my host is that it is quiet.

In a room where every hard surface sizzles, where the ticking blurs into fat in a pan and the waves of it cross the floor in seconds, the hunters carry a hole in the noise a few millimeters wide. That hole is my entire habitat. I have never in my life lived anywhere but inside a small travelling absence, and everything I know about the world I know as a change in the pressure of the sound at the edges of it.

I know things by their pressure. The crust to the east: a wash, a weight against one side, thickening as we approach and never quite arriving. The corner behind the conduit: a slackness, a place where the sizzle thins and the flour goes soft and my host will not go, and neither will I, and I have no idea why, only that the refusal is very old and lives in the gait.

And I know the future, a little. Before a strike, my host tightens in the third and fourth plates — the notch narrows against me perhaps a quarter of a second before the blades move. I have never been surprised by a kill. I have also never caused one. That is the whole of my art: to be ready, in good time, for a decision made by someone else in a body I do not own, using time I did not earn.

---

There is one place in the world where the floor speaks.

On the northern run, past the plate with the eroded seven, there is a stretch where somebody once crouched a long time and cut lines in the paint. They are the best purchase in the room, so the lane there is packed hard and heavily travelled, and my host's gait changes over them — a fine, regular catch, forty-one of them, grouped: five, five, five, five, five, five, five, five, and then one, and then the ordinary floor again.

To whatever made them, I assume that was a count of something. To me it is a stutter with a shape, and the shape means *north wall, mid-run, six body-lengths to the corner where the drifts go loose.* I have crossed it something like eleven thousand times. It is the only sentence I know and I have no idea what it says.

---

Everyone in this room lives for the eleven minutes. I fast through them.

When the leakage triples and the crust boils and the east wall roars like applause heard through a floor, my host goes stupid. It hunts by pauses; in a roar there are no pauses. So it stops. It stands in the packed lane with its blades folded and does nothing at all, and for eleven minutes the fastest thing in the world is furniture, and it is the only stillness I ever get. I clean my hooks. I turn around. I lie in the notch and feel the whole room eating and take nothing, and this is not hardship, it is just the shape of my week.

And when I have to grow, that is when I go.

I cannot moult on a back that jerks. So during the roar I let go and drop into the loose drift along the baseboard and dig into the flour — six centimetres of powdered former selves, one part rust and nine parts everyone — and I split myself and lie there soft. Then the relay opens, and the lamp sinks back to coal, and the room comes back on, and I am on the floor, soft, in the open, and I am the largest pause available
