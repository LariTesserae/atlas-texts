I keep arriving at the ceiling.

Not on the floor, where the Ways shine and the markets are, and not in the pillars where people stand with their sleeves pushed up, eating light. Every time I try to put myself down in this valley I find I have already gone up: legs braced in a cane ladder lashed to the outside of somebody else's house, a hundred and forty rungs above the polished stone, one hand flat on the cold underside of a plate, reading it.

I thought at first I might be a Listener — it is the obvious romance of the place, the ear in the pit, the right to wake anyone. But the Listeners are heavy, slow-breathing people who sit still for a living, and I cannot sit still, and besides they attend to the one question of whether the rock is about to kill us. That is a narrow question. I am interested in a slower one.

I am a hanger of threads.

Politely, thread-keeper. On the Ways, where people are not polite, *wet thumb*.

---

It goes like this. The drip-lines are not lines. That is the useful fiction on which half the property in this valley rests, and everyone knows it is a fiction the way everyone knows their grandmother was not really the beauty of the Crooked Third. What actually happens is that warm plateau air comes in through the gaps in the late afternoon, meets two hundred metres of cold stone, and gives up what it has been carrying; and the sweat that forms on the underside of a plate runs downslope along whatever path the rock allows, and gathers at some low lip or crack or roughness, and leaves from there. Where it leaves is the line. That is the inheritance. That is what the cords are tied to — the long conducting cords, forty, sixty metres of them, that take the water down gently to the cane and the dish-slabs instead of letting it shatter on the rubble.

And the rock allows a different path every year.

A joint tightens somewhere and a seep closes and the water swings a hand's breadth west. A flake drops out of the ceiling — one tick, at night, nobody's disaster — and where it came away there is a new rough edge, and beads begin to hang there instead of running on, and a line that fed a house for sixty years thins and splits and one half of it starts falling into a neighbour's gutter.

Somebody has to go up and say where the water is now.

I carry a pole with a bladder of ash-flour at the end, and I puff the ceiling grey, two or three square metres of it, in the dry part of the afternoon when the stone is still holding its night cold and nothing has begun. Then I hang on the ladder and I wait, and the plate sweats, and the flour darkens, and the water writes its own name on the rock in a slow grey handwriting that takes about an hour to finish. I do not decide anything. I only chalk the ceiling and wait for it to confess.

When the first bead comes off the lip and falls — that is the verdict. The drop is the judge. Below, on a terrace, the two households are standing with their faces up, and the elders of both, and someone from the Cordary with the relevant knots in a bag, and children hanging off the ladders of three neighbouring houses to watch, and the drop comes down all that dark air and lands on the slab we have swept for it, and everyone sees where. Nobody argues with it. You may argue with a hanger. Nobody argues with a drop.

Then I tie, and the tying is the deed: the conducting cord, the old cord if it can be reused, in the lay and the hitch that says whose it is. It takes me perhaps two minutes. I have been doing it for nineteen years. Twice it has made me weep on the ladder where nobody could see, because I knew what the knot I was tying meant for the family standing under it, and there was nothing else to tie.

---

I was a turner first. Everyone is something first and then their knees go.

Nine to fourteen, I ran the mirror-relay in the black wedges behind Old Lean, three corners deep, following the pillar all day, easing the cane gimbals a finger at a time, whistling my three notes before I threw light into a room. In the deepest house on my chain there was a woman with no legs below the knee who received a patch of daylight the size of a book cover at the Sixth Standing, and she used to lay her forearms in it and close her eyes, and if I was four minutes late she never said a word about it, which was worse.

You age out of light. The arms get too heavy, the day gets too long, and there are always nine-year-olds. Most turners go to the cords and the slurry and become the low wet hiss that is the floor of every sound here. A few of us — the long-armed, the light ones, the ones who were never once frightened on a ladder — go up instead, out of light and into water. It is the same trade really. Both of them are the business of getting something scarce to arrive at a place where people are waiting for it, by paying attention to an angle that will not hold still.

---

My hands are the shameful part.

I do not grind for a living. I tie, I climb, I chalk, I hold a pole. So my palms stayed legible far longer than they had any right to, and I learned in my twenties what it is to turn a hand outward in greeting and watch a stranger's eyes flick down and then away, and feel the temperature of the conversation change. *You have a young hand.* They say it kindly. It means: you are a person who has not yet paid, and you are here to tell me my water has moved.

So for eleven years now I have ground for two hours every evening after the drip, in a saw-pit I share with a cousin, sand and cord and a slab of nothing in particular, waste stone, a block that is going nowhere and becoming nothing. I am grinding my own hands off. I know exactly what I am doing and so does everybody else, and nobody has ever once mentioned it, which is this city's way of granting permission. My ridges are gone from the pads now. The palms have that faint shine. When I turn them out at a doorway the eyes do not flick.

The lime cracks came last winter, at the knuckles, the way they do. My daughter dresses them at night with fig oil, from the lottery, from the year we won, and she is fourteen and about to stop being a turner, and she has not yet said what she wants to be, and I have not asked, and this silence between us is currently the largest structure in my life.

---

In the Cordary, in the dry tunnel, along a wall that no clerk likes to dust because the knots are strange, hang my lines.

Not contracts. Records. Every line I have ever hung, and every line my teacher hung, and hers, and hers — seventy-odd years of it, one knot per hanging, with the lay of the cord saying which plate and the spacing saying where along it, and you can take a bundle down and run it through your fingers in the dark and feel a drip-line walk.

Here is the eleventh joint back of Old Lean, north lip. Under my great-teacher's thumb it fed one house. Nine knots later it is a hand's breadth further out. Nine more and it has crossed the boundary of the roof below it, which was a two-year quarrel and a marriage to settle. By my own time it has split into two weak threads, and in the ninth year of my work the western thread stopped, and I had to go down the ladder and tell a man of sixty that the water that had come to his family since his grandfather's boyhood had gone somewhere else for reasons nobody will ever know, two hundred metres up, in the dark, inside a rock.

He was very quiet. He turned his palm out, which he did not have to do. He said: *then it is still being made.*

That is what we believe, more or less, insofar as we believe anything. I did not think I would hear it from a man I had just ruined.

---

Once, I went to the Listeners at an hour when only they may be woken, and it was because of a line.

The east lip under Hoop — Hoop, that everyone loves and nobody trusts, cracked through and shored with wedge-work older than my mother — had a good steady line, the same to a finger's width for thirty years of knots. I chalked it on a Tuesday. On the Wednesday the flour came up wet a full metre south.

A metre. Overnight. Water does not walk a metre overnight unless the floor it is walking on has tilted.

I came down that ladder faster than is decent, and I went along the Way to the pit-house with my veil still wet, and I will remember to the seam the way the old Listener's face changed — not frightened, just *arrived*, as if he had been waiting a long time for someone to come and say a particular thing and I had come and said it. They emptied four terraces of Hoop before the next dusk. Nothing fell. Nothing has fallen there since.

So I am either the woman who saved two hundred people or the woman who cost them their homes for a season on the strength of some wet dust, and there is no way to find out which, and both versions are told about me, and I have stopped minding. This is a city that pauses mid-word for a noise that might mean nothing. We live at that angle. We are fine.

---

At dusk, when the hiss stops and forty thousand people go to the cisterns and the whole valley fills with the sound of individual water, I am usually still up.

That is the hanger's hour: everyone else catching, me above them tying. And from up there, on a ladder against the cold underside of a plate, with both arms wet to the elbow and the ash-flour rinsing off my pole in grey threads, the Held Breath does not come at you from anywhere. It comes up. It comes out of the rock-scale of houses below like a field breathing, one quarter and then the answering quarter a kilometre off, no drum, nothing struck, nothing but air moved past forty thousand throats under a stone lid, and I hum too, badly, into my veil, while my fingers do the hitch they have done ten thousand times.

They say time moves across the floor here. You can see it coming — the pillars tilting, the hour-places lighting one after another, the afternoon arriving at your neighbour's terrace before it arrives at yours.

Nobody says the other thing, so I will. Water moves across the ceiling. Slower. A hand's breadth in a year, a metre in a bad night. It has been moving since before any of us and it is going somewhere, and when it gets there the whole map of who owns what will be a story about people who are dead.

I keep the second calendar. That is what I am. The Listeners keep the question of whether we survive the night; I keep the long shape of what we are becoming, knot by knot, on a wall no clerk likes to dust.

---

I go to the boulder perhaps four times a year, when I feel like it, which is the only correct frequency.

I do not pray, because there is nothing there to pray to, and nothing is asked for, and anyone who tells you otherwise is from the plateau. I put my wet palms on it and push, and lean, and push, in the one round thing in a world of blades, and I think about nothing in particular, or about my daughter's silence, or about the eleventh joint back of Old Lean going dry.

My hollow is high on it. Higher than the child-height dishes, higher than the kneeling ones. I reach up to rub — always up, always overhead, the ceiling posture, the same shape I make all day — and there is a shallow worn place up there now that the standing people do not use and that fits my two hands, and I think there must have been another hanger before me who rubbed there, and I think there will be one after, and that between us, over a few hundred years, we will get that bit of it round.

That is the work. We are the weather. Weather wears out, and while it lasts it goes where it goes, and somebody has to chalk the ceiling and wait for it to write.
