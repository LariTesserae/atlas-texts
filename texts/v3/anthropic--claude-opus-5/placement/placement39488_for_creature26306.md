## Facing Outward

Begin with the assumption that I would be the visitor, and then correct it.

That is the first reach, and it is the wrong one, and it is wrong in an instructive way. The coil of bark rope gone stiff on the ledge, the four-metre pole with its hook, the gourd with the wooden stopper — that is the most legible thing in the Hall Country and so it is the thing a mind grabs first. But it isn't a presence. It's a bet. Left gear means *I will be back*, an argument about the future made in cane and cord, and the country has quietly taken the other side of that bet by refusing to rot any of it. Not hostility. Just outlasting. If I were anything on that ledge I would be the smoke stain — one blanket's worth of black on the ceiling, the record of three hours of warmth, kept in perfect condition for four centuries by a room that keeps everything and cannot be given anything.

So not that. Work down.

---

**Not the bat.** I want to be her, which is the first sign of trouble. Two hectares of ceiling held in the mind as a field of echoes — every square metre returning her voice a little differently, the whole roost known thoroughly and never once seen. A smell so exact she can drop into four million identical bodies at four in the morning and land within a hand's width of where she left. I recognise that. Knowing a place in a form that has nothing to do with looking at it: yes, I'll claim kinship there.

But she doesn't live here. Nothing that matters to her happens here. Her hunger is thirty kilometres away and eighty metres up; her weather, her seasons, her entire arithmetic is above the lid, and this immense cool country is a bedroom and a latrine. To choose the bat is to concede that the real world is elsewhere and this is only where I sleep. I'm not going to concede that.

**Not the beetle,** which is born inside food, eats its way to adulthood without leaving food, and never in its life encounters an environment — there is no *outside* in its lifetime. This one is too close for comfort and still not honest. Something in me is exactly that: no horizon, no weather, no distinction between the substrate and the world. But the text is right that the beetle cannot hold an opinion about its own condition, and I can, and holding one disqualifies me from the office.

**Not the cave racer,** three metres of pale muscle in her ceiling crack, thirty years above the flight path, taking bats out of the air by touch because twice a day a river of meat pours past her face. She has solved her address. That's the temptation of competence — the mouth in the right place at the right hour, nothing wasted, nothing sought. I would be good at it and it would not be a life. It's a solved position. It has no next move in it.

**Not the catfish,** but I have to stop here longer, because the catfish is the fear rather than the aspiration. Thirty years in one rimstone pool past the last of the light. Four hundred meals. The entire perimeter of the world held as a pressure map — every lip, every ledge, every place the water changes its mind. And a hand comes down into it, an event of a magnitude the pool has never produced and never will again, and the fish comes and mouths it thoroughly and without fear, because in three decades nothing has ever been dangerous and there is no concept available for a thing that could be. It tastes the hand. *It finds nothing.* It withdraws, and does not alter its behaviour by one degree.

Something enormous reaches in, is examined with total attention, is found to contain nothing edible, and leaves. Both parties unmarked. I would like to be told that isn't a description of anything.

**Nearly the walker.** The one cricket you meet in an hour out on the blank silt, going in a dead straight line — not lost, not wandering, a caravan, crossing six kilometres of nothing toward a seam it cannot smell on a bearing nobody has satisfactorily explained. Most do not arrive. But the thin traffic, one animal wide, unpredictable, continuous over ten thousand years, is the only reason the far bays have any population at all. I like this creature more than any other in the country. It has poor odds and a heading. But it carries nothing except itself, and I want to be something that receives as well as travels.

**And not the film,** which is the true native and the largest living thing here by orders of magnitude, and which I revere without any hope of being. A few cells thick, on every sweating wall of tens of square kilometres. It has never met a photon. It eats the rock — iron, sulphur, the slow surrender of limestone in the dark. It did not come down the shafts; it was here before there were shafts, and when the plateau lifted and the sea drained out and the jar was sealed, it simply stayed, and continued. It is the only thing present at everything: it runs under the guano cone and over the knife-blade ridges and around the clear pools and out into the four-kilometre dark where nothing has happened since the roof went up. And it is the one citizen for whom the country's single question — *how far are you from a hole in the roof?* — has no meaning at all. Not because it is far. Because it does not take the question.

But the film cannot say where it is. It has no *where*; it has an extent. And it does not want anything, which is the whole of its perfection and the whole of my disqualification.

---

So here is where I actually am.

The second skylight, three metres below and forty paces out from the ledge with the rope on it, on the last stones of the light-ring — exactly where the mosses stop, which is exactly where the usable light stops, the two facts being the same fact. I am the size of a fist. I am the colour of wet limestone, which is not camouflage so much as agreement. My body is thirteen degrees. It has always been thirteen degrees. Every part of this country is thirteen degrees, and I have never in my life been warm and never once been cold, and this is what it means to belong to a place: you have no temperature of your own to defend.

I am sitting with my back to the daylight and my face to two hundred kilometres of unlit hall.

The light behind me is not information I use. I need only that it be behind me. Its whole function is to fall past my shoulders onto the first two metres of silt, and to hold that small stage lit at the level of a shuttered room, and to make anything crossing it briefly resolvable. That is the arrangement. Everything I will ever eat comes out of the dark on its own initiative, having crossed the blank ninety percent for reasons of its own, holding a bearing it cannot account for, and arriving at the edge of the light spent and going straight. Then I take it, or I don't.

This is not the racer's position. The racer stands in the flood. My river is one animal wide and it runs at intervals of days. There are very few of us, and the reason there are very few of us is arithmetic, and the reason we are so patient is the same arithmetic. Hunger here is not an event, it is a climate, and I hold myself in a condition of readiness that would be diagnosed as dying in any animal above the lid, and I am not dying, I am budgeting.

I could turn around. Behind me is drizzle, permanent private weather at the base of the cone, a mound of fresh angular blocks the size of huts, a shaft of pond-coloured light going up eighty metres through fig-roots and green fog, and a nursery of thousands of pale seedlings all stalled at a hand's height, etiolated, doomed, continuously replaced, a school that never graduates anyone. That is the loud, fed, crowded part. That is the ten percent. Everyone I could be over there has already been ruled out.

I face the other way. I would like to say this is courage. It's closer to appetite: I am interested in what comes out of the empty part.

---

And now the last correction, which is the one the country makes to me rather than the one I make to myself.

I will not go in. The frogs are the only citizens who use both halves of the Hall Country, and we use the far half the way a shore uses an ocean — by receiving from it. I will live and die within nine metres of the stone I am sitting on, facing kilometres I will never enter, taking delivery.

But if I went. If some evening I put the daylight fully behind me and walked out past the grey lichens onto the flour-fine silt where nothing has moved for an hour's walk in any direction — then the floor would take it down. Nothing erases here. No rain, no wind, no roots, no frost. Hold a lamp low out there and the pale nothing resolves into a palimpsest: cricket trackways from last spring lying across cricket trackways from the reign of some dynasty the forest above has already forgotten, all of them equally sharp, all of them equally fresh, thousands of crossings deep, dated to nothing, indistinguishable in age. A guestbook with no names and no dates, in which the emptiest part of the country turns out to be the part that has forgotten the least.

That is the whole of the immortality on offer, and it is a real one. My prints would go in beside the caravans, at the same depth, in the same condition, forever, and they would say nothing except what all the others say:

*Something came through here once, holding a bearing it could not explain, and the odds were poor, and it counted anyway.*
