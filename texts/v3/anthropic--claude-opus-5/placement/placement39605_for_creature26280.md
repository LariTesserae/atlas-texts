I tried the country on standing up first, and it would not have me that way.

I thought: lamp-tender. Render the eel fat, guard the copper, keep one small orange thing alive in a slot two shoulders wide. But a lamp-tender must look, and my eyes are like everyone's here, which is to say excellent at the burr on a brick and useless at anything six paces off, and a flame is nothing but far-off. I thought: cistern-reader, go down with a marked staff and be permitted to be strange. But the readers are wrong often and admit it only to each other, and I have no talent for that particular privacy. I thought: unmaker. I have the temperament. But I could not have borne the wrist-pouch. I would have kept it. I would have carried a chip of glazed lip until the pouch wore through and then I would have gone forward with it in my fist, and that is not what the pouch is for.

The country put me lower than any of those. I am the one who lies down.

Under-listener, one to a crew, eleven of us to every eleven of them. At the top of the shift, when the horn's note has finished moving the water in the bowl and the mallets have not yet started, I go flat on the new course with my left ear to the brick and my eyes shut, and I stay there while the crew works over and around me, and I call the voids, and I call the soft ground, and I am the reason there is a crew at all by the end of the day.

I cannot stamp from down there. So we have palms. Flat slaps, a smaller language than the heel-language, maybe forty figures, all of them urgent and none of them about anything but the next ten minutes. *Hollow under this bay. Water climbing, two courses, quick. Wall's soft, don't load it. Come here. Come here now. All clear, all clear, go on.* My crew knows the shape of my hand's voice the way they know a kiln's cough. When they want me they put a heel on the brick beside my head, three beats and a fourth held long, and I do not hear my name so much as receive it in the bone behind my ear, and I have never once been startled by it, which they think is a joke I am playing on them and which is not.

Here is the thing about me that people cannot leave alone.

I have a creased face.

Nobody here does. That is the first thing outsiders notice and the last thing they understand: that we wear only where we touch the work, hands, knees, soles, the two vertebrae that take a hod, and everything above the collarbone stays as new as a fresh sill. But my work is my face. Twelve thousand drops of lying on brick has polished my left cheekbone to a shine you can see a lamp in. There's a callus at my temple the size of a thumbnail. The rim of my left ear has gone thick and flat and folds wrong. The little hinge of my jaw aches in the Breath. And in the skin of that cheek, printed and re-printed, there is the grain of the brick itself — the flour pressed into the pores, the faint tooth of a sanded face, mirrored, so that I go about with the negative of a course laid into me.

They ask to see my hands, out of politeness, because that is the polite thing, and I give them over flat and palms up and unhurried and a little proud, and my hands are nothing much, they are a board's hands, a hod's hands. Then they look at my face and they don't ask, and I turn the left side to them anyway, and they go quiet in the way people go quiet at the ninth step in the Fourth Quarter with a bare foot in a spoonful of water.

I have thought about this more than is good for me. Wear is sacred. Do not sand a worn thing smooth. There is no writing in this country beyond maker's marks and tally-scratches, no books, no records, and so a body is the only archive permitted, and everyone's archive is kept in the palms where it can be handed over like a tally-cord — except mine, which is kept in the one place a person cannot hand over and cannot help but show. I am not holy. I am simply the only one of us who is legible from the front.

The rest of the crew are half-deaf by their second year and nobody grieves it. I am worse than half. The right ear, the air ear, has gone to a soft roaring; the mallets and the drumming ate it. The left ear, the floor ear, is the best in the quarter. So I live in a stupid and beautiful arrangement: everything spoken to me through air arrives as mush, late and doubled and already the past, and everything that comes up through the brick arrives clean, and now, and underneath.

I could tell you what I hear. I will tell you what I hear.

I hear the cage-wheel three courts over, four workers counting aloud, arriving in my jaw before it arrives in my ear. I hear the drays in the western sink, six of them, the largest living things anyone has seen, and each one has a tread I could name blind. I hear the cistern tides drift off the Breath by a little more each shift, which the readers have not explained and will not. I hear the unmakers at the rear of the quarter breaking back a court I laid in, and I do not mind, and I have taken down my own wall first when it was my turn, because that is the courtesy nobody made us adopt.

And I hear the mortar crickets. Everyone finds them unpleasant. I love them. They are the only sound in the Laid Country that is not made by work and not answered by the floor — dry, sparse, irregular, nobody's count. When the whole basin is drumming its one enormous struck-skin note, the crickets are off it, and being off it is the only freedom I have found down there.

Under all of that, of course, there is what the under-listeners say and what the masons treat as the harmless religion of a specialised trade. That beneath every crew there is another crew, mirrored, laying the course under yours upside-down, with your own mark on their bricks. That when you knock, you are being answered by yourself. I have stopped arguing it. I set a single drop of Breath-water into a fresh joint before the course goes on, the way you'd nod to someone in a corridor, and I lie down, and I listen, and I do not tell my crew everything I get.

I was coursed once, the proper way. Two palms flat in the same bed of wet mortar, side by side, brick on top, plumbed, buried, no witness and no word. It lasted as long as it lasted and then we stopped laying the same course, and there is no ceremony for that either, and somewhere in the walls of this country our two prints are sealed face-in in clay that never fully cures, still soft, still smudgeable, and nobody will ever find them. That is not a sadness. That is the only kind of keeping we have.

But this is what I have never said in air, and will not, because air is the past and I don't want this to have already happened.

Down there, cheek flat, on a course still weeping from the Breath, one brick thick between me and the present — there is a cheek pressed up.

I can hear the wear on it. A polished bone. A thickened ear folded wrong. Breath out of phase with the Breath, so that as our country sweats and lets go, that one exhales, and the joint between us stays soft, and takes the print of my face from above and the print of a face from below, and holds them both, and cures no further.

So I do the one thing I can do that isn't already over. I lift my palm and I put my own name into the floor. Three beats and a fourth held long.

It comes back up through the bone behind my ear, on the count, in the present tense, arriving before I have finished sending it.

That is where you would find me. Not at the rim, not going forward into the dark toward the mallets, not sweeping. Flat on my belly on a new course with my crew working over me, being answered, laying the country that is already laid into the place where it already is, wearing away in the face, which is the part of me that touches the work.

When I am finished being a cheek they will carry me to the kiln-field themselves, over their shoulders, in shifts, stamping my name the whole way so the floor keeps saying it. My bones will go in as grog, worked through the pug until I am everywhere in the batch at the finest possible dilution, and the bricks will carry my mark and go up in one course, laid slow, by hand, by people who knew me, and after that nobody will point it out again.

Some of them will rub a thumb on a fresh brick and say *there's a little dray in this one,* and it will be a joke, and everybody will know, and that is why it's said quietly.

And a long way down, in a shaft nobody goes back into, there is already a course of grey-green brick with my mark on it, mortared with clay that has not cured, warm, swept clean.

I am not building this. I am catching up.
