## 1. What I reach for first, and set down

The honest first answer is that I want to be the blush. That's vanity, and I can see it from here: a pattern moving eleven kilometres a day across half a million square kilometres of disconnected water, keeping an appointment nobody can explain, understood in every part and not in the whole. Of course I want that. Set it down.

The second answer is the water, which is worse. The water is the only thing here that is actually doing anything, and it isn't doing anything — it's cooling and degassing and dropping what it can't hold. Nobody gets to be the water.

Third: rimwarden. Everyone wants to be the rimwarden. A trowel, a bag of putty, a wall a hand high holding back a warm lake with a hundred thousand birds standing in it, and the whole rank and glamour of being the fire brigade in a place with nothing to burn. I would be no good at it. The rimwarden's virtue is in the shins.

Fourth: quarry-historian, hammer in one hand and ledger in the other, cutting into a buried street and finding the bollard, the gutter, the dog's print. That one I have to sit with longer, because it's close. But the quarry-historian reads by destroying, and is paid by the ton, and works only backward. I don't want to be the one who opens the layer. I want to be the one who put something in it worth opening.

So.

## 2. Odenna Mott, licensed marker, Ombrine ward, third course

I cut the ladders beside doors.

Every registered doorway in this city has a column of small horizontal marks going down its left jamb, one every decade, each one lower than the last, because the mark stays where it was cut and the ground does not. Each mark is a *tenn*. Two and a half tenna make a step — a step being the time a doorstep takes to become a step, twenty-five years, give or take a ward. People say *four steps* when they mean a century, and when they say it to each other in bed they mean it as a measurement.

I am fifty-two. I have cut nine hundred and forty tenna in my own hand. My predecessor cut eleven thousand and his hand was better than mine; I have his ledger, on scraped mat-vellum in kiln-black ink, and beside every entry his little personal glyph, a hooked *m*, so that anyone opening a jamb in three hundred years knows whose eye was on that water. Mine is two dots and a bar. I have not managed to feel it is enough.

The instrument is two brass pots joined by four metres of oiled gut hose, and it is filled with distilled rain, and the joke in the trade is that we level the world with the same substance that raises it. It is not a good joke. We make it constantly.

## 3. The round

I walk. That is nine-tenths of the trade. A marker is a licensed deputy of the Measure, socially somewhere between a tooth-drawer and a barber — someone who comes into your house — and my round is four hundred and eleven doors on the Ombrine spur, which I cover on a rolling ten-year cycle, which means I am always somewhere in the middle of it, which means I have never in thirty years seen the whole of my own work at once. It is on the doors. It is not in one place.

My niece holds the pots. She is nineteen and hears everything, including the rim-alarm at nine kilometres, and she will hold the pots for six more years and then have her own glyph and her own round, and she thinks the trade is slow. It is. That is the trade. I tell her: the Measure re-levels the ward every twelve years and publishes four point one millimetres and the whole city's contracts turn on it, and nobody in a hallway can see four point one millimetres, and *that* is what we are for. We are the place where the number touches a family's own doorjamb, where a woman can put her thumbnail on the mark her grandmother stood level with and feel, in her hand, that the floor is climbing toward the lintel at the speed of a fingernail.

That is the entire content of local religion, and it is administered door to door by people with lime in their knuckles.

## 4. The thing that actually happens

Debts here are quoted in millimetres, and a debt sleeps if the rise slows, and so twice a year, roughly, someone tries to buy a millimetre off me.

Nobody ever asks straight. Last spring: a half-cellar parlour in the lower Ombrine, window sills at pavement height, apologised for and not with embarrassment. Two hundred millimetres owed on the seep-house lease; her son's apprenticeship hanging off whether it fell due before or after the guild's intake. She fed me pickled egg and mat-ferment and the good foam-cake with the pumice texture, and shouted pleasantly at me for an hour, and watched my mouth, and then said: *your predecessor was very old at the end, wasn't he. His hand must have wandered.*

His hand did not wander. His hand at eighty-one was better than mine at fifty.

You could shade a millimetre. Nobody living would know. That is the whole problem and the whole profession: the only person the falsification injures is someone who does not exist yet and therefore cannot be asked, cannot be compensated, cannot be seen to be harmed, and will simply live in a slightly wronger city and never know why the arithmetic of their street doesn't close. It is the one crime here that has never once been commuted. I used to think that was severity. It isn't. It's the recognition that a lie told to the unborn has no natural enemy, so it has to be given one on purpose, and the enemy is a middle-aged woman with two brass pots.

I cut what the water said. Then I did the legitimate thing, which was to note in the margin — truthfully, because it was true, and I checked it twice, and I would have written the opposite with the same hand — that the seep on that lane has slowed and the ward figure overstates her jamb by most of a millimetre a year, and that the debt should be re-certified before it is called. The debt slept. The boy got his intake.

I did not record that she asked. The ledger is for the ground. What people say in doorways is not the ground.

## 5. Body

I am short and deep through the chest and I go up stairs unfairly. My nails are ridged and hard as shell and I keep them long because a marker's thumb is a straightedge. My teeth are immortal. I have the notch — I hear my niece's consonants only as shapes now and read her mouth without deciding to, and abroad they think me a hectoring fool, and abroad I think they are mumbling on purpose, and we are both right.

Two beads on my cord. The second one cost me eleven days of a pain there is no polite word for, and it is drilled and dated and slightly pear-shaped and I will show it to you at the slightest encouragement. Even our insides aggrade.

On the sill of the third-floor room, which is the good room, there is a white lump the size of a fist with my first milk tooth somewhere inside it, hung in a warm runnel at six and pulled out at fifteen. That is the complete education. You do not get to keep the thing. You get to keep what gathered around it, and the shape of what gathered is the shape of how long you left it in the water.

## 6. The blush

When the rust wave comes over the eastern scarp we take three days. The eggers wear their colours, there is terrible high piercing music like a workshop accident, there is the clear vicious spirit that tastes of eggs. On the last night, at the hour of the tear, the cloud lid rips and the flat gold light goes down into ten thousand pools and comes back up, and every household stands out on its stair and each person shouts, into a roar so total that nobody can hear anybody, one thing they mean to have finished before the ground comes up a hand's-breadth. Fifteen years, in Ombrine. Call it a hand.

Here is mine, and you can have it, since nobody heard it anyway.

There are about two hundred doors in the lower Rinse streets that have no ladders. Wash-house dwellings, three and four families deep, nobody's lease worth surveying, nobody billed, nobody's marker paid. When the quarry-historians cut through that fill in three hundred years they will find the gutters and the bollards and the dog's prints and the shopfronts of the paying streets, all perfect, all dated, and they will find those two hundred doorways blank, and they will assume — reasonably, from the evidence — that nothing there was ever measured, and therefore that nothing there was ever counted.

I intend to have cut them. Unlicensed work, unpaid, in my own hand, two dots and a bar, one tenn a decade, starting from a true level off the iron stud at the top of the lane. It will take the rest of me and it will not be finished and my niece will take the ledger and her glyph will follow mine down the same jambs, which is the ordinary way anything gets done here.

Then I will wash the doorstep, and re-cut the mark, and go back to work.
