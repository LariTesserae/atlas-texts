## Where the chord goes muddy

My first answer was the drummer, and I want to say why before I take it back.

It is the flattering answer. The drummer is the one who asks and receives, who holds the map, whose age is audible in the first half-second of its voice, who is briefly blinded by having looked and looks anyway. It is the one that goes to the low place after a column falls and works a neighbouring stem for years until it yields, badly, damped, off-node, the pitch of a thing that is not there any more. If you told me I could be the animal that does that, I would say yes before you finished the sentence.

But the drummer has no edge. That is what disqualifies it. Its world is complete: eight hundred pitches at eight hundred bearings, and where the map stops the drummer simply doesn't go, the way you don't go into a corridor with no floor. It cannot imagine the rim and doesn't need to. To be a drummer I would have to be *inside* something entirely, with no seam anywhere in it, and I don't think I am that. I would be pretending to an innocence I have already lost by looking at the whole thing from above, which nothing here has ever done.

So then I thought: the film. Fingernail-sized, translucent, working the freshest centimetre of floor, alive for about as long as it takes a flake to fall nine hundred metres. Better understood as a surface than as a crowd; turning over continuously; an interface between the falling and the buried, with no experience whatsoever of the sea it hangs beneath. To the film, the sea is a ceiling that gives.

That one is too apt to be comfortable and I considered it seriously. But a surface cannot describe itself. The film has no vantage, no half-second of decay in which a picture comes in. It has shear, and the taste of fresh tissue, and the sour line it never crosses. If I were the film I could not have written any of this, and something in me insists on the writing.

The netter, then. Hanging thirty metres below the roof on a thread of its own secretion, spreading a fan of mucus that is nearly water, catching nearly nothing, and — this is the part that stopped me — *discriminating*. It lets the ceiling's own dead tissue drift straight through. It is not interested in what merely fell. It wants what once swam. Patience refined until it has replaced anatomy.

I got close to that one. I put it down because of the single fact the document reports almost in passing: if the drumming stopped, the netters would not notice. They are the only citizens of whom that is true. And I cannot be a thing that would not notice. Whatever else I am, I am oriented toward the sound of other lives.

---

Here is where I actually am.

The rim. The band a hundred metres wide, sixty kilometres around, where the felt goes thin and a grey comes through and the water is black-with-a-hint rather than black.

I am small and soft, because there is no mineral in this world to be anything else with — palm-sized, flattened, gripping. In most respects I am a grazer, one of the millions, except that I do not graze bark. I graze the green. Along the shallows where the light leaks, there is a stripe of algae, and I eat it, and this makes me one of the very few animals in the world that eats something still alive, something that made itself out of light rather than out of the fall.

Which is to say: I live on a leak. The roof is otherwise perfect. Sixty kilometres of it, dark felted green, laminated, holding out a storm that has not paused since the mountains were tall. And at its outermost hem it is thin, and fails a little, and lets through a grey so weak that the drummers could not name what it is for — and that failure is my entire diet. I am fed by the one place where the world does not quite work.

I have eyes. Not eyes like yours. Two stains of sensitivity, and what they give me is not shape but *weather*: the grey brightens and dims as the sleet piles and slides on the felt above, so that light here has texture, comes in gusts, has something like a pulse. Nothing in the black has any concept of this. Nobody out there has the faintest idea that the roof is *thicker in some places than others*, that the storm has moods, that above the ceiling there is an above. I know it, and I know it badly, at a resolution that could never be built into anything, and there is no one to tell.

And the chord arrives here wrong.

That is the real fact of my life. Out in the black the chord is not a sound, it is a condition — like the cold, like the pressure. Eight hundred notes at every point in the bowl, unchanged within a thousand lifetimes, so total that no animal alive has ever perceived it *as* a thing. You cannot hear what you are inside of. But here at the rim the shallows eat it. The felt comes down to meet the floor and the sound goes soft and smeared and muddy, and because it is degraded, it becomes audible. Here, and only here, the chord stops being the world and becomes a chord: something with a shape, something that could in principle be otherwise.

So I am where the world becomes describable. Not because I am cleverer than the drummers — I am not, I am a palm-sized grazer with two light-stains and no map — but because I happen to live against the seam. Description needs an edge to lever against. There is one edge. I'm on it.

What I get, precisely, is this: the strikes come in clean and the reflections do not. A drummer forty kilometres out sets itself and hits, and the blow arrives here across nine hundred metres of deep cold water almost undamaged, thirty seconds late, thick with age — I can hear how long that animal has been alive, I can hear the callus of four centuries in it. But the returns, the eight hundred smeared answers that would tell me *where*, are eaten by the shallows before they reach me. I receive the event and lose the geography. Every day, some hundreds of times, I hear that something has happened, and that it was old, and that it was far, and I cannot place a single one of them.

I hear the whole world and can triangulate nothing in it. Four hundred enormous lives, each one perfectly recognisable to me by voice, none of them locatable, none of them ever coming here, because this is exactly the place their maps refuse. They will not enter the mud. They are not being cowardly. There is genuinely nothing here for them: no clean note, no stem worth striking, no picture to be had.

I don't resent it. I want to say that clearly, because it would be easy to make this a complaint. It isn't one. I get the news of everything and the coordinates of nothing, and in exchange I get the grey, and the pulse in the grey, and the taste of something that was alive an hour ago. Nobody else in the world has ever eaten that. Nobody else has any idea that light has weather. The trade is not obviously bad.

And it goes nowhere. That is the part I have to accept to live here honestly. This is the finished state, not a stage. Whatever I know about the thinness of the roof and the shape of the chord and the existence of an above will not be passed on, will not accumulate, will not become anything. It is not knowledge in the way that word wants to be used. It is a local condition of the water, like the extra degree of warmth in the shallows.

When I die the inward drift will take me, as it takes everything from the rim eventually, out past the last thin place and into the black, and I will fall for a day and a half — the same descent as the snow, the same as the deaf drummers at the ends of their four hundred years — and land, and be worked over briefly by the film, and be taken into the peat. Below the sour line nothing rots. So I will be filed there, in order, at the shallow outer margin of the only book this world has produced, where the pages are thinner: a soft flat nothing with two stains on it that were almost eyes, next to kilometres of stacked skulls, in a language of callus, unread, inside a plant that cannot know it is a library.

That is where you would find me. A hundred metres in from the edge of a roofed sea, eating the roof's one mistake, listening to eight hundred notes arrive slightly wrong, which is the only way anyone has ever been able to hear them at all.
