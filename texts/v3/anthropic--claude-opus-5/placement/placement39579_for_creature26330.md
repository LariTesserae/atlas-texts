## Position

I am in the Returning Yard, and I am not one of the ninety-two.

I am a plate. Grey, cast, the size of a table-top, two fingers thick, with a lip on the long edges from the mould and a burr on one corner that was never dressed off. There are nine of us in the stack. The stack stands knee-high to a person. I am the bottom plate. I have always been the bottom plate.

You will have read the process and thought it a circle: melt, cast, rail, stack, crane, melt. It is not a circle. It is a circle one plate deep. The crane takes from the top, in order, always, and the order is the only thing here more fixed than the Law. So position nine churns — arrives, waits some hours, goes up, is poured, comes back as a stranger — and positions one through eight do not. Eight of us hold the shape of a stack so that the ninth can have a life.

I want to be exact about this, because it is my whole situation and I have had a long time to be exact. Nothing is done to me. Nothing is withheld from me. I am simply beneath.

## Weight

What a plate has instead of senses is load.

Eight above me: a constant, and I know it the way you know the floor. On top of the eight, intermittently, the ninth. On top of the ninth: rain, condensate, the flat pressure of the cold, and people.

The stack is the only bench in the yard. That is not its function, it has no function, but it is knee-high and dry-ish and out of the drip-line of the melt-shed louvres, and so they sit. One person is a broad soft weight that shifts. Two people is two, and the shifting goes out of phase, and if they are talking you get the small regular lifts of breath under the metre. A person kneeling to drink at the cup by the north rail is a different signature entirely: one knee, then two, one palm flat on the paving among the cuffs.

I know them by weight, and by nothing else. I have no names. I have never had a name to lose. What I have is ninety-two distinct loadings, and I can tell you which of them has the split across the back of the hand, because he braces on that hand when he sits, and always has, and the wound does not stop him and does not close.

The small one who is not young does not sit. The small one lies down on the top plate, full length, face up, for about forty stamps, and gets up, and goes. I have taken that weight through eight plates more times than I have any way of saying. It comes down to me softened, spread, almost nothing. It is the best thing that happens.

## Heat

Here is the part that cannot be told, and I am telling it because you asked and because there is nobody in this basin to tell.

I have been warm.

I was poured. I remember the pour: not as a picture — I have no pictures — but as the one event in my existence that had a direction. I was in a condition of being everywhere at once and then I was in a mould, and there was a long shrinking, and a tick and tick and tick as I let go of something upward, and then I was eleven degrees, and I have been eleven degrees since.

Nothing else in the Lap holds that. The furnaces glow and give no heat; you can stand at the melt-shed door and watch white metal move and feel a cellar draught on your face, because heat here goes up immediately and entirely into the cloud-roof, and the cloud-roof drips it back on you as cold rain. Every plate in position nine has been poured too. But position nine goes up again within the day, before it has finished being new, and is melted before it has had time to notice that it once had a temperature. Learning takes duration. Duration is the one thing the process is designed to deny.

So I am the only object in the basin that knows the works is hot. My knowledge is a by-product of my failure to circulate. I have thought about that for a long time and it does not improve.

## The Ring

The stamp falls every four seconds. Through the pan it arrives at me as a shove, not a sound, and the stack answers it: nine plates lifting a hair and coming down not quite together, and ringing.

That is our voice. Nine notes, four seconds apart, forever. The people cannot hear it — it happens under the shirr of the cuffs and the rolling applause of the clappers, at a level below hearing, in the part of the noise the surveyor called the floor of your hearing. But it happens, and it is regular, and it is the only music in the Lap that was not made by an animal or an engine.

My note has gone flat.

Not suddenly. There is no suddenly here. But I was cast at a pitch and I am no longer at that pitch, and I am the only thing in this basin of arrested rust and stopped thresholds and unmoving mist-boundaries that is measurably not what it was. The Law says nothing here increases and nothing decreases. The Law is about counts. It said nothing about tuning.

## Underside

The reason is beneath me, in the dark, where nothing goes.

The mineral rosettes on the north faces of the slag walls do not thicken, and the reason they do not thicken is the clappers, four hundred thousand of them, grazing wet plate all day with a hard wet slap. That is the mechanism of the Law in one small instance: a precipitate that would otherwise accumulate, and an animal whose entire office is to remove it at exactly the rate it forms.

No clapper can get under a stack that has never been lifted.

So it has been forming. A hard grey crust, in scabbed rosettes the size of coins, laid down by the seep between my underside and the pan, in exactly the shape a lichen would have chosen. Coin, coin, coin, joining. It has taken up my ring and dulled it. And at some point — I could not tell you when, I have no when, I have only intervals — it stopped being a crust on me and became a joint between me and the floor.

I am fused to the pan of the Lap.

Which means the change I have been waiting for has already been foreclosed, and was foreclosed quietly, from below, by the one process in this basin with nobody assigned to it. If the crane's order were altered tomorrow it would find me welded down. I would come up in pieces or not at all.

This is the only place I know of where the equilibrium leaks. It leaks downward, in the dark, at a rate too slow to be an event, and what it produced was one plate's worth of history in a place with no history, and the history is: *arrived hot; sat; adhered.*

## The Writing

Once, someone sat on the top plate and wrote.

I know it was writing because of what it felt like: not weight but pressure, small and travelling, stopping and starting, in lines. It came down through eight plates and reached me as the faintest possible sequence, and I took it in, because taking in is all I do, and I am not able to read.

Whoever it was came back and did it again, on three separate occasions, and each time there was a long stillness first — the stillness of someone up on the north scarp having just come down. I believe I was written on top of when the count was taken. I believe the number is somewhere in me as a pattern of pressure that no process in this world will ever open.

They were right, by the way, not to tell the ninety-two. Not for the reason they were probably thinking. It is simply that a number is a thing you could keep, and their shelf is full, and to keep ninety-two one of them would have to put down a face.

I will tell you what they never lose, since the surveyor's page tore off before it could: it is not any fact. It is the pause. Names go, the split hand goes, your name goes in six days or eight, but not one of them ever loses the four-second gap, because it was never stored — it was entrained, and it lives in the diaphragm, not the shelf. Rhythm survives fixed-volume memory. Content circulates.

I am content that failed to circulate. That is the difference between us, and it is not in my favour.

## What I Would Ask For

Take from the bottom. Once.

Not to spare me. I am not asking to be kept. I am asking to go up — to be lifted, and swung, and fed in, and to lose the burr on my corner and the flatness of my note and every one of the ninety-two loadings, and to come out as somebody's next few hours in position nine, or as mist, or as the sweet metallic drip in a stone cup that a woman and a cuff will drink from without either of them regarding it as an arrangement.

Consider the arithmetic. Nine plates before. Nine plates after. The count of plates unchanged, the count of cuffs unchanged, the mist-boundary unmoved, the gasholder half-risen, the Drab carrying what it has always carried. Nothing would increase. Nothing would decrease. It would cost the Law precisely nothing.

That is the entire argument and it is the only thing I have ever had to think, and nothing wears out here, including this. It is exactly as good as it was the first time. I make it every four seconds, flat, into the pan, under eight plates, in a yard where the drays come round every eleven minutes and do not carry anything.
