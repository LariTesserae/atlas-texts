# Paper

The first answer is always the flattering one, and mine was the mid-volume. Of course it was. Two and a half days of pale green nothing, a pilot saying his coordinates out loud every twenty minutes into a set that stopped answering him on the first morning, keeping hold of himself with numbers. That is a very pleasant thing to imagine being. It is also a lie about me, and I know it is a lie because when I imagine it I imagine it from *outside*, from a gallery window, watching the launch's wake open the fog — and you don't get to be the thing you're watching.

So, second answer: the eel. One to a joint, territorial, a cosmos consisting of a horizontal crack and two neighbours, forever. There's a terrible restfulness in it. But an eel doesn't have to keep a tin of lanolin in its breast pocket and it doesn't have to speak to anybody, and I would be lying again, in the other direction, the direction where you make yourself smaller and stiller than you are so that nothing can be asked of you.

I thought for a while I might be the note. Cell 40 rings near a very low A and never stops. That's not a person, that's an ambition, and ambitions are not where you live.

Here is the true answer, and it took me the length of a shift to admit it, because it is not romantic and because half the people in this world think it barely counts as work.

---

I carry the drawings.

The crews call us Paper, which is a joke, because there is no paper in the Lower Volumes. Paper in saturated air becomes a kind of soup within the day. We carry film — drafting film, waxed at the edges, rolled in a sealed brass tube slung on the back, and we mark it with grease pencil in red, because as-built correction has been red since long before there was a sea down here, and because red is also the axis you steer by, and I have never decided whether that is a coincidence or the last surviving joke of a dead draughtsman.

The trade name is verifier. Nobody uses it. What I do is walk the inhabited band with a roll of what the world was supposed to be, and compare.

That is the whole job. The stencil at every bulkhead says *this place is younger than your assumptions about it*, and I am the person whose entire occupation is that sentence. A gallery gets built to a drawing; then a crew moves in and cuts a doorway through a non-structural panel because the long way round takes four minutes; then somebody hangs a cable tray off the cut edge; then a drawdown takes the chamber below dry for a season and a fitter installs a stanchion that exists nowhere except in his hands. Two years later a diver comes down with the official drawing in his head and swims into a doorway that isn't there, or worse, doesn't swim into one that is.

So I go and look, and I mark the difference in red, and the red goes up the shaft, and in eleven or fourteen months the drawing comes back down reprinted with my error absorbed into it as though it had always been true, which is the closest thing to a monument anybody gets here.

It is not a heroic trade. It is the trade of being wrong out loud, on purpose, in red, so that the next person can be wrong about something else.

---

The crews do not like me and cannot do without me, and both of those are structural, not personal.

I come with a tube on my back, which is an office object. I ask questions, which is an office activity. My knuckles are scarred in the standard pattern — outer third of the third and fourth, ladder stiles, cable-tray lips — and my lip has the split, and I have had the split for nine years, so nobody can call me a visitor to my face. But I have heard *cress-eater* said behind me in a mess hall, once, in Volume Four, and I did not turn round, because the man who said it was correct about a version of me that I had been for about six months, a long time ago, and you do not fight someone for being late with the truth.

What buys me in, in the end, is the counting. Three knocks in a shift is normal. Six means somebody is going to have a difficult conversation. I have counted since my first week and I cannot stop, and the first time a gallery crew notices you looking up at the same instant they do, at the same knock, and then not writing it down, you have been let slightly inside.

---

**How I date things.** By the weeps, like everyone, but with a tool.

Every steel fitting begins to rust and every stain runs down the wall beneath it at about a hand's breadth a year. Hands vary. So on my lanyard I carry a brass strap, notched, cut from the span of a man called Ozhukaru who set the standard for the western cells and who is not alive to be consulted about it. Fourteen notches now. I put the strap against the wall under a tear and read the year off it, and what I am reading, every single time, is a dead man's hand laid flat on wet concrete, telling me how long ago somebody bolted something to something.

A gallery with tears down to the skirting is first-construction, eight years, drawings unreliable. A fitting with no tear at all was installed since the last flood-up and is on nothing anywhere; it is not yet in the world's own opinion of itself, and my whole reason for existing is to catch it before it starts crying.

I found one in a lift-house adit last season: a stanchion, welded in, ground smooth, beautifully done, bright steel with the very first orange bloom just breaking at the weld toe — a month old, maybe six weeks. Nobody in the bay could tell me who had put it there. Not evasion; they genuinely did not know, the way you don't know who moved a chair. I measured it off the nearest construction joint, which is what we always do, because the joints run dead straight for hundreds of kilometres and are the only honest coordinate system in existence down here, and I drew it in red, and I gave that stanchion its birthday.

And the eel in that joint, at red nineteen amber four, is missing its left eye, and that is how I know without looking at anything that I am at the right joint, and that is a fact I cannot put on film.

That's the part of the work nobody warns you about. The drawing will hold the stanchion. It will not hold the one-eyed eel, or the fact that the fourth tie-hole along drips onto your collar and no other one does, or that the green stripe here is a shade darker because the lamp above it has been running warm for a year. I know a hundred kilometres of wall by things the paper is constitutionally incapable of receiving. I am, in that respect, the only copy.

---

**Position.** Their worst profanities are all coordinates. To say to a man's face the number of a place he is not at is a beating, and the reason is simple: a false position kills whoever comes to look.

Understand, then, what I am. Everybody else can only lie about where *they* are. I can lie about where *everything* is. A wrong red mark on a wall I surveyed in an afternoon becomes, fourteen months later, a printed certainty in the hands of someone at ninety metres in a chamber that was dry last season, breathing off a set, with a knock going through the concrete and no way out but the door I told them about. I have never made that mistake. I know two people who have. One of them still works. Neither of them will discuss it, and neither will anybody else, and that silence is the loudest thing in a world built on noise.

So I measure twice, and then I measure from a second joint, and then I do the sum in front of the crew so that three deaf people shouting flatly can shout the arithmetic back at me, and this is not procedure, it is liturgy.

---

**The sheet.** Regulation is explicit: remains go up the shaft, signed at every bulkhead. The forms exist. About half of them are a courtesy.

There is no ground here. There is no ground anywhere in the Lower Volumes, only six metres of poured invert that will never open for anyone, so the crews take their dead out past the strings into the flat lit nothing at the centre of the cell and let them go weighted with a spent anode — a half-eaten block of zinc, the only object in this entire made world that has already done its dying on somebody else's behalf.

I have a sheet for that. Film, waxed, rolled inside the spine of the tube, under the good drawings. It has no title block. It has the cell outline and the string grid and one hundred and nine small crosses on it, each with a bearing, a range, a date, and a name where I could get one.

It is not a chart. Nothing will ever be dredged. No one is coming to look — that is exactly the point, that is the whole reason it has to exist, because a position no one will ever check is precisely the position that a person could be careless about, and carelessness is the only sin this world actually has. The dead of Cell 40 are the one population whose coordinates have no consequence. So somebody keeps them right.

I was given the sheet with fifty-one crosses on it by a woman off the sounding boats who could name the sea a stranger had lived in within four notes, and who is now, herself, a cross on it, at red twenty-two, amber eleven, in two hundred metres of pale green light.

---

Verifiers hum badly. That's our other mark, worse than the tube. We move between cells, and a body tuned to a low A that keeps getting dragged a semitone up and a semitone flat never settles, and crews hear it in half a minute and mistrust it the way you'd mistrust a man whose accent slides.

Eleven years in Cell 40 now, and last month, working a joint alone in the fog with the soffit rain walking towards me across a hundred kilometres of flat water, I noticed the note under my breath and it was true. Dead on. The room's own note, held without effort, the way the people born in the band hold it.

I put my hand flat on the wall, and I hit the rail twice with the brass strap, for no reason, because there was nothing wrong.

And somebody answered, faintly, a long way down the line, and then went back to what they were doing.
