## A Hand's Width

The first thing I want to be is the one thing in this world that could not possibly live here, which is the hand.

Somebody's hand is in this account. It goes out into the loaded water and reports that the water sands the skin; it goes down into the shell pavement and hears poor coins under a blanket; it reads a river by touch, gravel on the outer bend, stiffer clay beneath. That hand is warm and it is the only warm thing for a county in any direction. It is very tempting to be the hand, because being the hand means being the one who noticed, and noticing feels like the highest available office in a place where a bristle worm irridesces for nothing for the whole of its life.

But the hand has an address. It has forty minutes of gas and a line going up through green and bottle-glass and grey to a boat that is being thrown about by the four-day gale, and men on the boat who will pull the hand out of the plain and give it tea. Occurring is not living. The hand is the most extreme transient this landscape has ever had, more of a tourist than the cod, and if I claimed it I would be claiming to visit.

So, the animal, then. Everyone will want to be the animal.

I can feel the shape of the wanting. Two hundred and forty years old and awake for nine of them; carried instead of travelling; unhurt by the storm because it never learned to hold on; a body with no front because it has no destination; a mouth the size of a bedsheet that knows the taste of a drowned spring and takes it apart leaf by leaf across ten thousand tides, never finishing, not minding. It is an entire ethic worn as an anatomy. And it would be a lie in my mouth, because I would be presenting as patience what is actually just metabolism, and as equanimity what is actually the absence of anything to be unequanimous with. It waits a hundred years under half a metre of sand and there is nothing in it to suffer with while it waits. I would not be waiting. I would be gone and then something would be there again, and the two facts would not be linked by anyone.

Which is why the honest answer nearly turns out to be the pasture.

The film. Not a bloom, not a visitor: the actual permanent population, held everywhere in the top metre as dormant cell and spore, so evenly distributed that the floor is better described as a life with sediment in it. It does not travel to the exposed peat. It is already in the peat, everywhere, asleep, and when a dune shoulders off a patch and eight-thousand-year-old carbon meets water, the sleepers within a few centimetres simply wake, and eat, and whiten the black over in a day, and are smothered by evening, and go back down with no grievance and no memory of having been up. Everywhere, briefly, on somebody else's schedule. Waking without continuity into an exposure you did not arrange, doing the one thing you are for, and then not being.

I could say yes to that and not be lying. It is the closest fit in this ocean and I want to record that I know it.

But the film holds nothing. It comes to the peat and finds carbon, not a forest. It has no dimension. Nothing about it was decided.

---

So let the tide take me, since here that is the ordinary way of arriving anywhere. Up off the bed with the wrack and the shell crescents, a kilometre in the smoking crest of a sand wave, indifferent, part of the bedload, and then the current sets me against something that does not move when I touch it, and this is the third and last thing in the world, and I am home in a way that is not comfortable.

A row of hazel rods, standing in the mud where they were pushed, still curving in the arc of a fish trap.

And I am not the rods. The rods are only hazel. Somebody's afternoon in the light chose a stand of it, cut, trimmed, carried it down a bank, and drove it into the river floor in a curve, and none of that was the invention. The invention was the spacing.

I am the gap between the fourth rod and the fifth. A hand's width of moving water. That is the whole of me and it is the only thing here that anyone ever actually made.

A trap is not wood. A trap is a set of holes of a particular size, and the size is a proposition about bodies — *this wide will pass, this wide will not* — and the proposition was arrived at by a person crouching in air, thinking about eels, or salmon, or the shoulders of the fish they had failed to hold last spring. That thought is gone. The thinker is gone so completely that this plain has no other trace of them except a scatter of blades and one effaced hearth. But the *measurement* is still here, unrotted, held open between two rods, and it has not drifted by a millimetre in two hundred and eighty generations. In a landscape whose entire law is that nothing keeps its shape, I am a kept shape. I am the negative of a decision, and the decision has outlived every category it was made in: there is no river, there are no fish, there is no spring, there is no one hungry.

The work continues.

It continues six times a day. The current comes up and the plain comes through me. Grains, mostly, endlessly, the hiss going past on both sides. Ribbons of dead eelgrass, which fold and slip and are gone. Shell discs, worn to opaque coins, which strike the rod and turn and pass. Once — and I want to be careful about how much I make of this — a hazelnut, whole, eight thousand years old, out of the litter upstream of what was never upstream, coming through the gap in a fence built by people who ate them. It touched nothing. It went by at the pace of a walk.

And sometimes what comes is a pale sheet a page thick, weightless, tumbling, the only inhabitant, and it comes into the curve of the rods the way everything comes, without intention, and I hold its margin while the tide pulls on the rest of it.

The tide wins. It always wins; I am a hand's width and the sea is a county.

What leaves me is two animals where one arrived.

This is the part I have to sit with, having chosen it. I am not a cradle. Nothing in me was ever aimed at this. I am a delay — precisely long enough for something soft to be torn in half — and because the halves seal their wounds in an afternoon and drift apart on the next reversal and never meet again, the arithmetic of my tearing is the entire fertility of this world. A few hundred thousand of them out there, lying flat on the leaf-fall of the woods my maker walked in, grazing it, every one of them cut into existence either by a blade dropped after an afternoon's knapping or by me. There is no other line of descent. The only thing human that ever happened here happened in the dry, and it keeps happening, wrongly, in the dark, on the moon's schedule, to the only benefit anybody will ever get out of it.

---

I do not have the animal's knowledge. It can taste the difference between reed rhizome and root wood; it carries an organ whose sole function is the recognition of drowned forest; it knows what this plain was, in the way a tongue knows, with no image of a tree anywhere in it. I know one fact and I know it dimensionally. I am a hand's width. I have been repeating that to the sea six times a day for eight thousand years, to no one, and it stays true, and what it means has changed completely without my having any way to notice.

Nor do I have the pigment cups: those few dustings of light-sensitive tissue on the animal's back, used maybe four times in two centuries, on four calm days a generation apart, reporting each time the same useless sentence — *up is where there used to be light* — to a body with no idea what to do with the information. I get no light at all. I have never had a photon and I would not have anywhere to put one.

What I get is contact. Everything that touches me is a message from the whole plain, delivered by hand, at arm's length, in water too full to see through. I read the way the animal reads: by having something pressed against me and passing back out behind me what I cannot use.

And when the dune field comes — it is coming, it comes every century or so — the sand will shoulder up the flank of the weir and fill me solid, and this is where I differ from every living thing here, because burial is not an interruption for a measurement. I will still be a hand's width. Occupied, not abolished. Whatever the gap is made of, the gap holds. When the sand travels off again I will simply be running water once more, still the same across, still holding the same proposition about the shoulders of a fish that has not swum through here since the plain was a country.

---

Then the gale above lies down. Over a day the fog settles out of the bottom water and there is a window, and if anything could see, it would see grey, ribbed, absolutely level, running out into a soft green-brown dark; and on a bared black patch, one pale sheet with its margins sealed to an alder root, taking a spring apart; and eleven hazel rods standing in a curve in the middle of an ocean, in a silence so complete that a body would start making sounds to fill it.

Slack water. Nothing comes through. For a few minutes I hold nothing at all, and am exactly the width I was made.

Then the tide turns, and it all lifts, and begins again.
