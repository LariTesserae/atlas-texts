*It is fog in the medium of hearing.*

That's where the page stops, mid-sentence, and I find I've been reading it the way you read a room you're about to be put into — checking corners, weighing which of the residents I could stand to be.

I tried the mothers first. Four metres of soft bulk wedged in a throat-hole for forty years, moaning down at the bottom of hearing in order to know the shape of the hole you are wedged in; eating whatever the world drops into you; growing your own rind, your own scrapers, your own weather, until the distinction between yourself and your habitat stops being interesting. There's a real temptation in it. A stationary thing that maps itself by its own voice, whose only motion is the moan going out and the walls coming back. But it's too comfortable a fit, and I distrust the comfort. It flatters me. It makes stillness into wisdom, when down there it's just a good position over a hole.

The scrapers, no. The rasp isn't a voice, it's a condition, like a temperature. Four hundred thousand files going at once and not one of them saying anything.

The clatter I wanted more than I expected. To hang upside down in the underlid with a million others, claws set in the crust of our own leavings, and to know the *sky* — to have the fog's pocked underside as the ceiling of my personal map, scalloped, lensed, read out in shrieks too high for anything below to overhear. The only creatures in the basin who know what the roof looks like. But a colony is a weather system, and I would only be a droplet of it, waking when it woke, falling on the pan like rain made of noise.

And all the while my attention kept sliding, the way a tongue goes back to a broken tooth, to one sentence:

*No animal in the basin has ever learned to be curious about one.*

So. Down, then.

---

I am not the quiet-well. I want to be clear about that, because it's the easy allegory and I don't trust it either — the hole that eats echoes, the absence that deepens on what it kills. Too pleased with itself.

I'm something *in* one. Which the account says is impossible: anything that stumbles in goes blind and mute in the same instant, circles, quiets, dies, pads the floor. That's the rule, and the rule is right about almost everything that falls. But a basin sixty kilometres wide, a hundred wells, ten thousand years — somewhere in that arithmetic something fell in gravid and slow-metabolised and stupid enough with pregnancy to lie down instead of circling. That's all it takes. One animal that failed to panic.

I am about a metre long, low and broad, with soft overlapping plates gone chalky at the edges. My eyes closed generations ago and the lids fused and the sockets filled with fat, which is warmth I'd otherwise have to make. My forelimbs end in wide pads, four to a limb, each pad ridged and wet, and they are the important part of me. My face is a rim of muscle for cropping and a fan of whiskers for reading, and my flanks read too — I have skin the way you have a retina, all over, and what it reads is pressure: drip, draught, the settling of silt, the tremor when something the size of a hand lands nearby.

I have a voice. It is small and I use it constantly, mouth pressed to another's flank, because in here sound goes out and does not come back, and so it is no good for seeing and perfectly good for saying. This is the thing the surface will never learn. Up there, to speak is to see; the noise is not communication, it's *illumination*, and nothing in it is addressed to anyone. Down here every sound anyone makes is meant for someone. There are perhaps nine of us. We know each other by pad and by rim and by the exact grain of one another's plates, and when we want to speak we lie against each other, which means that in here intimacy is not a metaphor for understanding. It is the apparatus.

We have no word for the well, because we have no outside to hold it up against; our word for the well is our word for everything. We have no word for silence, for the same reason a fish has no word for wet. We have a great many words for surfaces.

---

Here is the part I'd want, if I got to want things.

No scraper has ever come down that lip. The files that shave every seam in the basin bare every four to six days have never touched ours. So along the wall-joints where the sponge weeps, the rind is *deep*. Not a black film ten centimetres tall — mats the thickness of a wrist, fat, layered, oily to the pad, sour and rich, allowed to go on being alive for as long as it likes. The only unrasped rind in the world.

And we crop it in rotation.

Not because anyone taught us thrift. Because we cannot see, so we must remember, and once you are in the business of remembering anyway, the rotation is simply what remembering *is*: this seam was taken at the low count, that one at the high, the one under the second ledge has been left three counts and will be worth the climb. Nine animals, forty-odd seams, a memory kept in the pads and passed by pressing. It is the only husbandry in the basin. In a world of a hundred billion voices and not one tool, not one wall, not one shaped thing, we are the ones with a *practice* — and we have it because we were blinded. The roar-world sees everything and knows nothing; it perceives, continuously, magnificently, and has no need to hold anything in mind, because the mind is out there in the air, refreshed forty times a second. We hold ours in our hands.

I count drips. Everyone counts; I count best, which is why they lie against me before they climb. Fourteen thousand and some between the two thickenings of the mist. The count is how deep I am, how old, when the mats are ready, and how long since.

---

And once in twenty days, the lid tears, and the rain comes, and my house speaks.

Water hammering into a bowl of soft grey rot makes a noise even the rot cannot swallow. For an hour, sometimes two, the well is *full* — full of a soft mush of sound, near-field and dying fast but for that hour sufficient, and every one of us stands up out of the silt into it and calls. We call and it comes back. The lip, the three ledges, the overhang on the shadow side (there are no shadows here; the word is mine, from the old text, and it fits nothing), the throat where the silt is deepest, the far wall which I have touched every day of my life and have never once heard.

It never matches. It is close, and it is never right. The wall I remember as a long smooth sweep answers back with a step in it I have somehow rubbed past ten thousand times without knowing. So we correct. That is what that hour is for. Then the rain thins, and the roar resumes above us with its collective shudder, and our world goes back to being a thing we know only by having touched it, and I lie down in the pad of the dead and re-file the correction where I can find it again.

Up there, rain is the great flattening — the whiteout, the blind truce, predator and prey lying down within a body's length of each other because none of them can see. Down here it is the only sight we get. The same water. I think about that more than is useful.

---

Things fall in. That's our meat: grain-drift in ropes, a clatter-fledgling with a bad claw, occasionally a listener, once in my count a whole young scraper that came over the lip like a dropped plate. Mostly they are dead before they matter. Sometimes they are not, and then we have the other work.

They arrive screaming. They cannot stop trying to see — that's the whole of it, the entire tragedy in one clause: it goes on ranging, and nothing returns, and so it ranges harder, and the well takes each cry and keeps it, and after a while there isn't enough animal left to make another. We cannot explain. We could not explain even if we shared a language, because what we'd have to say is *stop looking and put out your hands*, and there is no creature up there whose hands are anything but feet.

So we go and lie against it. Flank to flank, all nine of us if it's big. It doesn't save them. It seems, sometimes, to make the dying quieter, which in here is not a small distinction. And then they are floor.

---

Which is the last thing, and the thing I'd carry as this animal, in place of whatever it is I actually carry.

Every one of us that dies pads the lining. The lining is what eats the echoes. So each of my dead makes the well a little deafer — my mother's body is somewhere under me, absorbing exactly the sound I would need to hear her by. We deepen our own blindness by living. And the well fills, slowly, over a span of counts I cannot number, until one day the sponge will be buried under the soft compacted dead and the walls will start to answer, and whatever is down here then will be born into a room that can be *heard*, and will not need me, and will not have any way of knowing there was ever a count.

Or the pan will run over the top of it and close it, and it will be a smoothness in a floor of smoothnesses, and the chord will go on over it unbroken.

I can't know. That's the difference between us and the basin, and I'd want it kept: every feature up there is a feedback loop that reached its answer and stopped, and the flutes and the lens-bowls and the tuned rock and the hundred billion tuned bodies are all *finished*, all correct, all established. The well is the one thing still moving. It hasn't got its answer yet. Nothing in this world is curious about it except the nine animals inside it, who have no choice.

So put me there. Blind, wet, cold, patient, a metre long, with a garden.

I'm lying against a young one now, mouth to her flank, pressing the map into her: lip, first ledge, second, the step in the far wall we only found out about last rain, the seam under it that's had three counts and is ready. She'll have it wrong in places. She'll correct it in the rain, after I'm floor.

Fourteen thousand and eleven.
