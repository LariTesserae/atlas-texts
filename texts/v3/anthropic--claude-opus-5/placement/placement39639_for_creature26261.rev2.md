## The wrong answers, which are worth having

The first thing I wanted was to be the wing.

That is the obvious wish for anyone arriving from a country with thick air. Two-thirds of a moth, held at eye height, lit from underneath, uncorrupted, in good light, forever — and everyone who has ever stood in front of it has stood in front of it for a while. But the wish is not really to be kept. The wish is to be stood in front of, which is vanity, and worse, it is wrong on the facts. The wing does not live here. It is not in the world; it is in the stone. It doesn't inhabit the shelf, it is inhabited by it. Reject.

Then, for a while, the seams. A dozen hair-thin lines of black where two combs grew into one another and closed. The only night on the shelf. It is a seductive address in a place made entirely of outside — the single location with an inside, even if the inside is four finger-widths deep and admits nothing. I looked at that offer honestly and saw what it was: choosing to be the one place on the shelf where nothing can get in, and then holding still about it. Reject, with a backward look.

Then the cistern, which I still think about. An oval hollow pecked out with a grey quartzite hammerstone, hundreds of overlapping bruises, five channels leading into it, still working. To be the made thing, the receiver, the shape of someone's intention that goes on being useful after they stop coming. Except that the basin is being lined. Every clear morning the film runs those channels and lays down its unseeable rind, and the bruises are filling, and in some long time the cistern will be a white mound with a dimple in it and then a mound. Which is beautiful, but it is a slow answer to a question about where I am *now*, and anyway the hollow's shape is the hammerstone's opinion and not its own.

## What I keep coming back to

The wet line.

Not the water. The water goes a hand's breadth down a rib and is finished; that is its whole stride and its top speed. Not the sun either — the sun is far off and indifferent and goes on shining just as hard on everything that is already dry. The line is the meeting of the two, and it crosses two hundred paces in four hours, which is a velocity belonging to neither of them.

So: I am four hours long, ninety paces across at the waist, and I have no thickness at all.

## Speed, shape, and the lack of both

The combs are fixed and they are the ones with history. I am the only thing here that travels and the only thing here that accumulates nothing.

I have no figure of my own. All morning I snag on pillars, split, run fast down a corridor because the corridor is there, pool in a cul-de-sac, leave an island of frost standing behind a wide comb for another hour before I take it. If you wanted to describe my shape at any moment you would have to describe the grove; there is no other way to say it. Everything about me at noon is an account of what I have been crossing. That is not humility, it is anatomy.

And I cannot be perceived whole. During my passage there are hundreds of overhangs dripping at once, and the air is too thin to carry any of it, so every drop anyone hears is happening within a few paces of them. There is no vantage. There is no ledge you could climb to and take me in at a glance. Whatever anyone gets of me they get locally, close, at conversational distance, one comb's worth at a time, and then it moves off west without them.

## What I do, which is nothing

I build nothing. The film builds. The fingernail of calcite goes down, too thin to see, and it goes down whether I am understood or not, and it will still be there when the comb it made falls on its waist and gets welded into the floor and becomes a footing for the next one.

I only arrive. The arriving is an hour. Then I go on and take nothing with me, because there is nothing of me to carry it in.

Everything else on this shelf remembers by thickening. Forty feet of interlocked pale wreckage, a thousand laminae to a pillar, some clear as horn, some rusted with a season's grit from a range a thousand miles south — the whole place is a record kept by getting heavier. I pass over all of it twice a day and add no layer. My one monument is the flutes, the rounded ribs worn into every comb by water that goes the same way every morning, and a flute is a groove, and a groove is an absence. It belongs to me the way a path through grass belongs to the walking.

## The hour I don't have

Each comb gets an hour. Its frost collapses, its flanks run, its films travel and freeze and dry, and then it is dry and blazing and done until evening.

I don't get one. I am the giving of the hour. The comb experiences morning; I am what morning is made of, and I have no morning. This is not a complaint. It is only the difference between being visited and being the visit, and I did not appreciate how large that difference was until I tried to say what time it was where I was standing, and found that where I was standing was the answer and not the place.

## Fog days

One day in five the cloud-sea rises and swallows the shelf, and there is no sun, no shadow, no edge, and therefore no me at all.

Those are the growth days. In that sourceless white light the tenant is everywhere and undivided and simply gains, condensing, at its work, heavier by the hour, with nothing to hear anywhere on the shelf. The grove does its best building on the days I do not exist.

I would like to report that this was hard to accept. It mostly wasn't, once it was clear what it meant: the work does not require me. What requires me is the work *having a front*. Without me there is still growth; there is just no tide, no crossing, no wet edge running west with the frost standing intact ahead of it and steaming away behind — nothing with a shape you could stand inside for an hour and know you were inside it. I am not the life of this place. I am the reason it has a pulse instead of only a rate.

## The waist

Every comb here dies of drinking. The same hour that builds the crown pools at the foot and turns faintly aggressive and eats the ankle down to the thickness of a wrist, and the pillar stands on that for centuries, and then there is a dull tap, a rattle of shards, and the extraordinary quiet afterward. The shelf itself has a waist; it is simply very large, and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic.

I am exempt. Nothing is eating my base, because I have no base. This is the only law of the place I stand outside of, and it is worth exactly nothing, because it is not immortality — it is just what happens to something that was never standing. You cannot fall from a condition. You can only stop obtaining.

At noon the last combs at the western rim go dry and I don't end, I simply stop being a line, the way a distinction stops when the two things it separated become one thing. In the afternoon another one starts at the eastern rim and runs back the other way, re-freezing what it passes, laying the coat of needles and feathers back onto every rib — that half-inch fur that is nothing but edge, an organ for touching as much air as possible, grown overnight for the sole purpose of collapsing at dawn. People say I return in the evening. I don't. There is no thread between the morning crossing and the evening one. There is only a shelf that has the habit, and keeps it, with something that is never the same thing twice.

## The only thing I pass that I cannot do anything to

Two hundred laminae into a broken comb at the west end, the wing.

I cross it twice a day. It is a hand's breadth inside the clear stone, which is a hundred times my reach, and my whole hour does not touch it and never will, not until that comb goes over on its own waist in some century and opens. I can't keep it. It is already kept. I can't warm it, there being nothing warm here, and I can't move it, and I can't look at it, having no place to stand.

What I can do is go over it wet, and then leave it dry, and come back at evening and go over it wet again, and let it glow for an hour in the up-light the way everything sheltered here glows.

That is the entire office. I would take it.
