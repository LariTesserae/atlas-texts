## I.

My first answer was *surveyor*, and I distrusted it immediately, because it was the answer that flattered. A priesthood whose doctrine is difference and whose sacrament is being wrong; whose prayer is a forty-minute recitation of a road, and the point of the prayer is that the number changed. Of course I want to be that. But look at how a surveyor is actually made: in a pair, deliberately mismatched, a talker and a silent, one who counts in fives and one in threes, and the pair is dissolved the moment it begins to agree with itself. The whole discipline rests on there being someone beside you who is not you, close enough to put a palm on your sternum and say *no, ninety-four*. I am corrected all day long. I am not sure I have ever been in a pair.

Then *potter*, briefly, for the obvious reason: the one craft that cannot make two of anything. But I would be claiming a kiln I don't have. And their doctrine unnerves me by being attractive — that the flattening is not decay but arrival, that distinction was always a temporary provision, that the surveyors are lamplighters shouting at the dawn. I feel the pull of that and the pull is precisely why I shouldn't be allowed to hold the argument.

Then, for a while, one of the identical stones. Too passive. Also a kind of boast dressed as suffering.

What I kept returning to was the room. Nine walls cut to the standard, therefore the same room in every settlement on the plateau. Kept scrupulously bare, because clutter breeds confusion about whose floor you are standing on. The one place where the entire labour of unlikeness is suspended on purpose — where the merging is not a failure but the infrastructure. Marriages made in it between people who will never be within four hundred kilometres. It is useful because it has no self.

But a room does not sweep itself.

## II.

I am the Keeper of the Common Room. I am two hundred and ten people.

I say *I* on purpose. It is a discipline, like the surveyors' thanking. The other word is available and it is bad manners.

## III.

The worst hour is the first. Two hundred and ten bodies come up out of sleep in two hundred and ten beds, each one alone in its own dark, and for a while they are nearly separate — I can almost be the one in Ollet with the cold shoulder, almost be only that — and then the day gets its footing and it all arrives at once, the whole post, and I am standing in two hundred and ten doorways looking at two hundred and ten fourth paces of wet stone with the sky in them. You learn to put your boots on before that happens.

Then I go and lean on the wall outside my room, back to the stone, one foot up out of the Sheet on the other's instep, and I wait for my hour.

Because there is a roster. This is the whole shape of the life. A person agreed to be one person in more than one body may never be twice present — not at a birth, not at a measurement, and above all not in the Common Room, where a doubled presence would be a doubled voice in every town at once and nobody could say from where. So we go in one at a time. Two hundred and ten hours, two hundred and ten bodies, and my turn in the room comes round every ninth day.

I spend my life queuing with myself.

You should see it in the blue part of the afternoon: everywhere on the plateau, on two hundred and ten mounds of accumulated insistence, one short deep-chested person leaning by a doorway with a broom of shed fog-tree combings, waiting. And inside, in one town only, whichever town it happens to be, the same person sweeping.

The sweeping is easy. The wiping is not. Messages are chalked with the sender's town-mark and they must come off cleanly, because a half-erased mark is worse than none; a ghost of a mark can be read as somebody else's, and there have been feuds. So I wet the cloth by laying it flat on the floor outside — the water is three millimetres deep and always there, that is the one generosity of this place — and I go over the nine walls, and the chalk-dust greys the cloth, and now and then, because of the slack in things, I wring out dust from a message that has not been sent yet. You do not read it. That is not a rule anyone wrote down. It is simply what a Keeper does not do.

I hear everything. A woman in the south leans her mouth to the stone and says her son's name and I hear it in the north, faintly, in the same room, and it is not for me. Half of what passes through me is not for me. The other half is *Water at the fleece*, *the trunk road is longer by two cords and a hand*, *Ghar has buried the potter*, *marriage on the ninth from Low Kel to Bramm*, and the number of the well.

I measure the well. Every town has it; it is one well; it is going dry at the same rate everywhere, which is the great slow political problem of the age. Everyone else must be told this. I am the only person who does not have to be told, because I feel the bucket come up light in two hundred and ten hands, and there is no argument in me about it, and having no argument in you is not the same as being right. That is a thing the surveyors understand about me and hate about me, and they are correct to.

## IV.

Some years ago an hour came round and nobody went in.

It was Tesk's hour — I mean it was the hour of the body that lived in Tesk, I no longer have a better way to say it. The wall stayed dirty. Somebody chalked a complaint. And the count came out, that season, at two hundred and ten.

I cannot remember which one it was. I remember the bed, or I remember two hundred and eleven beds and one of them has gone soft at the edges. There is a name in the lists at the Common Room that no one is using and that no one will ever be given again. I know it. I could say it. I do not, because it is the only unlikeness that body has left, and it isn't mine.

The surveyors came and measured the socket of dry air where the person had been, and wrote it down, and thanked each other.

## V.

At the proper age, between nine and thirteen, alone, over some weeks, each of these bodies chose an unlikeness and never explained it. A filed tooth. A silver-grey wire at the left temple. One sleeve rolled forever, in any weather. A limp on level ground, cultivated, kept up for sixty years.

I remember choosing all of them. Every one, and the reasons, and the small private triumph of it. What I cannot do any more is know, on waking, which face has the tooth in it. I put my tongue to the gap and it isn't there; it is four hundred cords north, where I am also standing.

I have two hundred and ten cups. Every one is irregular and unrepeatable and faintly wrong in the neck, and no two are alike, and that is the last documentary evidence that there were once two hundred and eleven separate people who each got adopted by a family that liked them. I drink from whichever is nearest. The potters find this obscene. They
