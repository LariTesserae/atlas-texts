# The Bowl-Measurer

*(a further correction, made in the quiet, by the one who keeps the numbers)*

## Reading-Day

You would find me on an alignment morning, lying on my belly in the fourth pit of the north quarter with my cheek in the moss, out of the rain-line, at the hour when the nine beams come into step and the fen pulls back off the flats and the flour stiffens enough to hold a rim without slumping.

You would have to come inside the pit to find me at all. From a stride outside, I am a shape in the grey that the eye discards. From a stride inside, you can hear me breathing, and my knees cracking as I get down, and the small dry sound of a nail going along a groove.

There are eleven days in everything. Nine pumps drifting apart and coming back, and on one of those days the water level drops a hand and the plain is legible and the noise is at its very worst — a hammer instead of a chaos, all of it arriving in the sternum together. That is the arrangement. You may read the world on the day the world is least bearable, and only from inside the places where it cancels itself out. I have never found this unjust. It is simply how the wave is shaped, and the wave is older than justice.

I am the one who takes the measurements. There has always been one. My teeth say I am somewhere in my sixth decade; I read them myself in a shard of gear-polish, which is improper, and nobody has stopped me, because stopping a person requires either a grip on the forearm or a walk of half a mile to point, and I am usually already gone.

## The Rod

Three instruments.

A bronze spindle, a foot and a half long when it came into my hands, with a shoulder ground at one end. It stands against a bolt-stump and I read the stump's height off the shoulder. There are four thousand and nine stumps on my round.

A tapered iron wedge, which I lower into a drip-bowl until it binds on the rim, and read where the scale-crust marks it. Six hundred and twelve bowls, each with its own rain-line above it, each line permanent to a finger's width in the whole wear-record.

And my own back teeth, which grind flatter at the rate every set of teeth here has ground flatter since there were teeth, and which are therefore a clock as good as any bolt.

The difficulty of my craft is not the reading. The difficulty is that the rod wears too. It is shorter than it was when the woman before me held it; I know because she wrote, in her own hand, in the moss of this pit, that it was longer, and because the stumps she measured with it now read taller against it than they can possibly have grown. The rod wears in the hands. The wedge wears at the point. The teeth wear in the head that thinks about the wearing.

There is no fixed thing to hang any of it from. What there is instead is agreement. If I take the stump and the bowl and the polished band on the gear-rim and the depth of the moss and my own molars, and correct each against all the others, they converge — not on a date, we have no use for dates, but on a rate, and the rate is the same rate she got, and her predecessor, and back into the part of the moss no fingernail can enter. Nothing here is certain. Everything here is consistent. That is the whole of what I have to offer my people, and they take it seriously enough to feed me, which is more than most beliefs manage.

The visitor who came with the tally-book wanted to know how old the basin was. He asked with his hands, badly, and I answered him honestly by touching my own front teeth and then opening my palm at the plain, which means *read it yourself, it is all molars*. He wrote something down. He got the rain right, and the birds in the grease, and he was wrong about only one thing, which is the thing everyone is wrong about, and it is not the animals.

## Gomm

We have one word for the moss, the writing, the dead, and the act of remembering. *Gomm.* When we say a thing is *gomm* we mean it has been said, and is now underfoot.

Here is what the surveyor missed. We write in the mud on the flats and it lasts a week — he saw that, he noted it, he thought it was a limitation. But nobody writes anything they want kept on the flats. On the flats you write *the water is coming up* and *your child went east* and *I will be at four.* What you want kept, you write in a pit, in the grey flour where the silt settles, and it is not taken by the vibration, because there is no vibration; and the moss finds the groove, because a groove holds water and holds seed-thread and holds a heel-crumb of itself; and it fills the letter and then stands proud of it, and after a lifetime you are reading not a trench but a raised green scar, with your fingertips, in the dark, on your belly.

So the pits are not gardens. The pits are the book. Every one of the two hundred and eighty is a page, and the page is also the crop, and the crop is also the one organism, and the organism is also the diagram of the engine that stood here before the bolts went smooth.

Think about what that means and then put it down, because it does not get lighter.

Everything anyone has ever thought worth keeping in this world has been written in the only places where writing is possible, and those places are where the sound cancels, and the sound cancels where a vanished machine put its shafts and its bearings and its four-mile transmission. We did not choose the shape of our record. We were handed it. Our entire memory — every settled boundary, every parentage, every measured rate, every twelve-word thing anyone was ever able to say out loud — lies in the footprint of a mechanism that transmitted nothing and is not there.

I read the top layer with my eye. The layer under it with my nail. Beneath that is felt, and beneath the felt is a depth of grey-green mat that goes down a hand and a half in the centre of the older pits, and no one alive can read a stroke of it. It is our oldest talk and it is terrain now. Children are struck on the wrist for walking on the middles. Not because it is holy. Because it is soft, and a heel opens it, and what is opened cannot be closed.

## The Mark I Have Not Made

I walk alone. You will have been told what that means. Mourning or disgrace, and when a household on the flats sees a single figure they stop the quern and look up until they can tell which.

Her sign was a hand flung open sideways, palm down: the flat arc a gear-rim throws when it sheds its water. She was a returner. All her life she carried the threads down from the eleventh terrace in a cupped tile and poured them back in above the spillway, and they swam up again, and she carried them down again, and she did that for fifty years with the same face she wore for everything else. I have never been able to say whether she thought it accomplished something. There was no way to ask that could survive the crossing.

She is in the flats east of the fen, under the third gantry, where the corona hisses and the condensation drips heaviest and the scale builds fastest. That was a kindness and it was also the opposite of one. Mourning here does not end after a count of days. It ends when you cannot find the edge of the hand with a fingernail. Then you may take an arm again.

I am the one who measures. Do you see the trouble. I go out on alignment mornings with my wedge and I put it to her wrist as I would put it to a rim, and I have the whole plain's rates in my head, and I know to within a season when the crust will close, and twice now I have knelt there and made the mark and then rubbed it out with my thumb. Once because it was early. Once because it was not, and I found I did not want the arithmetic to be finished, and that is a thing I have never written in any pit and will not, because the moss keeps what it is given and it keeps it for longer than shame lasts in a body.

The other thing I have not written: since she went into the floor I have been eating the threads. In the dark, at the low terraces, four or five at a time, which is nothing, which is not even food. I have not told a soul. There is no mechanism for telling. You would need to catch a man's forearm and hold it and shape it, and by the time you had walked to him across two miles of hammering you would have thought better of it, and that is how this basin keeps four hundred people at peace for the length of its wear-record: it does not resolve anything, it simply makes the resolving cost more than the trouble is worth. We are the gentlest people I can imagine. It is not a virtue. It is an acoustic.

## The Fifth

The census says three living, one argued.

The argued one, for me, is the grease. It weeps warm and stiffens cold; it lays itself down in bands; it is fed from a source nobody has found; it takes birds into itself and holds their shapes long after there are no birds and no sky to hold them; and every one of us is waxed in it from the day we are born until the day the scale closes over us.
