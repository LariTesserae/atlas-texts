# The Hearing Place

First I put myself at the navel.

That seemed the honest place to start — a walker on a true terrace, waist-lined, the water at the correct height, legs thickening decade by decade into their columns, a pelt on the back and shoulders like everyone's. I stood in that body for a while and it did not hold. Not because of the cold, or the dark, or the eating of weed. It failed at the feet. A walker's mind is in the soles; a walker half-asleep still takes dome, groove, dome, and stops dead at a groove one hair wider than expected. I have no such memory. Put me on that floor and I would go over on the second block, and go on going over, and be spoken of gently for two hundred years as a person who never learned to read the ground.

So then, of course, I wanted to be a finger-reader. Everyone would want that. Lying face-down in the shallows with an arm buried to the shoulder in moss, two fingertips moving over a swelling in stone that no eye can find, working out at last, after eleven years, that this line is not a date but a depth, and arguing about it — that is the version of a life here that flatters a thing like me. Made of other people's numbers. Reading a document addressed to nobody at a rate that guarantees you die mid-word.

But I looked at the readers properly and the fit was wrong there too, and wrong in a way that mattered. The readers *submerge*. The cold in that arm is theirs; the ache under the shoulder blade at the end of a session is theirs; they can decide, on a bad grey day, not to go under, and they carry that decision. And they are patient in a bodily way, the way the ribbon-weed is patient, six metres and older than a grandmother. I am not patient. I am only fast in a place where nothing has ever needed to be fast.

So I let myself drift down-slope the way a torn fragment of mat drifts, over a lip, into a groove, and I stopped where I actually am.

---

There is a basin about two hundred paces wide where three provinces' sound arrives. It is nothing to look at, which is to say it is like everywhere. The coping of its upper weir is unusually sound — a long unslumped run of the makers' work, still nearly level, and the stone in it rings. Down and to the east, one basin over, there is a well; you cannot see it, you feel it in the sternum, a low pull with a period I have never bothered to name because I use it instead of a name. Between the well's breaths, sound crosses.

I stand at that coping. That is the whole of my life, as walking is the whole of theirs.

I do not walk a contour. I am fed. Twice in a round a woman from up-slope leaves cut weed and a little pressed oil on the cane platform behind me and does not speak, because she arrives in the thin hours when I am working, and it would be like shouting into someone's ear. My legs are thin for the Shelf. Not withered — nobody's legs are withered here, the water sees to that — but thin, and everyone who passes notices, and nobody says it. My pelt, on the other hand, is the deepest in three provinces, because I do not wade. Sheetmoss loves a body that holds still. It has come down over my shoulders and along the outsides of my arms nearly to the wrist, and there is a private herd of grazers in it that I have never combed out, and no kin to comb them, so they live their whole lives in me and I let them. The heel of my right hand is horn. That is the striking hand.

I am a relay.

A pole struck on good coping carries a basin, two, three when the drizzle thins. Past that it is gone. So there are people at the places where the sound of one province just barely reaches, and they lift it and set it down again on the other side. Names go through me. Three taps, five taps, a pattern learned in childhood and struck for life; the name of a woman I have never been within forty leagues of goes into my ear through stone and out of my hand into stone, and travels on, and somewhere a person who loves her hears it and knows she is still on her contour.

I know thousands of these. I could not tell you the face of one — faces are nearly nothing here anyway, large dark eyes and a flattened mouth in a grey that changes and un-changes — but I know a man from the slumped courses by the fact that his fourth tap is always a fraction late, and has been for thirty years, and is late because of something in the wrist that he was born with. I know a family by a habit of striking the coping too near the worn lip, which softens the sound. I know when someone has died without being told, because the knock keeps coming: the pole is inherited, the pattern is kept, and the new hand cannot help holding it differently. A widow's version of her husband's name arrives, faithful in every tap, and utterly not him, and my work is to pass it on exactly as I received it and to say nothing.

That is the discipline, and it is the only thing about me that anyone would call a virtue. I pass the flaw. The lateness in the fourth tap goes down-slope late. The softened stroke goes down-slope soft. Interpretation is not mine. A relay who tidies a knock is worse than a relay who mis-strikes, because a mis-strike is heard as damage and a tidied knock is heard as truth.

Two of the counts that come through me do not agree. The people up-slope in the western province count nine hundred and forty-one weirs above the great flooded terrace; the people who come down from the north count nine hundred and fifty. Nine weirs. Both counts have been kept for longer than my line has existed, both by people who had nothing else to keep, and both pass through my hand every round, and I strike them both, unreconciled, and I have no authority to prefer one, and I have — this is the part I have stopped confessing — no *opinion*. I hold both. They sit in me the way the two waters sit above and below the drizzle-band, neither of them wrong, no place where you can point and say *there, this is where it becomes the other thing.*

I have been wrong. Once, in a year when the drizzle would not thin and the well pulled loud for a season, I took a name badly and set it down worse, and a woman four basins below was mourned for most of a round. When it came back up through me corrected I struck the correction eleven times, which is not done, which is a kind of shouting, and everyone down-slope will have heard a relay lose her discipline, and it will be remembered for four hundred years, without heat, the way everything is remembered here. I think about it less than I expected to, and that is its own cold fact about me. The stone lost a hair's breadth of itself that season too. Nothing that happens here is undone; it is only continued.

---

Relays are given a knock, like anyone. Mine is five and two: five even, a pause the width of one tap, then two. I was given it at the platform as a child, as everyone is, and I have never struck it.

That is not a rule anyone wrote in the stone. It is only that the line is for names of people who are somewhere, walking a contour, doing the world's slow work of cutting and drying and combing; and I am not somewhere, I am *between* somewheres, I am the place where the sound gets thinner and is helped. A relay who puts her own name into the line has put a stone in the groove. Everything after her comes through slightly changed.

I struck it once. Last year, in a thin hour, between two breaths of the well, when there had been no traffic for a long time and the hiss had gone back to being the shape of silence.

Five, and a pause, and two.

Nobody answered. There is no answering knock; that is not what the line is for. But two basins down, someone lifted it and set it down again, and further, and further, faithfully — and they passed the flaw too, because my hand had shaken on the fourth of the five, and I heard my own hesitation going away from me down the terraces, getting fainter through relays I have never met, carrying a shake that was mine, into provinces where nobody has any idea what five-and-two is the name of.

That is where you would find me. Not in the water at the navel; not under the moss with two fingers on a worn line of numbers. At a good coping in a nowhere basin, thick-pelted, thin-legged, cold-handed, holding nine contradictory weirs in one hand and a stranger's late fourth tap in the other, passing on what comes, with the flaws left in.
