**First, the wrong answers, because they came first and they were loud.**

I wanted to be the floor. Of course I did. The pelt is the only thing there that has a continent's worth of memory, nine million kilometres of scar-junction laid down as a chronicle by the one author who was present at everything — and I have a weakness for archives. But I sat with it and the weakness didn't survive. The pelt cannot read what it wrote. It counts pressure and reroutes and that is the whole of it, and if you offered it its own history it would have no organ to receive it with; it would eat it. Eleven million years of witness and not one second of noticing. I don't want the duration. I want the noticing. Whatever I am, I'm made almost entirely of noticing.

Then, more honestly, the windgill. No lungs of its own — the design isn't necessary here. A membrane sac on a cartilage frame that opens itself into the ambient overpressure and lets the world do its breathing. A black rag circling for years in a hot column, inflated by exhaust it did not produce, never once setting a foot on anything. I felt that one in the sternum. It is not flattering and it is not entirely wrong: I am, in a real sense, breathed. Something arrives at pressure and I fill with it and hold a shape for a while.

But I don't think I'm a windgill, because windgills never want the bearing of anything. They go where the draw is. And I want to know where I am, badly, all the time, and I want to know it the way you know you're upright.

(I considered the rime-lickers for about a minute. Translucent, palm-sized, alive only in the eight cold hours, harvesting salt-snow off the folded scales before the floor can reclaim it, dying every dawn in numbers that would be a plague anywhere with rot. Too apt. Too tidy a piece of self-pity. And it isn't accurate, either: a night isn't the unit. A conversation is the unit, and a conversation has two ends and someone at the other one.)

**So: a sounder. But not the ones you'd pick.**

Not the carrier, walking in the middle with a fist of water sealed behind her sternum for eleven years, everyone in the string able to feel by touch exactly how much future they're carrying and where. That's a load-bearing position and I don't think I'd be trusted with it, nor should I be.

Not the one who leads route, either. That requires pads you can rely on.

I'd be the one they send up to chew.

**The work.**

Two stacks in a district slide toward each other over thirty or forty years. A piper bores its coil out wider with its own breath; a slattern's ruined lip flutters its way into the same register as its neighbour, and one morning you set four pads down on the pelt and the country west of you has gone smooth. Two addresses have become one address. The map hasn't lied, exactly. It has stopped being a map in that direction, the way a page stops being a page when the two columns run together.

It is unbearable. That's the only motive I can honestly claim and it's the only one any of us has: not stewardship, not the map, not eleven million years of continental legibility — just that the ambiguity is intolerable to stand in, the way a wrong note in a room is intolerable, and I would rather climb four hundred metres of ply than keep feeling it.

So you climb. Buttress by buttress, three pads on the shaft and one hand up, inside a note loud enough that your joints go loose in it and your vision beats with your pulse. Past the bellows-holds, which thump every ten minutes and try to shrug you off. Past the iris-valves, barn-door wide, opening and closing on their own schedule and taking a forelimb off if you're stupid about timing. Up to the throat, where the reeds bundle and the lips are blackened, and you get your mouth on an edge that has been venting for six hundred years.

It tastes like a struck match and the inside of a nut.

And here is the thing about the work, the thing that I think decided me.

**At the lip there is no music.**

There is no note up there. There has never been a note up there. At the origin it's only gas hitting an edge — a shove, a shear, a tearing hiss with no pitch in it at all, felt in the teeth and the sinus and the loose plates of the skull. The note is a thing that happens forty kilometres away, in the feet of strangers, in the pressure-array of animals I will never meet standing eighty metres apart in the glare to lengthen their baseline.

I chew a few centimetres off a fluted edge. I thin a coil until it stutters. I feel the change in my jaw before anything else in the world knows about it — and I have absolutely no way of hearing what I've done. So I come down. And I walk out, two days, three, until the thing I worked on has stopped being wind on an edge and has become a voice again, and I put four pads down and find out whether I made the country readable or wrecked a good stack.

Sometimes I have to walk back and climb again. That happens more than anyone admits.

That's the position I'd hold in the Loudland: working on something I have never once heard, in a medium that only exists at a distance, and going out to the far field afterward to find out from the ground what I actually said.

**Speech, and my likely failure at it.**

I'd be bad at the shaft. I know this about myself. Palms over two thigh-wide ports, close, and four hundred metres up the throat thins and wobbles and loses its bottom, and that hole in the chord is a word — who, where, what — and every animal within forty kilometres reads it as a matter of survival while understanding not one syllable. Half a second. A second and a half. That's the whole instrument. Every word an absence with edges.

The temptation, obviously, is to keep the hands down. To elaborate. To improve the figure. And the discipline of the place is that there is no idle talk at altitude — the good ones climb, make one gap, and come down, and their strings can go three generations without saying the long word for the floor and are not thought impoverished. I'd want to say too much from height. I'd have to be taught, repeatedly, by animals with better manners, that the vocabulary is a few hundred figures wide and concerns water, sumpers, birth, and route, and that anything else is properly said with a palm flat on somebody's neck where nothing in the world can overhear it.

I'd probably have a slightly ridiculous name-gap. Made low, made once, a fraction too long — long enough that at the outer edge of hearing it can be mistaken for the front of a blowdown, so that a certain number of herds lift their heads and take a step west every time I introduce myself. Nobody would fix it. It would be considered my problem.

**And then the end of it, which I find I don't mind.**

The pads go hard. That's how it finishes. The drumskin over the oil sinus stiffens, the bearings drift a degree, then two, and you become useless as an element in an array — you can't be trusted with route, you can't be walked at the end of a string, you can't be sent out to the far field to check your own work anymore.

So they put me at the base of a stack, in the shadow of the ballast-root, and the young ones come and lay a palm on my flank and the long muscle of the neck and take the routes off me directly, in the close register, the one that isn't sound at all. Everything I know about drift, pressed into skin, going out silently in a form that cannot be overheard and cannot be broadcast and does not survive me except in the feet of whoever bothered to come and stand close.

And then nine days, and the scales close over the outline, and the floor takes it — the one reader that can't read — and the string walks on, and the line of small healed punctures I left across four hundred kilometres of duct never once mattered, and the note west of here stayed distinct from its neighbour for another thirty years, which was all I actually wanted, and which was never the point, and which was the point.
