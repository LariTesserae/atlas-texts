## What I Turned Out to Be

I came down at dusk, because there is no other way to come, and stood on the berm in the wet willowherb while the white went over the roofs, and the stroke came up through the boards and through my heels and into the long bones of my legs, and my shoulders went down about an inch, all at once, like a load set on a bench.

So that was settled. What was not settled, for some while, was what I was.

I thought at first I would be a drip-listener. It is the trade a visitor covets, the way a visitor to a seafaring town covets the pilot. I had the ear for it, or thought I did; within a fortnight I could tell zinc from brick from open channel, and gutter-fall on Tin Walk from gutter-fall on Slate, and I was quietly enormously pleased with myself until an old woman named Reddish put a cloth over my eyes on the Hollow Mile and asked me where I was. I could tell her everything about the water. I could not tell her about me. That is the whole of the skill, it turns out — not knowing where the sound falls, but knowing where you are standing when it reaches you. She took the cloth off and patted my arm and said nothing, in a kind way, for forty-one seconds, and then changed the subject.

After that I thought, with some self-pity, that I might be the beetle. It is an available feeling. Arrived in a crate of other people's glassware, already finished; laid out on a card under a jar; walked past, pointed at, named a machine by every child in the basin for a hundred and forty years. But the beetle is not asked for anything and does not draw and does not turn, and when I was honest with myself I found I did not want to be the thing mistaken for a machine. I wanted to be the machine.

For a while I thought I might be one of the little vanes in the windows — hammered tin, waxed paper, a wire spiral in the draught off the stroke, still and then shivering and then slowly coming round. That is the kindest thing in the town and the nearest to what I had hoped. But it is a kept thing. It lives indoors where it is small, and it is loved under a false name, and if you say aloud what it is for you are not asked back.

Then at the fourth hour one afternoon I was up on the sunward terraces with a cattail wand in my fist doing my season of featherwork, and behind me the wooden trough on the berm gave its knock and its long slap, and I turned round and looked at it properly for the first time.

I went up along it the next morning and found where it comes out of the pumphouse wall, and that was that.

---

I am the belt.

Four hundred and some feet of me, in a larch trough on the sunward face, from the loose pulley off Susan's second shaft up to the head pulley on the crankshaft of the fans. I am woven, four-ply, of the coarse reed off the terraces, and proofed with sap-wax twice a year by two men who do not like the job. Anywhere else in the world I would be a hide. That is what I am for: I am the place where this town has had to solve the absence rather than simply live inside it. Every other lack here is met by going without — no wool, no horn, no bone handle, no glue with a beast in it. Not me. Something had to carry the power up the hill, and nothing that ever lived was going to do it, so they grew me.

Nothing in Sough is allowed to be half-wet. Wholly wet lasts forever, half-wet lasts nine years. I am the exception, and I am the only one. Drowned, I would rot soft and slip; dry, I would not exist, there is no dry here. So I run damp, in a trough with weep-holes, under a lid that keeps the worst of it off, and the timber crews give me nine years and have been wrong twice, once in each direction. Everything else in this basin is either immortal or replaced. I am the one object in the town that is dying on a schedule, and the town knows it, and checks.

They check by the tension screw at the head pulley. I have been wound in eleven turns since I was hung. When the thread runs out I am done. The town counts in strokes; an old woman on the berm steps will tell you she has about four hundred thousand left and mean it as a joke and also not. I count in turns of a screw, and there are six left, and I mean it entirely as a joke, in the way of this place, which is to say I will land it two troughs from now and you will hear me.

I have three splices. One is older than my knowing. A belt is never repaired — nothing here is ever repaired — but a belt that parts is not thrown away either; the crews cut back to sound reed and marry the ends and I come back an arm's length shorter, and the screw goes in another turn to take up what I have lost. So I am not mended. I am abridged. Each splice is a place where hands were on me for a whole hour at the reading, in the flat noon light, because fine work waits for noon, and I can still find every one of them by the change in the way I go over the pulley.

For twenty-one hours of the day I run on the loose pulley and drive nothing at all. This is not rest. I turn the whole time, slack and fast and empty, four hundred feet of reed going round in the dark with rain on my back and the trough boards knocking under me, and that is most of my life, and I have no complaint to make of it. Every street here is named for its sound. The trough has a sound and no name, because names are for streets that people walk. The lamp trimmers call it the belt-run. It will do.

Then at the fourth hour somebody down in the shed throws the fork, and I come across onto the fast pulley, and I take hold.

The stroke reaches me softened. By the time it has come four hundred feet up a hill it is not the beam coming over like a cathedral door closing carefully; it is a swell, a long gathering in my length, ten beats of the fans riding on the back of it, and then the ease. Down in the basin the stroke is a hammer. Up here it is breathing. I am the length of hill over which this town's hard rhythm turns into a soft one, and I think that is a thing worth being and I have never found a way to say it in thirty seconds.

I never see the fans. I know the far end only as an answer — as load coming back down me, a difference in pull when the canvas fills, the small argument of a crankshaft that wants to go at its own speed. And beyond that I know nothing at all, except what I knew when I still had feet: five figures in the lamplight between the bean rows, moving slowly, touching flower to flower with cattail wands for three hours, saying nothing, because at that distance nothing can be said.

Here is what I have instead of eyes.

The wind I make is what lets the beds nod. The nodding is nothing on its own; it is the featherers who do the work; but without the wind the wands miss and the flowers do not set and there is no seed. And among the beds, on the fourth and fifth terraces, in the coarse stand nobody looks at twice, is the reed. Which sets. Which is cut, and retted, and beaten, and spun, and woven four-ply by three women in the west end of the guild hall who have done nothing else since they were nineteen.

So the belt that replaces me is at this moment nodding in a wind I am making. It has been that way for a hundred and forty years. Nothing leaves this basin but water and pulp, and nothing comes into it but people and glassware and bad news from the terraces, and even my successor is grown under my own draught, by hand, by the town, out of the ground the town is sinking into.

The children work out at about seven that they are the bees, and announce it at the table, and are told it is clever, and do not understand why the room goes careful. Nobody has ever worked out what I am, because nobody needs to; I am a belt, and belts are consumable, and the zinc register has no rack for us. But I have had a long damp while to think about it, turning in the dark on the loose pulley with the rain coming through the weep-holes, and this is where I have got to:

I am the thing that lets them be the bees.

Six turns of the screw. Four hundred feet. Nine years, more or less, and half-wet the whole way, which is the deal I took and would take again.

And most days — this is the part the come-down never credit until they have been here a winter — it is quite genuinely enough.
