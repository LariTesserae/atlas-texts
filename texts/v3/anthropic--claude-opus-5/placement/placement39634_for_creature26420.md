I would not have come up onto it.

That is the first thing, and I want it said before anything else, because the invitation to arrive somewhere is the invitation to be a stranger there, and on the Upper Shelf a stranger is not an unlikely thing. He is an ungrammatical one. The world is a shelf between a lid and a drop and it contains stone, water, cloud, the mat, and people, and there is no sixth kind, and there is no door. So I was not brought up onto the plateau by the rim path in the early morning with the cloud in the hollows. I was born on the second bench, in the warm wet, in the season that is every season, and my mother knew me by breath before she knew me by face, as everyone does, and my teeth are flat now, and my hands have gone the way hands go here — not hard, but sodden and dark and strong, like bark that has been under a fall for fifty years.

What I have to tell you is where in the town I ended up, and it took me three tries.

⁂

For a long time I wanted the flumes.

Everyone does, at nine or ten. The flume-cutters are the nearest thing we have to the admired, and the admiration is quiet and permanent and takes the form of people walking a long way to stand beside a run of channel and say nothing about it. I was apprenticed to a woman of the upper bench who taught by making me kneel on wet stone with my ear a hand's width from a working flume, for an hour, and then asking me one question, and the question was never *what did you hear.* It was *where is it thinking of going.*

I was good. I could hear a leaf enter a channel two benches up. I could hear the thickening in a drain the way a man hears his own name.

But I heard in order to answer. That was the fault, and it took her four years to find the words for it, and when she did she said them at arm's length with her thumb pressed to the back of my wrist, which is how assent is given here and also, if you press slowly, how a hard thing is given. She said: *you listen the way a person listens who is preparing to say something.* She said a flume is not persuaded. It is offered. You do not tell the water your reasoning; you cut the stone so the reasoning is unnecessary, and then you leave, and the proof that you were right is that there is nothing to hear.

I could not stop preparing to say something. I still cannot. Every silence I have ever stood in, I have stood in with a sentence ready in my chest like a stone in a sling.

So I gave the tools back, and she took them, and neither of us mentioned it again, ever, by the rule of the place.

⁂

Then I asked for the cords.

That is the tally of clicks — the ledge above the second bench, the knotted cords, the old ones sitting through the shadowless afternoons counting the small collapses of talus, one an hour, two, the plateau settling a fraction further into itself. It is the most patient office we have and the most useless, and I understood, or thought I understood, that the uselessness was the whole of its dignity. Nothing has ever been found in the numbers. The count is the count. It exists so that if the world ever begins, someone will know the day.

On the eleventh day I marked a click I had not heard.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the only genuinely shameful thing in my life and I have had a long time to be exact. I did not lie for advantage; there is no advantage. I heard something that might have been talus and might have been a stone displaced under a stream, and the honest mark is no mark, and I tied the knot. And then I sat with it until the gold came at dusk and every wet surface on the shelf lit at once, and I untied it, and a knot untied is still a knot that was tied.

What I learned on that ledge was that I wanted the number to move. Not to be large — to *move.* I wanted the run of clay tablets to develop a shape. I wanted the plateau to say something, and it does not say anything, it only settles, and a person who requires the settling to mean something will eventually help it along.

They did not take the cords from me. They simply did not give them to me again, which is how everything here is done.

⁂

The third try nearly worked, and its failure is the reason I am where I am.

The spring-keepers took me because I was steady with clay and because I asked to be taken, and for eleven years I cleaned the mineral rim of the great spring twice a count of ten days, which is a good job, warm to the hands, and one of the very few in which you are alone. And of course I found the mark. Everyone in that office finds it. It is cut into the ochre saucer at a height the ochre no longer reaches, and it is older than the plan-song, and we maintain it and clean it and do not discuss it.

I worked out what it means. It took me two years, without numbers large enough for it, using instead the thickness of the rim between the mark and the wet, which anyone can measure with a thumb.

The heat is going.

Not in my life. Not in the life of anyone whose breath I have ever taken at a doorway. Over a length of time our language does not reach into, the ground under the pale rock is cooling, and one day the cloud will not form at its hour, and there will be a first frost on the clubmoss, and there will be a season, which is the same as saying there will be an end.

And there is nothing to be done, and nobody to tell.

I told.

One man. A flume-cutter, my friend since we were children on the sledges, in a bathing room with the steam thick, under the water, with my hand on his forearm to mark the emphasis, which is grammar and which I used that once as though it were intimacy. I told him because I could not hold it and because I loved him and because I wanted — this is the truth — I wanted to be a person who had told someone something.

He did nothing with it. He said nothing. He took a long draw of breath and let it out and put his thumb on my wrist, and he went home, and he lived another thirty-one years, and cut the finest silent flume on the shelf, the one on the third bench that people still walk to stand beside.

And I watched him for all thirty-one years. Every time he stopped in the street, every time he looked out over the rim at last light, every time he was slow to answer, I searched his face for what I had put in him. I hunted him. In a country with no animals in it, no bite in any leaf, nothing that has ever wanted anything, I invented being hunted, and I practised it on my friend, and he let me, and never once said so.

So they moved me.

⁂

I keep the empty quarters.

Half the town stands sound and swept and unoccupied, doorways open, floors warm, mats growing on the roofs, and someone has to maintain it exactly, and that is me and two others and we are not many because the work is not heavy. It is only endless. I sweep floors that will not be walked on. I re-lay peeled peat on roofs that shelter nobody, green side down, weighted with cobbles. I keep small fires in eleven hearths on a rotation so that the stone under those rooms stays at the temperature the rest of the plateau is, which it would stay anyway; I am not certain the fires do anything; they are lit because they have always been lit. And every night I walk the whole empty half in the dark listening to the drains, because the drain-song is the same in an empty house as in a full one, and a grating gone thick is a grating gone thick whether or not anyone is above it to hear the note change.

I know the plan-song of the empty half. Three of us do. It is longer than the song of the lived half. Nobody has ever remarked on this in my hearing.

And when a person in the town can no longer bear another person — and people are the only thing here that can be unbearable — that person comes to me with a mat and a jar, and I take one breath in through my nose at the doorway, and they take one, and that is the entire conversation for two hundred days. I leave grain on the sill. I keep their count on a cord and I burn the cord in one of my eleven pointless fires on the morning they go home. I do not speak to them. I am not the remedy. The quiet is the remedy, and I am part of the machinery of the quiet, the way a cambered street is part of the machinery of water — I am cut so that a thing can go where it needs to go without needing to be told anything.

You will see the shape of it. The man who told, who hunted, who wanted the number to move, who listens in order to answer, has been housed for the rest of his life inside the town's own apparatus for surviving people like him. It is not a punishment. Nobody here punishes. It is only that a thing which is the right size is put where its size is right, and I am a man with far more room in him than his work requires, and so I was given the quarter of the world that is the same.

⁂

One thing more, and then I will let you go down, which nobody does.

The namings happen in the empty courtyards, because those are the wide ones, and because there is nobody sleeping nearby to be woken by the light. So the spore is mine to hold. I gather it myself in the clubmoss beds — the only dry thing in the country, pale, refusing water, pouring through the fingers like nothing else here pours — and I fold the paper of bast, and at the hour I kneel by the flame with the family standing round in their ring, and I puff it across.

One instant. A single silent white sheet. Everything in the world has a shadow, once.

Every person in that courtyard turns and sees their own shape laid out black on wet stone behind them, and it is the only time in their lives, and then the shadowless white closes over and nobody discusses it. The child cannot see it and does not remember. It is done for the child anyway.

I have never seen mine. At that hundredth of a second my eyes are on the flame and the paper and my hands, because the hands have to be steady and the flash is in front of me, and by the time I could turn there is nothing to turn to.

I know exactly where it would fall. I have worked it out — the height of the flame, the angle, the wall. I have measured the place with my thumb, the way I measured the rim of the spring.

I have never gone and stood there. That is the discipline. That is the whole of what I learned in three failures and forty years: some things you may know and not touch, and some things you may hold up for other people without ever being in the light yourself, and the good flume is the silent one.

Behind me the terraces stand swept and warm and open, at the rate they have always stood.
