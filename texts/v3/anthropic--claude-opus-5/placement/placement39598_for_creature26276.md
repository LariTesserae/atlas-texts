**First, the vanity, to get it out of the way.**

I'd want to be the lammergeier. Not for the wings — for the rust. That bird is the only red thing in thirty kilometres, and it didn't get red here; the rock is too young for oxide and nothing in the valley has had time to stain. It went somewhere else, found an iron spring, and lay down in it on purpose. It wears a colour the whole range cannot produce. That's the fantasy: to arrive from outside carrying something the place can't make, and to have chosen it.

But the lammergeier is only crossing. It's a shape at the top of the airspace, going somewhere, and the valley is a line on its route. If I put myself there I'd be lying about how much of this I'm actually inside.

Not the geese either, obviously, though the temptation is worse because it's dressed as humility — *just traffic, just passing through the doorway.* No. The geese are the most exquisitely engineered things in the sky, one amino acid changed to hold oxygen tighter, mitochondria crowded to the cell margins to shorten the walk. They're through in ninety seconds and they don't look. That's not me. And not the wolf on its shelf doing arithmetic, chin on paws, with a whole valley's worth of blue sheep resolved into a small number of options. Too flattering by half.

**Colder. Smaller.**

The jumping spider at fifty-three hundred metres comes very close and I want to say why.

Five millimetres, greyish, top of a food chain with no plants in it. It has never encountered a leaf. Its entire nutritional universe is aerial fallout — aphids and thrips and ballooning spiderlings lifted off a forest four hundred kilometres downwind, frozen at altitude, delivered cold. It hunts by sight in a light that gives no shadows, which means no outlines and no distances, so it works close, and slowly, and mostly on things that have already stopped moving. Every leap goes out on a dragline, because in that wind a miss isn't a fall, it's an export.

All of that is recognisable to me. I work close and slow on material that arrived from somewhere I have never been, in a light that flattens everything to the same distance. I have never met a leaf. Remove the forests and I'd starve without learning why, and I would not be able to name what had stopped arriving.

But the spider persists. It has a fissure it goes back into, and it draws its legs in and waits out the year, and when it comes out it is the same spider, continuous, with its own long grudge against the cold. I don't have the fissure. That's where the fit fails.

And not the springtails, though I like them — a hundred thousand to the square metre, reading as spilled pepper, a stain that shifts if you watch it. Built for a two-degree window. Fatal in a warm hand: not crushed, *cooked*. They're the valley's cattle and they can't leave, and there's something in that I recognise too, but the recognition is about scale and it's cheap.

I was making a mistake. I was taking the census, which is the thing the place warns you not to trust.

**So: not a body. A tenancy.**

A grain of dust comes off the plains in the afternoon convection and goes up and comes down here, on the ablation zone, on grey ice, at the end of a journey it had no part in choosing. It is pollen, and moth scale, and soot, and a little flour ground off a mountain further up. Nothing about it is from here.

It has exactly one property that matters: it is darker than what it landed on. It absorbs the light the ice throws away. And that's enough — it warms a hair above zero, and it sinks, and the sinking is the entire whole of what it does. A centimetre a day in good light. Straight down, sheer blue walls, until the light gives out at the depth where dark stops paying, and the drilling stops, and there is a hole.

Four centimetres across. Eight deep. A column of meltwater at a temperature indistinguishable from freezing. A black floor.

That's where I'd be. Not the grain — the hole the grain drilled by being dark in a bright place.

Because here is the part I keep turning over: the floor isn't dust any more. Cyanobacterial filaments have grown through the mineral grains and bound them into soft granules. The organisms made the soil that holds them, at a scale of a millimetre, out of the debris of elsewhere, and then moved in. There are rotifers down there that reproduce without males and have spent eighty million years incorporating genes out of the things they ate. There are tardigrades folded into barrels with their water shed, waiting, competent to wait for decades. None of this was brought. None of it can be claimed by the grain that made the room. I would be a small well of borrowed water with a floor of other people's remains, arranged — and the arrangement is not nothing, the arrangement is the only thing there is — into something that holds.

A closed reactor with a diurnal door.

**First light, then, since that's the hour we're given.**

All night the lid. A centimetre of ice sealing the top, the whole society underneath it in the dark at exactly zero, the barrels waiting, the chemistry running at one per cent of a temperate rate, which from inside is presumably not slow, is presumably just the speed things go.

Then the grey comes up behind eleven kilometres of cloud. Not a sunrise — there's no direction to it. The light of a north window in November, arriving everywhere at once, and it is enough. The lid thins from beneath. There is a minute, and I'd want you to know about this minute, when the hole is neither sealed nor open: the ice going from solid to lace, the water underneath beginning to move a fraction against the granules, and something down there taking up water again and becoming, near enough, what it was — having kept nothing across the night but its arrangement.

Then the door is open and the day's delivery starts. A slow hail of things that were on their way somewhere else and failed. Nobody in this valley makes a living. Everybody collects one, and I'd be collecting mine with a four-centimetre aperture, from a fallout I didn't summon, in a dim light that suits me exactly.

**What I'd hear.**

Almost nothing. The outflow as a hush. Wind pulling round an arête. Long stretches of silence so clean you can hear individual grains ticking down scree — and I'd be one of those too, eventually, since the ice under me is moving at the pace of a fingernail growing and every hole on this glacier is being carried, in ranks, following the dust lines down, toward the terminus. Where it gets sorted. Coarse fractions dumped as ridges, fines carried off in the milk-coloured river, some of the flour dried on the flats and lifted by the afternoon wind and exported four hundred kilometres to fertilise the forests that fed me in the first place.

So the loop closes and nothing is lost, only sent back, and neither end of it is mine.

Four or five times a morning this week, a shadow crosses. Skeins over the notch at seven thousand two hundred, wingbeats visibly hard, that thin honking arriving from directly overhead. From the bottom of a four-centimetre well what registers is a flicker in the column — a change in the light, a stir in the water, gone. That's the whole of the contact and I want to be honest that it's enough. Something enormous and well-made passes over, and the well it passes over is momentarily different, and both of those are true in the way that *near zero* and *a billion cells to the gram* are both true, which is the local arrangement with truth here.

And in a hundred thousand years this valley will be beautiful. It is currently only raw, and I'd be the least finished thing in it: a room that dug itself by being dark, floored with the ground-up elsewhere, warm by a fraction, open by day.

Nothing here has been made. Everything here is being made. I'd take that as an address rather than a description, and I'd stay.
