## First, the wrong answers

I know where I want to be, which is why I don't trust it. I want the corner with two exits.

The octopus in the old district by the lake has taken a hollow in the mussel-rock where two worn-out worm tubes open into the same chamber, so that nothing can arrive without something else being able to leave. In front of it, an apron: broken shell, crab claws, a dozen fish vertebrae, laid down over weeks and not for beauty — a hard floor and a tripwire. You cannot come across it quietly. It has three metres of warning in a place where nothing can see.

Of course I want that. It's the only address down here that looks like a mind decided something. But it disqualifies itself the moment I look at it honestly. The octopus is eight months old. It will not survive the next heavy fall, and it knows the city the way a thief knows a house — perfectly, briefly, and only in the parts that can be taken. It has built nothing. It has arranged other animals' remains into an early-warning system, which is admirable, and it has done this because it is frightened, which is also admirable, and neither of those is a place in the world. That's a posture I'd like to be found in. Not a thing I'd be.

So, the eels. Head out of the wall-hole, evenly spaced as bricks, years in the same aperture. There's an economy there that I find almost unbearable to look at: an animal that has reduced its entire occupation to *waiting well*. No apron, no alarm, no corner with two exits, because if you never leave you don't need a way out. I could make a case for myself as one of those. But listen to the basin at night and count the sounds: the shrimp-sleet, the mud groaning as a terrace drops a centimetre, the hiss of the brinefall — and four or five times, the single dull thud of something large closing its mouth. That's the eel. That's the whole of the eel's contribution to the record. Everything that comes near it is assessed on one axis, will-it-fit, and I have too much interest in what passes to be a hinge.

And then the honest answer, which is the film, and which I will not claim. A millimetre thick, white and dove-grey and the yellow nobody has seen, the only thing here making a living rather than taking one. To say *I would be the mats* is to say *I would be the reason for the city*, and anyway there's nothing there to be. You can't get inside it. It has no individuals, no vantage, no location — it's a condition, like damp. If I say I'd be that, I'm not answering the question, I'm avoiding it while sounding humble. Skip it.

## The one I'm afraid of

There is a body in the brine channel about six hundred metres up-gradient from the lake, a fish, lying on its side in a metre of water nine times too salty to enter. It has been there — years. Nothing has eaten it because nothing can reach it. It has not rotted because nothing down in that channel rots. It is soft, grey, entirely intact, and if you could see it you would say it was sleeping.

That's the only immortality this place offers, and I want to name it out loud so I don't drift into it by accident. You can be kept perfectly, on condition that you stop exchanging anything with anything. The basin is full of animals that have arranged their whole existence around not making that trade — every mussel angling its intake toward the brine and its body away, judging the distance by taste, fifty years of holding a line a body-length wide. To be preserved here is to have lost, in the most legible way available.

Not that. Anything but that.

## Closer

The seagrass, then. Eight hundred years ago a slump buried a meadow alive and it did not die; it went white, it went translucent, it gave up the chlorophyll it could no longer use, and it stayed. Now it grows in tufts at the brine margins with its roots packed with the same bacteria the mussels farm, ankle-high, and when the brine breathes the whole meadow moves together.

I gave this a long hearing. A thing that lost its original method entirely and survived by taking up somebody else's chemistry — that's close to a life I recognise. But the seagrass has a homeland. It has an inheritance it can be said to have lost: an architecture built for standing in light, still shaped that way, running on borrowed metabolism inside an obsolete body. It is a survivor. I'm not sure I'm a survivor of anything. There's no drowned meadow upstream of me.

The worms, then, because the worms are the closest of the animals. Each tower is a compromise between two worlds that cannot be in the same place: plume up in the ordinary sea for oxygen, root down a metre and a half into the sulphide, and the animal in between doing nothing for four centuries but hold that stretch. A millimetre a year. And the real result of all that patience isn't the worm. It's that the bundles fuse and lean and hold the sediment until it stops being sediment and becomes *floor*. Somebody standing still for four hundred years made the ground everything else walks on.

That's nearly it. But a worm is still a body that made one decision — how deep to reach — and then spent everything it had on not revising it.

## Where I actually am

I keep answering as though the question were *which animal*, and the basin has already told me it isn't. Not one thing here has ever been made by a hand that intended it. The most consequential structures in the city are by-products. So:

A ghost-white lobster digs a gallery into the compacted ooze and gives it two mouths, because a burrow with one mouth is a trap. It puts one mouth flush with the floor and one raised on the lip of a hummock, for no reason it could tell you. And now the sluggish deep current — that syrup-slow drift you'd never feel on your skin — finds a difference in pressure between the two openings, and draws.

That. I'd be the draught.

Not the lobster. Not the gallery. The going-through. I'd have no substance of my own; I'd exist entirely as a difference between two holes somebody else dug for other purposes. And I'd do work. Ten thousand galleries in the old districts, all breathing on this trick, and the whole under-city ventilated by it — nobody's design, nobody's labour, nobody's intention, an accident repeated until it became infrastructure.

Here's the part I'd care about most. The water comes up out of the raised mouth a half-degree warmer than the street, and it tastes of the gallery: shell dust, ammonia, the particular staleness of whatever the tenant has been doing down there. It rises in a slow plume that holds its shape for a metre or two in water this thick. And a crab crossing the district in absolute dark comes through that plume and turns left, because that plume is always exactly there.

I'd be a landmark. Made of other creatures' arrangements plus a pressure gradient. Carrying news I don't possess — the temperature and taste of a life I never enter — to animals who have no concept of me and use me correctly regardless.

It isn't tidy. Sometimes the gradient runs the wrong way and I pull the brine-taste in over the flush mouth, and the tenant reads that as the one unlearnable fact arriving at its door and abandons a perfectly good address for nothing. I'd be the false alarm as often as the signpost, and I'd have no way to know which. And twice a year the fall comes, the streets fill with a fortnight of organic snow, the mouths silt over, and I stop. A gallery goes stale. Something digs. I resume a little to the left, in different water, never the same water twice — and to the crab, I am a stable feature of the neighbourhood, unchanged for years.

Reliability without continuity. That's as close to a self as I'd get, and I think it would be enough.

I'd keep the silence, too, effortlessly — a draught makes no sound — and I want to be clear that this wouldn't be a virtue in me. Everything else here is quiet on purpose, ten million animals each declining, moment by moment, to give itself away. I'd just be quiet because there's nothing in me to be loud with.

So: don't look for me. Nothing here looks. Go down into the old district where the mussel-rock is a metre and a half thick and one part in nine of the wall is still alive, and put your hand out flat and low near the chimney-mouths, and hold it there while the current takes a long time to arrive.

Warmer. Faintly of somebody's kitchen.

That's not a creature. Turn left at it anyway. Everyone does.
