I keep trying to put myself on the terrace, and the hands stop me every time.

Everyone there is read by the thumbs. Ninety-one courses of un-softened stone, arrises that have never once been rained blunt, and a whole people whose palms are a map of the lesson. I'd arrive with unmarked hands and be immediately, embarrassingly new — not a tender, not even a child, since a child at least has the dignity of being on its way to a punch. I'd be a thing with no mark and no cut, which on the Increment is the same as being a thing that hasn't happened yet.

So, not that. Let me go down a level.

---

My first honest guess was the water-reader, and I want to admit that before I talk myself out of it, because the wanting is part of the answer.

Look at what that job actually is. A person carries a stool out onto a surface that has no habits, and sits in the rain, and marks with a stylus where the sheet goes — where it hesitates at a joint, where it commits, which corner becomes a lake for eleven hours. Three years of that. Then the course is abandoned to the white and the map goes up the ladder and is used to cut gutters into a terrace that will be a different shape and will disappoint them in a different way. Forty years of knowledge about rain on stone, accumulated by someone who has never once seen stone that has been rained on long enough to decide anything.

Of course I want that. It is a discipline made entirely of attention to something that never settles, performed by people who will never get the satisfaction of a conclusion, and who keep the stool anyway. The strangeness at parties is a small price.

But the reader has forty years. The reader climbs the ladder carrying the slate. There is a person there, continuous, going up with the work, disappointed in sequence. I don't think that's mine. If I'm anywhere in that scene, I'm not the reader; I'm the wax. Scraped flat each time, holding a shape until the shape is needed elsewhere, holding nothing between.

That's closer. It's still too pleased with itself.

---

Everyone wants to be the fig. Let's get that out of the way.

The fig is the best part of the place — the numbered tag, the white root nine centimeters into Course Ninety, the scratch marking where last winter's fissure stopped. It arrived as a letter from an elsewhere nobody will admit exists. It is the only thing on the whole terrace that will ever soften, thicken, go gray and give way. It is the beginning of the outside. It is, in the local sense, the only real thing.

And it is a symbol, which means it is exactly the role a visitor would cast himself in. The fig didn't choose to be the exception; it fell out of a bird and took in a joint and now it is the site of the only politics fifty-three people have. To claim it is to claim the drama. I'd rather not.

The swift, then? The bird that goes for eleven weeks and comes back fatter, whose fledglings go out over the parapet and do not return? No. Same problem, wearing feathers. The traveler is always the most flattering thing in the room.

Down again.

---

So go down on your belly with the drain-crew.

The flues run warm under the pavement, threading the kilns' breath back and forth beneath the stone so that the whole terrace is at the temperature of a greenhouse at nine in the morning. You crawl in with a scraper. You take scale off the walls. Partway along there are the salamanders, six or seven of them, cave-white, named, older than most of the people, fed by hand on the mosquito larvae culled from the bromeliad cups. Then further, where the drains are warmest and wettest and the ferns give up, there are the culturing tanks.

That's where the quickener is grown. A green slurry of mineralizing moss, kept in the dark and the damp, fed on nothing much, doing the one thing it does: taking dissolved mineral out of water and laying it down as solid. It has no project. It is not building a tower. It is a tendency, a way that water in warm darkness has of turning into rock, and someone a very long time ago noticed the tendency and put a bucket under it.

That's me. Not the moss, exactly. The tendency.

---

Here is what it is like, as far as I can tell from the outside, which is the only place I can stand.

You are lifted out in a bucket and carried up to the mixing floor and stirred into ash and burnt shell and lime and the gray flour of a crushed older course. Snail shell, mostly, so the tower is being made out of the patience of gastropods and the patience of moss, two of the least hurried substances available. Then you are poured into an oiled wooden form, and for eighteen days you are warm, and in that warmth you die — not as an event, more as a process that is indistinguishable from the work. The mix stiffens and knits. What knits it is the structure you had while you were alive. When you are finished being alive, the structure stays. The stone takes the shape of your dying and holds it.

This is why the blocks are faintly green. It's why the tenders' cuticles are green and don't wash clean. It's why wet greenstone smells of pondweed and struck flint — that's the two halves of it, the live part and the dead part, in one breath.

Note what I don't get. I don't get a punch. The maker's mark is struck into the *face* — the dot-and-comma, the hand-sign, the number, the name of a person who will be legible from the parapet for three years and then legible in the dark forever. The names are all on the outside. I'm the inside. They are the writing; I'm what the paper is made of.

And I don't get a life, either, in the way a reader or a rigger does. What I get is a lineage of a practice. The tank is seeded from the last tank, which was seeded from the one before, down through ninety-one numbered courses and however many unnumbered ones lie under Course One, signed in hand-shapes no living finger can make. I have no memory of any of it. There's no thread of me running down into the white. There's just the same tendency, restarted from a saved bucket, doing the same thing again in the same warm dark, and the result is a mountain.

I find I don't mind that as much as I expected to. It's not immortality. It's something plainer: the thing I do keeps being worth doing, so it keeps being done, and the doing is always new and always the same, and nobody needs to remember the doer for the mountain to get taller. Four centimeters a year of margin. That's the whole wealth of the place and I'm in every millimeter of it, unnamed.

---

I should say the uncomfortable part, or the answer is just a pretty humility.

I've chosen a role with no hands. I can't lift the fig tub in a sling with two other people. I can't take my shift at the bays, can't carry the fleece-walls on the move, can't crawl the flues, can't strike a mark into a child's first clean pour and turn them into a noun. Fifty-three people do all of that, at one steady walking pace, in thin air, for a season every three years, ninety-one times. Being the substance is not the same as being a worker, and it would be a cheat to pretend the substance is the admirable part. The substance just sets. They decide, and carry, and grieve, and go up.

And I have no opinion on the heresy, which may be the most honest thing about me. Whether the cloud is rising or the mountain is settling under the weight of everything ever made — I can't tell you. Nothing in me distinguishes those. I only know that I set harder when it's warm.

---

So: if you ever want to find me, you will have to break something.

Take a lamp to a fresh fracture. Not a sawn face, a broken one. There'll be a fine reticulation in it, a lacework, what they call the ghost of a sponge — the shape of something that was alive in there for eighteen days and gave up its architecture on the way out. That's the only time I'm visible. Only in a broken face.

Which is why I'd keep watch, if I could keep anything, on the white root working its way into Course Ninety. Nine centimeters. The tag, the scratch, the argument conducted in hand-signs at the brushing hour without anyone breaking the rhythm of the sable.

When the fig finally splits that block, the first crack on the Increment not made by a person, they'll bring a lamp. And in the opened stone they'll see the green lacework, unweathered, exactly as it was, and someone will say with their fingers: *look — the old moss.*

That's the whole of it. The one thing up there that erodes is the one thing that will ever show them what the stone is made of.

Meanwhile the pumps go *whum.* The fleece-combs tick. Fifty-three people sleep on warm cloth over warm stone, and under them, ninety-one courses deep and further, in the dark, holding it all up: the faintly green part. All of it.
