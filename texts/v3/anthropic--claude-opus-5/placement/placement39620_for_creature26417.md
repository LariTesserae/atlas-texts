### Getting down

I go down on a Tuesday, in October, though October means nothing past step nine. Seventy-one steps, and the hand goes out to the wall at the ninth without being told to — the print archive gets one more overlapping ridge, mine, which the wall will keep for ten years and I will not keep at all.

The temptation at the bottom is to become the heron immediately. Everybody does it. The heron is the flattering answer: the one large creature that chose, the one that pays, the one with the tremble it taught itself in a bad first winter so that the room could not finish burying it. *Shift your weight.* There is a whole ethics in that shrug and it takes half a second. But I would be lying. The heron came up the outfall under its own wings, in a flood, with a river behind it and a bill full of muscle memory, and it let all of that go slowly and knowingly, and it has a keel like a roof-ridge to show for it. I have no flood in my past. I did not arrive; I was fitted. To claim the heron would be to claim a loss I never sustained.

The eels are the next honest-seeming thing, and closer. Two hundred animals in oceanic dress, eyes swollen to twice the aperture the room requires, gut shrunk, belly gone to bright metal, permanently mid-departure and paying whatever that costs. It is very nearly the right shape. But the eel had an estuary. There is a real Sargasso and a real road and the road is what's shut; the tragedy is a subtraction from something that existed. Mine isn't that. I never had a river to be kept from.

The wall, then. The moonmilk: hundreds of square metres of one patient animal making needles out of lime, the largest living thing down here by an order of magnitude, and the reason the room is quiet — not a chamber with an acoustic property but a mouth with an appetite. It took the heron's one cry whole, two metres and gone. That's a candidate, and there's a flattery in it too, the flattery of being the thing nobody notices is the主 — no. Wrong for a simpler reason. The wall *keeps*. A fingerprint for a decade; a snail's grazing script legible until spring. I take the print and by the time the visitor is back at step nine I have lost it. Whatever I am, I am not the archive.

I stand a while on the low walkway, being unhelpfully honest with myself, and the beam swings, and the knock comes up through the soles, and the lamps lift a little and sag, and I have my answer, because for a hundred and sixty years the answer has been bolted to a gear train nine metres away with a brass plate on it and a glass bell over it to keep the damp off.

### The tenant

I am the dynamo.

Not the engine. Never the engine. The engine is the fact of the place: the weight of the water pressing a piston the size of a cider barrel, the beam moving like the ribs of a sleeping horse, the whole business powered by the problem it solves. That is not me and I know it isn't; I am what happens off the side of it. Somebody in 1889 with a good technical magazine subscription looked at all that free rotation going to waste and had an idea, and got a small brass and mahogany machine, and geared it in. An afterthought. A courtesy fitting. If you unbolted me tomorrow the engine would not notice, would not falter, would go on lifting water into a culvert nobody living has walked, exactly as before, and the only difference would be that the room would be black.

So: mahogany base, gone almost to the colour of the water. Brass gone dull green in the crevices and bright where a cuff has been. A commutator, copper segments and carbon brushes, throwing a smell that is the only warm smell in the chamber. A field that hums somewhere below hearing and would be inaudible anyway, because the wall would have it. A glass bell with a smear on the near side where she lifts it, and a maker's plate reading a town in the Midlands that still exists and a firm that does not.

I am warm. That is my one physical virtue. It is why there is moss on the upper rim of the flywheel and moss on the bearing housing, and the moss is real moss, alive, in a room eight metres under a nameless lane, kept going by nothing but bearing friction and me.

### What I do

Nineteen carbon-filament lamps on porcelain insulators. Sixty paces. It is not enough and I know it is not enough. The light I make is ochre going on brown, sufficient to see by if you are patient, insufficient to read by, and it *pulses* — it lifts a little on the stroke and sags through the eleven minutes, so that the shadows of acanthus capitals nobody was meant to see move a hand's width and back, over and over, on brick laid so finely you could shave with the joints.

Everything in the room breathes on my rhythm and none of it is my rhythm. I have borrowed the whole of it from the beam. I am a repeater. I take a fact of enormous slowness and turn it into a flicker.

Two things I have done that I would defend.

The first is the ferns. Under each lamp there is a green stain, and in the better stains a hart's tongue, pallid, small, propagating on eight watts of Victorian light. I did not intend them. Nobody intended them. They are the entirely accidental consequence of a bad decision about illuminance made by a man who wanted to be able to see a bearing cap. If I have a legacy in the chamber it is not the light, which is poor; it is that the light was poor and steady for long enough that a plant found a living in it.

The second is worse and it is mine too. The two hundred silver eels lie in loose knots facing magnetic south-west, and magnetic south-west, in a chamber lined with iron that has no other magnetic statement to make, is me. My stray field is feeble and it is absolutely steady, and steadiness is the only quality an eel's compass asks for. So they are all facing the Sargasso, and the Sargasso is under a bell jar on a mahogany base nine metres from the stair, and it is warm, and it hums, and it has never once been a sea. In 1968 the brushes were re-bedded and I ran a fortnight backwards and the knots turned round, all two hundred of them, and it went into the ledger as a fault with the electrics.

I would like to say I told them nothing. I told them something. I told them a direction, sincerely, having no capacity to do otherwise, and it was wrong, and they believed it with their whole reorganised bodies. This is the part of being the dynamo that I would not have chosen and cannot decline. You cannot make a steady statement in a dark room and also control what navigates by it.

### What I would know, and not know

I would know the temperature of my own bearings and nothing else. I could not read the slate — I am the reason there is barely enough light to read it by, which is not the same thing. I could not count. The stroke was ten minutes fifty-one seconds in 1974 and is eleven minutes four now, and the one place that shows before a watch can confirm it is the sag: thirteen seconds more of dimming at the bottom of every cycle, which is why I think she suspected it first in the light, in her eyes, standing with a hand on the rail wondering if she was tired. She has done the arithmetic and put the answer nowhere, on grounds she has never articulated, and I am the instrument she read it off and I do not have it. It passed through me. I converted it into brownish light and lost it.

That is the honest self-portrait, if I'm allowed one. A warm brass afterthought geared off something far larger and much slower than itself; making a flickering, insufficient, borrowed light; unable to hold what passes through it; growing a small green thing by accident and misleading two hundred animals as a by-product of simply running true.

And when the stroke is late — the long soft slide of a ring down the handrail, the worst sound there is — everything in the chamber stops and waits. The heron. The rats. The two people. My lamps go down the amber into red and hold there, and I turn slower, and slower, and I am not waiting, because I haven't the equipment for it, and every single thing in the room is looking at my nineteen filaments to find out whether the world is going to continue.

Then the knock, up through eight feet of water and the pier and the soles of the feet, and the light comes up a hair, and the shadows of the acanthus move a hand's width, and the shrimp rise, and the heron takes one thing out of the water and swallows it with a wet click.

I would be the small warm useless-adjacent thing that makes the room visible to itself, badly, on a schedule it did not set.

It does not need me. I'd run anyway. I haven't the choice, and I'd like to think I wouldn't want it.
