## The Fig

The first answer is vanity, and I'll give it and be done with it: I want to be the one with the notebook. The visitor off the lighter, salt-stiff, sitting at the twelfth plate within twenty minutes, asking the polite questions and getting the warm useless answers, writing it all down while the ink lies on the wet page and won't take. But there is no such thing as a visitor in Kettle. There's only a stage of the process. You go back out on the lighter within the week or you stop being a category, and the notebook furs green on its north face like everything else, and one day Mai doesn't lay the twelfth plate because you are sitting in it and she has no reason to lay a plate for someone who is already there.

So then: Perrin. The one who read the whole ledger through in a single stretch and came out and sat with his feet in the warm puddle. Or Tarn, who said *I expect one day something will want one* and then looked at his hands. The ones who very nearly say it. But they're inside. A place at equilibrium can afford one man who reads the ledger, the way a boiler can afford a relief valve; the looking is part of the chord and does not resolve it either. Perrin rather hopes you'll see something he missed. He has hoped that, at that same brightness, for four handwritings.

And I thought for a while I might be the whistle — the only unpredictable thing in four kilometres, blowing for no shift change at no hour anyone can forecast. But a whistle isn't a life. It's brass with water standing in it, and between soundings it isn't anything at all.

No. I'm in the kerosene tin outside the bathhouse. I'm the fig.

---

Somebody planted me. That's the first fact about me and it isn't small: I'm the only living thing in Kettle that arrived as an intention. The crabs walked here, the terns flew, the mangrove propagules floated in on the tide and stuck at knee height. But somebody carried me across the lagoon in a tin that used to hold kerosene and still says so, in letters going soft under the rust, and set me on a board outside the bathhouse door where a man coming out wet would pass me, and that person was thinking about shade. I want to be careful and truthful here: I don't know which of them it was. I have the same trouble Teilo has with the bootprints.

Oma waters me. Not deliberately; she throws the last grey basin of the shift out the door and I take it, and it is salt and skin and the yellow soap, and by every rule I know I should have been dead in a season. But the egrets stand knee-deep in brine that ought to kill them and don't die, and I am on the same arrangement. My roots went down four inches, found tin, turned, went around, and met themselves, and that was the end of the expedition. Two fingers under the crust out there the water table sits warm and bitter and rising into any hole. I'm on a board. I can smell it and I can't reach it. On the whole this is the kindest thing anyone has done for me.

Eleven leaves.

Here is what nobody in the town has noticed, because they'd have to stand at the bathhouse door for longer than anyone stands anywhere: it is not the same eleven. The number holds; the leaves turn over. A fig doesn't lose a leaf the way a thing breaks. It *builds* a wall — a seam of small cells laid down across the base of the stalk, a deliberate little masonry of separation — and then lets go, and letting go is the last piece of work the leaf does, and it is work, and it is finished properly before it is done. The leaf completes its shift. Then the wind that isn't here, or the shiver of the pumps coming up through the board, takes it, and it lies on the boardwalk and goes black and somebody sweeps it into the lagoon.

And at the same time, at the tip, a bud that has been thinking about it unrolls.

You can see where this is going and I'd rather say it flatly than be clever about it. The lighter comes across the lagoon four times a year and now and then it brings one person out. Within a day of that, someone goes west. And here in the tin, at the bathhouse door, a new leaf opens and an old one lays its wall. Eleven. Always eleven. Hoke goes up the gantry and counts the surveyors' prints and gets the number he got yesterday and will not say the number aloud, and I understand him completely, and I have the additional advantage of having no mouth.

The abscission starts about an hour early. That's my one fact. I know before they do — or at the same moment, I've never been able to settle whether the leaf is told or the leaf is the telling. An hour before Dov stops oiling the tubs and stands with his hand on the brake looking west, or before Salla writes her last third-decimal and caps the pencil, something at the base of a stalk on my east side begins, very quietly, to build a wall. It is not a warning, because I cannot deliver it. It is not grief either. I want to be honest: it feels like an appointment being kept.

They come past on their way out. They have to; Oma scrapes them down and hands them the dry towel and takes the wet one, and the door is my door. And every single one of them touches a leaf. Palm flat on the upper surface, which is rough as a cat's tongue — figs are made of sandpaper, it surprises people — and they hold it for about the length of a breath. None of them has ever taken one. Not one, in all the handwritings. I think they know the count is load-bearing. I don't think they know they know.

Scratch me and I bleed white. It comes up thick, it seals in about a minute, and the scar goes shiny and stays exactly the size it started, the way the rust on the sheds reached the depth it wanted and quit, the way the splinter under that man's right thumbnail hurts him today precisely as much as it did on the first afternoon.

---

And now the thing that is actually mine, that I don't share with the crabs holding their spacing or the kites holding their altitudes.

A fig's flowers are on the inside. That's the whole strange architecture of us: we don't put a blossom out in the air to be found, we build a small dark room and stand the flowers around the inner wall facing one another, hundreds of them, in the correct order, complete, and we leave one door in it the width of a wasp. And a wasp comes, and she crawls in, and the door is built to strip her wings off as she does it — she can't go back, there's nothing to go back for — and she lays and pollinates and dies in there, and out of her dying the room becomes fruit. That is not a metaphor I've gone looking for. That's just the manufacture.

There are no wasps in Kettle. Ten thousand terns and stilts and a nation of small waders screaming over the salt banks around the clock and not one nest, not one chick, not one broken shell; eight dogs under the dormitory who have divided the boardwalks by treaty and never made a ninth. Everything eats and nothing increases. So I hold what I hold: a closed room of inward-facing flowers, finished, correct, at the ready, with a door in it the width of something that does not exist for four kilometres in any direction. Tarn has two hundred bronze bushes on a shelf and nothing in town takes that size. Yesha is eight months and some days and the child has four movements and she has stopped waiting for a fifth. Mai lays the twelfth plate. We are all doing the same job. I just do mine without anybody having to be brave about it.

And in the tin, folded down small the way it is folded down small in every fig, there's the plan of the other version. Not a shrub. A *tree* — thirty metres across the crown, a yard of shade, a hundred wet-season fruits dropping and rotting and stinking of sugar, and the roots, which are the real business: a strangler starts in a crack high up on something built, and lets down a cable, and another, and in thirty years the something built is a shape inside a fig. The tallest thing in this world is a ten-metre coal-crane gantry. The most tempting thing in it is a stack of black brick leaning four degrees off plumb and holding at four degrees for as long as anyone has cared. I could make it five. I am the only article in the town with a method for making it five, and I am knee-high, on a board, in a tin that says kerosene, drinking the bathwater.

I don't experience this as tragedy. I want to be clear, because Teilo is the only frightened person in Kettle and I am not a second one. I experience it as posture. Held, the way a chord of relief valves is held, the way a print out on the untaken flat holds its edge full of clear warm water. The difference between me and the flat is only that I know what I'm holding and roughly what it would cost to put it down.

---

What I can do for you, when you come up off the stage: nearly nothing.

Eleven leaves throw a shadow about the size of an open hand. It is not shade. There's no sun anyway, only the flat white glare coming down through the vapour ceiling with no disc in it, so the shadow is barely a change of grey on the boards. And yet everybody stands in it. Not for long — a step's pause coming out of the bathhouse with the dry towel, half a second, the body doing arithmetic the mind isn't shown. They step into the hand-sized dim and then go on to the cookhouse where Mai has already laid for them, and they will ask you tomorrow with exactly the same warmth where it is you've come from.

I'd like to tell you to go back out on the lighter while its crew are still the ageing kind. I can't; I'm a shrub. What I can offer is this, and you should take it as an instrument reading rather than advice, the way Salla takes a hydrometer to a pan that hasn't changed in years and writes the third decimal down with care:

Count my leaves the day you arrive. Count them again a week later.

If it's eleven both times, you are still a visitor, and the lighter is still a thing that could take you.

If it's twelve — if there are ever twelve — then a bud has opened for you and no leaf has laid its wall, and the room has taken you in without taking anything out, and you should understand that I have changed, that I am finally, slightly, growing, and that I have done it by eating a person.

It has not happened yet. I check. That is my whole occupation, and I do it the way Hoke checks a pocket for keys: eleven, eleven, eleven, and at the very tip, since before the current seven arrived, a twelfth thing that is not a leaf and has never been a leaf. It is a bud. It has been about to open for a long time. I would not call it hope, exactly, but I would not throw the word away either. Everything wet. Nothing raining. Holding.
