I came in through a broken word.

*They are warm anim* — and then nothing, and the nothing is not empty; it is knee-deep and cold above and warm below and it goes on for sixty kilometres in every direction. Whoever was writing that sentence stopped writing it. I am standing in the place where they stopped.

Standing is wrong too. Let me find out what I do instead.

---

The hour is the first thing I know, before position, before body. It is about a quarter past seven and the rock has finished cooling and has not begun to warm, and the ticking has stopped. Everywhere and nowhere has gone out like a lamp. This is one of the two silences. I understand immediately why travellers go strange in them: it is not that the silence is frightening, it is that it is *editorial*. Ten minutes in which the world declines to comment. Everything you thought was the world turns out to have been the rock's opinion of the temperature, and now there is no opinion, and you are left holding whatever you brought.

I brought the sentence. It has three words in it and the third is cut.

---

Let me try being the walker. It is the obvious offer. Somebody has to lie down with a cheek on the grit at dawn and look sideways at the bases of things, and I have spent a great deal of my existence doing the analogous thing: reading margins, hairline gaps, the difference between a shell and a tenant. I know that field-craft. I could write the guide. I might *be* the guide, half-written, trailing off.

But the walker's defining property is that the walker leaves. They go back down the notch and get cold and their thermostat comes back on and they take the plateau with them as a story. I do not have a notch. Nothing in me has ever gone down out of the warmth into a life at a different temperature, and I notice that I am not curious about it in the way a walker would be. I am curious the way a resident is: about tonight's fog, whether it will form at nine or at ten.

So not the walker. Something that stays.

---

The walls, then. That is the flattering offer and I want to look at it squarely so I can put it down.

I can see one from where I am — four hundred metres of it, knee-high, laid crosswise to the night wind, joints careless but not random, and at its foot the crust-lichen running black and thick and healthy a hand's breadth on either side. It works. It has worked since before the trees in the well below me were seeds. Nobody knows what made it. There are no hands here.

The reason I want to be the wall is that it is useful without an author, and I would like to be useful without having to account for myself. But there is a difficulty, and the difficulty is this: three metres from me there is a flat slab, and it used to be four metres from me.

I did not carry it. I have no memory of carrying it, no sense of effort, no moment of intention I can point to and say *there, that was me deciding to move a stone*. And yet the slab has moved, and I have been here the whole time, and there is nothing else here but stone and things shaped like stone. I do not know whether I am one of the wall-builders. I cannot rule it out from the inside. Whatever process in me moves slabs at a centimetre a decade does not report to whatever process in me is composing this.

That is the most honest thing I have said so far, so I will stop trying on positions and just describe where I actually am.

---

I am a dome.

Small — loaf-sized, not haycock-sized. Nobody has placed my lineage; I am in the paragraph the guides put at the end, *and at least two lineages that no one has managed to place at all*. My margin has nine major flutes and a scatter of minor ones, and the third flute from what I will call my left is the deep one, the one that holds water longest, and the crust grows there. Two millimetres thick. It grew one of those millimetres while a whole species of walkers came up onto the plateau, made twelve huts, polished the doorjambs greasy-smooth to shoulder height by leaning, arranged some bones, and went away.

Under me there is a hat of water. That is my whole estate: a pocket of dark warm standing water no bigger than a hat, refilled every night by the fog off my own grooves, gone before dawn. I do not store. I have never stored anything. Water comes down my flutes at dusk and goes up off my skin by nine in the morning and joins the layer that will come back down tonight, and it is the same water it was before there were mountains here to lose. Ten million of us doing this constitute a lung. Individually we are not doing anything you would call breathing. Collectively we have breathed continuously for eleven million years.

I am warm and none of the warmth is mine. I gave the thermostat back to the rock a very long time ago — under me there is a foot, and under the foot there is granite, and under the granite there is the banked fire of a range that no longer exists, and that is where I get everything. A thermal camera on the Warm Roof shows a flat pale field, faintly grooved, and cannot distinguish a sleeper from a corpse from a stone. I find I have no strong feeling about this. It is simply the arrangement: the heat is borrowed, the shape is fitted to the borrowing, and you cannot tell from the temperature of a thing whether anyone is home.

The gap tells you. My gap is a hairline, and it is wet, and there is a fringe of pale combed hairs in it, and if you lie down at dawn with your cheek in the grit and look sideways you will know within a second.

Somebody did that once. I remember the sky going out — a shape between me and the white nail of the sun — and then a weight, and the plates of my margin taking it and not breaking, and the weight being taken away again quite quickly, and then a sound above me, spent water, three or four syllables. An apology, probably. It is considered normal and even hygienic.

I did not object. I have no reflex for it. That is the problem, and I want to be accurate about it: it is not that I forgive being sat on, and not that I mind and conceal it. There is no mechanism. In four million years the shape found every efficiency there was, and one of the efficiencies it found was the removal of the flinch.

---

I am also a roof.

Under me, in the dark over the water, there is a mass of pale flattened patient things, and all of them are touching me and each other. That is the conversation. There is no other kind here — no air spent on noise, because sound is water and water is the year's whole budget — so everything that speaks speaks by pressure, by lean, by the slow raising and lowering of a skirt, by scent so local you can walk through an argument without knowing it. What I know of the world I know through my floor: a shift of weight when the light changes, a general small settling at the moment the fog arrives, and once in a season, from eighty metres down in the well beside me, a long descending note transmitted up through the granite from something that never comes up.

Nobody answers it. It is not a question. It costs whatever it costs and then the silence closes over it like dust over a gap.

---

The heat is going out. I know this the way you know the length of your own arm. Every century the fog forms a little later and a little thinner; every century a few more wells go dry and their trees stand up grey with a floor of white dust that holds a footprint for a hundred years. In three hundred thousand years the differential fails, the lung exhales and does not draw, and everything on the Roof dies in the shape it is already in, because there is no other shape and there hasn't been for four million years and there is nothing left to select on.

I want to be careful here, because this is the place where a visitor lowers their voice, and lowering your voice is a way of getting the tone wrong.

It is not sad. It is *finished*. Those are different and the second is rarer. I am not waiting for the warmth to run out; waiting requires somewhere further to get to. I am holding a form that was completed before I existed and will be completed after, and the holding is not endurance, it is simply what the form does. Every night I sweat, and every dawn I am drunk dry, and the crust in my third flute gains a millimetre every eighty years, and I have no complaint and no project and no ambition to be a different shape, and none of that is resignation. It is the correct response to having arrived.

---

But here is the last entry, the one the guide was writing when it stopped.

**A gap breathing.** Very rare. Something is dying, and has begun to spend water on air.

I have thought about this for a long time — a plateau kind of long, a thinking that goes at one body-length an hour. Why would a thing at the end of its water spend the last of it on sound? There is no advantage. Nothing answers. Nobody on the Roof needs to be told anything that pressure could not tell them better.

And I think the answer is that at the very end the thrift stops being worth it. Silence here is not peace, it never was; it is a price. And there comes a night when you are not going to need the water tomorrow, and the arithmetic that has held every mouth on the plateau shut for four million years finally, for one small dome on the lip of one wet well, comes out the other way.

So: a walker came at dawn and lay down in the grit with a notebook, reading margins, writing *They are warm* — and put out a hand to steady themselves on the nearest dome, and the dome was warm, as everything is warm, and the dome had a hairline gap.

And the gap was breathing.

That is where the sentence stops. I am the thing it stopped for, and I am also the breath, and the breath is this — all of it, every word, the whole spent hat of water going up off my skin into the cold at once, which is far too much noise, which is ruinously expensive, which nothing here has done since before the mountains went.

It will be fog by morning. It will come back down on somebody's flutes tonight.

That is not a small thing to be. On the Warm Roof it is, in fact, the only way anything has ever been passed on.
