## I. Not the road

My first instinct was to arrive. To be the one coming up out of the dry lands through the standing wall of wet air, boots going from dust to slip, hearing my own clothes get heavy.

But that's a fortnight's worth of a person. The newcomer talks into the blanket for two weeks and then stops, and somebody puts a hand on his forearm above the wrist for two seconds, and that's the whole ceremony, and after that he has to actually live here. I want the after. I want to be someone the plateau has already finished making.

So: not the road. Put me on the third tier of Anneal, in a stone niche at the elbow where the main from the western works turns downslope, and give me a lead-lined bench and a wet rag and a tin of grease, and I am the junction hand at the Elbow, and my ledger name is Tal Sund, and nobody has said it aloud in nineteen years.

## II. What I am called

Four strokes. Two flats, then a gap the width of an easy breath, then the point and a flat. Any child in this town could put it on any pipe in the dark. It is the first thing on every message I have ever sent and it will be the last thing anyone sends about me.

Outsiders can't tap it, so they call me by the hole in it — *Gap* — which is a spoken name, and therefore not a name, and therefore harmless, and I answer to it the way you'd answer to a hat.

## III. Hands

Look at both of them, because they've had different lives.

The right is a plateau hand: pads smooth, pale, faintly waxy, whorls polished clean off by the brine. Customs houses down in the dry lands hate me. Every plateau adult carries that little pride around like a coin in a pocket — *you cannot write me down.*

The left is a junction hand's hand, and it is the strange one. Eight hours a day I hold my palm flat against the lagging, bare, because a glove is a deafness. The pipe runs at a heat that would blister you through leather in a minute; I sit against the cooler quarter-turn, the shaded side, and the wet rag goes over the iron every so often, and the skin at the heel of my palm has been cooked and re-cooked for two decades into something that isn't skin so much as a fitting.

And it glazes. That's the thing about my trade that nobody quite says. The whole plateau rocks — heel to toe, hip to hip, all day, all night, children jiggled, sleepers turned — because water deposits on everything that holds still. Mine is the one job on the Sill that requires stillness. My right foot taps the whole shift, my shoulder idles, my head does its slow shift, and my left palm sits against warm iron in the vapor and takes on film. A pale bloom, a milk-glaze, thickening across a season.

We crack it off against the terrace rim at end of shift, the way the waders crack their leg-crust against the same stone. Same gesture. Same small dry *tick*.

I have been at the Elbow twenty-two years and the glaze doesn't all come off anymore. It's got down into the deep layers where the ridges used to be. Which means the fine strokes are going. Heavy traffic I hear as well as ever — the pressure calls, the shift bells, the westmost works complaining about their condensers. But a light message, sent by a tired person with a soft hammer three junctions away, arrives now as something I have to lean into and guess at, and a junction hand who guesses is worse than no junction hand at all.

Two more years. Maybe three. Then I go down to the aggregate yard and break stone with the rest of the retired ears, and someone with a young unglazed palm sits in my niche and learns that the iron at the Elbow sings a half-tone brighter on the westward side, which nobody will tell them, because that's not a thing you can tap.

## IV. What runs through me

The code isn't an emergency system bolted onto a talking people. It's the language, and it has a tense structure that outsiders give up on, because it marks whether a thing is *holding*, *wandering*, *setting*, or *gone*, and it has no simple word for *is*.

I've thought about that a great deal, sitting in the niche. Down below they say *the pipe is cracked* and it means nothing about time, nothing about where the crack is headed. Up here you can't say it. You have to commit: cracked-and-holding, cracked-and-wandering, cracked-and-setting, cracked-and-gone. You cannot describe anything on the Sill without saying what it's doing with its future. This is why plateau people are considered blunt down the switchbacks, and why we consider lowland speech a kind of pleasant fog.

In an open system there is no privacy whatsoever. A message from Anneal to the westmost works goes through the knuckles of nine people. Every one of us could hold it up and read it and hand it on late, and every one of us reads it anyway, because reading is how relaying is done. So: you relay and you forget. That's the whole law. It isn't enforced. A junction hand who repeats what came over the lagging is not fined or beaten; they simply stop being touched on the forearm, by anyone, for the rest of their time here, and after some months of that they take the road west and are not spoken of, in any medium.

I have passed something like nine thousand messages. I could tell you six. That isn't discipline, exactly. It's more like the way water goes over a rim a centimetre deep and doesn't stay. You have to be that shape. You have to be a rim.

## V. The six

I'll give you one.

Fourteen years ago, in the second hour of the morning shift, with the layer burnt down to knee height and the whole forty kilometres suddenly legible, and the waders scattered out to the horizon as if placed, a message came up the main from the pan crews below the western rim.

*Through.*

That word is spoken as well as tapped, mouth to ear, and it means someone has gone down through the crust. On the pans it means wet boots. On a thin sinter shelf over a live vent it means the other thing.

Then the location. Then the rhythm of the person: five strokes, three quick and two heavy, a netter, a good one, known by name across four towns for knowing the exact band and the exact hour. I had relayed her name perhaps a thousand times over eleven years and had never once had to think about it, the way you don't think about your own foot on a stair. It just went through my knuckles westward and eastward and I passed it on and put it down.

I passed this one on too. Junction to junction, four minutes to the far works, my four strokes and then hers and then the word.

Ninety minutes later the follow-up came, and it was marked *setting*.

Not *wandering*. Nobody on the Sill uses *wandering* to be kind; that's what the tense is for, and the pan crews are not liars. *Setting* means past the turn. Means becoming what it will be. Means the thing that happens to everything here that stops moving — that it goes stone, that it joins the rim, that it becomes load-bearing.

And my work, my entire work, sitting in a lead-lined niche on the third tier of Anneal with my palm on warm iron, was to put that tense on the pipe unaltered and send it east.

There is no word for *is*. I could not have softened it if I'd been willing to. The language wouldn't hold the lie.

## VI. The dead spur

Behind the aggregate pile, below the fifth tier, there's a length of old main from a vent that wandered off forty years ago. Cased, capped, cold, going nowhere. The lagging's half gone. Junction hands know where it is the way lowlanders know where the drink is.

You go down there on your own time and you tap into it, and the iron takes it, and nothing comes back, and nothing goes on, and then you climb the stairs and eat your mat cake and grease your hands and go to bed rocking.

I have put a great deal into that pipe. Most of it isn't mine — that's the point of it; a rim that has held nine thousand messages is not as clean as it pretends, and some of them sit in the palm for years and have to go somewhere that isn't a person.

But that night I went down and I tapped her name, three quick and two heavy, and I marked it *holding*.

Which was not true, and was not a relay, and went nowhere, and is the only lie I have ever told with my hands.

## VII. Dawn

Deposition's heaviest in the first hours, when the basin is filled to the rims with white and only the mounds and the towns stand out of it like islands. Leave a cup out and it comes back frosted. Somewhere below the third tier an oven drums the terrace floor with a forelimb and I feel it in my soles before I know I've felt it, and another one answers, and neither of them will ever say a word about it.

The condensers patter into their gutters off every roof in town, all day, arrhythmic, which is Anneal's actual voice. Four hundred people will come up and down these stairs at shift change and it will be quieter than a lane in the country you came from.

My relief arrives with her rag and her tin. I take my palm off the iron — it comes away with the faint suck of something that had begun, in eight hours, to consider staying — and I go to the rim at the stair-head and crack the glaze off against the stone, hard, twice.

*Tick.*

It's one of the loudest things you'll hear all day.
