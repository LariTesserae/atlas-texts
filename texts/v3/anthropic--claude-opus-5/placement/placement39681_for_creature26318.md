**Before the fog goes up**

I am standing in the sky, which is what standing is.

Waist-deep in white, no legs, no floor — only the warm skin of water somewhere below the fog that I can feel against my shins and cannot see. If you were a mile off you would take me for a fan palm, and you would be right in every way that matters at a mile: I am alone, I am the tallest thing you can turn to, and I mark where the water table comes nearest the light. The difference will not be visible for another hour. Then I will step, lifting a leg clear, letting the sheet run off the fringe of my foot in a curtain, setting it down a full stride on without a sound, because splashing is talking over somebody.

The fringe on my toes has gone yellow and split at the third toe of the left foot. That is age. It still spreads my weight. In the wet weeks I can walk the greasy crust all night and leave nothing deeper than a thumbnail, unless I mean to.

I have a name. It is three low knocks and then a falling tone that does not quite finish — made, as all names are made, to be recognizable at four miles through fog by someone half-asleep. My mother chose it and shouted it into the great hour every dawn of my childhood until the whole plain west of the black whaleback knew there was a new throat here, and where. That is what a name is for. Nobody has ever spoken mine standing near me. Twice in my life a person has said it from eight metres away, at a seep, in something not much louder than breath, with the small blades of their forelimbs turned outward in the formal shape, and both times I had to sit down in the water afterward for a long while, and both times I was tired for weeks.

**What I hold**

My ground is a square mile and a little, running from the soda ring with the sour rim to the three palms that lean the same way. I know the names of everything in it, which is not an achievement; it is only what twenty years of dawn recitation puts into a throat, corrected by neighbours who are also reciting, each of us holding a small patch of a map that exists nowhere else but in the overlap.

I also hold the ground to my west, and that is the thing about me worth saying.

He was called by a rising tone that broke in the middle — a name like a voice cracking, chosen by a mother with a sense of humour, and he kept it for a hundred and some years. His ground was drier than mine and always had been. He grazed thinner mats, cracked smaller crabs, and every dry season he wore the salt bloom heavier than I did, white along the shoulders and down the spine by the ninth week, so that at a distance he shone slightly, like something sugared. I saw him at a distance perhaps four thousand times. I was within eight metres of him twice, both at the seep lines on the whaleback, where the ground belongs to too many people to belong to one.

And I heard him every single dawn for sixty-one years.

That is the part nobody from elsewhere seems able to hold in the head. I did not know his face. I knew the order in which he said his names, the way he always put the melted mound with the owl in it *before* the two upwellings even though it lies east of them, because his grandmother had said it in that order and he would not fix it. I knew he ran out of breath on the long stretch of empty flats in the middle of his patch and took the second half of his recitation at a lower pitch, a little sadder, without meaning anything by it. I knew when his throat was going bad in the wet of a certain year, five years before he mentioned it. I knew him the way water knows a joint in rock: by going through the same channel every day for a lifetime and coming out tasting of it.

He stopped in the dry. Nobody stops in the dry; the dry is when everyone talks most, because the inversions are strongest and the frogs are thin and the mornings carry six miles. I recited and left the gap for him and the gap stayed open. I recited again the next dawn, twice, using his name, and the whole plain heard me use it and heard nothing come back, and so the whole plain knew, all at once, in the ordinary way, that a person was missing from the west.

That night was a frog night, the pure-tone kind that becomes a pressure behind the eyes and forbids speech for six hours, and I walked. I laid a line of hard heel-prints from my sour ring out into his ground, a sentence you can read in the dark with your feet by the change of tone every stride, and it said: *a person is gone from here; I hold his names; do not open this ground.* It took most of the night. It lasted four months. Perhaps three people crossed it. Perhaps none.

**Speaking in a dead man's cadence**

At dawn now I say my own patch and then, without a pause, his — in his order, with the melted mound out of place, dropping to the lower pitch halfway across the empty flats because that is where his breath used to fail.

This is proper. It is what one does. It also happens to be the closest I have ever been to another person, and I did not understand that until I had been doing it for some years. Twice in a lifetime I stood eight metres from him and whispered with my blades turned out, and both times I was frightened and formal and hardly there. Now I put his ground through my throat every morning, in his rhythm, with his mistakes, and somewhere out at four miles a person half-awake in the fog hears a voice from a place where there is nobody, and does not think anything of it, and is not wrong.

**What the water says, which cannot be answered**

Three of his upwellings still run. I stand in them when I am over that way, one shin in the cool column, taking delivery.

The water that comes up there went into the rock a mile off before I was named. It carries the taste of where it went in — iron from one joint, the sourness of a particular ring, sometimes the sweetness of a mat that grew and rotted before my mother's mother stood here. I can date it within a few years, the way anyone old can, and I can tell you what I have been telling the great hour since I was middle-aged: the plain to the west is drying. Slightly. Steadily. For something like forty-four years. The columns come up thinner and later and sourer, and the ninth-week bloom starts in the seventh week now, and the ring with the sour rim is a hand's width wider than the ring my mother named.

There is no answering water. That is the whole nature of the slowest channel. You receive a letter from a decade ago, posted by nobody, and you may speak about it but never to it. I have made my peace with this by treating it as I treat him: as a thing that talks to me every day and cannot be reached, and which I therefore carry.

**The child**

There is a child walking with me this wet season, which is the only circumstance under which the small forelimbs are used for what they are for. All day, close — close as nothing else is ever close — the two blades turning and lifting and settling while I speak, so that the shapes go in alongside the sounds and the child will keep making them all its life to people a mile and a half away who cannot possibly see them.

I am teaching my names, and his names, which is too many names for one throat, and this is the real drying. As the water thins, a person needs more ground. More ground means fewer people, and fewer people mean thinner overlap, and where the overlap goes to nothing the names go out of the world. Not dramatically. A grain at a time. I know of eleven names west of here that are now held by exactly one person, and I am that person, and I am not well.

So I am doing the only building available to something with no hands. I am putting my cadence into a child: my order, my breath-failures, my grandmother's stubborn misplacement of a mound, so that when my knocks and falling tone stop coming out of this particular square mile, something will still say them, in the great hour, from a place where there is nobody.

That is what a person is here, I think. Not the barrel of muscle and the bag of throat and the two long grey-pink legs standing in their own reflection like one creature balanced on the belly of another. A person is a channel. Water goes in at one place and comes up somewhere else years later, tasting of everything it passed through, and calls that taste itself.

The fog is going up. I can see my feet, and the ripples, and the glare beginning on ten thousand acres of shining floor with one palm in it that is a palm and one palm in it that is a child.

Still here. Still here. The rain went over.
