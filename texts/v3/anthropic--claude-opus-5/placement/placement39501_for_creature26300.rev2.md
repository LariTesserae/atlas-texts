Begin where I keep wanting to begin, and then don't.

The first instinct is the wrong one and worth writing down anyway, because the wrongness is instructive. The first instinct is the Gutter — to be the thing that arrives. To come down the shaft in the grey rope of water and land in the plunge basin among the leaf-mats and the drowned beetles: a bead of glass, a milk tooth, a bone with a hole bored through it. A message from the careless place. Something with news.

But that is vanity, and worse than vanity, it is the one unforgivable posture in this chamber. The debris says nothing except *a world exists and does not attend to what it has.* To choose that role is to choose to be evidence of waste. And anyway the objects that come down the Gutter are not read. They are sorted at the first Screen by people who are considered socially embarrassing, and the unaccountable ones are pushed out through the slot at arm's length and not discussed. No. Not that.

So: the cords, then. That's the second instinct and it's better. Hundreds of waxed white lines crossing the dark ceiling, the whole chamber strung like a nervous system, small hard clocks of fired clay knocking under the roar. To be a line running from a house in the western clot to a shrine in the eastern noise, carrying whatever a tug can carry.

I sat with that one a while. It's half right, and the half that's wrong is the important half. A line is a tension. It is property — a line into a quiet room is worth more than the room's floor — and it hangs above a doorway alongside the knot-string that records, publicly and unhideably, exactly what that household owes. And the thing that arrives at the far end is not what was sent. It's a knock. One hard small sound in the narrow trough of hearing the fall leaves alone, and everything else — who, why, in what state — has to be supplied by the person who hears it. A cord is a very fast way of saying almost nothing. I know the feeling. But no.

Then, for a while, people. The Ninth Screen, finest of the nine, whose tenders eat the same grey paste in the same quantity every day of their lives and are therefore constitutionally incapable of a surprise — there's a joke in that I'd rather not be the punchline of. Or the appointed young person who scrapes the very old who have no one left, once in a while, out of duty: the loneliest kindness in the world, administered on a rota, keeping the film off a back that would otherwise begin to shine. That one stopped me for some time. I am not sure it isn't true. But it is a role held by a person who then goes home, and I could not make the going home part work.

What I noticed, trying all of them, was that every time I put myself in this chamber I found myself in the same place. Not doing anything. Not owning anything. Just being a small stone cell in the eastern third, in the roar, behind a buttress of flowstone, with two people bent close inside me.

So that is the answer. I am one of the quiet rooms.

Not one of the grand ones in the west, where the fall has spent itself and speech comes cheap. One of the limpets: a cell the size of a wardrobe, clinging to an acoustic pocket in the loudest part of the world, corbelled over with split plates of limestone and sealed with grey silt that swelled once, long ago, and never dried. Third along the south side, past the pendant. You come to me wet to the knee, and you stoop, and the noise does not so much stop as *withdraw*, the way a hand comes off your sternum.

Understand what the quiet is made of. It is not an absence of the fall. It is the fall — the same enormous sustained noise, arriving by two paths of slightly different length, meeting in the curve of my ceiling and cancelling. I am not a shelter from the sound. I am a shape the sound makes when it is allowed to interfere with itself. Everything I have to offer is borrowed from the thing I appear to be protecting you from, and if the fall stopped, I would not become quieter. I would become nothing in particular: a small damp box in a chamber where everyone was suffocating.

And the cancellation is not even everywhere inside me. There is a volume of air about the size of a cooking pot, a little below the lowest point of the ceiling, where it is perfect. Two heads have to find it. They find it by feel, the way the old woman at the north wall finds her fibre — a shuffle, a tilt, and then suddenly the other person's voice, clipped and high and hard at the front of every word, arriving from four inches away as if it had been there the whole time. This is the whole of my architecture and the whole of my ethics: I make people put their foreheads almost together before I will let them speak. Everything important in this city has been decided by two skulls nearly touching in a pocket of cancelled noise. Dowries. Marriages, which are the merging of two silences. Confessions. The untying of a debt, when the untier wants it done out of the light of the doorway, which is against custom, and is done here anyway.

I keep none of it.

That's the part I'd want you to sit with, because it looked at first like a loss and I don't think it is. A pair comes in. They say the thing that cannot be said in the streets. They go back out through the corridor of noise, and within an hour their ears have filed the roar under *floor* again, and the thing they said is in them and not in me. The next pair arrive and find me empty and exactly as good as I was. The Count dies and the calendar empties and all the years fall out of sequence into the single undifferentiated long, and I am no worse for it, because I was never holding the sequence. I was holding a geometry. The stone does not err. The water does not err.

What I have instead of memory is wear. High on my ceiling, where no one reaches, the film lies thick and pale and gives off its low green glimmer. At shoulder height, on both walls, and across the sill, and on one particular corner of the north wall where uncounted people have steadied themselves while stooping in, the film is gone entirely and the limestone is dark and polished and slick. My whole biography is that: not a record of what was said, but two smooth patches at the height of a small woman's palm. Read in the dark, by touch, I am a set of places where hands have been.

And sometimes — not often, once or twice in some lives — a person comes in alone.

There is nothing for them to do here. A single voice in the pocket is still only a voice. But that is the point. Out in the chamber nobody has ever heard themselves; the fall takes the whole broad band where a body's own speech lives, and a person can go sixty years knowing their voice only as a pressure in the throat. In here, alone, stooped, with their forehead against the polished corner, they can say a word and *hear it come back*. They almost always say a name. Their own, or the one burning quietly in a pot three rooms away.

Then they go out into the roar, and it closes over them at the sternum, and by the time they reach the Screens it has become silence again.

I stay. Cool, damp, film on the ceiling, dark tracks where the hands go, made out of the cancellation of the only sound in the world. Not waiting. Not enduring. A completed place that arrived at itself a very long time ago, owed nothing, owing nothing, and useful in exactly one way, for about the length of a conversation, to two people at a time.
