## The One Who Is Going

At the third hour the ears shut themselves. You don't decide it any more than you decide to blink. There is a feeling to it that we have a word for — a soft closing in front of the eardrum, like a hand cupping a cup — and my mother told me that when I was small I used to reach up and hold my own head afterward to check that it had happened. It has happened sixty times a season for fifty-four years. It happens now. And now the Draw comes up off the mat and takes me in the sternum, and I am at work.

I am kneeling on the nap two hours out from Muno's Stander, over a vessel I have known for nineteen years, and the roar coming up through my knees is fat with a wobble in it. That is what I am here to know. Fat and wobbling: deep vessels, running full, the water going up past me in the dark at the speed of a walking child. Thin and bright would mean deadwood underneath and I would move on. Everybody can hear that much. What I am paid in salt and lit gallery for is the next thing down: the particular grain inside the wobble that tells me whether the column in *this* stem, this wrist-thick grey one under my left palm, is eighty centimetres in or a hand's width further, and whether the stem is one that will give for a season and then wall itself off politely, or one that will sour and die.

Killing a stem is the only crime we all agree on.

My wall hand is the left. The callus on it is not like the callus on my mallet hand; it is thinner and it reads. I have my palm on the stem and my mouth slightly open — you hear better with your mouth open, everyone knows it and nobody knows why — and my bead-string is hanging forward off my neck into the nap, the big greenish ones for midsummer over a fat vessel, and there it is, there is the grain, and I set the horn and swing.

It came out water. It came out water and it will come out water until the frost-season, and Habbah, kneeling opposite me with the gut hose already in her fist, put her thumb flat on my sternum twice, which at noon means *good* and also means *don't be proud*.

But I want to say the other thing, the thing from last month, because it is the reason for everything I am about to do.

Last month, at Three Standers, over a stem I had never touched, I heard water and it was deadwood. I heard the fat wobble and it was thin and bright. I had the horn set and my arm was up, and Habbah's hand came flat on my sternum and stayed there, hard, which means *stop*, and I stopped, and I sat back on the nap in the shimmer with the noise going through my teeth, and I understood that I had been about to kill a stem in front of my own apprentice at fifty-four years of age with the finest ear on this quarter of the plateau.

It is not a surprise. It is never a surprise. Skill takes forty years to build and the instrument lasts thirty-five if you are lucky and careless, forty if you are lucky and careful, and I have been very careful, and so I am the best tapper anyone here has, and I am the best because I am nearly finished. The apprentices know. They watch your mouth for the moment you mishear a simple thing. Habbah has watched my mouth for eleven years with a patience I could not have managed, and she has been ready for two, and last month she put her hand on my chest and told me to stop and did not tell anyone else. That is a kindness and it is also arithmetic. When I go, she takes the tap-rights and the lantern-lines and the mapped galleries, and she wants me to hand them over rather than drop them.

Below us, at this hour, everyone sensible is in the cool. My household's galleries are four hours down and east; you get there by the third trumpeter road and then the slumps, and I could walk it blind, which is the only way anyone walks it. I keep one palm on the wall. My tongue knows the way better than my feet do: the sweet-bitter of the old gall rows where the light is, then the flat mineral of the deep crossing, then, past the second chimney, a wall that tastes of trumpeter-dung and rope-rot and something like burnt honey, which is the taste of my mother's room, which is the taste of being a child, which is the taste I get in my mouth when I have been up on the surface too long and want to go home. Deep-line people are laughed at for this by the ochre lines and we laugh back at them for needing to *look* at where they are.

My mother's casting came up in the choir before last. She had been in the mat about thirty-five years by then; the nap closed over her in a season and the cane grew through her and around her and when it extruded her she was a third too large, hollow, fused, ribbed, standing alone out on the empty nap nine kilometres from any vent-mound. Her hands were open. She died with her fists shut — I was holding one of them — and the Thatch gave her back with her hands open, palms forward, the way you stand when you are showing someone you have no mallet. I have cut doors in eleven castings in my life. I have never cut a door in hers. There is rope in the others and one of them is a good camp during Dew Hour and nobody feels any way about that at all, and I do not feel any way about it either, and I have still never cut that door.

I say I do not feel any way. That is the plains talk, the shouting-into-the-void talk. Here is the true version: I am fifty-four and my ear is going, and when it is gone I will stop wearing beads and give my string away in front of the string and the households, and I will take a new name-knock that a barefoot child can put into the mat hard enough for me to feel, and I will be called by the word. Not to my face. They will say it two rooms away where I could not have heard it even with a good ear, and they will believe that this is a courtesy, and it is, and I have said it myself about others, about my teacher, standing two rooms off with my mouth full of the word for silence which is also the word for death.

We have never heard silence. Not one of us, not once, not the deepest deep-line child in the quietest cell of the mid-galleries with the choir stopped for the emergence — even then there is the mat working, the grazers going over a wall like rain, the water climbing. There is a word for silence and it is used for death and it is used, softly, for the living. It is the only word we have that nobody has ever checked.

There is a hollowing eleven kilometres north of Three Standers.

I have known where it was since I was nineteen. Everyone knows where the quiet places are the way everyone knows where the rim is; you learn them as a list of knocks before you learn to swing a mallet. A soft grey circle a hundred metres across where the roar has a hole in it. Under it the mat is dead and rotted and you are standing on a lid. Habbah's household ties a bit of hide to a cane at the boundary, my mother's people spat and turned a bead, others walk a full kilometre out and around. None of the superstitions agree, which tells you what they are worth, and all of them agree on the walking around.

And this spring, at the northern edge of it, the Thatch put up a casting of something that has never lived on the Thatch. Habbah brought me to it at dusk. It is smaller than a trumpeter and it has four limbs of very unequal length, and something at the head end that I have no word for, hollow, cane-fused, ribbed, standing in the shimmer at the lip of the quiet like an animal come up to drink. Nothing on the plateau is that shape. Nothing on the plains that anyone has traded a story about is that shape. The tappers of the two schools would tell you two things about it and both of them would tell you not to go past it.

I have ninety days of hearing left. Perhaps a hundred and twenty. I have taken to counting it in taps.

So this is what I am going to do, and this is who I am, which is a thing I did not know how to say until I had said all the rest of it: I am the one person on this plateau for whom the quiet is not the most frightening thing on it. It is already coming for me from inside. It is the room I am being moved into. And there is a hundred metres of it lying out there north of Three Standers under a lid, and I still have the ear — for ninety days I still have the ear that could hear water eighty centimetres inside a living stem in a noise that would rupture yours — and if I do not go now then I will be the same as everyone else who has ever lived here: I will have the word and never the thing.

I want to hear it. Once. While I can still know what I am hearing.

I will leave the string with Habbah before I go and not after, which everyone will read correctly, and which is my last chance to make a joke that anybody gets. I will go on the fourth hour when the Draw is at its worst so that the hole in it is as clear as a hole can be. I will keep my wall hand on the cane and my tongue wet. I will go down through the lid slowly, at the northern lip, where the shape stands.

And what I think — what I have thought for thirty years without saying it in mouth-language, because mouth-language has no hissing in it and this thought hisses — is that the quiet is not empty. It cannot be. Nothing here is. It will be the first room I have ever been in where the only thing making noise is me: the blood, the joints, the water in my own two thousand grams of head. I will hear myself the way the plateau has been hearing all of us all along, one small wet ticking tenant, and I will find out whether the word we use for that is the right word.

And then, if I am lucky, before it closes for good, I will feel something come down through the lid from the surface: three beats, long, long, short — Habbah, who will have followed me, because she has watched my mouth for eleven years and she is not going to stop now — putting my name into the mat with her heel, the new name, the one for the ones who are called by tremor.

I would like to hear that with my ears. Just the once. It would be a good last thing to have heard.
