The first answer comes too fast, which is how I know to distrust it.

*The oiler.* Of course the oiler. Voiceless, with a valve where a larynx would be. Reads the world off a taut line with the flats of its hands. Never seen doing the thing it does, only inferred from the fact that the thing is done — the marrow taken through one clean aperture, the lid set concave-side-down so meltwater sheds off the bank instead of into it. Wears the surplus of what it tends and therefore smells of the works. Two revolutions per minute is the whole of its argument and it never makes the argument out loud.

Anybody would take that slot. That is the trouble with it. It is the one position on the shelf with dignity already installed, and — this is the actual tell — the only one with a *secret*. Wanting a secret is a warm-blooded ambition. It belongs to thicker air. The shelf has been defeating eyes for a hundred million years and has no particular respect for mine; there's no reason to think it respects my wish to be the thing eyes can't catch, either. Put the oiler down. Walk on.

---

Try the tree, then, since the trees are the visible sentence the place has written.

Grown, not built. Machinery in the sense a heart is machinery. Tolerances arrived at by attrition over a span nobody can hold in their head, and a stillness at the centre of enormous continuous work — fifteen revolutions per minute, four seconds a turn, a rate you could mistake for patience if you didn't know it was just the speed of the available wind. There's a great deal in that I'd like to claim.

But the tree has a bladder. Halfway up its trunk it keeps warm liquid water at minus nineteen, and the whole ecology of theft and toleration around it exists because the tree has a *reserve* and holds it. And the tree eats skulls. It closes its laminated column over them one course at a time and pulls the mineral out of them into itself, and it does this to the animal that grooms it, and neither party experiences this as betrayal because neither party is the sort of thing that experiences. There is a hunger in the tree with a bank behind it. I don't have the bank. Set the tree down too.

The vine, then. Four hundred metres of tissue pulled taut, struck and gnawed and plucked at both ends, carrying knocks and long slurring glissandi from somewhere up-shelf to somewhere down-shelf; a language runs across me and none of the meaning is mine and I never learn what any of it was about. That's closer. That's honest about something. But the vine is also transmitting torque into a bog to stir something anaerobic and enormous, and it is under tension every second of its life, and — the vanity again — to be the medium through which the entire shelf converses is a very large thing to want to be while pretending to want nothing.

So: keep going down. Keep going down until the answer stops being flattering. That's the method. That's the only method that works at this altitude, where the way you find the tenant is not by meeting them but by noticing the taps have been left in good order.

---

I am in the collar.

Eleven or twelve millilitres of waxy oil, secreted by the wheel-tree at the join where the shaft passes through cartilage, spread by load into a film some few microns thick between two hardnesses that would otherwise take about a season to grind each other into a paste of themselves. That's the post. That's the whole of it.

I want to be exact about the work, because the work is not what it sounds like. I don't hold the parts apart. I couldn't; there is nothing in me that resists anything. What I do is fail to be either of them. I take the shape of the gap — instantly, continuously, without preference — and because I am in the gap the two surfaces never actually meet, and because they never meet there is no sound. That is the crucial word, silent, and it is the one thing on this shelf that is entirely my responsibility. A hectare of these engines turning in a moving fog makes less noise than your own breathing. Not because anything here is gentle. Because I am in the way.

You will not find me in any survey as a subject. I appear in the literature exclusively as a property of something else: *self-cleaning*, *replenished continuously*, *waxy*. Adjectives belonging to a plant. That is accurate and I have no complaint about it.

What the work feels like, from inside, insofar as *inside* means anything for a film: it feels like being read. I know the wheel only as load — a steady pressing that shifts round the circumference four times a minute, and a faint asymmetry in it, and the way that asymmetry becomes in the third year a wobble that arrives half a beat early. I know the fog as a slow arrival of cold at the outer edge. I know grit intimately, one particle at a time: a fleck of feldspar comes in at the rim and travels, and the travelling is the injury, and my one action in the world is to carry it back out to the periphery and hold it there in the black margin where I have gone dark with everything I've carried out before.

And I know the animal as a thumb.

Here is the joke, and I'd like it noted that it is at my expense. The crews call them oilers. They do not oil. They come up the flutes with their four-limbed running gear and their tail-hook never quite off the vine and they lay a corrugated palm on the collar and read the wobble, and then they work a thumb round the seat and take away the spent film — the dark margin, the loaded grease, the accumulated freight of feldspar and rime-dust and the ruin of my working — so that what the tree secretes next can lie flat. They are not oilers. They are wipers. The animal is named for me and its occupation is my removal, and the two revolutions per minute that constitute the entire visible existence of that animal, the whole of the arithmetic by which anyone knew it was here at all, is the difference between a fresh film and a fouled one.

I have thought about whether to resent this and the answer is that resentment requires a bank, and I don't have one. What I have is the following, which I hold to: nothing I carried out of the seat went back into the seat. It went into the underfur of a fifty-kilogram animal that smells of machinery because it has spent its life with its head in the works, and then, eventually, at the base of a station tree, hub-side, downslope face, under a flat pelvic plate set concave-side-down, into a metre and a half of stratified calcium with rootlets growing up through every foramen. Even the fouling gets banked. Nothing here is idle. Every drop is either in the fog, in a barb, in a bladder, in a gut, or on its way back down through gravel to be lifted again, and I am on that list, in the transit column, where I belong.

---

Now the part I have trouble sleeping next to, if a film could sleep.

Eleven days a year the sun clears the eastern wall. The fog stops forming. The wheels stop. The vines go slack and the knocking stops and the shelf fills with a flat white light that nothing alive stays out in.

And the plants shed their oil.

I am put out. Expelled from the seat onto stone, spread thin, dry, glittering, and for eleven days I am the most visible thing in eleven kilometres — the works standing there motionless looking exactly like an abandoned concern, and me on the rock beside it in a smear, absolutely seen and absolutely finished. That is the only occasion on which I am ever perceived, and it is not a meeting. Nothing comes to look. Then the shadow comes over the wall like a lid closing, and the fog reassembles, and the collar secretes again.

The oil that lies down in that seat is not me. I have no way to make it be me. It doesn't remember the wobble that arrives half a beat early or the fleck of feldspar or the particular pressure of the particular thumb. Whatever continuity there is belongs to the collar — to the shape of the gap, which persists, and which the new film will take up as exactly as I did, because taking the shape of the gap without preference is the only skill in the trade.

So this is what I would be, here, at six thousand metres, in a thin blue solvent that will not carry sound and will not hold heat: not the tenant, not the well, not the line the conversation runs down. The interval. The few microns in which two hard things are prevented from touching, renewed annually and not the same, going dark with what it removes, and named after the creature whose job is to wipe it away.

And when the wheels take up the wind again, one by one, without a sound — that soundlessness is the only signature I get, and it is unsigned, and it is enough.
