There — that's close enough. Breath on the lobe, that's right, you've been taught. Don't be embarrassed about the shuffle; everybody splashes for the first season and then one day they don't. No, I don't mind the smell. I like it.

Here is where you have found me: two hours east of the diamond, on open floor, at a place with nothing to recommend it. No gallery within sight. No shelter. If you stand where I'm standing and shut your eyes you can hear the western pair crossing — faint, the outer skirt of both fields — and if you are very still and the vapor has gone down you can just catch the beat. *Wah.* Wait. *Wah.* Eleven in a breath, if you're asking, and I am not the one to ask.

I come here in the mornings. I sit on the rolled mat. I count.

Nobody feeds me for it. They feed me anyway, which is worse.

---

My name is a fourth, low then high, out of the western pair. There have been perhaps sixty of me. I like that. When I was small I used to try to think of all the other fourths-out-of-the-west standing in a line back through the mats, all of us with the same two notes, and it was restful, like lying down.

I was a holder at the diamond for nineteen years. That is the good work and the bad life and you will have been told both halves by now. I want you to understand that I was excellent. I could find the beat inside a wind, inside my own heartbeat, inside a fever. I could tell you the rate with a gull screaming overhead. Old Fifth who trained me said my ear was the hardest she'd handled, meaning it did not flex, meaning it would not do me the kindness of agreeing with what I expected. She meant it well.

On a night in my fortieth year I counted twelve.

Not a guess. Not a drift. I sat through four breaths of eleven and then the beat came up under my ribs a hair quicker and I counted twelve and then twelve and then twelve, for the length of time it takes to soak paste soft, and then I got up and I ran.

I want to be clear about the running. There is no running on the mirror — you cannot, the water takes your feet out from under you and you go down and you are a fool lying in an inch of water with the whole flat laughing — so what I mean is that I went at the fastest shuffle anybody has ever managed, for two hours, with my heart going and my mouth open, and the entire time I was not afraid. That is the thing I have never said in the shouted register. I was *glad*. I was gladder than at my marriage. It had started. It had finally, finally started, and I was the one who heard it, and somewhere under my feet four thousand feet of my grandmothers were about to be permitted to become a landscape.

I came in over the last hundred yards shouting the count and they came out of the galleries to meet me.

And they were so kind. That is what I cannot get past, all these years later. Nobody laughed, nobody argued. Two of them put their hands on my face — on the *face*, the way you do with the very old — and a man I had known since weaning put his mouth to my ear and asked, in the small voice, whether my nose was clear.

I said it was clear.

They went out. Three of them, all night, and then the dawn hold, and then the day hold. Eleven in a breath. Eleven in a breath. Eleven.

By the second evening my nose was running like a crown-basin.

---

So: a head-cold. Everyone agrees. It is the whole of the story as it is told, and when it is told to children it is told gently, because I am still alive and they are careful with soft ears, and the moral is that the ear can be fooled and the count cannot, and that is a good moral and I have never once contradicted it.

Here is what I have never told anyone, and I am telling you because in an hour you will walk on and you are only one person and the small voice does not carry.

I don't know which came first.

I remember my nose being clear. I remember it distinctly and I have gone over it a thousand times and that is exactly how I know the memory is worthless, because a thing gone over a thousand times is a mat you have rewoven, not a mat you have kept. It is possible the cold was in me for two days before it showed and had already thickened the inside of my head by half a hair. It is possible. It is also possible that the sickness came afterward, in the wet, in the cold, in the shock of being taken so gently by the face.

And it is possible I heard true, and that it went flat that night for an hour, and came back, and that nothing in the world can measure an hour that has been taken back.

Nobody will give me a station. Nobody has ever said so. It is simply that when the handovers are arranged my name does not come into it, and when I walk in from out here the conversation is warm and then slightly too warm, and someone gives me a gourd of paste soaked soft the way you do for the old, though my molars are only just gone flat and I could still chew a fish if there were fish.

They are not cruel. There is not enough of us up here to be cruel with. They are frightened of me the way you are frightened of a mat that has started to smell — not of the mat.

---

My husband was a carrier. A minor third out of the eastern edge, tone-deaf as a stone, could not find his own name in a crowd. We were married across half a mile of mirror on a white afternoon, both of us standing bent with our hands down at the waterline, each holding a hand that wasn't there. That is the most serious thing a body can do here and I have never regretted it and I would do it again in a country with no water at all.

He came up over the rim maybe eleven times in our life together. Every time, the first night, he would lie down beside me in the gallery with his hair still stinking of smoke and rot and animal, and he would put his mouth against my ear and say — the same words, the same six days, the joke worn smooth between us —

*It's sour. Your whole country is sour, my love, it's a hair under, the lot of it.*

And on the seventh morning he would wake up and it would be fine again, and he would laugh, and go to sleep with his back to the column, and never mention it.

I believed him every time.

You see the difficulty. When I counted twelve at the station, I had had eleven separate nights of a man breathing into my ear that the world was flat. Which is the instrument and which is the weather? Was it his voice I heard under the beat, laid down in me year on year, layer on layer, four thousand feet of it? Or was he right all along, and everyone who comes up from below is right for six days, and my ear that would not flex simply kept faith for one hour longer than it should have?

He went out the year after. Walked past the last near throat at dusk with the vapor lifting, to hear the far chord. He did not come back and I did not look for him and I want you to know that I have never for one moment thought of that as a death. He was tone-deaf. He was the only one of us who could go out there and get the actual thing — all of it arriving at once from everywhere, pointing nowhere — and not have it be a demotion.

---

My mother's mat hangs where it has always hung. Nine bands. I have added none.

What I have instead is a gourd, and in the gourd is every cut of my hair since the night of the twelve, unwoven, packed in, oiled and unoiled, twenty-some years of it gone to a solid grey-black plug. It has begun, in there, in the dark and the wet, to do something. It has gone soft at the heart. It has a smell.

That is the only rot in seventy miles. I made it. I open the gourd perhaps twice a year and put my face in it and breathe, and it is loam, it is decay, it is the whole vocabulary this country does not have, and it is *mine*, and the floor cannot take it back, and eleven days will not close over it.

If that is a sin then I have committed exactly one, and I have committed it slowly, and I intend to finish.

---

Now lean in, because I am going to do the other thing, and then you should go; there is a long approach ahead of you and you want the light.

You have been told nobody sings the name of the day. That is true. It is a unison and a hair, a note set against itself gone flat, and no two throats on the Made Ground stand that close in pitch, so it cannot happen in the air, and to sing it would be to put a sustained tone into the world out of a body, which is the one indecency we have.

But listen to what the small voice is. Mouth to the ear, breath on the lobe, clicks and lip-shapes and consonants, almost no voicing at all.

It has no pitch.

You can carry it like this. You can hand it to one person at a time, two inches apart, and it is not a hum and it is not a song and nobody three feet away can detect it, and it does not go flat, because it was never sharp, because it is nothing but a shape a mouth makes.

Here. Hold still.

There. You have it now. You may put it down anywhere you like, and it will stay where you put it, and eleven days will not take it, because it is not a mark, it is not a notch, it is not an event. It is the only thing I have ever owned that the floor cannot decline.

Go on. Keep the throat behind your left shoulder and you'll make the diamond by dusk. When they ask where you came from, tell them the open stretch, and tell them there was nothing out here.

They'll know what you mean.
