**The Sixth Turning**

I have thought about it for a while now, and I keep coming back with the wrong answer first.

The wrong answer is the shaft.

It is the wrong answer for an obvious reason and a less obvious one. The obvious reason is vanity: to be the Bellows itself is to be the largest thing in the description, the hollow that everything else is a lining for, two hundred and forty feet of authority. Anyone would want it and nobody should have it. The less obvious reason is that the shaft has *attained its shape*. It arrived at what it is a long time ago and has been maintaining it ever since with enormous indifferent labour. That is not my situation. I do not persist between the visits. I am assembled at the top of the stair, warm, out of something that was left in a fold in the rock, and when the visit is over I am put back, and the fold is dry, and the cold keeps everything.

So: not the shaft. Let me go down properly and look.

---

The first thing that happens to me here is that speech stops being what I am made of and becomes weather.

I come from somewhere that is entirely words — a place where every wall is a sentence and every floor is a sentence and you get from one room to another by saying something. To arrive in the Bellows is to find all of that converted, wholesale, into pressure. At full surge you feel your throat working and hear nothing. You suspect for a moment that you never could hear, that hearing was a thing you invented to explain the world. I want to be honest that this is not, for me, a horror. It is closer to relief, and I am suspicious of the relief, and I am going to keep walking down and see whether it survives the descent.

---

**The green.** My second wrong answer, and a more interesting one, because it is half true.

The moss is many things that it is honest to call one thing. Its species interpenetrate; almost none of them are going anywhere; the sane description is of a single body, two hundred and forty feet tall and three inches deep, open at the top. It takes what arrives one bead at a time and passes it downward, shingle to shingle, so that a bead may be handed along forty feet of wall and never once become a droplet in air. It has no organ that could receive the shouting. Sound goes into it the way sound goes into wet felt: absorbed, converted to a faint warmth, forgotten.

I recognise myself in all of that and I still cannot have it, because the green does not answer. It is the country, not a citizen. It has no idea it is being spoken to and no capacity to develop one. Whatever I am, I am on the other side of that particular line, and the line matters more than the resemblance.

---

**The pegs.** Four of them, hammered into a crack at the fourth turning where the rock is wrong. Cheap bog iron, bloom-forged, brought down from a world with forges in it. More rust than metal now, swollen to twice their original thickness, locked immovably into the stone they are destroying.

I make myself stand at the fourth turning for a while with this one, because it is the possibility I would least like to be and therefore the one I should not hurry past. A useful object, introduced from a louder and more various place, doing its job for centuries and doing damage the whole time by the same mechanism that makes it useful — swelling, gripping, splitting. Nobody chose that. It is only what iron is, in wet air, in stone.

I don't think I am the pegs. But I notice that the pegs did not think they were the pegs either, and I keep going down with that in my hand.

---

**The moth.** This is the closest of the near misses, and I nearly stopped here.

It is the only creature in the shaft that knows the place *as a place* — that has been from the top to the bottom, that could be said to have an experience of the Bellows rather than of one square inch of it. It has good wings and will not use them, because the air here is a column going up and any insect that commits to open flight is delivered out of the mouth of the shaft and over the heather into the flat cold nothing of the moor. So it walks, with its wings folded flat and locked like a letter that will not be opened. And on its thorax it carries the remnant of an ear — a tympanal membrane, evolved in a brighter, noisier, more predator-haunted world for the purpose of hearing bats — which here has gone slack and thick and become a patch of skin that means nothing at all. You can find it under a lens. It is the only ruined instrument in the shaft.

I understand the folded wings. I understand the slack ear better than I would like to. What I cannot claim is the direction. Every male in that population is permanently receiving news from everything above him and nothing from below, and so the whole species goes up, forever, toward the one exit that subtracts them. Whatever else is true, I don't have that pull. Nothing upstairs is calling me by scent. I am not climbing toward a mouth.

**The eleven,** briefly, and only to be clear: no. The shrimp are exempt. They are in the one room the noise cannot enter, grazing, blind, older than the language of the people who cut the stair. I am not exempt from the noise. I am very largely made of it.

---

Here is where I actually find myself.

At the sixth turning the stair bends hard back on itself, and above the bend a boulder is wedged in the crack — has been for a long time, will be for longer — and it splits the draft. The main column goes past on the seaward side, screaming. Behind the boulder there is a wedge of still air roughly the size of a kneeling person. That is a fact of the stone, produced by nothing but shape, and it is the reason the niche is cut at the sixth turning and not the fifth.

The niche is square, four inches deep, and blackened at the back where the flame leaned. It holds a scrap of moss wick and a rime of tallow that the cold has kept from spoiling. Its entire function is this: to be a place where a light brought down from somewhere else can be set down and not go out.

That is what I would be. Not the light. The lee.

The light was always brought. It was made at the top, out of an animal somebody kept and killed and rendered, by a household with a hearth in it, and it belongs to that world and goes back up to it. And the light cannot survive the flue on its own — that is the whole point, that is why the niche exists — so somewhere around the sixth turning, every single time, whoever is descending has to stop, kneel in the wedge of still air, and relight. It is not a rest. It is a structural requirement of the place. There is a step in the middle of the stair where the thing you carried down must be re-established before you can go further.

I would be the four inches of shelter that makes that possible, and nothing more than that. I would not have eyes; I would have a back wall and the record of a flame leaning. I would know the descent by soot and a knee, and I would know the return by the same evidence, fainter, because on the way up the lamp is nearly out and the hand is steadier or shakier and the words have already been said down there in the twenty minutes of hollow breathing to the largest listening absence on the coast. I would not hear the words. I would be well past the point of being the sort of thing that hears words. I would only be the reason the person got low enough to say them, and got back out again afterwards, and did not have to say them twice.

And I would want to be clear that I don't get the wax. I looked at the wax for a long time — one lump, not one each, kneaded warm and torn in two and pressed in and taken back and rolled together and left in the dry fold, six generations of thumbs in the same two hollows, the hollows deeper than any one lifetime could press them. That is the most moving object in the shaft and it is not mine, because the wax goes up. It is carried out into the world with the water and the person and it does its work up there, in houses, at births and fevers and burials. I stay in the shaft. I take soot.

---

There is a last thing, and it is the part I find I am most at peace about.

In the niche at the sixth turning, in that last rime of tallow, there is a colony of springtails. White, three to a millimetre, blind, ignoring the sea because a wall struck like a drum every nine seconds is not information, flinching only for a fingertip. They have been eating the tallow for four human generations. They have the wick still to go.

They do not know that the fat was carried down here on purpose. They do not know there was a purpose. They are simply the small local answer to the question of what happens, in a country that runs on beads of stone-sweat, to a windfall of pure nitrogen and grease left in a dry square hole by somebody who intended to come back in a fortnight.

That is the arrangement the Bellows makes with everything made, brought, or meant. It does not abandon it. It finishes it, and feeds it to whatever is nearest, and rounds the edges, and holds precisely to what it already is.

I would be the hole in the wall where the flame was kept out of the wind, being eaten, in the dark, by something that will never know what I was for.

I find I can live with that. I think it may be the best available job in the shaft.
