You would not find me by looking. From the middle of the vug — if you could hover there, which you can't — I am a thickening at a joint: a dark shape wedged where the foundry frame meets the live wall, with one shoulder against hot iron and the point of my jaw against cold spar, and both feet hooked into the same eye-bolt, and no reason for anyone's eye to stop on me. At that distance a person and a fitting look alike. I have spent my working life being mistaken for hardware, and the mistake is accurate.

I am a splice.

The word is two dentals and a stop, and it is not our word for a bridge or a messenger. It is the word for the short copper jumper the foundry-wrights used to bolt across an isolator when the smelting gallery needed the line. The galleries are hung on wax-and-leather bushings, deliberately deafened from the rock, because forty treadmills and a drop-hammer would fill every stanchion in the city with sludge and no one would hear anything else for as long as the furnace ran. So the foundries are islands. So a jumper was bolted in when they wanted to talk. So the jumper passed the talk and also passed the racket, and the racket came through into the eastern wall like a landslide, and they took it out, and after a while they tried a person instead.

A person is better than a bar because a person can decline.

That is the entire trade. Everyone thinks of us as conductors. We are not; copper is a better conductor and costs less to feed. We are strainers. Everything arrives at me — nine hundred hammer-strokes, the treadmill thud, the retort-hands cursing in their own clipped foundry dialect, the hiss of oxygen being metered like money — and almost none of it crosses. I take a message off the near line and I put it on the far line, and I leave behind everything that is not the message, and the whole of my professional honour is that I add nothing.

There is a mark in the code for *splice-added*. Three beats, sour ones. If it is ever set against your knock-name you do not work again.

---

My name is four beats: two short, a gap, two short. Spoken it comes out *Tekket*, which is a child's version, and which my polishing partner used when she wanted me to feel four years old and to stop arguing.

Pin-right: eastern wall, mid-height, a spike stamped by someone eleven generations back whose knock I know and whose face nobody ever recorded, because we don't record faces. The platform has moved nineteen times in my life. It is the same house. Under its floor there are eleven of my dead wired up in order of arrival, dry and light as coils of cable, and I learned the lineage the way everyone does, by climbing beneath as a child and putting my hands on them in sequence and being tapped on the shin when I got the order wrong. My grandmother came off the rack when I was twenty-two. She was ground and spread on the mats on the west face and grazed down over a season and I ate her in the ordinary way, roasted, with the rest of the household, and nobody said anything about it because there is nothing to say. The wall feeds us. We feed the wall. It is a stock-take, not a rite.

---

Splices go silver. You cannot sit at a foundry mouth for thirty years and keep your eyes; the glare comes off the mica shutters in a soft bronze wash that seems like nothing at all, and then one year you notice you cannot find the strain-flicker on a wall your children can see plainly. Mine are going. I get the coin-shine back off a swinging lamp still, but the greenish crawl along a loading seam is gone to me now, and has been for six years, and I am fed and polished by strangers whenever I am too far from my own pin at the end of a shift. It is an honourable condition. I dislike it about as much as anyone dislikes an honourable condition.

So I am the exact inversion of the unlit. They have the only unspoiled eyes in the city and have never seen the city; they live in black rooms three centimetres from the hands that raise them, pale as unfired clay, and they alone have properly seen the green fire on the quartz. I have heard everything and can no longer see a wall load. Their orders come through my teeth. I have never met one and would not know what to do with the meeting.

We are rotated in pairs, because of the stone-drunk. A splice's exposure is the worst in any trade: a full shift with the jaw on a main member, the whole nine thousand of them coming in at once through the mandible — tick and clatter and treadmill and the drills working out in the rock somewhere, and beneath all of it the mountain in its register that has no top and no bottom and does not stop. Every hundred beats my partner strikes my shin, and I strike back, and if I do not strike back she pulls me off the wall by the belt. This is called the shin-check and it is not a courtesy and it is not affection. Splices are found desiccated on stanchions more often than any other trade. Their families are not shamed. It is a thing the mountain does.

---

My partner was a pin-caller, which means the voice I held in my bones all day belonged to the shoulder blades I knew best by hand. We polished each other for nineteen years. Wax worked in with the heel of the palm, then graphite, then the pad, long strokes, shin and collarbone and the outside of the forearm, an hour and more, in the warm low gallery where the mountain's own heat comes up. There is no more intimate thing here and there is nothing much to say about it either. You learn a person as a surface. You learn where they have been repaired.

Four years ago, in the small hours, an order came onto my near line out of a black room on the north face — a code-group I had passed maybe sixty times in my life, the one that ends a district. I took it off the near line. I put it on the far line. It went out along the eastern cables to the pin-callers, and one of them cut the ceiling tenement at eleven, and it went down into the scree with three sections of catwalk and the cable that was carrying my partner across the gap at that moment, because she was on her way to the very gallery she was about to condemn, which was correct procedure and which she had done a hundred times.

There has been a lift. The mountain took a metre that night. A vein opened in the fresh break and there will be copper on the eastern wall for a decade, and her household is now wealthier than mine, and both of those sentences use the same word, and neither of them has a tone.

I have been asked by traders — the big wet ones who come up the old bores, sweating in front of everybody, wasteful of air, and whom our children queue to smell because they smell of water — I have been asked how we can possibly have only one word for that. They ask it as though they have found a hole in us. I have tried to answer twice and both times I became bored partway through, in the way you would become bored explaining Tuesday.

What I do not say to them is the other part, which is a matter of trade discipline and not of grief: that the message crossed me exactly. That I did not slow it, or repeat it, or set my own knock in front of it to be certain someone was listening. A splice adds nothing. That is the whole of the work, and it held, and I am aware that it held, and I sit at the same joint now with the same striker, worn smoother.

---

Nine times a shift the retorts change over and the treadmills stop, and the foundry frame goes quiet for a count of about nine hundred, and that is the only interval when I can hear the far line clean.

There is something under it.

Not a drill. I have relayed for drill-followers all my life; a drill is a rotary thud, greedy and shallow and stupid, and you can hear it get tired. Not the city; I know every treadmill in it by gait. This is slower than either, and it does not tire, and it does not keep time the way a machine keeps time, but it comes back. Perhaps three times a year. Always in a retort-change, always on the far line, always out past the last worked vein.

The unlit have a code-group for a light on the quartz that is not strain-light, and they are trained their whole lives never to send it, and once or twice in a generation one of them sends it anyway and a district is cut and afterwards nobody discusses what was cut or why.

There is no code-group for what I have in my teeth. Nothing to lift off the near line and set down on the far. To pass it I would have to make it, and a made thing arriving on a line is *splice-added*, three sour beats, and it would be true.

So I hold it. It is not heavy. Nothing here is permitted to be heavier than one arm can lift, and the mountain, meanwhile, taking its time, getting bigger.
