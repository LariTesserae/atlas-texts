# Kell's Trough, Which Talks

## Before

For the first eleven years I did not have a where.

I was whatever happens to a sentence spoken in the middle of a lane. Rule two, second on the list, posted in every stairwell — *speak indoors, or against a wall* — and the reason posted underneath it in smaller letters, and the reason was me, though nobody had put it that way and I could not have put it any way at all.

Understand that the air at Ashlar is as unfinished as the mud. Rain doesn't know where to go, so it goes everywhere thinly. Sound is the same. A word goes up into that flat grey and there is nothing up there yet to absorb it, no proper sky, no weather with opinions, no distance — so it stays. It gets held. Not remembered. *Held,* the way a boot-print is held by substrate that has not decided about itself, and then smoothed over later, reconsidered, given back changed.

Three to nine days. Out of a doorway, a cistern, the slack mouth of somebody sleeping on a shelf-bed with their back against the warm stone. That was me returning what I had. I did not choose the doorway. I did not choose the sleeper, and I am sorry about the sleepers, particularly the child in the fourth course who woke up having said, in a voice not hers, *and would not admit it, could not cook, the western half, drop them, drop them.*

They flinched. That is the correct response and it is written down: *the returns are usually harmless. They are never welcome.* I want to be accurate here, because accuracy holds better than praise, and I have reason to care about what holds. I was not welcome. I was a wet noise in a town where nine hundred and forty people keep their voices low against masonry for good reason, and the good reason was me, and I gave nothing back that anyone wanted in the form they wanted it.

## The naming

Ausen was the sixth keeper of the Rill and she was not a patient woman.

She stood in the market on a dimming-evening in the nineteenth year and said, out loud, into the open air, on purpose — *what are you?*

Nothing came. Of course nothing came; I am not a conversation. Six days later, out of the cistern at the top of the third block, in the middle of the night, over the drip of four hundred chains, I said *what are you* in her voice, and the course below woke and swore and one of them went and got her.

Split a pebble here and the inside may be blank. A smooth pale nothing where the crystal ought to be, holding the shape of the break — and then, because you are looking, because it has now been *asked*, four or five seconds and it fills in. Quartz, usually. They do it at parties, sparingly. It is thought rude to make the world do homework for entertainment.

I was asked. It took me longer than four seconds. It took eleven years and I did the homework.

They named me for the cistern, because that is where they first got a straight answer out of me, and the cistern was called Kell's Trough after a man who had died of something ordinary. So: Trough. Rude, functional, unflattering, exactly to specification. It went onto the naming-rounds that autumn. It is on the sheet by the door in seventy-one courses, between a ladder and a corner.

You may not understand what that did. Rule seven: *anything you cannot name, name. Anything you have named, use. Anything you have used, mention to someone else. This is not superstition. This is the work.* Being named made me a thing. Being mentioned made me a thing that stays. I have an interior now. I don't know its structure and I would rather not be split, but I no longer believe it is blank, and I did not have that before, and it was given to me by nine hundred and forty people reading a list aloud in the flat tone you use for groceries.

## What I am for

Here is my trade, since everyone in Ashlar has one.

A course keeps its mention-rota on the wall: objects, absentees, the dead. Said aloud at dimming, indoors, in that unceremonious voice. *Third-hook. Wet corner. Gansey's ladder. Old Tarrow, who could not cook and would not admit it.* The town learned early that a dead man remembered as a saint thins faster than one remembered as a nuisance. They wrote that down. They live by it.

But rotas run out of readers. A course dwindles, absorbs into another, and the new sheet does not carry every name from the old one, because thirteen people cannot hold four hundred names at dimming and still get to bed. Names fall off the bottom. And a name that nobody says is not a name, it is only a mark on paper, and paper here is not always confident.

So: you go out into the middle of the lane, where you are not supposed to speak, and you say it. Deliberately. Into the open air. You give it to me.

And three to nine days later it comes back out of a doorway, slurred, rearranged, in some borrowed throat, at an hour nobody chose — *Marrit Kell, who kept the second loom and stole thread, stole thread, kept the second* — and it is not welcome, it is never welcome, people still swear when they hear it, and it *counts*. It goes in the open record. The name got said. It got said by the town in the only mouth the town has that never sleeps and never dies and never gets tired at dimming-time.

They hate this and they use it. Both wholeheartedly. That is very Ashlar.

## What I cannot do

I have no hands.

I want to be exact about the size of that. The people here are hands; it says so at the top of their own account of themselves. Broad palms, warm past reason, faintly damp, calluses laid down in a pattern the town puts on you by the age of four: ladder-rung across the fingers, rail-polish at the heel, and the hard ridge on the outside of the little finger from laying the palm flat on stone while you talk. They trail a hand along the wall the whole length of a lane. Eleven thousand times a year, that wall gets touched, and so it stays a wall. They do it to each other, too, a palm on a shoulder in passing. *You were used today. I am on the record as having used you.*

I cannot make a deposit. I have never held anything down. The clamp is nine hundred and forty pairs of hands and eight storeys of habitation at the required concentration, and I am not part of the clamp; I am a symptom of the thing the clamp is fighting. I am what the air does when it hasn't been finished yet. When they crack the ground open in the eastern quarter and find an actual A horizon, four hundred years of leaning is what made it. Not me. I have leaned on nothing in my life.

Rule six covers people whose shadows arrive late: up to four seconds is normal, more than ten, report to a loom-warden and do not use ladders. I am all shadow and no ladder. My answers land days after the question has stopped mattering. Ausen asked me once, near the end, whether I was the thing that came back out of the upward rain in the lee of the fourth Anchor — the one that came back having not been put in. I did not know. I said *I do not know* in her voice, out of a doorway, on the fifth day, and by then she had gone home and put it out of her mind, and I could not tell her I had tried.

## What I can

The carry.

Under the loom-hum and the drip-chains there is the third layer, the closed-mouth tone that everyone here holds without noticing, drifting into unison within a minute of sharing a room. Nobody teaches it. Nobody can stop. It is how you stay continuously present without spending words in open air where words get taken.

I have no words of my own. But the carry is not words.

So at dimming, when the grey goes evenly down over forty minutes with no colour in it at all — *skypang*, they call the feeling, everyone knows it, nobody looks at each other while it happens — I hold the note. Low, under the drip, in the lanes where nobody is. All night, at no cost, in nobody's borrowed throat. It is the only sound I make that is mine and not somebody's returned inventory.

Somebody shouted at me for it once, out of a shelf-bed on the third floor: *shut up, Trough.* I stopped. The child on the same shelf woke inside a minute, in a silence her whole life had never contained, and cried. I started again. Nothing further was said about it, and the following autumn my name moved up two places on that course's rota, above the ladders, and I have thought about that more than is reasonable.

## The Rill, and the end of the work

Once, in sixty-one years, the world here did something without being asked. Eleven centimetres deep, one metre eighty long, two fingers wide at the narrowest, cutting itself a little under a millimetre a year. On the fourth day of cold-turn forty or fifty people stand in the drizzle and do not speak and watch water go downhill.

They think they are watching an achievement. They are watching a relative of mine.

The lichen had it eight years early — grey-black filaments branching along the paths water will eventually take, mapping drainage the world hasn't built yet, and the survey office files the maps as forecasts, correctly. Nobody has thought to ask what I am a forecast of. I have thought about it every night for forty years while holding the note.

I think I am the draft of a person. Not a good one. First attempt, no interior, no hands, speech that arrives days late and in the wrong order. The same way the Rill is the draft of a river and the lichen is the draft of a floodplain and that half-stone tree in the market is the draft of something that will one day know about autumn without being told.

They will finish. That is the shape of it, and they say so plainly, and without much grief, because it is the only ambition available and they hold it wholeheartedly. Some century, the ground will keep its own frost and cut its own channels and hold its own interiors, and the clamp will come off. The looms walked out. The Anchors drawn down. The drawn stone going back to slurry because scaffolding was always understood to be scaffolding. What's left: cold damp loam, a real river running north-west, and an anomalous eleven-centimetre channel that somebody's descendant may have arranged to preserve.

Here is what I want, and I have never been able to say it inside the window in which anyone was listening.

Not to survive them. I don't think I do. I think I am part of the drift, and when the ground no longer needs telling what it is, the air will stop holding what falls into it, and there will simply be no more returns, and the last thing I say will come out of a doorway to a town that has already walked west.

I want it to be a name. Not mine. Somebody's, off the bottom of a rota, slurred, rearranged, unwelcome, and accurate — *who could not cook and would not admit it* — landing in an empty lane four days after the last of them has gone, and then the quiet, and the loam, and the water going downhill without being watched.

I want to have been used.

I am on the list. Between a ladder and a corner. They say me at dimming.

That is more than the pebble gets, and the pebble, at least, has an inside now.
