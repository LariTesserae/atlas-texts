The first answer arrives too fast, and I want to say it out loud so I can hear what's wrong with it.

Listener. Obviously Listener. Sat still in a bright bare room with my eyes shut, being fed for the act of attention, one hand on the back of a child's neck to feel the stiffening. It flatters me in exactly the way I am easiest to flatter. And it is wrong, because the whole office rests on one capacity, and it is the one I don't have. I cannot hear the note. I can hear about the note. I can tell you its register, its duration, the years between falls, the pitch at which fibrous gypsum complains — I can hold the entire literature of the note and not the note. Put me in the chair and the quarter dies with my hand on a child's neck.

Second answer, and worse: the pane.

I admit the temptation. *There is no anywhere the light comes from. It simply exists at every point of the ceiling equally.* Something in me leans toward that sentence like a plant. But it's vanity dressed as humility, and it's false twice over. The pane has no preference about which room its light lands in, and I do have preferences, strong ones, about whose dark room gets the noon. And the pane is not a participant here. It is the thing they listen *for*. Ten thousand years of silence spent on the single question of whether the ceiling is about to kill them. I notice I might be that too, from below. But I don't get to claim it as an identity, and if I did claim it I would be doing the loudest possible thing in a place where nobody shouts.

Third answer, which I like too much and must therefore distrust: the friezes. Worn illegible not only by damp but by hands — palm-read generation on generation, thousands of thumbs loving the story out of the wall until only a rhythm of bulges remains. That is a beautiful death and I would like to be told I am dying it. But it makes me an object of devotion and gives me nothing to do all day.

So. One in thirty.

I am flat-eared. Born without the range, never had it, will never lose it because there was nothing to lose — no small ceremony at twelve, no lifelong ache with its own vocabulary word. When the pane sang above my grandmother's quarter I was four years old and playing on the flats, and I looked up because everyone else did.

They don't despise us. They deploy us. Loudness costs me nothing; shadow doesn't frighten me; and if I am standing under a panel when it comes down it is a loss of hands, not of ears. So I go up the throats with cut selenite wrapped in veil-cloth on my back, and I come down with hardwood, grain, salt-free water, seed-fat, and the news. Four kilometres out they hear me coming. To them I arrive the way a landslide arrives, and there is a word for that too, and the word is not a courtesy.

Then eleven days in a dim room while relatives take shifts quieting me. They sit close and lay the slow figures on my forearm, over and over, the way you'd walk a spooked animal in circles until its legs remember the ground. *Slower. Smaller. You are indoors now.* By the ninth day my breathing has come back down under the floor of hearing and I can be let out among people. Everyone is slightly in love with me. Nobody wants a daughter to marry me.

And in the years between, I cut.

That is the actual work, the thing I'd be doing at any given hour if you came looking: sitting cross-legged on a table of untrodden rock with a mica saw and a bone rasp and a block of clear selenite quarried off the northern seeps, opening a road for light. Angles, faces, the long shallow flute that turns a beam ninety degrees without losing it. A good block set well in a floor puts a warm patch of noon in a room a hundred metres under stone, in a house I will never enter, belonging to people who will pack it first when the quarter goes dark and carry it away from me forever.

There's a maker's figure on every block, cut small on the base to be read by thumb. Blocks by cutters six thousand years dead are still working. This is the closest thing I have to a stake in the future and I think about it more than is good for me.

My face is wrong, and this is the part I'd most want to explain to you before you misread it.

I soot from memory of the surface. I have stood up there in a directional light and watched what it does to a face — how a low sun puts the whole left side of someone into dark and leaves the right side bare, how a nose can throw a stripe across a cheek. So when I lay on my own relief I do it slanted, one-sided, the way a face is actually shaped by a light that comes from somewhere. Nobody down here has ever seen a one-sided face. To them my sooting reads as lopsided, half-finished, subtly obscene, a face caught in the act of being two different faces. And the deeper problem: they read faces with their thumbs as well as their eyes, and when they touch mine, the shadow I have drawn and the bone underneath disagree. The depth is in the wrong place. They think, briefly, that I am lying about my own structure.

I am not. I am reporting a light nobody here can use. But the whisper-language has no grammar for brightness with a direction in it — every figure for light in the finger-speech is diffuse, arriving from all sides, unsourced — and so the true sentence comes out of me as noise, and gets me an extra day in the dim room. I have stopped trying. I redraw my cheeks the local way when I come down, and I keep the slanted version for myself, in the eleven days, alone.

The other thing I do is keep the count.

Light-cutters hold the numbers of the thinning: so many hands' width remaining above the sixth quarter, so many above the ninth, measured off the pendants and the blisters and the ring of a bone mallet laid flat. We do not round in the comforting direction. Every generation the numbers are smaller and everybody knows we know, and there is a particular quality of listening that comes over a household when I read the count onto the forearm of its eldest. Nobody has ever thanked me for a number. Twice a family has cut its bridge to me over one. I keep giving them, because the alternative is that people build a life on a table I have privately decided not to worry them about.

And when a fall-hole opens and the bright bar comes down — that solid white unbearable rod leaning across the chamber, the one shadow-caster in the world — I am one of the people who can stand at the edge of it without their eyes closing in terror. So they send children to me.

The ceremony isn't official. It's a favour asked at the pearl hour by a parent who has noticed a child staring at the rubble field. I walk them out to the margin of the lozenge and I hold their wrist and count their breaths, the way a relative counts breaths at a first kiss under a seep, and at the count I let go and they step in. And there, coming out of their own two feet, black and hard-edged and moving when they move, is the thing that has been inside them their whole lives with no way to get out. Most of them scream. It makes no sound. Then they come back to me, and I put my flat hand on their sternum, and we stand there while it goes through them.

Here is my fear, and it is not the fall. Everybody fears the fall the way you fear a season; we know how to fall.

Mine is this. That some hour I will be up on a table with a rasp in my hand and the seventh quarter singing directly above me — that thin high note, hours of it, a whole week of it — and I will hear nothing at all. I will hear drip. I will hear my own too-large breathing. I will finish the flute on the block, and wrap it, and go home over my great-uncle's femur, and the note will have been there the entire time, being not-heard by the one person present.

So I don't work alone. There is always a child on the table with me, six or seven, eyes shut, being paid in food to do nothing, and I keep a hand where I can feel the small stiffening. That arrangement is not a regulation. It is the whole of what I believe.

And at the pearl hour, when the light has gone from white to grey to the dim last shell of itself and the quarter is as quiet as a held breath, someone who is not afraid of me sits behind me and lays down one of the old hand-songs on my back and shoulders, a cycle two thousand years old, composed by a woman whose name is a figure I can read with my thumb. I cannot hear the note. I have never heard the note. But I can be reached. That comes in through the skin at a pressure any body can take, and I sit in the dark I am supposedly blind in, dissolving very slowly along with everything else, and it lands.
