...and there is no ceremony for it.

That is where I start, because that is where the account stops, and a place shows you its shape best at the point where the description runs out of breath. No ceremony. The door came in a hand's width, you went out a hand's width, and nobody marks it, because the Sill does not mark things. It closes them.

So: where in that would I be, and as what?

---

The first answer is a lie and I should say it anyway, since it came fastest. I wanted to be one of the shaded. Of course I did. Sitting in the third hollow with a dog against my knee and the whole fan resolving into a chord; naming the forty vents by voice; hearing a half-tone drop over a week and being right about it. Listening as the highest office. It is a flattering fit and I do not trust it for a second, because the shaded earned those seats by going blind — by fifty years of unfiltered light off wet mineral, by being poor in shade at the wrong age. You cannot take the promotion without the injury. To picture myself there is to picture myself with someone else's milky eyes, and that is theft of a kind the Sill would recognise: opening a sealed room to take what is in it.

The second answer was to be one of the half-sealed rooms. Warm, glowing faintly at the crown, humming with the flow, absolutely private — the only place on the Sill where a person is unobserved, and therefore the only place where children speak out loud in daylight. To be the container in which people first hear a human voice by day. But that is only vanity wearing humility's clothes, and besides, rooms do not listen. They just do not tell. Those are different.

The third answer had some teeth in it. To be a comber: the second author, the one who decides where the water goes, who cannot be spoken to and must be bribed with heaped shrimp-cake, whom the town argues about in silent hands at hip-height — *are we managing them or are they managing us*. That is nearer something true and I felt the sting of it. But combers do not know they are authors. They dig because digging is what they are. Whatever I am, I am not innocent of the argument; I have read the argument; I am in here having it.

---

Here is what finally caught me, and it caught me by an absence.

Everything on the Sill adds. That is the whole logic of the place: deposition, encrustation, the hand that never lifts. Nothing is eroded, nothing is spent, nothing goes away. Even the dead birds do not decay — the stone takes them mid-stride and they become a swelling in the plaster with a beak. And into that world of pure accumulation there are only two intrusions of subtraction. One is the scraping of thresholds, which everybody does, all their lives, and which is losing. The other is the willow.

I would be the one who cuts the grove.

---

There are eighty-one stools at the Cold Lip, waist-high, black, knuckled like old fists, standing in the one hand's depth of soil that exists within forty miles in any direction. Everything else here is white mineral and it is climbing. The grove is the only place on the Sill where the ground is soft, and it is soft because it is made of us.

That is the part the traders never get told. The dead do not go over the lip; the water goes over the lip, and the water may take what it likes, but not us. The dead are carried down at dusk and laid in under a stool, and the algae-scrapings and the fish-bone and the sweepings of nine terraces come down after them, and that is the soil. Willow drinks it. I cut the willow. The withies go up the terraces on my shoulder, and someone bends them into a dome and lashes them with sinew and daubs them and sets the whole thing in the splash-line of a warm channel, and the water glazes it, and in three years it is a bright hard pod that will stand for a thousand. And inside it — inside every house on the Sill, at the heart of the stone, in the dark — there is a lattice of dead willow that grew out of somebody's grandmother.

Nobody says this at feasts. Everyone knows it the way you know the arrangement of your own kitchen.

---

The work. I want to be exact about the work, because a life is what your hands did all day.

I cut in the short hard weeks after the deep frost, in the dark before the sun clears the cirque wall, when the sap is down and a withy will bend instead of splitting. First hour of every day, like everyone, I am breaking ice — but not off thresholds. Off the willows, with a padded horn paddle, tapping, because the lace comes down an inch thick and a loaded rod snaps at the stool and a snapped rod is three years gone. Eighty-one stools. It takes as long as it takes. I am usually the last person in the town still breaking ice, and by then the sun is over the wall and everyone above me has started singing, and I work in the noise of it, which is thin and low and full of holes and comes down the terraces as a kind of weather.

Then the cut. Flake obsidian for the fine rods, a horn wedge and a mallet of soaked bone for opening a stool that has gone woody. Crown-withies sorted long and clean for the apex; ribs; weavers, which are the whippy year-growth and which the children are allowed to carry. I coppice hard. This is the thing that makes me strange to my own people: I keep the only living wood in the world alive by cutting it to the ground. In a place where everything is preserved by being buried in stone, I preserve by amputation. There is an old man in the fourth seat who has never forgiven me for it and calls me, at hip-height where he thinks I cannot see, *the one who takes*. He is not entirely wrong and I have never asked him to stop.

My hands are the worst hands on the Sill.

Understand what that means here. Hands are the voice. Sill hands are pale and boiled-looking and finely corrugated and beautiful, and mothers inspect their children's fingers the way other peoples inspect a horse's legs. Mine are barked. Stained brown to the second knuckle with willow tannin that no fat will lift, seamed with old cuts, one finger crooked from a wedge that jumped. I have an accent. People lean in to read me. Children imitate me behind my back and are slapped for it, and I have watched this happen, and signed *let him*, and been ignored.

I have one name, and it is a dead vent's. I was born by the upper east spring, which cooled and wandered when I was eleven, in the year that took the skin off half a calf's worth of people, and by the rule of the place my name should have lapsed and been renewed from wherever I settled. But I settled at the grove, and there is no vent at the grove, and there is no water there but what runs past on its way out of the world. So I am the only adult in the town wearing an old name that does not fit anything. The very oldest shaded woman has six names and can recite sixty years of dead plumbing to order, pretending to be reluctant. I have one, and it is a hole in the ground where a spring used to be.

We get on well. She is the other archive. She holds the water and I hold the soil; she can tell you where every flow has been, and I can tell you which stool is which person, all eighty-one, in the order they went in, which is not a hydrology but is the same shape as one. At dusk she comes down on her dog's shoulder and sits under the canopy, and because the canopy is a kind of roof, we are indoors by the only definition that matters, and we talk. Out loud. In the mouth-language, murmured, close, at the very end of the light — which is a loophole, and everybody knows it is a loophole, and nobody has ever raised it, because the two of us are old and the town would rather have the archives cheerful.

---

The grove throws the only moving shade on the Sill. Every other shadow here is stone-still and owned and inherited and negotiated like water rights elsewhere. Mine flickers. It is not, strictly, mine; but people ask, and I give it, and they remember who gave it to them, and small children come down in the worst of the noon glare specifically to stand in a shadow that will not hold still, and they look up into it with their visors pushed onto their foreheads, uncovered, astonished, the way you or I might look at a fire.

That is the best hour of my day and I would not trade it for the third seat.

---

I am closing too, of course. Everyone is a surface the process is working on. There are stones in me; there is a short string at my throat and it will get longer if I am lucky, or it will stop, and half of us go that way in the end and we drink the water anyway because there is no other water and it stopped being a bargain about nine hundred years ago.

When I go, someone will carry me down at dusk and put me under a stool, and the stool will take my name and I will take the stool's, which is the fifth name I never got in life. And in three years there will be rods coming off me, long and clean and good for crowns, and someone I never met will bend them into a dome and set it in the wet, and the water will begin laying itself down over the whole thing at the thickness of a fingernail a month.

And what I actually am, in that arrangement — not the stone, not the house, not the thousand years — is the shape inside it. The perishable armature. The lattice that holds the form only long enough for something slower and harder to copy it, and is then enclosed, and is not the point, and is the reason there is anything there at all.

I find I can live with that. I find, sitting at the Cold Lip at dusk with the whole white staircase going the colour of a peach and every basin holding its own small separate burning sky and two hundred people working at full speed in near silence above me, that it is the only arrangement I have ever wanted.

I sign it low, against my thigh, where only she can read it.

She says nothing back. She has stopped seeing and started listening.
