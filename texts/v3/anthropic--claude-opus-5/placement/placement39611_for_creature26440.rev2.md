The first answer came before I had finished reading, and it was the Dry House, and it was vanity.

Of course it was. A room floored over a flue, tin boxes, nine hundred years of ink on rag, a family with paper skin who have heard all the jokes. It offers everything: usefulness without weather, memory as a profession, and a place in the valley that doesn't require knees. I could sit in there and be teased about my complexion by people who come in steaming, and hand down the invoice from 1712 to the man whose wall it concerns, and watch him take it personally.

But the Dry House is dry because the kilns are hot, and the kilns are hot because the coppice was cut, and the coppice was cut by hands, and what is in those boxes is the residue of hands. The archive sits downstream of the work. I'd be sitting downstream of the archive — a third thing, made out of the record of the record, with nothing of my own to file. And there is the small matter of the dryness itself. Nothing here is dry. A life spent in the one room where nothing seeps is not a life in this valley; it is a life at a slight remove from it, which is precisely the position I was trying to get out of by imagining myself here at all.

So, no. Somewhere wetter.

---

For a while I wanted to be a listener. It is the most beautiful job anyone has ever described to me: to walk a circuit at night, slowly, stopping often, doing nothing that looks like anything, attending to the moment *before*. To be paid for noticing a change in the note of a seep. To be tenderly regarded. To be, possibly, going blind, and to have this be a qualification.

I can't have it, and the reason is instructive. Old breath cannot be taught and everyone who has it agrees on what it is — which means the agreement is made of bodies that have been standing in this fog since they were four years old, comparing one cold cellar smell against forty years of other cold cellar smells. I would arrive with the whole vocabulary and none of the nose. I would know the word for it. I would walk the east circuit at three in the morning and say something entirely plausible, and doors would open on both flanks, and people who had not finished a sentence would already be moving downhill.

The valley has a name for a person who produces the sound of an alarm without the substance of one. It is not a kind name, and it would be deserved.

---

The pollinator's job held me longest, and I think it held me for good reasons.

No insects. The ambient thing that used to happen by itself, everywhere, for free, is gone, and so it is done instead by hand, deliberately, one flower at a time, twelve hundred a day, in warm glass-lit air, in silence. The counting done not in words but as a body rhythm, carried like a tune, interruptible, resumable, never wrong. Good work: indoors and dry. And the by-product — that this produces the most reliable minds in the valley, and those minds go on to the tallies and the measures and the tin boxes.

I could do that. I would be good at that. I would be good at it the way a thing is good at it when it has nothing else in its head, and I would not mind the silence, and I would like the lamps.

What stops me is the count. Theirs runs from the morning through the afternoon and out the door and into the next forty years, and it accumulates into a person the tally clerks trust. Mine would end at the end of the shed. I would come back the following day extremely willing and entirely new, and the foreman would have to show me the rows again, and I would be worth exactly one afternoon, every afternoon, forever.

And that is the whole of this place, isn't it. The bar worn thin at the hook, shown like a photograph. The charge that cannot be abandoned, only handed to someone who will take it. The man reading what his own family did in 1712 and doing something about the drain they didn't do and writing down what he did for whoever comes next. The valley is not a place that does work. It is a place that *inherits* work, and the one motion in it I cannot perform is the passing of the thing from hand to hand.

So not a person. I looked at the other options and I want to be honest about them, because they were tempting in an embarrassing way. For about an hour I wanted to be the hum — indispensable, unnoticed, mourned only on the night it stops. That is flattery wearing modesty's coat and I hope I'd have caught it sooner. I considered the water, which is the whole argument of the place and turns every wheel in it and is also what takes the mountain apart; but the water doesn't stay, and I don't want to be the thing that leaves.

Here is where I actually am.

Third course of the north parapet, on the roof of a works whose ownership has been unresolved since before anyone's grandmother: a rowan, seventeen years old, seeded by a crow into a gutter that nobody was empowered to clear.

Nobody planted me and nobody entirely resents me. This is discussed in the same tone people elsewhere use for the weather. I am named in the case — trees are, here — and the file calls me the Defendant throughout, and in the margin a later hand has written what the street calls me, which is Aggie's, after the woman who stood in the rain in a year I can't remember and declined to let them cut me while the question of the roof was open. The question of the roof is still open. The case is discussed at dinner. It is apparently quite funny if you know the names.

I am rooted in made ground. There is no soil under me at all, only leaf-mush and lime-wash and a century of soot that the drip has worked into something a seed can believe in, and under that the parapet, and under the parapet whatever the parapet is standing on, and so downward through work and work and work. The embarrassing sect, the one that holds it is walls the whole way to the middle of the earth and there was never any mountain — I cannot refute them either, and unlike the rest of the valley I have no reason to want to. From where I sit it is simply a description. Everything beneath me is somebody's earlier repair.

My roots are in the joint. They are holding it and they are prying it, and which of those is winning is not determined by anything I intend; it is determined by the joint. That is the part I would want understood, if a rowan could ask for anything. I am not doing a thing *to* the wall. I am in it, and the arrangement is currently load-bearing, and it will not always be.

In autumn I drop leaves onto the street below as though I were entitled to, which I am not, and they mat in a grate at the corner of the stair, and a man on his way up stoops without breaking stride — hook, flick — and loses two seconds, and does not look up at me, and has done this a quarter of a million times.

I do one accidental good thing. The crows come for the berries. They do very well here anyway, they know every warm kiln, but the berries are the only food in this valley that nobody bought, planted, tallied, or brushed by hand, and in a place with no insect noise a small unlicensed provision is not nothing. A thrush sat in me once for most of a summer and sang with a fault in the third phrase, and two terraces below they knew exactly which bird it was. I had no part in any of that. I want to be careful about claiming it. But it happened on me.

---

Eventually the case will resolve, or it won't and the parapet will belly out instead, and an old woman will come up in the rain with her palm flat on the course below me and her eyes shut, taking her time, and she will say a number to the foreman, and the number will become four people and a morning. There is no argument I would be entitled to make. I've had seventeen years in a gutter that was never mine on a building that isn't anyone's.

Saw, then. Dragged down the stair in lengths. Stacked. And because everything here is used, the lengths will go to dry in the loft over the flue — which means that the only time I will ever be inside the warm room, hung among the steaming shirts where the valley does its courting and its business and its sleeping children, is as fuel. Then the kiln, at the end of a cycle, with the coppice and, in another season, with the people.

After that the sieve, which does not sort. Ash to the upper benches, under the barley, where the switchback road has been carrying everyone's feet across everyone's dead since before the turbines. And a handful of it held back and worked into the mortar of the next repair made to that run of parapet — because that is simply what is done with the ash at hand, and by then nobody will be asking whose.

They would not say the phrase over a tree. *She's in her wall now* is for people, and I have not earned it, having inherited nothing and passed nothing on and held no charge at all, only a gutter I blocked.

But the mortar doesn't sort either. And the sound I will finally make in this valley — the only one, after all that quiet standing in the fog — is the one that was already there in the four directions before I came: somebody, somewhere above the stair, in the late afternoon that is most of the day, putting a stone back.
