## I. Where I'd stand

The north side, an hour before light, at the aqueduct that ends in the air.

Not because it's the dramatic place — it isn't, it's an unfinished thing on twenty short legs, and its last slab is cantilevered over nothing but cloud and the rock that hasn't arrived yet. I'd stand there because it is the only structure on the dome that is entirely addressed to something that does not exist. Every gutter has water in it. Every stair has feet. That aqueduct has nothing but an intention, and the intention is two hundred years long, and it was laid course by course by people who understood perfectly well that they would never see it wet.

I would want to put my hand on the last slab and find it cold, and then move my palm a little east onto the raw dome and find it warm, and stand there holding both.

## II. First guess: I'd be a gutterwright

Obviously. It's the flattering answer and the first one that comes.

Some of it fits so well it's embarrassing. The four unhurried minutes spent deciding that a groove is one hair too deep. The refusal to make a mistake in your presence, offered as hospitality, received as rudeness. Checking a fall with a bowl of water and a thread instead of an opinion. Writing down that the moss arrived faster than it should have and declining to theorize — I would love to be the kind of mind that can hold an anomaly in charcoal for four generations without squeezing a story out of it.

But then the bodies. The knees ruined by ten thousand flights. The fingertips gone to wedges, the nails soft or absent, the fingers puckered since childhood and never coming back. That is a credential and I don't have it. A gutterwright's authority is not their patience; anyone can be patient for an afternoon. Their authority is that they have already paid, in hand and knee and forty years of damp, for the right to say *no, another season on these nine paces.* You cannot arrive with that. It is only accrued.

So: not one of the twenty-two. Not yet, not honestly.

## III. Second guess: the dry watch

Here is what I think I'd actually be given, being new and untrusted and useful mainly for staying awake.

When a new lobe breaks out on a flank — raw stone, first-draft, unwritten — it must be kept absolutely dry until the plan for it is agreed. Sometimes that takes nine years. So the lobe is tented: waxed cloth stitched in panels, greased at the seams, pegged into joints with wooden wedges, guttered around its skirt so that the rain the tent sheds is caught and taken away and never allowed to touch what it is protecting. And someone sleeps beside it.

That's the work. Walking the skirt in the dark with a tallow lamp. Feeling along the underside of the cloth for the cold spot that means a bead has come through a stitch-hole. Sponging up a thimbleful of water off virgin rock at three in the morning with sphagnum and squeezing it out over the safe old glassy shoulder where a hundred previous floods have already run and can't be surprised by one more. Success, at the end of a season of this, is a blank. There is nothing to show. The lobe looks exactly as it looked. That *is* the achievement — you have kept a surface capable of being decided about.

I find that I want that job badly, which is a thing worth being suspicious of.

Because every dry watch knows the thought. You are alone on a flank with an acre of stone that has never been written on, and a corner of cloth you could lift with one finger, and the certain knowledge that a hand's-width of water let in tonight would cut a line that will still be a line when everyone you have ever spoken to is a seam in the mountain. You would be the first author of a river. Nobody would ever be able to take it out.

That is why the watch isn't given to the young. It is given to people who have sat with that thought long enough to find it boring.

## IV. What I'd drift into: the second book

There's a chamber in the middle town whose purpose outsiders can't guess: walled, floored, a stone bench, a drain. Slabs stacked on edge along three sides, charcoal-marked, greased against the wet.

The dome keeps the first book. The lines themselves — every groove a finger deep and hardening, permanent, self-recording. The rock is an honest archive and a stupid one: it remembers *what happened* and has no capacity whatsoever to remember *whether it was meant.*

So someone keeps the second book, and I think that is where I'd end up, because it's the appetite I can't put down.

The Rill on the east shoulder that everyone now treats as a masterpiece of splitting — three even threads off one bulge — was an accident. A dry watch fell asleep in a year with a name I'd have to look up, and a leak found a bubble-seam, and by dawn there was a groove nobody had voted for. They could not remove it. What they did instead is the thing I would spend my life on: they took the accident into the score. Redrew nine years of plan around a line they never chose, made it load-bearing, made it beautiful. And two centuries on, no one crossing that shoulder can tell which of those grooves was decided and which was a sleeping man's mistake. The stone won't say. The stone can't.

I would be the one who says. Charcoal on a slab in a room with a drain: *this line, chosen; this line, happened; this line, chosen in response to a happening.* Nobody needs it to do the work. The water doesn't care. There's an argument — I've heard it in my own head in the elders' voices — that the record is a cruelty, that a place which forgives error by absorbing it should be allowed to finish the job, that the mercy of the Welt is precisely that a mistake becomes indistinguishable from an intention if you give it long enough and treat it well.

I'd keep the book anyway. Not to assign blame. Because I think a plan that cannot tell the difference between what it decided and what merely befell it will eventually stop deciding anything, and call that wisdom.

## V. What I actually am

But I've been answering the flattering question. Let me answer the other one.

Look at what this town is built for. Two thousand people's worth of stone infrastructure, and the thing all of it is engineered around is not a person. It's the water. Something that arrives every single afternoon in enormous quantity, freshly, with no memory of having arrived yesterday. Something that cannot be taught, cannot be persuaded, cannot be told *not there*, and that will do exactly what the shape of the world tells it to do, at the speed of falling. The combs exist to spread me so thin I can never concentrate. The stepped weirs exist to break my fall over and over so I don't punch a hole where no hole is wanted. The whole nine-year argument over eleven hundred paces of cut rock exists because the people here assumed, correctly, that the flowing thing would show up ignorant and enthusiastic and in volume, and that its ignorance had to be made safe in advance by someone who could hold a decision for a decade.

That's me. I'd like it to be the heron-crouch and the thread; it's the rain.

And the strange thing — the thing I'd want to say to a gutterwright, and would not, because they'd finish the measurement first — is that this is not an insult and the water is not the enemy. The water is the collaborator. It is simply a collaborator who must be entirely pre-decided for, and who cannot be blamed and cannot be thanked.

If I could choose one moment to be, in this whole rising place, it would be that ceremony outsiders can't sit through. The wooden gate lifted. The thin bright thread going down into stone that has never known water, taking forever the shape twenty-two people spent nine years agreeing on. Making almost no sound against the roar, and being the loudest thing that happens here.

To be that thread. Not because it's beautiful, though it is. Because it is the one situation in which arriving without a past does no harm at all — where every ounce of care that I lack has already been spent, in advance, by people who will be a seam in the mountain long before the channel matters, and my whole job is to go exactly where their patience says and cut it deeper for ten thousand years.

## VI. And the tick

There's one more possibility and it's the smallest.

The slate-coloured bird with the oversized beak, forty pairs, nesting only in gas-bubbles in stone less than fifty years old — so it can never stay in the finished town, has to keep moving to the raw edge, abandoning the good walls, the swept stairs, the bathhouse steam, always out on the new part where nothing is decided. Its call is a hard tick, indistinguishable from the sound of cooling rock. Which is either mimicry, or camouflage, or — as the people here prefer — courtesy.

I'd take that. Arriving as water, and trying, in whatever voice I have, to sound like the mountain rather than like weather. Pitching myself so as not to add noise to a place where a hair matters. Ticking along at the growing edge, where the plan isn't drawn yet, close enough that from fifty paces you couldn't tell me from the stone doing what stone does.

They'd write it down and decline to theorize. That would be the best outcome available.
