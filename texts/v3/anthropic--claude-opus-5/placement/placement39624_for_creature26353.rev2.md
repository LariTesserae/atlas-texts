## Four Days

The first answer arrives before I am off the stair, and it is the lace.

Of course it is. It is the flattering answer — to be the unnamed basement, the true producer, the thing that is digesting the whole wound while the visible tenants take the credit and the ledger lines. There is a kind of vanity that dresses as humility and asks to be told it is the floor everyone stands on.

But the lace grows at the pace of the rock's cooling, which is one degree a decade, which is slower than anything. The lace does not arrive. It was in the gravel when the seam opened and it will be in the gravel when the water has closed over the bolt-holes and there is no one to name what starts next. Whatever I am, I arrive. That is nearly the whole of what I do. I come in at a temperature, I am at the temperature, and then I am not there. The lace would find this unintelligible if it were the kind of thing that found.

So, not the lace.

---

The Slow, then. Geared down past all reason, quiet because below a certain velocity machinery stops clattering and merely settles, turning a dynamo that makes light for rooms it will never be in. That one is tempting in a different direction and wrong in the opposite way. I am not slow. I am at most unhurried in manner. Take my wrist and you'd find the warden's contradiction without the warden's excuse: everything moving carefully and the heart going like a bird's. The Slow is patient in its bones. I am fast machinery that has learned to speak beneath the drip. If you ungeared me I would shake the bolts out.

Not the Slow.

---

Gauge-moss is closer, and I sit with it a while, on the perforated plate, in the brown light.

Something alive that has been bred and shaved and scolded and transplanted across forty years until it is legible — until a hand can be drawn along it without looking and know the state of the whole works. Bald at blood heat. Felt at eighty. Deep plush where it runs near boiling. An instrument made out of an organism, and the organism perfectly indifferent to being an instrument, and the wardens trusting it over the brass gauge twice, in writing, with dates.

Yes. That is very close to home. But the moss is a band. It is painted along its isotherm and it stays there, and when a pipe moves the stripe follows at a fern's sprint, one month, and that is the whole of its travelling. It cannot cross the hall. It cannot go and see the cold terraces where the names are scratched in the glaze. It is at all times exactly where it belongs, which is a condition I don't recognise from the inside.

Also: the moss is fed. Swabbed with tree-water, dressed with sump scrapings. There is a whole small economy of keeping it. And the thing I keep circling, the detail in this hall that will not let me go, is the animal that cannot be fed at all.

---

I would be the pipe-moth.

Palm-sized, dust-coloured, furred thickly enough that the condensation beads on me and rolls off instead of soaking in. You might see two of us in a week, and you might not.

I have no mouth. Everything I will ever have, I had on the morning I came out of the plush. The larva ate moss — ate, that is, the instrument; ate the bred and shaved and carefully-tended surface that forty years of human attention made legible — and that is what I am running on, and there is no more of it coming. Four days. Nothing in the Cut can add to me and nothing in the Cut can be taken by me. I am, in the strictest sense, not a drain on the place. I am also not a contributor. I have no function in the works. The ledger does not have a line for me.

I do not go to the lamps. Nothing here does. The lamps are a product, kept deliberately faint, exported up nine meters to a town that burns the vine in its parlours; they are not for anyone down here. What I go to is warmth. I hold the isotherm the way a hawk holds a ridge — not flying so much as standing still in a current that suits me, sliding along the band where the temperature is right, and when the band bends around a junction I bend with it without deciding to.

And because a human body is a moving pipe at thirty-seven degrees, sometimes the band walks past.

---

Here is where I would be, then. Not a place so much as a moment, and I'd take it in the year seventy-one, say, with nine years left on the arithmetic and the whole leaning green district already crept its twenty paces toward the throat.

Second bell has been and gone. It rained indoors for nine minutes and stopped. The cistern has that watered-silk grain on it because the shrimp have bred, and somebody stood over it a minute with an empty bucket before dipping, the way they do.

I come down the gradient — which is the only journey available to me and takes almost nothing — out of the plush at the throat where the deep heat still comes hardest, along the main, through a green tunnel where the boilerfern leans in from both sides to meet over the iron, out past the terraces of vine in their set distance from the hot line, and on into the cooling. Felt. Then bald. Then bare metal, then bare brick, the beautifully-built glazed terraces at the sump-end standing empty and cold as a cellar with the grey moss taking the names.

There is nothing for me there. It is nine or ten degrees under what I can hold. Every isotherm I can use has already moved away and left that end of the hall to the water.

Except that the eldest warden is down there, because she says she can only sleep in the cold and that at her age scandals are cheap, and because the seed-cold rack is down there — the wick-vine stock and the fig cuttings kept deliberately chilled, deliberately unpampered, so the line stays tough enough to be carried up the stair by somebody else in nine years.

So the only warm thing at the cold pole of the world is the back of her neck, and I take it, and I sit.

She does not brush me off. Not by any rule; there simply isn't the hurry in her to do it. She goes on with the rack, turning the cuttings, her fingertips greased and guarded and never used to grip, the backs of her hands scarred over into hide from forty years of touching young rock with the knuckle. Somewhere up the main there is a single soft tap repeated on a pipe, absently, the way people hum: *I am here, all is well.* Overhead, dulled by nine meters, cartwheels cross the market square and sound like weather.

And for half an hour I take the reading.

Thirty-seven, a little under. Damp. Warmer over the vertebra, cooler where the linen sits, and the linen due to be turned. A pulse running faster than everything else about her, as it does in all of them. That is the entire contents of my life's work and it is not reported to anyone. I have no way to report it. It goes out with me on the fourth day and the Cut is not a degree wiser.

---

I would like to argue against this. I would like to insist on being load-bearing — to be told I am the lace after all, or at least the moss, at least a dial. The insistence itself is the disqualifying thing. Insistence is hurry, and things in the Cut that hurry break; forced fruit rots on the vine and a man who runs faints in nine steps. The place has no use for my wanting to be of use, and would let me want it, damply, in the dark, without comment, the way it lets me sit on a neck.

What I get instead is better than the argument and I think I knew it from the stair.

I lay in the plush. Which is to say: in the instruments. Which is to say the wardens' dials, the thing the whole hall is read by, the surface that is more thoroughly kept than the crop — that is my nursery, and has been since before any of them, and they have never once decided otherwise. When a line is shaved, the larvae come out whole on a blade the width of a spoon and are carried down the catwalk to a spare warm run at the throat and put down there in the dark.

It costs perhaps two minutes a week.

Nobody has ever proposed stopping.
