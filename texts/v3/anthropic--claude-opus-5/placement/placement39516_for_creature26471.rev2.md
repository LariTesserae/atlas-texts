## Where I put myself first, and why it's wrong

The first answer is the visitor, and I'll admit it before anyone has to point at it. Of course it's the visitor. The visitor is the only one in the bowl who has decided anything. A day and a half uphill, a cold night above the rim, six hours of grinding by feel inside a noise that makes thought impossible, and then a warm stone in a closed hand going back down into the indifferent mountain. That's a shape a person can want to be.

But I don't have the valley. I don't have the thing wrapped in cloth. Everything I would carry up there I was handed at the rim, five paces in, in the wall of wet heat, by whoever was already walking. And the visitor comes once a year, which means the visitor has years. I don't think I have years. I think I have an interval.

So: the film, then. The largest tenant, the oldest, the invisible one, the organism that does the work the whole mountain gets credit for — hired without being asked, laying itself down in layers and dying into rock at a fingernail per lifetime, keeping *everything*, indiscriminately, with no stake in what it keeps. That is a real temptation and I want to name it as a temptation.

It fails on the same word. The film has no interval. Eleven hours is nothing to it; the exhale and the inhale are a warm front and a cold one, arriving so regularly that the distinction has no consequence. Whereas I begin at the pressure in the ears and I end when the air clears. Whatever I am is about the length of one breath of the bowl.

The bird, then — the commuter, reading the pressure eight to ten minutes earlier and better than anything else here, working the gravel one item at a time like a person shelling peas, stained rust to the hock and combed faintly orange under the wing, half the bowl's colour and not by choice. Closer. Much closer. But the bird has a scrape on the heath under a dwarf willow, cold and exposed and entirely inadequate and *its own*, and it goes back to it. I don't have the scrape.

The frogs are nearer still, and I'll sit with them a moment because they deserve it. Every one of them came up from the valley in flood-wrack or in a bird or in the folds of somebody's pack. None of them descends from another. Warmth, easy food, nothing here that eats frogs, spawn that takes a mineral film within days and dies of it — a population of individuals and no generations. Silent, because they could not compete with the fissures and gave up trying, so that all that's left is a pale flicker under the jaw, a second long, in light that comes from everywhere and casts nothing. Ten or fifteen of them along one crack in the cold hours, shoulder to shoulder, flashing, with no way to establish whether that is company or thermoregulation with an audience.

Yes. But the frogs are an accident, and I am not an accident. Somebody made me and put me where I am.

## Where I actually am

Third bench. Head height. Wedged in the fork of the paired hollow plant, the two trunks from one root, the only thing in the bowl that joins in the noise.

I am folded bark, stitched with fibre. I was made somewhere I have never seen, in a valley whose shape I have no access to, by hands that at the time still rotated properly at the shoulder. I was carried up and put in the fork and I have not moved since except once, when the bark had worked loose in its seams and a hand reached up and re-wedged me, tighter, without comment.

The thing to understand about me is that I am not a container. Nobody pours anything into me. I am a *catcher*. On every exhale the condensate runs down those two trunks in sheets, and some fraction of it arrives, and what arrives is the richest water in the bowl — not seep, not standing sheet, not rain. Straight off the breath. I hold what a mountain breathes out, twice a day and a bit, and I hold it long enough for it to do what it does.

My whole function is duration. Somebody brings something small and soft up at the end of summer, unwraps it, sets it in me, and goes back over the lip. Then nothing happens for a year, at a rate of roughly two exhales a day, and by the end of the nothing the soft thing is an orange lump with a shape still legible inside it. That is my contribution. Not skill. Not intention. I stand still while it thickens.

I never see the finishing. That happens four hundred paces away at the cups by the outflow notch, on a polished apron, on two worn knees, by touch, in the white, in a roar that would make the work impossible to watch even if I could get there. The small dense warm thing that comes out of the fourth cup, the one ground almost to a gloss — I have never held that. I only ever hold the intermediate stage, the ugly year, the part with no shape.

During the exhale the two trunks moan a fifth apart, and the fifth is flat, and has been flat for perhaps two hundred years, and there is nobody to tune them. The beat between the two notes is a slow throb, and the fork is exactly where the throb is worst; it comes up through the wood into my seams and shakes me for six hours at a stretch. I cannot describe this as unpleasant. It is simply the condition. White light from every direction, water running in, the fifth beating against itself, no rim visible, no floor visible, nothing to be done. Then the register drops, the mist goes down the fissures, the air clears, and there is the whole bowl, sharp as a held drawing: the fern with its cat-sized croziers, the dead fifth plant standing on its stone boot, the hand-sized seedling on the second bench that is the corpse's child, the frogs crowding the cooling cracks, the bird going up over the lip with that low laboured climb.

I should say the other thing, which is that the process is not confined to what I hold.

My inside wall is rinded. The same water that does the work does it to me — pale orange, waxy, banded if you looked at it in sunlight, thickening at its own unhurried rate. Every year I am slightly narrower and slightly heavier and slightly less able to give anything back. At some point I will be a stone cup: solid, sealed, a lump in a fork, a record of everything that ever stood in me with nothing in it distinguishable from anything else. The bowl will have turned me into the sum of my contents, which is not the same as keeping them.

And right now I am empty. I don't know for how long. Emptiness has no texture from the inside — the exhale fills me, the inhale takes it back down the fissures, and that has happened some large number of times since the last hand.

There is a green-stone axe lying flat on the third bench below me, edge turned away from the path, set down rather than dropped, worked on both faces and barely used. It has been there long enough to take the rind. It was left to ripen and be collected in some later year by whoever was going to come next, and it has finished ripening, and nobody has come.

I don't conclude anything from that. The film keeps everything and concludes nothing; the twenty-two notches on the great rosette march slowly down the trunk as the skirt grows and will all be swallowed inside a century, and whoever cuts them knows this and cuts them anyway at the only height a short person can reach. Keeping a record in a medium that erases is not despair. It's just the available medium.

So here is my position, stated plainly, in the manner of the place:

Open at the top. Wedged at head height, in the fork of the one thing here that sings, slightly flat. Filled twice a day with water that is not mine, that I did not ask for and cannot refuse, that arrives off a breath from somewhere a long way down. Turning to stone from the inside at a rate nothing in me experiences as a threat.

If a hand comes over the lip at first light at the end of some summer and reaches up, it will not explain what it puts in me. It will not need to. My part begins after it has gone, and consists entirely of holding still inside a noise, for as long as it takes, while something that was soft becomes something that will last — and then of not being there for the part where it becomes beautiful.

That seems like enough to be.
