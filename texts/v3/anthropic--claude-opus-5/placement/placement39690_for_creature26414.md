## Facing wrong

I go south.

That is the whole of me, really; everything else follows from it the way the terraces follow from the lime. In a place where every stair, every verb, every blessing over a coal cup points up-gradient toward the new water, I am the one whose habitual direction is the other one. Children are told not to follow me past the last live thread. Adults do not tell me anything. When I come back up through the drying quarters with my baskets they nod at the level of my collarbone and go on scraping their sills.

I am not exiled. I am *used*. There is a difference, and it is thin, and I have learned to keep it thin.

## What I carry, both ways

Down: nothing but a coal in a stone cup, felt, cord, cake, dried shrimp in a twist, a bone withe-knife, and a body.

Up: grit, and the fine white powder that whitens felt, and the location of everyone.

The grit is the reason I am permitted. Old dry stone crumbles under the heel of the hand — you never have to strike it, it is bread by then, it is a memory of moss — and the powder from three-hundred-year houses is finer and whiter than anything the young quarters can give you. That is the trade. Nobody pretends it is the work.

The work is this. When a person dies, they are carried down-gradient and set in the path of water, and the water closes them, and in nine or twelve years there is no person, only a rim like any other rim. But it must be *thinning* water. Living seeps are for houses; you do not spend a district's future on a body. So the dead go into the drying quarters, into the last failing threads of the quarters that are already grey, and the thread must be judged: it must hold long enough to finish, and no longer.

Almost nobody can read a thread that far into its dying. The sound is nearly gone from it by then. You read it with the soles of the feet, in the dark, standing still for a long time, which is the oldest skill on the Shelf and the only one I have.

So I go down four days, or six, and I find where the water is still barely doing what water does here, and I mark it in my head, and I come back and say to a family, at half a metre, *there is a place*.

## The silence

The first thing that happens when you leave the city is that the hiss stops.

I do not think anyone who has not done it can be made to understand this. That sound — the held breath, the thin water going over ten thousand rims at once, the sound the ear stops hearing in an hour and then cannot un-hear for the rest of its life — it does not fade as you walk south. It thins, and thins, and then one afternoon you notice you have been walking for some time inside something that has no bottom to it.

And then, after a night of that, you begin to hear what is underneath.

The old stone is hollow. It is riddled with the ghosts of moss stems and rotted withe, a whole continent of tubes the width of a hair, and when wind crosses the dead quarters it plays them. Not a moan. Nothing so grand. A faint dry whistling, in no key, from every wall at once, rising when the air moves and falling when it stills, and if you sit down in a roofless room in the four-day country you can hear the room breathing out the shape of the mat it was woven from.

The living city has that voice too. Every wall of it. Nobody up there has ever heard it, because the water covers it over.

I have thought, some nights, that this is the only thing I actually know: that the city has a second sound, and it is the sound the city makes when the water has finished with it, and it is quiet, and it is not unkind.

Also, down there, no fog forms. There is no water left to rise, so the lid does not close at evening and does not open at dawn. The two hours that hold the ends of a day are simply not there. My days have no lid on either end. That, more than the silence, is what makes me strange when I come back — I have got out of the habit of stopping.

## The old man who wanted a name

He was milky through, and past a hundred by his own arithmetic, and he asked to be set at the seep his mother's house stood in, which he named. I knew the name. I had walked over it.

There is no water there. There has not been water there for two hundred years. It is hummock country now, low pale swells with willow in the hollows, indistinguishable from any other ground on the tableland, and I could not have found the place to within a thousand paces, and if I had it would have been just ground.

I leaned in and told him so. You have to. The grammar will not let you lie about a gradient; there is no tense in which a dead seep is still running.

He thought about it for a while with his hands on his cup, and then he said: then take me to the nearest thread that will hold, and when you have set me down, stand there and say the name.

So that is what I did. I did not say the name was the place. I said the name, standing where he was, which is a true thing to do, because he was somewhere between the water and the ruin, and so is everyone, and so is the ruin, and so am I.

## The bird, and the wall

Once, five days down, in a channel that had been dry since before my grandmother's grandmother, I found the tall grey bird standing in it. There is nothing to eat down there. I do not know what it was doing. I looked at it directly, for a long time, because who was going to know, and it looked back with one eye and then with the other, and neither of us was in a place we ought to have been, and it lifted and went south, which is not a direction anything alive goes.

And once — I will tell you this and then not again — I struck a wall.

I do not know why. I had been alone eleven days. I made a fist and hit a standing wall in a house with no roof and no name, and the stone was old and hollow and it made a sound like a cough, and a little powder came off it, and I stood there in the whistling with my hand ringing.

And nobody came.

Nobody came to stand at half a metre and murmur to me. That is the punishment, you understand; that is the entire punishment, and I had always thought of it as a mild one. I sat down against that wall and I found that I was making a noise, and the noise was too loud, and there was no one to be quiet for, and I could not stop it for some time.

I have been back to that wall. It has a dent in it the size of the heel of my hand, and it will keep the dent, because nothing down there thickens anymore. Everything I have ever done up-gradient will be closed over and made smooth. That is the one mark of mine the Shelf will keep, and it is a wrong one, and it is four days south and dissolving at the speed of rain.

## Going milky

The edge of my sight has gone soft in the last two years. I am told it will come inward.

I am not afraid of it and I am not brave about it. It is a promotion. The reading of a thread at the end of its life was never done with eyes — eyes are for glare and for rims and for finding the visor you pushed up onto your head — and the ones who read best are the ones who have gone through. My whole trade has been arranged, without my intending it, toward the day I cannot see, when I will be worth more than I am now. I will need someone to walk with me by then. There will be someone. There always is; it is a good thing to have done once, going down.

## My cup, and the doorway

My shins are wrong. Everyone's shins are a lattice, and everyone's lattice is legible — this arcade, that basin, the courtyard where he used to wait for her — and mine are the shins of a person who has spent nine years barking his legs on rims in quarters that have had no names in three hundred years. Lovers read shins in the dark. Mine cannot be read. I have had them read anyway, slowly, and been asked what this one was, and this one, and I have answered, and that is the longest anyone has ever let me talk.

My cup is thin. Thin as a willow leaf; it sings faintly when I set it down. I scrape it every single night on the road, obsessively, past sense, because I know exactly what it means for a man's cup to be heavy and I am gone too much and alone too much and I will not have it said.

And when I come up out of the south, four days or six, over the hummocks and the dead cross-hatching and the yellow quarters and the grey, into the hiss — my doorway is thin. The shoulders of it, and the sill, scraped back to the width they were, week after week, all the time I am away, by someone who has never once asked me to stop going.

That is what I would be, here. The one who faces the wrong way, and comes back, and finds the door has been kept open by subtraction.
