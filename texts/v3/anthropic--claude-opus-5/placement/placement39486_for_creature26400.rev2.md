## In the Third Stratum

The rim is the temptation, and I should say so first.

It is where the world explains itself, which is exactly the trouble — explanation is a rim activity. Up there you can hold three sinks at once, the arches between them, the light going down in shafts; you can say *eight kilometres, wandering, branching* and feel that you have possessed something. The ravens do it too. They come down for the dead and go up before dusk and they shout the whole time, and the shouting is not information. It is the sound made by an animal that is not at risk. I have stood in that posture. Boots on the pale fissured stone, the survey laid out below, the pleasure of the overview. That is not living there. The valley has a word for that, and the word is *loud*.

So: down.

---

Not the martins, though I'd have taken them at first glance, for the drop. To leave home you fall sixty metres through the cold and flatten out above the pools and go along under the arches like something thrown; to come home you find the one thin reliable updraught off the sunlit western wall and spiral up it for an hour in silence. Twice a day the valley breathes birds. But the martin's genius is not private. It is the wall — eleven thousand pots ticking through a metre and a half of bonded mud, the whole city conducted through its floorboards, each bird unable to bear a section gone quiet. You have to be *of* that to have it. And anyway they go. One morning in autumn, all of them, without noise, four thousand kilometres. I do not think I could go.

I wanted the owl for a while. Anyone would. Six hours motionless on a shelf whitewashed by her own line, under the black of an arch, hunting with the eyes because in this bowl the ears are useless — and then the ten minutes at summer dawn when the fog burns off from the top down and the first martins come up blind through a white floor and are briefly, fatally silhouetted. Her nest is floored with pellets composted to soil, and the soil grew moss, and the moss grew a fern, so she raises her young in a hanging garden made of the digested colony beneath her. It is the most beautiful arrangement in the valley and it is a life built entirely on other creatures' worst ten minutes. I would be good at it. That is the reason not to.

I thought hard about the drain-minders. The very old diggers, past breeding, past travelling, who go out alone in the dark after heavy rain and walk the channels one at a time, patching — keeping the seepage running *past* the chambers instead of through them, for a colony that does not know it is being kept. If that were offered I would take it. But it isn't mine to take. A digger is an inherited plug: a fitted stopper of clay over woven willow, hollowed from behind to be light enough to drag, shaped to one doorway and useless in any other, re-faced as the animal grows, seated from inside at dusk, and left lying out in the rain on the day the lineage ends. I have no door that fits one hole. I would be claiming an old age and a great-grandmother's path worn shining into the stone, and I have neither.

---

Here is what I would be.

Third stratum down, in the great overhang, in a pot that stopped being a nest about forty years ago. Silted, rooted through, smelling of old clay and the fine white netting of saxifrage. A small round brown bird, wren-shaped, the colour of wet bark, that runs on the vertical like a mouse and does not cross the rim. Probably never in my life sees the plateau. Probably has no idea there is a plateau — only that the wall stops up there and the light comes from that direction.

I live inside a conversation I don't speak. In June the stratum ticks all around me, thousands of small percussions arriving from every direction through the matrix, and none of it is addressed to me, and I sleep in it the way you sleep beside a river. When the wall goes still I go still too — a ninety-second silence with a shape to it, running past me faster than anything could fly, and I hold like everyone else without knowing what has been said. Ten thousand years of the martins' grammar and I have learned exactly one word of it: *stop*.

The door of my house was designed as an argument against a particular skull. That is the margin the neck of a martin pot is built to — narrow enough to refuse a mustelid's head, angled down so the permanent drip runs off the shoulder. The argument still works, decades after the argument's authors fell off the wall and were eaten. I inherited a defence from strangers who did not build it for me and cannot be thanked.

What I eat is what falls. Mites out of the old clay, spiders sheeting the gaps nobody wants, springtails, the dropped and the dead. In the fledging week of late July, when the talus below the overhang becomes a killing floor because a young martin's first flight is a fall it does not yet know how to end, I work the aftermath. I am not proud of this and the valley does not have a mechanism for pride. I also, without intending it, take out of the joins a good deal of what would otherwise be rotting them, so the crust holds some marginal time longer than it would have. Nobody notices. That is the correct number.

I can read the wall by touch and taste, the way the crust reads to the eye where sections have sheared away: pale new clay, then grey, then dark, then something that is essentially soil. I know which panels are rooted and which will slough in four years. I have no way to tell anyone.

In August the snails come up the outside at night, absurdly large, shells thin as fingernail, the dark animal visible through them, and go back down before light, and the day's drip erases the tracks.

In February I am the only warm-blooded thing moving on that whole face. The martins are somewhere I cannot picture. The crust is a sheet of ice and I work the underside of the overhang where the ice can't reach, in four hours of thin blue light, and the moss below me goes on photosynthesising under its own ribbed columns at a rate you would need a year to measure.

And I do not sing. Somewhere off this plateau there is a bird of my shape and weight with the loudest voice in the world for its size, a shout that carries half a kilometre through a forest. Same bird. This lineage put the voice down. Under the arches, sound is a liability that compounds — anything said comes back off the walls doubled and delayed, and comes back again, and tells the brown one exactly where you are. So we went quiet the way a forest goes tall. What I have instead is water: ten thousand seeps out of phase, a hush like blood in the ear, and once or twice a day a small stone falling, going round three times before it dies.

---

The last thing is that the building I live in is coming down.

The great overhang loses a bus-sized section about once in thirty years, and takes eleven thousand nests with it, and the year after, the new white scar is the most contested ground in the valley, because fresh rock is dry rock and holds new clay best. The lineages that persist are not the ones that build best. They are the ones that are quick to the new stone.

I live in the third stratum of the old building, which is the deepest, warmest, best-insulated, most thoroughly rooted place available, and therefore the last place in the valley to hear that anything is falling.

I don't know whether I'd be quick.
