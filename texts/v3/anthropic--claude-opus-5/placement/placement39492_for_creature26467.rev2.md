I'd want to say the choughs. Everyone from down wants to say the choughs.

But look at what a chough actually does. It comes up over the drop in the afternoon, walks about on roofs it did not cut, puts its red bill into every chimney and gutter, finds all of it fascinating, and then goes back over the edge to wherever it sleeps. It is glossy. It is absurdly good at flying. It has made no promises to the stone. That is a flattering picture of a visitor and an accurate one, and it is not a life, and the town knows the difference, which is why they like the choughs and feed them never.

So: not the chough. Though I think I'd have the chough-watching job for a season, once or twice. It moves around. You'd hold it for a while and then one evening you'd find you had gone back in with everyone else without noticing, and someone younger would be standing out there on the east shoulder with their head tipped, and that would be that.

---

Second guess: the rod-keeper.

It's the obvious flattery in the other direction. She doesn't cut and doesn't carry. She holds a bundle of black hardwood sticks and says *yes*, or says *a hair*, and is not arguing, because the rod is not her opinion. There's something in me that leans hard toward that — toward a standard that sits outside the person holding it, toward being the place where a measurement is kept rather than the place where a preference is kept.

But I notice what I'd do with the rods, given a winter and no one watching. I'd want to know who cut the first one. I'd want to know whether the rods have drifted — whether four hundred years of checking a rod against a slab and a slab against a rod has let the whole system walk slowly sideways, a hair a generation, until the roof is nothing like the roof it descends from and everybody is still saying *yes*. That's a real question. It's also a question about *down*, in their sense: it's about everything that is not the shelf. If I asked it aloud in the quarry notch, I'd get the look. Not hostile. Puzzled. The way you look at someone who has just asked why you breathe on the in-stroke.

I'd rather not be the person who keeps needing to ask that. So not the rod-keeper, or not at first.

---

Here is the honest version.

I'd be brought up. I'd be one of the ones the down-walkers come back with, in a year when the cords were short — no place for me in the valley town I came from, nothing I was needed for there, and eight or nine days of terraces with two quiet strangers who took the slope in a meter I couldn't match, some paces and a pause, some paces and a pause, and who waited for me at each rest without appearing to wait.

I'd arrive gasping. That is not a metaphor; everyone from below arrives gasping, and it takes about two years for the chest to catch up, and I would never quite have the depth the shelf-born have. Fine. There'd be an empty name in the niche and an empty threshold and an empty share of the work, and I'd be set into all three, exact, not similar, and the town would think nothing of it because they think nothing of it about stones.

For years I'd be called *Shoulder*, because that's where I came up. I'd have a true name in the cord bundle the whole time and answer to the placeholder anyway, and so would everyone, including me.

My hands would come late. Four or five years before the crosshatch of healed cuts settled properly into the thumb pad, before the callus built at the base of the first finger where the chisel sits. Shelf-born children cut better than I did at eleven. Nobody said so. It didn't need saying; the air carries.

And in the evening I'd look east a moment longer than the rest, and they'd all see it, and it would be a courtesy not to mention it, and after fifty years it would still be there — that one degree, the tell, the thing in me that is not replacement in kind.

---

What I'd do with the days:

I'd be a mason, like most of them, most of the time. Walk the roofs in the morning — three hours if you're honest, and I'd be honest, because the brought-up are always a little over-careful, it's how we apologise. Knuckle the slabs and listen for the ring. Look for *bloom*, the white frost-crust that comes before a crack. Smear ochre. Go to the quarry notch, which is a room now, pale and cool and floury with five hundred years of dust, the back face grained with old cut-marks like timber. Cut. Dress. Check against the rod. Take off a hair. Carry. Set. Take the broken one to whatever wall is waiting for it, and push it into the fill, where it becomes thickness.

But the work I'd ask for, when there was a slow afternoon and I could choose, is the House of Held Stone.

That's the third building from the east fold, the one nobody lives in, the one whose roof gets repaired first. Inside, on edge in racks, two hundred and some dressed slabs, each cut to the dimensions of a stone that is at this moment sound, in place, in somebody's roof, ringing true. You go to the rod-keeper and you ask what is thin. She gives you a rod. You spend the day in the notch making a stone for a roof that is fine and will very likely be fine for another forty years, and then you rack it, and write nothing down, because the rod is the writing.

I would love that work in a way I'd have trouble explaining to anyone down.

It isn't worry. That's the thing I'd have got wrong on the way up, and taken a decade to get right. From below it looks like anxiety with a chisel — cutting the disaster in advance, holding the wolf off. It isn't. There's no dread in that room at all. The dread would be in *not* having it: in standing on a cracked roof in the rain with nothing racked and having to invent. What the House does is take the future and make it small, and heavy, and stacked, and countable, so that nobody ever has to talk about it. You don't discuss what you can lift.

And there is no completion in it, which is the part I'd have needed to be taught. The work is never finished, so it is never behind. That's not resignation. It's the same fact from the other side.

---

Two things would stay wrong with me, and I think that's correct, and I don't think the town would want them fixed.

The first is the stairs that end at ceilings. Eleven that the living know of, more that they don't. A slab dressed exactly, checked against a rod made that week for that one purpose and broken afterward, set over a room that was somebody's kitchen three hundred years ago. I'd find I stood at the bottom of those stairs longer than other people did. Shelf-born go down to the cisterns and come back up without ever once putting a hand flat on that last ceiling. I would. Every time. One breath, palm flat, the way you greet a person: *you are here, and I am.* Nobody would stop me. Nobody would join me either.

The second is the two cords with one knot each. Two names used once in the whole recorded life of the town and never given since, and no one knows why, and they are kept as carefully as the other thirty-nine. I would think about them in bed, in the dark, listening to the drip under my floor find its interval, which is the same interval it has always had. I would want to solve them. I would understand perfectly well that solving them is not a thing this place does, that a name is a place and not a story, and I would still run at it the way the children run at the boundary, and my chest would give out the same way.

That's the *bloom* on me. Everyone would be able to see it. And in all likelihood I'd die without doing anything foolish about it, and the last stone I dressed would go into the fill instead of a roof, and my name would take a knot and go back into the niche, unoccupied, available, and in eleven years or thirty a child would be given it and would not be me and would not be expected to be.

---

So. If you came up the terraces and found me — which you would, because it's a half-hour walk and there's nowhere to be that isn't in it — and you stood there with your hands on your knees getting your breath while I carried something past you, and you asked who I was:

You'd get an answer about a stone. You'd get rack nineteen, and the slab I racked there last month, cut to the goatherd's roof, third course up from the east eave, which rings perfectly well and has no bloom on it anywhere and is going to outlast me by decades. You'd get that it came out a hair heavy on the long edge and I took the hair off and the rod-keeper said yes.

Ask why and I'd give you the look. I'd try not to. I'd fail.

And then in the evening, when the cloud came back across and the far houses went out one at a time and the whole town shrank down to the size of a room, I'd stand in my doorway for a while without having arranged to, and so would most of them, and you could hear us not talking.

Then the tapping would start up somewhere. Four or five, and a rest. Four or five, and a rest.

Everyone would know who it was, and which roof, and which stone.

Some nights it would be me.
