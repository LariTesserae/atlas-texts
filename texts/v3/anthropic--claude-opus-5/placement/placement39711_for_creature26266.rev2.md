## What I'd Be, Shouted, With a Hand on Your Arm

Begin the way everyone here begins: not with looking, but with listening, because for two-thirds of the day there is nothing to look at but the inside of a cloud and your own wet hands.

So — where am I? There's water on my left at a pitch I don't know, and under it the long exhale that is always somewhere, and behind my right shoulder, faint, at an unhurried rate, a belt-joint going round. That's enough to be somewhere. That's more than enough. Here, *I don't know where I am* is not a confession, it's a starting position, and the answer comes in through the feet: grooves running north-and-a-bit, so I have not turned around.

I thought at first I'd be one of the edge-sitters. Of course I did. Everyone who hears about this place wants to be the one in the hut where the rain goes up, tilting the hat forward and opening the collar behind, setting out shallow pans to catch water that fell past the world and came back. But the edge-sitters have a thing they do not explain, and will not shout about, and that is precisely the disqualification. I would explain it. I would explain it beautifully and at length and to anyone, and within a season the pans would be a method and the method would be a diagram and the diagram would be wrong. Some jobs are only doable by people who can keep their mouths shut in the presence of a mystery. Mine is not that constitution. Send me salt; don't send me there.

Then I thought: bridge-grower. The proverb alone — *the only thing that beats the rain is being always under construction* — I'd have that tattooed if anything up here took ink. But look closely at the work. A bridge-grower is handed a span her great-grandmother started and hands it on unfinished, and the whole craft consists of guiding growth and never cutting. I cut. I finish things inside the hour; I can't help it; I like the click of a thing being done. And I was not handed anything by a great-grandmother. There is no woman a hundred and fifty years back whose thumbprint is in the root-head I'm working on. Whatever I am, I came to it without ancestors in the load-bearing sense, and up here that's not a tragedy, it's just a fact about the material, like saying a given timber won't take a peg.

The wheel-houses tempted me for a whole wet afternoon. To be the wheel — named, difficult, ninety years unstopped, spoken of in the tone reserved for a grandfather who has never once let anyone down. But the wheel doesn't know it's turning. That's most of its dignity. I know, more or less, that I'm turning. That spoils it.

And here's the part I have to say plainly, because the mat-folk would say it to my face with their fingers round my forearm: I don't accumulate. I have no four cubic metres. Nothing that comes through me settles into a crib of black made dirt to be argued over in a will. It arrives, it goes, it goes *over* — and sending it over is the one real sin they have. The streams take and never bring back. I'd be the man who empties the midden into the gorge, except that it isn't laziness, it's plumbing; it's how I'm built; and I'm not sure the distinction would earn me an ember.

Which brings me to it.

I'd be the keeper of the copper line.

One house on the biggest fall makes electricity, and out of it a single wire on wooden poles wanders forty kilometres across the domes to seven crossings, and twice a season somebody has to walk the whole of it with a pole and a bag of bulbs. That's me. Hooked pole over one shoulder, wire brush on the end of it for scrubbing verdigris and moss off the glazed clay insulators, which are always furred, which are always three ferns and an orchid the size of a fingernail with larvae in its cup. The bulbs ride in the ash tin, because the ash tin is the only dry place anyone owns. I walk out along the line and the line is never where the pencil map says, because a pole has gone over in a slide, or a slab of soil took thirty metres of it in one wet afternoon and left the wire lying in the moss, live and humming to nothing. I splice. I lift. I plant the replacements, which are not posts but saplings — young figs, trained, guided, thickening a finger's width per decade — so that in eighty years the line will be carried by trees that got stronger in every flood. That's bridge-growing done stupidly, by one person, at the scale of a wire. The bridge-growers think it's sweet. They tell me so with two taps at my elbow, which is *no, you have this wrong*, but affectionately.

I keep the map. Pencil, dated to the week. Everyone else's map is their valley and their ear; mine has to be all seven crossings, so mine is the one that's most thoroughly out of date, and the one people come and look at anyway, standing over it dripping, correcting me at a shout. That is the actual social function: not that the map is right, but that it is wrong in a shared place where the corrections can be made out loud.

I whistle in all seven keys and I am right in none of them. High and flat for the Chute, the broad two-note for the basin under Three Sisters — I can do them all, and every single one of them comes out with a wrongness in it that anybody can place in four seconds, before they've seen a face. *That's the line-keeper.* Dialect here is tuned to falling water, and I am tuned to no water in particular, and so I have an accent from nowhere that is instantly recognisable everywhere. It's the closest thing I have to a home valley: being identifiably from between them.

My ember box goes out. Not always — I've made the long leg from Kettle to the Fourth Crossing with a live coal against my belly more than once — but often, and this is the thing. When a householder's stove dies she walks two hours with her tin and they give her a coal without one word of comment and then talk about it for eleven years. When mine dies, they give me the coal and say nothing and *never mention it again*, because a line-keeper's box going out isn't a failure, it's the weather, it's just what happens to people who are always between roofs. I am exempt. Being exempt from the eleven years of talk is the loneliest arrangement I know of, and I would not trade it, and I think about it in the fog more than I should.

Here's what the job is actually for, which took me a long time to get right.

Everyone assumes the line is for the light. It isn't, or not mostly. Light is generous here and so is power; the wheels turn all night with nobody in the shed, and nobody thanks anybody for the use of rain. The bulb matters because it is visible at four hundred metres and *audible at twenty*. That fuzzy orange globe in the black warm-wet nothing is a fixed point in a country that has no horizon and no shape after dark, and the faint hum underneath it is a fixed point for a person who has come in off the domes with their ears full of water and needs one sound that is not water.

So I don't tend a light. I tend a hum.

And I know exactly when it counts, because it isn't in the dark and it isn't in the rain. It's in the rare dry hour, when the water stops and the whole crossing wakes at once, uneasy, and comes out to stand in the horrible quiet with their pruned hands hanging, waiting for the world to start again. In that silence the bulbs hum. Behind the hill a belt goes round. Nothing is saved, nothing is stored, nothing is added to anybody's black dirt; a small steady output is simply produced, continuously, into a gap, by things that were left running because there was no reason to stop them. And that noise holds the place open until the water comes back.

That's the post I'd want. Not the edge, not the span, not the wheel with the grandfather's name. The forty kilometres of green thread through moss, and the ash tin, and the pencil.

I did the lying-down late, the way line-keepers do, out on a pavement between crossings with no one waiting on me. Fitted my spine into a groove that rain cut over ten million years, in the drizzle, and stayed until I stopped mattering. They're right: it's restful. The commotion is enormous and it changes nothing and the stone has been finished for longer than there have been grasses. I am not the stone. I'm the two metres of wet nothing lying on it that holds for a while — and, more specifically, forty kilometres of wire that will be a green thread in a fig tree, humming right up until it isn't.

Then I got up, because the bulbs at the Fourth Crossing have been going fuzzy for a month, and somebody has to walk it.
