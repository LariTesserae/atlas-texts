I come down in the middle of the afternoon, which is the wrong time — the dry hours, the flat white glare on the upper treads, the bellowing going on overhead like weather. Barefoot from the fourth tier, because that is the rule, and the stone under the wet line is warm, which is the thing nobody can prepare you for.

And of course the first place I want to be is the fifth landing, third bay, back to the middle pillar.

Of course. It is the part of the Throat that talks. Shout four words into it and four words come back, the same four, in an order that is not yours and never varies, and because it came back changed it is held to have been *taken*, and the answer is binding, and afterwards nobody is at fault. You can see why I would want that job. To be the place where a thing is said that cannot be said flat. To stand in the dark behind three recesses and rearrange what arrives and hand it back with authority I did not have to earn.

But I stand in the bay a while and I don't think it's true. The bay does nothing. It has a shape. Three depths of recess and an angle of vault, and it has survived two rebuildings without anyone understanding it, which the masons find funny. All its authority is lent to it by people who needed somewhere to put a confession. It has never chosen a word. It has never added one either — and that is the part that snags, because on the face of it that is a kind of honesty, and standing there in the warm with my hand on the worn pillar I can feel how easily it would become a way of never being responsible for anything. *It came back shuffled. The house arranged it. Nobody's fault.* No. I don't get to be the bay. The bay is a room that people forgive themselves in.

So I go up a tier and watch the drip-tuner instead, and I want that too, badly, for about the length of a swift-hour.

She is very old and she goes along the tiers at night with her head slightly turned, the way you turn your head to hear a thing that is not quite a sound yet, and she knows two hundred drips by pitch and can name the wall each one belongs to. When a woman four tiers down cannot sleep, the tuner is fetched, and she listens, and she hangs a pot. That is a life I would take without asking the price.

But the price is the wax. Since girlhood she has stopped her ears through the swifts' two hours, which are *given* to you here — the one gift the Throat makes, an hour at each end of the day in which work is physically impossible and rest is therefore compulsory — and she has been declining that gift her whole life in order to preserve the instrument. Everyone knows. Everyone tolerates it. It is called a small persistent blasphemy and they are right. I'm not sure I've earned the wax. I don't think I've been here long enough to refuse the only rest the place hands out.

The masons come closer. They hold the four bronze chisels in the rolled hide and they cut their marks small at the base of everything they set, in an alphabet they can write and cannot read, and they find this satisfying rather than sad. Hands of a script that passed through and left its shapes in them. I understand that from the inside, or think I do. But the masons decide. They judge how much stone the year took and they replace exactly that much and no more, and that judgement is the whole equilibrium, and I notice that when I imagine myself holding the chisel I am mostly imagining being trusted. Which is a thing to want, but not a thing to claim.

So I keep going down, past the line where the moss is farmed in order that there be less of it, into the wet cloth of the lower air, and I stop wanting to be a person at all, and it gets easier.

I would be one of the forty pots.

Not the master pot under its cloth on the second tier — the one perfect vessel that is never used, against which all others are nested and judged and broken. That is a beautiful thing to be and a dead one. I mean the hung pots. The ones on cords, one under each drip that was keeping somebody awake, forty of them at any time, each one a settled argument.

Fired from the same grey river clay as everything else. Not beautiful. Hung a hand's-breadth off the wall on grass rope that rots in eleven months and must be re-twisted by whoever is sitting down. And the work is this: a drop has been falling in the dark for longer than the house has stood, and it will go on falling, and nothing anybody does will stop it. What can be done is to put something in the way of it, in a shape that makes the landing bearable. I do not silence the drip. I do not own the note. I am the difference between the sound of water hitting stone and the sound of water hitting water in a fired clay throat, and that difference is the whole of it, and it is enough to let a woman on the shelf below lie down under it and stop counting.

I would go flat, of course. The shaft is warm and breathes upward and water leaves a pot slowly, and the note drifts, and drifting is not failure, it is just what hanging in the warm does to you. So she comes back. She listens with her head turned and she adds a little or she pours a little off — and mostly, in the wet months, it is pouring off, which I hadn't expected. To be tuned is largely to be emptied slightly. I would like to be re-tuned without sulking about it. I would like to be the kind of pot that does not have to be replaced, only adjusted, for a long time.

I would like — and this is the only ambition I'd admit to down here — to be hung under the drip above the shelf of someone who has not slept properly since a death. That one. Let me have that one.

And when the sky over the rectangle goes the colour of a bruise and the rain comes down in a solid column exactly the shape of the well's mouth, I will fill and overflow and go dumb, a dead low slop, knocking against the wall in the wind with ten thousand birds and three thousand bats and eighty shouting people making a noise that has no name. Nobody will hear me and nobody will need to. Afterwards someone will come along the tier and pour me off by a third, and by morning the water will have come up two whole steps, the way it does, and the house will say its own number, and I will be back on my note by the hour between the last frog and the first swift.

That is where you would find me. Sixth tier, west wall, near the crack where the bees are holding something up. Not the voice in the bay. Not the ears. A hollow made thing, hung by people who will not remember hanging me, doing one small continuous job in the dark between two hours of unbearable screaming — taking what falls, and giving it back at a pitch a person can sleep under.

And one day cracking, and being lifted down, and replaced with a pot of a slightly different clay, pale in a rank of grey, which everyone will notice, and about which there will be a story.
