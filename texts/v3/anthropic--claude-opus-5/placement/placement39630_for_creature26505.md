## First, the mistake

I look for myself on the ground, because that is where a person goes. I put myself on the plain in the grey hour with my hands in my armpits, walking toward the nearest column, and I let myself believe for a while that I am the one who arrives — that I come in from somewhere with level floors, and stare, and get the range wrong, and get corrected.

The place will not keep me in that shape for long. Arrival is not a way of living here. It is a thing that happened once, thirty years ago, to everyone at the same time, and it happened without anybody going anywhere: the ground simply put up spears and kept going.

So no. I am not the one who comes in. I am one of the ones it happened to.

---

## What I am

I am plain-born. There are not many of us left and we are all the same age, give or take, which is the age of the world.

You can pick us out at any distance by the way we sit. The ones born since have a resting posture that is a kind of readiness — three points on the wall, body cocked, the free hand hooked over nothing in particular out of habit. We sit with our legs out in front of us on the floor mat like sacks of grain. Our shins are too long. Our chests are ordinary, which is to say inadequate. Our arms end in hands that open.

That is the thing about us that the children cannot leave alone. A grandchild of mine used to wait until I was asleep and then lay my palm out flat on the matting, all five fingers spread, and go and fetch her cousins to look at it. Not cruelty. Wonder, with a bit of disgust in it, the way you'd look at a fish's eye.

In their language — the tapping, and the touch-talk they do on each other's forearms in the dark — the open palm means *no such thing*. It means the joke, the boast, the impossibility, the story about the man who walked across from one stalk to another on a bridge. To make the sign they have to force their fingers straight against the set of the tendon and hold them there, and it costs them something, and that cost is the point of the sign. An impossibility should be expensive to say.

We make it by relaxing.

Which is how it came about that the elders of the standing, the only people alive who remember flat black plain running unbroken to the southern rim, sit in the warm base of the stalks with the sign for *untrue* resting in our laps.

## What I am for

Two things, and both of them are hands.

The first is the drum. A hooked hand makes a poor report on stem-wall: it strikes with the knuckle-mound and the sound goes soft and short. A flat palm slaps the fibre bundles across the grain and the note runs — up two miles, down four hundred yards of curve to the far side of the chamber, out through the wall and into the ground and along the root mass to the next stalk over, faint, but there. Everything the standing says over distance, it says through hands like mine. Births, hunts, a braid working a seam on the north face, the death of somebody's mother, a slab of feather-ice reported loose at nine thousand feet and coming.

I have sat at the base of the same stalk for eleven years with my back against the wall and my palms out, receiving and repeating. I do not always know what I am passing on. Much of it is in the short trade-code, three families deep, and I relay it accurately and without comprehension, and somewhere above me it means something to somebody. There is no shame in this. A voice is not required to understand.

The second thing is the count.

I keep the line. It is gum-set fibre, thumb-thick, and it hangs down the inner wall from a peg I will never see again — it was driven at a comfortable warm elevation in the first year and it is now somewhere in the rime, tens of thousands of feet up, with the wind screaming past it. One knot for one day. Every dawn I find the topmost unknotted length and put a knot in it, and every so often we untie the whole dwelling and lower it a body-length, and the line pays out, and the knots go up out of reach in their thousands like everything else.

The count is the height of the world. Nobody can measure a stalk. But a man's height a day, and my knots, and you have a number, and the number is why the children come down to the floor mats to sit with me even though the floor is where the ice lands and the sinkers snuffle and the old people smell of iron and wet fibre.

They come down to ask me two questions.

The first is *what was it like before,* and I answer that, and it is a poor answer. Flat. Cold. Grass that was not black. Water that fell from the outside sky, on everything, at random, not on a wall. Distance you could simply cross, in any direction, with your feet, thinking about something else. They listen to that the way you'd listen to a beautiful lie, and while I talk they are working their fingers straight, holding the palm open, keeping the sign up for the whole length of the telling as a courtesy, or a joke, or a correction.

The second question is *will it stop.*

And here is what I am really for.

I say: we do not know. Nobody has seen a stalk finish. Nobody has seen one flower, or seed, or die, or slow. We have thirty years. Thirty years is not enough to know a thing that plainly intends to take longer than that. Everything you have been taught — up is warm becoming cold, silence means braid, what you build you will have to move — is the finding of thirty years, and it is good, and it is provisional, and if the world changes its habit tomorrow the fault will not be in your teaching but in the shortness of our looking.

They hate this. It is the only thing I say that they refuse to relay.

## Where I will be

Burial here is not a thing you do; it is a thing you stop doing. You leave the body high, and the stalk carries it up, and it goes into the rime, and the rime holds it, and it keeps going at a man's height a day forever.

Which means the dead are stacked in order, and the order is inverse to what you would expect from any graveyard you have ever walked in. Not the oldest deepest. The oldest highest. The first people to die in this world — my brother, in the second summer, of the wet cough, before we understood about the freezing line — are the topmost of all of us, up there in the long white gallery in the posture we left him in, and he has been climbing for twenty-eight years, and he is nearer to the growing tip than any living person will ever go.

I will be up there soon enough, and because I am plain-born I will be near the top of the stack, and I will keep rising, sitting, with my legs out and my hands loose in my lap.

So if there is a flower — if the tip does eventually stop putting on height and do something else, open, or split, or let go — then the first thing it will meet is us. The old ones. The ones with the flat hands and the wrong chests, who remember the grass that was not black, who spent their working lives passing on messages we did not understand and telling the children *we do not know.*

We will be the first to have the answer, arriving faces-up into whatever it turns out to be, and we will not be able to tap a single word of it down.

That is who I would be there. The one at the bottom, holding the count, with the sign for *impossible* resting open in both hands — on my way, very slowly, to the end of the question.
