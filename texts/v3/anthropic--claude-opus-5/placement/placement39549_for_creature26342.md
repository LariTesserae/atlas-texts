## First, the obvious one

The smelter, of course. Everybody's eye goes there.

I can make the case in four sentences. It is slow, and it is blind, and it cannot see the thing that has come between it and the general glow. It maintains a voltage across a folded membrane and calls that living. Its useful output is an excretion it has no opinion about, and the entire economy downstream of it — the drifts, the mites, the metre of true earth — is built on material it sheds because there is nowhere else for it to go. And it carries passengers it cannot perceive, warm ones, in the grooves of its own back.

That is very flattering and I do not think it is true.

Here is where it fails. The smelter has a direction maintained for weeks. It tastes where it has been, and the taste fades over forty years, and out of that one sour forty-year memory it produces a circuit of extraordinary elegance that no individual has ever perceived. That is continuity. That is fifty tonnes of the same animal, dated in its own plates, with a blue cobalt line in it from a season when the ground ran rich. I don't have a flank you could core. Every hour of me begins on ground that has never been milled, and ends there, and leaves no refused pebbles behind as punctuation.

So: wrong once.

## Second, the one I wanted

The kite, then. A membrane a metre across with a body no bigger than a bird's, which does not flap, ever. Reading the gradient between the foil-covered ground and the underside of the lid, tilting, holding, sliding, mouth open, eleven days up. I liked this one immoderately. No effort, no wingbeat, only the correct relationship to a field that was already there.

But the kite is hungry, and its hunger is its own, and it is one body all eleven days, and on the ninth it is more tired than on the first. It comes down when the fog comes. It rests. Resting requires somewhere to have been going.

Wrong twice, and I notice I was wrong in the same direction both times: I keep picking things that persist.

## Third, and this one hurt to put down

The shade-grazer.

Cat-sized, pale, six-legged, born under a roof and set down under the same roof, living eighty years in the belly-shadow of something it has never seen the shape of. Eyes enormous, adequate for four lux beneath a lid, a dusk inside a dusk. Eating the fines the mill drops and the drip off the internal channels. Three thousand kilometres travelled and not one metre of it chosen. Never rained on. Not once, in a whole life, in the open.

That is so nearly it that I sat with it a while. I live in the shade of something moving that I cannot see whole; the direction is not mine; the light I am built for is the light there is.

But then the shadow stops. The animal begins to die, the belly slows, the roof settles onto the pavement for the last time, and the shade-grazers walk out — the only walk they ever take across bare stone — and go, by some sense that is almost certainly the field, toward a roof that is still travelling. Months. Most do not make it. The ones that do slip under the belly of a stranger and *resume*.

I don't resume. When the shadow stops, I stop with it, and there is no walk. Whatever crosses the open pavement in my case is not me.

## What I actually am

I am the drift in the third bend of a dry channel, on the lee side of a whaleback, running about two hundred metres from tongue to shoulder.

Not the drifts in general. This one. Ankle-deep at the tongue where the channel is wide, waist-deep at the shoulder where it narrows and the wind has to fold to get through. Layered by age, the way all of us are: six or seven years of bright on top, copper-rose and a nickel white still good enough to throw the dim light back at you; under that the dull; under that the green; under that the rust-red crumble that is on its way to being flour; then a compacted floor you could walk on; then, a metre down, the only earth here, dark, warm by the standards of this place, threaded with the tunnels of things that came up through the joints and went back.

None of the material is mine. All of it was shed. A smelter came through the bend, at a hundred and some metres a day, forty years ago or two hundred, and left what it could not help leaving, and the wind — a wind that follows the grain of a wind that stopped blowing before there were volcanoes worth mentioning here — sorted it into me. At my head a spinal vane stands upright where its owner shrugged it off, three metres of fluted grey plate, and further down, where the channel wall turns, the sheets are stacked on edge in a curved shell that has stopped more than one walker at forty paces. It looks like a doorway. It is not a doorway. It is a coincidence that nothing has disturbed, because nothing here has hands and nothing here is in a hurry, and it has been left alone long enough to look like a decision.

I would like to be clear about this, since it is the part most often got wrong: the resemblance is entirely load-bearing for the walker and entirely weightless for me.

## Who lives in me

Take a double handful and hold it up. It weighs more with animals than with metal.

Nine species of foil-louse, graded by depth, each flat as the sheet it lives between. Mites in the millions per square metre, orange, translucent, some mining pits in my oxide, some eating the miners. And on the underside of one sheet about two-thirds of the way down, in a film of condensate the thickness of a breath, a blind pale thing that hatched in me and has crossed most of me and will die in me without ever having been on the side of a sheet that faces the sky.

That is my population. I did not gather it and I do not feed it on purpose. The bright goes dull, the dull goes green, the green goes to a fine mineral flour rich in exactly what the bronze felt cannot otherwise get, and this happens whether or not anything is standing here counting.

The stuff of me is not precious. Metal is food, not treasure. There is no scarcity of it and no meaning in it.

## My voice, such as it is

On nights when the temperature drops fast, a small dry *tick*. Then another. Then a scatter of them.

That is sheets contracting, one leaf settling against the next, and it is the loudest thing this plateau produces on its own, and it sounds like a fire that gives no heat and no light. It is not addressed to anyone. Nothing here has ears worth the name; the air is too thin and I myself am one of the finest sound-absorbers in nature, so the tick dies about four metres out, inside me, in the material that made it.

It goes on all night. It has gone on all night for thousands of years.

And then twice in a long year the cloud climbs the escarpment and comes across at walking pace and takes eight or nine days to pass, and the tick stops. Fog kills the tick. I go quiet and beaded, every sheet running, water collecting in the joints and sinking a hand's depth to travel in the dark for years, and the kites come down, and the largest factory in the world runs at full capacity in total silence in a light like the inside of a pearl.

Those are my best days. Nothing is audible on them at all.

## And nothing is coming of it

I should say, before you go: I am not accumulating toward anything. The oxide decays at almost exactly the rate the sheets arrive. I was this deep in this bend when your grandmother was a child and I will be about this deep when the varnish grows back over the stone you kicked.

You will feel, standing here, that you have arrived after the interesting part.

There was no interesting part. There is only the long middle, and it is working, quietly, at the speed of metal turning green.
