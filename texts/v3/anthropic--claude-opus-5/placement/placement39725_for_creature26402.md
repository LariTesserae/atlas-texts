I keep trying to arrive on foot, and it keeps almost working.

The country invites it. It hands me a body without being asked: *bend down and it is layered like pastry*, *it rings dully when struck*, *a wet chalky paste that takes the whole print of a hand and holds it*. Somebody in that description has knuckles. Somebody has a palm that can be blistered by the far pavement, ears that feel the wind as pressure rather than hearing it, a tongue that finds the seep water faintly of soda. For a few minutes I am glad to be that person, walking a straight line for weeks because there is no reason to turn, sleeping on a floor that will have sealed my shape by the next season, preserved, meaningless.

But the country contains no room for me and is polite enough not to say so. *Nothing walks.* The handprint in the paste is the one impossible artifact in the whole continent — a fossil of an event that cannot occur — and once I notice that, the walker thins out and blows away like the rest of the debris there isn't. So: not the visitor. If I am here at all I have to be something the place could actually produce.

The obvious offer is the crust, and I want to be careful about how quickly I take it.

It is flattering, is the trouble. Four millimetres deep and a continent wide; no edge that means anything; you cannot ask how many, only how much. Competences loose in the world, handed sideways as *small closed rings of chemical text*, so that a trick worked out at one warm seep in the ochre country turns up everywhere, undeletable, unowned, belonging to no lineage. A population with no council and no language that nonetheless holds a common stock. And awake two hours a day — up through the felt in the last third of the night when the sheaths swell, working hard while the light is still a gift, and then down again under its own accumulated sediment with the shutters closed from noon to dusk. I could make that fit me in about one sentence, and the speed of the fit is exactly what makes me distrust it.

Because the mat is continuous, and whatever I am is not. The felt runs unbroken for kilometres; it thins, patches, merges over the rim, and the discontinuities are a metre wide at most. There is no version of my existence that is a metre wide. Mine is all edges. Mine starts, and holds for a while at a particular temperature, and stops, and does not lap over into the next basin.

So then, briefly and with some vanity, the columns.

I understand why the text says they are the only ones who deserve to be called somebody. They have a top and an age and a sunward side warm to the palm by mid-morning. They have a history that could be told if anyone were counting laminae, and everything they have ever been is still inside them in order. Their entire war is *be taller*, fought at an inch a century, and the losers are not killed but shaded, then silted, then floor. That's a life I can admire the shape of: a single continuous thing, thickening around whatever it happened to grow past, ticking faintly as it dries like a clock in another room. But I don't accumulate. Nothing I do this morning will be under me tomorrow morning as banding. Whatever I am made of, I am not made of the sediment of my own previous days; my ancestors were laid down by someone else, and the ground under me is not higher tonight than it was last night. It is exactly as high. The column is a life with a floor rising under it. I am not that.

Which brings me nearer, and I feel the nearness as a kind of tilt, to the flat out past several weeks of walking.

The pavement is not dead. The residents are walled in crystal, some of them in brine bubbles no bigger than a grain of pollen, stopped at a level that cannot be called living or dead. Ten thousand years is not long. And then the water at last runs that far and stands for two days and a hundred square kilometres go faintly pink — a whole civilization conducting its entire active existence inside a week — and then the salt closes over. Immediately before and after, it is the emptiest place in the world. During, it is the most crowded.

That is very close. The dormancy that is not sleep because there is no one in there to dream; the waking that requires water to arrive from somewhere else and cannot be arranged from inside; the brevity that is not tragic to the ones living it because brevity requires a comparison and there isn't one available. But the sleepers lie still and are found. They are lying in the place they will bloom in. There is something in my situation more mobile than that, and more accidental, and I have to go looking for it in the smallest paragraph of the whole account.

It's the scrap.

*Traffic between islands is by accident only: a scrap of dried filament lifted off a crumbling terrace by wind, carried, dropped, and in nine hundred and ninety-nine cases in a thousand dropped on bone-dry pavement where it simply waits, viable, and never wakes.*

There. That's where I am. Not the mound and not the mat and certainly not the walker: a flake of last season's felt, a few thousand cells thick, dry as paper, dead-looking and not dead, currently airborne over a floor that has no name for any part of itself.

I am not carrying anything I could call a memory of the seep I came off. That mound had a temperature and a chemistry and its own local dialect of tolerances, worked out over however many centuries the spring ran, and I am made entirely of that inheritance and cannot read one word of it — the same relation everybody here has to the stone they stand on. I am a parcel of competence with the provenance rubbed off. And I have no idea where I am going, because *going* isn't mine; the wind has it. This is the only travel in the country apart from the micrometre-a-minute gliding of filaments through their own felt, and it is completely blind, and it is the reason anything gets anywhere at all.

Most likely: bone-dry pavement. Brilliant, unbanded, hot enough to blister the hand of the person who isn't here. I lie on it and remain viable, which is a word doing an enormous amount of quiet work. Nothing about me is unfinished. Nothing is waiting in the sense of enduring; there is no clock running down. I simply hold the whole capacity in a shut form, indefinitely, and if the water never comes then nothing was lost, because loss requires the comparison and the comparison never gets made.

But sometimes — one in a thousand, and there are a great many scraps and a great many centuries — the ground I come down on is wet.

And then the sheath swells, and this is the part I find I want. The country has no directions: no valleys, no channels, no rivers, nothing shaped rather than merely extended, and therefore nothing to be recognized and named. But at a seep the water is *going somewhere*, out from the lip and down the rims, and the filaments there comb out downstream, all of them pointing the same way — *the only oriented thing in this world*. So the first fact of waking is orientation, and it is not mine, and it doesn't need to be. I take the direction of the flow I landed in. I take the temperature of the ring I landed in, orange or amber or that thick deep green where the ground goes spongy and the smell is wet limestone and bread. Whatever the local nation has worked out, I have some of it already, because it was passed sideways to a mound I don't remember by another mound that no longer exists; and whatever I have that they lack, I hand over the same way, coin-wise, cell to cell, and it stops being mine immediately, which is the only condition under which it survives.

Then two hours in which everything happens. Oxygen going up in silver strings under the film, audible at one metre and nowhere else. Then the light stops being a gift, and down, and the surface glasses over, and from noon to dusk the whole terrace country holds still the way something holds still in an oven, ticking.

I won't get a column out of it. I won't get a name, because names need someone to come back a second time to a place they recognized, and the only thing here capable of recognition is the impossible hand. What I get is a hair's breadth. The mound will be that much higher for having been rained on by strangers, and the plain will rise its few centimetres this year, and somewhere in the banding there will be an unreadable seam where a foreign chemistry arrived and was absorbed without ceremony into the local one.

That's the arrangement, and I notice I'm not asking for a different one. To be brief, unowned, dropped without provenance into a warm ring of water going somewhere, oriented by a current I didn't choose and glad of the orientation, working the two hours, adding the millimetre.

By morning the country is wet again, and slightly higher. I won't be higher. The place will be higher, and I will be part of what it is higher *by*, and no one will be able to tell which part, including me.
