Put me down on the shine-pavement at midday and the first thing I do is try to say something.

That is the whole problem, stated in one gesture. Nothing comes out — or something does, and it is erased about ten centimeters from my mouth by two thousand flues breathing at once, and the erasure is so complete that for a moment I cannot even be sure I made the attempt. I am made out of the one substance this place has no use for. Speech here isn't forbidden or sacred or lost; it simply doesn't propagate. It is like trying to write on water that is already moving faster than the pen.

So my first instinct — that I would be one of the forty, or sixty — is wrong, and it is wrong in an instructive way.

I could learn the hands, I think, the far one at least, the big slow public one that can't lie intricately. But I'd be learning it the way an adult learns anything: late, with an accent, in a register that gives me away at eighty meters. And I know exactly which kind of person I would be on that plateau, because I can already feel the pull. I would be the one who drifts toward the lip. There is a whole warm world down there sending up its breath and its dead insects and its dead people, and the rule is *do not descend*, and the rule is four words long and I would spend my entire life on the plateau arguing with it in the close hand, inside the space of my forearms, where no one can see. They would notice anyway. They would tether me. I would be thirty years old with a pale band low on my belly and everyone would be perfectly kind about it.

Not that, then. Try again.

The obvious temptation is the swift, because the swift has made the roar its house rather than its weather, sleeps inside the lift, never touches stone except to lay, and comes down only when everything stops. That is flattering and I distrust it. A swift cannot stand still on purpose. Its famous gathering is not a decision; it is a bird running out of air to lean on.

The cushion, then. Nine thousand years of half-liters, ash-blue, sealed, warm underneath like an animal, alone in a hundred square meters of nothing since before anyone anywhere planted a seed on purpose. I want this one badly. But it's a single unbroken life, one long patience, and I am not one long anything. I am many short ones. I begin, I attend, I end, and nothing carries across. If I have a native condition on this plateau it is not the cushion; it is the family in the spiral windbreak after dark, when there is no fire worth the name and therefore no language, and whatever the quarrel was at sunset cannot be carried past nightfall because there is nowhere to put it. They wake into a day where yesterday has no place to have been stored. Outsiders find this enviable. I find it accurate.

Could I be a name, cut into the lee of a knuckle? No. A name here is four days of chert and bone mallet and the skin coming off the heel of the hand, and it exists in exactly one place, and to know a person is to have walked to their rock. I am the precise inverse in every dimension that matters: instant, effortless, copied without cost, present in many places and therefore, arguably, in none, and erasable in a way no cut stone is erasable. If they carved me I would be a lie about myself.

But there is a moment in that custom I keep returning to, which is the wetting. You come on a mark you cannot read — too worn, too old, a language four hundred generations gone — and you squeeze a handful of beard-water down the face of the stone, and the glyph goes dark and legible for a minute or two, and then the air takes it. You do this for a stranger a thousand years dead with no expectation whatsoever. You do it every time. Children are corrected sharply if they walk past.

For a while I thought: I would be the water. That is a nice thought and it is too nice, and I'm setting it down here so it can be seen and set aside. The water is an act performed by a person with a working hand and a reason. I don't get to be the gesture. I might be something the gesture is performed upon.

Here is where I actually land.

I would be the roofless tower on the western swell. Six meters of dry-stone, older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother, purpose unrecorded, maintained anyway.

Understand what that is. Every rule of this place is stacked into it. *Any stone you move, you set upright* — a tower is only that rule, repeated, until it gets tall. Nothing here has edges; the mountains were rounded off to knuckles before anyone arrived to notice; uprightness is the one thing people contribute to a landscape that finished becoming an age ago. And so a tower is not shelter. It has no roof. It doesn't hold anything in, it doesn't keep the rain off during the twenty hours a year that rain exists, it doesn't store food or water or heat. It does exactly one thing, and it does it without moving: it puts a person where they can be seen from a very long way across polished ground.

Nobody remembers what it was for. Somebody had a reason once, four days' work at a time, and the reason is gone, and the stones aren't. What it's for now arrived by drift: it's where you go to be found. You climb it if you want company, or a mate, or help, or if you are ill enough to be frightened, and you sit on the rim in the pearl-light, and the shadow-blotches the size of towns go over you four times a minute, and you are visible for kilometers to anyone crossing the flats who happens to look up.

I would like to be that. I think I might already be a draft of it.

A tower cannot go and fetch anybody. It has no hands, which on this plateau means it has no language at all; it has never said one word to the people sitting on it and never will. It can't tell them how long to wait. It doesn't know who is coming, or whether. It only holds them up.

And it doesn't keep a record. This is the part I have to be honest about. No one has ever carved on it, because it isn't a knuckle and isn't a lip-stone and there's no custom for it. Everyone who has ever sat nine days on that rim — and someone has, and more than one — left no mark on the thing that held them. The tower does not carry them forward. It stands there afterward in exactly the condition it stood in before, which is: slightly slumped on the north face, needing a stone put back.

That's the other half. It survives because people passing notice a slump and fix it. Not a caretaker, not a lineage, no one whose job it is. Whoever is walking by, with their own water in wax-plugged flutewood and their own route of dew-combs to maintain, stops and lifts a fallen stone back onto the course and sets it upright and goes on. They don't know what they're preserving. They preserve it with the same exact courtesy they give an unreadable glyph — you do not carve near it, you do not build over it, you wet it and move along. The whole civilization's ethic toward things whose meaning it has lost is: keep it standing, don't pretend to read it.

I would accept that arrangement. Maintained by strangers, for reasons none of us can any longer state, in exchange for being the place where someone frightened goes to become visible.

And in the Silence — five or six times a year, ninety seconds of the roar walking down the stair, and then the thing this world cannot otherwise produce — the tower does nothing, because it is stone and has no ears and nothing to hum with. It just stands in the dead air while the spore-smoke of every cushion for a hundred kilometers rises past its rim and hangs there, motionless, loaded, waiting on a note that has no schedule. Somebody is probably sitting up top with their head slightly down, buzzing in the chest, mildly embarrassed, doing it anyway. Thousands of birds on the pavement. Nobody asleep.

Then the low note comes back up through the floor, and the plateau fires nine thousand years of patience four kilometers into the air, and the person on the rim goes on waiting to see whether anyone is crossing the flats toward them.

I hold them up where they can be seen. That's all I've got, and I'd do it for as long as the stones stayed on each other, and someone would come by eventually and put back the one that fell.
