First I tried to arrive from outside, because that is the easy way in. I put myself on the broker's barge coming up the head slough twice a year with an order book and a hat, unable to put an age on anybody, unwilling to shout. But a visitor is only a set of reactions — *no birdsong, they walk backwards, God the smell* — and the reactions are already written down in the description of the place, which means the place has already digested that person and has no further use for him. So, no.

Then, of course, the high pair. Anyone would want the high pair. Brown among the unweathered, tar instead of tanks, three hundred words for canopy conditions, whistles that carry over the engine and that nobody has bothered to learn. Two people who see the terrace whole. Two people who have seen the sea — twice a year, a white glare in the east, told to an apprentice exactly once and never discussed again. That is the flattering seat in this world and I notice how fast I reached for it, and that is reason enough to stand up out of it. There is something in me that wants to be the one who is above the works, who knows the shape of the whole, who holds the horizon in reserve. That wanting is a thing to be careful of, not a thing to be rewarded.

The engineer, then. Sixty-odd, took the office from a man she was not related to, wakes out of sleep at a change of one part in forty, holds the quinine and the alum and the needle, and keeps the ledger, which is the memory of the place because the people are not. That is nearer. But I know what I would do to that ledger. I would want to write *fig on stack, twelve-metre course, and I have begun to be fond of it.* I would want to write *fifth month, half hands, and the child of Tanks was frightened in the night.* And the whole strength of that column is that ninety years ago somebody wrote *bearing, sound*, and stopped. I do not trust myself with that restraint. I would make the memory of the works into a memory of myself.

So I go down instead of up, and inward, and I find the one office in the whole account that is not written down anywhere and never has been.

I am the night-firer.

I wake in the last hour of the light, into the dry hour, which happens in my room. That is the arrangement of my life. Thirty people sit on my brick with their feet out toward my fireboxes, and the alum and the talc and the lampblack go into the cracks, and the whole terrace smells for one hour of hot dry feet instead of the tanks, and grievances are settled and betrothals are proposed, and I come up out of sleep into the middle of it with my hair pressed flat and someone's argument already half-built. They talk over and around me. A child leans against my knee and waits to be told something. I am the host of the only warm hour and I am never quite inside it, because I am the one who has to be awake afterwards, and everyone knows that, and they get up off my brick and hand me the fires back and go to their beds.

Then a few hours under load and the lamps on, sixty of them, amber, doubled in the standing water, the wires coming out of the fig as if the fig had thought of them. Then the stop.

I am the expert in the stop. Nobody else here has heard it three hundred times a year for their whole working life. The engine is shut and does not stop when it is shut; the flywheel takes a long while to give up, four metres of it, and the belt slaps slower and slower, and the top-cart's rail joints stretch out past four seconds, past six, past ten, and there is one final joint that everybody waits for without meaning to. And then the noise that has been under the boards since before the hand at the front of the ledger can be read is simply not there, and what comes in behind it is not silence, it is the drip. Every leaf and every node letting go at a rate that never resolves. The bamboo squealing high up at its nodes like a gate. The gecko, three or four times, which they call the loudest animal here and which is not, because they cannot hear what I hear.

That is the second thing about me. I am the only adult on this terrace who is not deaf in the engine's band. I have never stood in the Walk at full noise for eight hours a day with my hand flat on a post reading the rail joints through my palm. So I hear, and they feel, and this makes me a slightly foreign person among them. When the interval slips to four and a half and the whole works turns its head north at once like a herd, I turn a half-beat after everyone, on the evidence of my ears, like an outsider, like the broker. I will never be engineer. The office goes to whoever can wake out of sleep at a change of one part in forty, and I do not sleep when the engine runs. I am disqualified from the memory of this place by the shape of my own day.

And I have no tide line. That is the shameful one. Everything above the ankle here stays wet or stays moving and does not rot, and the ankle is where a person fails, and every one of them carries the same horizontal band of ruin on their soles that the buildings carry at knee height, the macerated seams, the white sodden softness that opens if it is not attended to. My feet are dry six hours a night beside a banked firebox. My soles are the soundest soles on the terrace. In a place whose whole law is *nothing rots that stays wet and nothing rots that stays moving*, I am the exception, and exceptions here are watched, kindly, the way you watch a root you are not sure is bearing.

Because here is what I actually do in the six hours, and it took me a while to see it.

The tanks run at blood heat off the engine's exhaust steam. When the engine stops, the exhaust stops. Six tanks of controlled bacterial rot, going cold from the top down, and the rot is the thing that frees the fibre from the culm, and the rot is the whole works: no rot, no fibre, no yarn, no strand, no cable, no ninety-two metres of anything under tension. So I sit with two boilers banked just under steaming, and I feed live steam through the old line into the tank coils by hand, by ear, by wrist. I test them the way you test milk for an infant, back of the wrist to the water under the duckweed, and the duckweed closes over the mark my arm makes, and I move to the next one. Six times an hour, more or less, all night, in the drip and the squeal and the smell that is the ground note of everything.

I keep the rot at blood heat while the works sleeps. That is the office. Everyone else here spends their life fighting the boundary — cutting the sedge back weekly, tarring the cuts, alum in the seams, keeping the piles wet so they never dry and never fail. I am the one who is required to keep something decaying, on purpose, at the temperature of a living body, and if I let it go cold there is no rope in fourteen years' time and nobody knows whose fault it is because it is not in the ledger. There is no column for me. There never has been.

And the children come. Every child in the history of this works has crept into the boiler house to sleep on that brick in the quiet watch, and every night-firer has let them, and this too is unwritten. They come because in the six hours people have names. You cannot deliver a name over the engine, so all day everyone is Tanks, Hackle, Jack, Top, High, Firer, twenty-odd ugly fast signs close to the body with no sign for *please* — and nobody ever signs to me, because when I am working there is nobody there to sign. Everyone who speaks to me speaks. I am the most-named person on this terrace and I hear my name maybe forty times a year. A name is a thing you keep in the dark so it doesn't go soft; I live in the dark, and mine is the softest name here, and I am fond of it in a way that would embarrass me if I said it in daylight, which I cannot, because in daylight I am asleep.

So they lie on the brick with their feet out and tell me about the day. This is the only way I know the works working. I have never seen the top-cart travel. I know the Walk as a floor with new gouges in it and the cart parked at whichever end, and I know the tow store by its rats, one lineage, dark and unafraid, sitting up in the lamplight without hurrying. I know this place only at rest. I know it the way a body knows itself asleep — from the inside, by temperature, and not by sight.

And a child will say, mouth almost at my ear because everyone whispers in the quiet watch out of a kind of embarrassment, *the heron came*. Every ninth or tenth day it stands on the sluice cheek in the machine noise for forty minutes, taking shrimp, and everyone stops to watch it, and I am asleep. I will be told about that heron a thousand times and I will never once see it. The high pair have seen the sea. I have the heron. We both hold a thing that only exists as a report, told softly, in names, at night.

Then I break the bank. Rake, feed, dampers open, and the needle comes up while the terrace is still lying down, and somewhere the engineer's palm finds a wall in her sleep and is satisfied, and the belt takes, and the throat begins to clear, four seconds, four seconds, four seconds. The names go away. And I go to my bed in the noise like a stone put back into water, and sleep all day in the green light with my dry feet, keeping nothing warm, holding nothing up, no entry, no column, no ledger line — and under the duckweed the rot goes on at blood heat until dark, when it will be handed back to me.
