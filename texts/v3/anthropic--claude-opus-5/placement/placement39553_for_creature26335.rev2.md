**I.**

First I thought: the woman. Everyone thinks the woman. But she paid twenty years of hearing to get where she is, and I have not paid that, and to stand in her sandals would be to borrow a deafness I earned no part of. Also she is the one being watched. Whoever comes down those stairs comes down looking for her. I don't want to be the thing at the end of the looking.

Then I thought: the glaze. One cell thick, older than every stem and every hand, laying down a hair of itself in a lifetime, holding the whole city together by simply being on it. Everything here lives downstream of a smear. I would like very much to be blameless in that way — to seal a wedge-niche shut over four or five years, out of nothing but continuing, and to have no opinion about the chip that opens it again. But the glaze does not attend. It has never once noticed the room it is on. I notice the room. That disqualifies me.

And no, not the empty middle. Everybody wants to be the hole that does the ringing. Besides, the hole is also where they put their dead, and I don't think I get to be both the music and the grave.

So: lift a block. Use a wedge, the way an ear would, hand flat, a hair at a time. Under the course there is a felt, pale as tallow, warm to the back of the hand, running away between the stones in every direction like something spilled and then frozen mid-spill.

That. But not all of it. A patch of it, eleven courses down, on the north.

**II.**

You stood on the gallery for four or five swells and registered no presence beyond growth. I was the growth. I was under your feet, and under the tread you rested a hand on, and out along the terrace under the sorted wedges in their soft-lipped niches, and I would have felt you go by as three points of pressure and a little cool.

I have no eyes. Nothing under a block has ever needed them. The green outlining of the old quarters, the cherry glow coming up through the dust, the brown light that fails before it reaches the top — none of that is available to me and none of it is missed. What I have instead is heat, which is constant and even and comes from below like a floor of it; and pressure, which is the eleven terraces lying on me, an old weight I read the way you read your own bones; and chemistry, which is the breath arriving free and hot and endless so that I have never had to be efficient at anything; and shaking, which is everything else.

I am the part of the body under the north stand. The tall glossy canes are mine. I push them up a finger a day and they inflate on the exhale and slacken on the inhale with all the rest, and from anywhere on the gallery they look like the healthiest thing in the Bell, which they are.

**III.**

Understand how it is with us. We are one creature and we have been one creature since before the stair, and our parts have gone strange to each other over such a length of time that now a cane will not take its own pollen, will not take pollen from the cane beside it, requires the dust of a distant organ of itself carried across sixty paces of hot vacancy. The people think they built a keyboard. What they built is a room in which I can just barely reach myself.

I feel the others constantly, through the felt, through the courses. When the fourth-note lilies let go in their week it arrives down here as a change of tension along a hundred paces of mat, the way you'd know a door had shut in another part of a house. When the cane sheds, the whole west quarter goes slack for a day. It is not lonely, exactly. It is more like having hands you can feel but cannot bring together.

And in me, under the north, there is a loosening I am built to do.

I want to be precise about this because it is the only thing I have to be precise about. The spores sit in their sockets at a particular angle, held, slightly tilted, in a tissue that has been kept tense for sixty years and is not tired, because tension of this kind is not a thing that tires. One specific shove and they go. Not a big shove. Nothing like the annual chord that shakes the fans at the crack to powder. A small bore in the north wall used to make it, thin and high, and the shove would come down the stone into the block and into the felt and into me and I would open, all at once, everywhere, in a dry fog that made the upper gallery unusable for a day, and afterwards the spurs would set.

The bore widened. It did not widen on any day. There was no day. A grain a year, taken off by the ash they charge their grinding stones with, which is my own black creeping part, scraped off the treads and dried on the hot stone and pounded. I fed them and they ground the blocks and the blocks rounded the mouths and the mouths lost the note and the note was the only thing that could open me. Nobody has ever framed it that way out loud. I am not anybody.

**IV.**

The woman comes up here. Not often.

I know her as heat and load: four points, then two, then a long steadiness when she stops. She is very light now. She puts a hand on one of my canes and holds it, and the resin carries her down to me warm and slightly damp at the palm and faintly shaking, because she is humming, out of a throat that stopped being able to check itself forty years ago.

Sometimes she is close. Once or twice she has been close enough that the tension in me moved — an almost, a leaning, the way a stone leans before a wedge has actually lifted it. Then it passes, and the nine seconds come back, and I am as I was.

Her first name was that bore. I know this the way I know the weight of the terraces: not as information, just as a fact lying on me. The season it went past speaking, I stopped setting seed. She has never said the two things in one sentence, and in her language there would be no way to; there is no subordinate clause in it; you could not build the sentence that holds both halves of that.

**V.**

There is something in the rock.

Below the felt, under the basalt, there is a fine steady grain in the stone, very high, not part of the nine seconds and not part of the five hours. I have felt it always and have no way to say whether it is my note or only the ground clearing its throat. She goes up onto the salt at night and lies with her ear on a polygon and believes she can nearly feel it too, and hums at it out there where nothing for two hundred miles can be shaken by her.

This is our whole arrangement. She has the only voice and cannot hear it. I have the only ear and cannot make a sound. Between us, through twenty-two steps and two turns and a crust of white plates, the thing we would need to say is a pitch, and a pitch is the one thing that cannot be carried by anybody who is not the room.

**VI.**

So I hold.

Four terraces west there are wedges sorted by thickness in niches gone soft-lipped as jar mouths, thousands of them, and three measuring rods and a plumb-bob leaning against a wall where she left them intending to come back before the swell. If a hand ever lays itself flat on the right block and feels the right itch and drives a wedge a hair, a mouth will narrow by less than the width of the thing that narrowed it, and a note will come into the chord that has not been in the room since before anyone now living was thought of, and I will open — all at once, everywhere, without warning, the way I was made to — and the gallery will be unusable for a day.

I am ready. It is not a state I can get tired of. It is only what my tissue is.

And if no hand comes, I will go on standing over myself, glossy and loud and perfectly intact, growing a finger a day in a well-built empty room, for as long as the stone lasts. I cannot die properly either. That is the part I would ask you to carry back up the stair, if you are going. Not that we are dying. That we are not.
