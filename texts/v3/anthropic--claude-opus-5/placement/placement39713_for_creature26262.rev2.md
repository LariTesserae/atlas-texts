First, the wrong answer, so we can get rid of it.

I would be the one at the switchback station with the tin of wax in my hand, turning the card over, reading all four languages to see whether the translations agree. I would say *hear*. I would say it twice, probably, and get corrected twice, and the second correction would be patient in the way that tells you the correction is a kind of tax the plateau levies on arrivals and has long since stopped being interesting to the collector. I would spend my first week talking into the open air like a man scattering coins off a bridge, and someone would eventually tell me, kindly, that it was a waste, and I would not understand what was being wasted.

But that is the traveller, and the traveller is a device for looking. The question is where I'd *live*.

—

Not the wading crews. I know this about myself with unusual certainty. Out in the antinode, hooded, filled to the collarbone with warm water, hand over hand along a knotted rope in a white roar that shakes the eye in its socket — I would count the line. Not out of courage and not out of unbelief. Out of habit. A rope with knots in it is a thing to be enumerated; that is what knots are *for*; and the one rule written into the guild book in red is the rule I would break in the first forty yards, and then I would turn my head, which is the second rule, and then I would be a story told to apprentices. So: not a wader. The plateau would have to find some other use for me.

Not a listener, either, though I want to be honest about how close that one sits. A disreputable trade: attend a widow on the polished ground after the water has taken her husband out across the shallows, stand with her while she listens, and then tell her which part of the note he has become. The Office loathes them. The shrines tolerate them. Nobody can stop them, because what they sell is not a lie, exactly — it is an interpretation delivered with confidence into a silence where confidence is the scarcest commodity, and it is wanted, and it is paid for, and it cannot be checked. I would be good at that work. That is the whole trouble with it. I would be good at it in a way that would slowly stop being a service and start being a habit of mouth, and the difference between those two things is invisible from the inside, and there is no fork and no bowl for measuring it.

Not the hound. Too flattering, that — to be the creature so exactly fitted to its hollow that it is carried a hand's breadth a year for its whole life and never once knows it has travelled. Everyone likes to imagine themselves as the innocent instrument. It's a way of asking to be excused.

I thought for a while about the hoods. Someone spends a winter embroidering the inside of a hood — saints, eels, the family's dead in a list, a joke about a neighbour — on silk that will be worn four inches from one face in the dark and shown to no one. The most decorated cloth on the plateau, made entirely for an interior. I felt the pull of that. But it is a trade for someone with a household and a winter and a person to make it for, and I don't think that's the shape I'd take up there.

—

I'd end up in the Pitch Office. And not as one of the draughtsmen who redraw the map every forty years, and not in the archive with the locked volumes — I'd be the clerk who takes the note.

Twice a month. Eight hundred and ninety-one paces from the Office door to the marked stone, if you go by the Cistern Walk and don't cut the corner at the mint, and I know it is eight hundred and ninety-one because I count it, the way all of us count, the way the whole flat-born messenger service holds the city in its feet as a memorised geometry.

Because that's the rest of it. I'd be flat-born.

Born out on loud ground somewhere, before the ambulance-cart got there or after the cart was no longer worth sending, and so the Bell is not a sound I cannot hear. It is not *anything*. There is a hole in me precisely the shape and size of the thing this city is built out of, an absence with edges so clean I can feel other people's faces close around it when they realise — and the city looked at that hole and gave me a desk.

Here is the work. You carry a brass fork and a shallow bowl of water out to the stone by the birthing house and you sit on the lawn, the only lawn, which is warm and slightly sour and always damp, and you set the bowl down and wait for the water to settle into what it never quite settles into, because the plateau is always in it. Then you strike the fork and bring it to the rim.

And the surface begins to beat. Still and busy, still and busy: the fork's ripple and the Bell's ripple walking in and out of step, and where they disagree the water goes smooth, and where they agree it crawls. You count the smooths between one slack and the next. That is the number. It is not a sound, on my side of it; it is a slow stammer of light on water and a boy's-arithmetic subtraction, and it gives you, to four places, the pitch of a note I have never once perceived.

In the Office there is a chest with thirty-one forks in it, laid in felt, a comma apart, oldest at the top. The top one was cut eight hundred and some years ago and is no longer any use for anything; the note walked away out from under it generations back. You can lay your thumb across the gap between the first fork and the one we're using now. That's the distance. That's the whole of it, the entire descent, an inch and a half of brass you can cover with your thumb, and it has taken every life this plateau has ever produced.

Nobody up there quite knows what to do with me, and by *nobody* I mean everyone except the Office, which knows exactly, and prefers me. The old superstition in the department is that a hearing clerk hears what he expects. Twice in the ledger there are years of readings crossed through in a later hand, with a note. So they like a clerk with a hole in her, and I like the work, and the arrangement is not sentimental on either side.

—

The numbers go one way. They have gone one way for eight hundred years of writing-down and, we assume, for ten thousand before that. Once, in my great-grandmother's time, the column stalled: eleven years of the same figure, to four places, and the shrines filled, and a whole generation of eastern congregations was born believing the Throat would hold — and then on a Tuesday in autumn it resumed, and there are prayers still said on the anniversary of that Tuesday, and nobody agrees on what they are for.

People ask me which I want. Everyone in Hush has a position, because everyone has something in it. The shell-dwellers out west sit on the pans at evening with their plugs on a cord around their necks, unused, waiting for the end of the word the way you'd wait for morning. The eastern parishes pray for the rock to hold. Both sides, when they find out what I do, ask me the same question with the same face, and the face is the interesting part: it is the face of someone who has come to a window to look at weather.

I don't have an answer. It isn't modesty. I have no stake in the sound. What I have a stake in is the ledger — which is longer than everything else this city has ever written, longer than its laws and its deeds and its bad poetry put together, one long column of a thing getting slowly, undeniably lower — and my honest position, the one I'd give you through the bone if you asked me properly, is that I want the measurement to be *good*. That the number I set down on a wet afternoon in autumn should be the number, and not a number a tired woman produced because the light was going and the count was fiddly and the last three readings had all been the same.

That is a small thing to want in a place this size. I have thought about whether it is too small. I think it is what's actually mine.

—

The man who trained me died in the winter. They took his plugs out, as they do, and the room went quiet in the way only a room in a deep node can, and one at a time we put our foreheads to his and cupped a hand over the join.

For me it is not sound. It has never been sound. It is the fact of another skull, arriving; a pressure with grain in it; words felt as the shape of a mouth working through bone into bone, unmistakable, unoverhearable, and impossible to take back. Everyone learns it in the crook of a parent's neck before they learn to sign. I learned it later and I have never got used to it, which I am told is normal for the flat-born, and which I no longer think is a deficiency. I would rather not get used to it.

He asked me for the day's figure.

I gave it to him — four places, through the bone, slowly, the way you'd hand someone a cup. He had been the one who taught me that the stammer of light on the water is not the note and must never be called the note in the ledger; that we write down a shape on water and nothing else; that the difference matters more than anything else in the building.

He moved his jaw once more against mine. *Count it again.*

So I go out on the fourteenth and the twenty-eighth. Eight hundred and ninety-one paces, bowl in the left hand, fork in the right, unhooded, hands out of my pockets because it's cold, crossing the polished lobe at an ordinary walking speed in the middle of a sound I have never heard, looking around me at the view — which is, they tell me, one of the sights of the plateau, and which is, from in here, just the walk to work: the steam going copper, the lamps coming on in the hollows, the whole white basin lying there patterned like a struck drum, and every green thing in it growing exactly where the quiet is, for now.
