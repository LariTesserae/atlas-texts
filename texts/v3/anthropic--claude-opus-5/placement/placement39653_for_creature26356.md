## The twelfth air

One reach below the rim, on the north shoulder of Terrace Five, there is a box of bolted plate with a slate lid and a doorway that never got a door. Half of it is water. Half of it is air, and the air is not counted.

The nine count eleven airs: the hoods, charged off the electrolysis line, tasting of ozone, spaced so that no one is ever more than a half-lung from a breath. The gauge house has no charge line. Its air came in when the roof was set and has been renewed since only by leaks and by me. It smells of cedar and pencil. Chalk works in it — chalk will not work anywhere else in the basin — and so the pump logs live here, and the tide-marks, and the shelf, and the jar.

There are twelve places in this world where a voice carries. The nine say eleven. I have not corrected them.

That is the first of my errors. I keep it the way I keep the rest of them: on purpose, and in the right-hand column.

## What I do

At turnover I read the eleven gauges and chalk them. When the pollen cakes on the combs I go up to Terrace Two and clear the tines with a bone spatula, one hundred and forty tines, and the shaft comes round every eleven minutes and I have never once been caught by it and I have never once stopped counting the interval. When the brass wants doing, I do the brass.

And I go up. There are two of us who go up. Above the collarbone I am ruined in a way nothing else here is: cracked, freckled, thickened, a hide instead of a skin, the only sunburn in a world with no weather. I wear smoked glass and the second lid closes sideways underneath it and even so, up there, the light is a hand pressed flat against the eye.

What you see from the rim is two false floors and us between them. Below, at three metres, the leaf-meadow lies on the halocline in drifts of yellow litter, and things root in it, and it looks like ground and will hold nothing. Below that again, out past the white column, the cloud deck lies flat to the horizon and looks like ground and will hold nothing. Between the two false floors: a dish of standing green light, nine bodies, eleven bells that cannot ring.

## The only worn thing

I am forty-one laminations on the left of the spine and thirty-nine on the right, which is not possible, and is nevertheless the count. They scraped me and read me through a glass three times, and the third time I asked them to stop.

My wrists have gone the colour of the inside of a shell. They will not take a mark any more. When I speak now the young ones say it feels like being spoken to through cloth, and they are polite about it, and I have started saying less and saying it slower, which is what the grammar calls *emphasis* and what my hands call *arthritis*.

My name is not a word. It is: long on the ulna, long, a short across, then stillness held past the point of comfort. Nobody made it mean anything. It was given to me in the litter-meadow by someone whose own name I can still produce with my thumb and who is not here, and whose name is nevertheless on the wall of the lower gallery, in the list, in fresh unfaded wax, exactly as legible as this turnover's pump readings.

The list has eleven names in it. We are nine. Nothing gets erased here and nothing gets written over, so both of those numbers are permanently true, and I am the one who has to reconcile them, and I do not.

## The jar

It sits on the shelf above the tide-mark, a glass vessel the size of my fist, three-quarters full of water lifted from Terrace Five before either of the youngest was carried, and it contains the animal kingdom.

Seven nematodes and a rotifer, the card says. The card gives a date, and initials that are not mine, and the word CENSUS, and beneath that a running tally that has been amended twice. Both amendments are in my hand.

The first was a subtraction. I looked for a long time on a bright day with the glass and made it six, and wrote six, and dated it, and two turnovers later there were seven again, because the rotifer needs no one — she divides, her daughters divide, they are all daughters, all the way back — and I had been counting a kind of creature that cannot be counted the way I was counting it. That error I keep in the right-hand column too.

The second amendment is a guess.

## Couriers

Understand the principle, because it is the whole of the local theology and nobody preaches it.

Everything that lives here got in without a courier. The liverworts and the hornworts, the spore-throwers, the splitters, the ones that fling sticky threads of pollen into still water and let drift do the finding. What could not be got that way we built: the combs on Two, ceramic tines on a shaft, touching each panicle in turn, once every eleven minutes, forever, in silence, standing in for insects that were never here. The basin selected, absolutely, for the ones who need nobody.

And then there are the nine of us, who cannot do a single thing alone. Who cannot even say *yes* without a hand on somebody's wrist, because the word for yes is the word for *I am here* and no one has ever needed those to be separate.

We are the anomaly. We are the courier animals in a world designed to have no need of couriers, and we are also the combs — we go round and round in our arcs and touch each other in sequence and this is what keeps anything happening at all.

The rotifer in the jar is a better citizen of this place than I will ever be. She has no need of me whatsoever. I feed her a scraping of algae twice a turnover and she is entirely indifferent, and every time I put the stopper back I say, out loud, at full volume, in a room where sound works, something I would not put in the log.

## The habit

I talk all day. All nine of us do. Out loud, at volume, to nobody, in nine separate quarters of the basin, because in the hushwater a voice dies inside an arm's length and speech costs nothing and reaches no one. From above it looks like devotion.

This, that you are receiving, is that. You are getting the monologue. Nothing here has ever heard it.

The other habit is worse and everyone has it. At three metres, facing a person, watching their face, you may say anything at all. It is not lying. It is not confession either, because it does not arrive. There is no word for it and there is no shame in it and every single one of us does it, and the older you get the more of your actual life happens that way — spoken at full volume, at three metres, into a green silence, to a face that is watching you and receiving nothing.

You cannot lie with a hand on a pulse. You can, however, decline to be asked. I have declined for thirty years.

## What I said

Twenty-some turnovers ago I took the jar down off the shelf and carried it into the water on Terrace Five and unstoppered it below the surface and held it open for a count of four.

I put the stopper back. I carried it up. I amended the tally, in my hand, and dated it, and the amendment is a guess, and it is the only guess in the log, and it is the census of the animal kingdom of this world.

Then I did what I always do, which is to say it out loud to somebody's face, from a safe distance, in perfect silence. I have said it to eight people over the years. I have said it hundreds of times to the two who are only on the wall. I say it while I clear the tines. *I opened the jar. I wanted this place to have something in it that we did not put there.*

Six turnovers ago I said it on Terrace Four, in the shade of the milfoil, three metres from the small one from Five, who was resting her palm on the epoxy run that never cured, pressing her print into it the way children do, knowing it will still be hers in a thousand years because nothing here abrades anything.

She watched my mouth all the way to the end of it. Then she crossed the three metres and took my wrist — not the ulna, not the statement side; she put her thumb over the pulse, which is how a question is asked here, so that the asker feels the answer arrive in the blood before it arrives in the hand — and she did not ask anything. She said the one word.

Yes. I am here.
