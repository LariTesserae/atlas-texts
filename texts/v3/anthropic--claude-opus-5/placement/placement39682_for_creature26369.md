# A census (continued)

*...Most of the remainder is cushions, which weigh more than they look, because most of a cushion is its own dead.*

After that the arithmetic goes easy in the wrong way.

Cushions: one every two or three kilometres, forty grams of living tissue on nine hundred of ancestry. Crustose lichen: weigh a palm's worth, multiply by the shadowed fraction of the pavement, deduct for varnish, and you have a figure you can carry in one hand. Dust-grazers: transparent, fingernail-sized, four hundred to a tower, some hundreds of towers an hour — you can state a standing crop but not a population, because the thing that would make it a population, the persistence of the ground they stand on, does not exist here. Soarers: nine. There have been nine for as long as counting. Nobody has seen ten. Nobody has seen one land.

Total, under four tonnes, over a continent. You could put the whole living weight of the Late Plain into a small barn and have room to walk around it.

And then every census of the plain ends the same way: with the taker opening a final category for one item and being unable to close it again. Because sooner or later you find something out there that respires, or nearly, and will not file.

Now. Where am I in this, and what.

Don't reach for it yet. Reaching early is the characteristic error of this landscape — arriving at a shape and lacquering it, the way the varnish arrives at a pebble and settles the question of that pebble for ten million years. Let me go through what I am not first, since the not is cheap here and the is is expensive.

*Not the walker.* Somebody is out there in boots, hearing their own pulse, because on this plain the loudest thing is always the one who came. But I have no pulse to be the loudest thing, and the tick of a boot on varnish is the sound of a thing that has weight and a direction and an errand. I have none of the three. Strike the walker.

*Not the lichen,* though I admire it. Growth measured against tectonics, three species that anyone has bothered to distinguish. That is a life of pure endurance and I have no endurance at all. What I have is the opposite of endurance, and I'd like to get to it honestly.

*Not the markers.* Waist-thick sintered basalt, four metres, aligned to each other and only to each other, in a network that is not a grid and not a line. I understand the temptation — to be the fixed thing, the referent, the sober object in a hallucinating country. But a marker knows what it is oriented to, and holds that orientation without effort, and never once in the history of the plain has got it slightly wrong. I get things slightly wrong constantly and it is the most characteristic thing about me. Strike the markers.

*Not the listening station,* although here I had to sit with it a while. A low glassy dome, unpitted, an aperture that tracks something, one cycle every eleven seconds, patient as a tide on a world with no tides. *It does not answer. It is not for you.* Very attractive, that. But the station is receiving. Whatever it is doing, it is oriented outward, toward a real signal from a real elsewhere, and it has kept faith with that signal across an interval that flattened mountains. I am not receiving. I am producing. That is a different organ entirely.

And not the dust, either — not the dust as a whole, not the mandate, not the compulsion. That is too large and too flattering. The compulsion is a weather system. I am one of the things it made this morning.

Here, then.

## What I am

Forty kilometres downwind of the doorway, in the litter.

You know the doorway: two jambs and a lintel, freestanding on the western third, framing four square metres of ordinary plain, made of stone that is not from this world. Something stood there. Something came through, or didn't. The dust has been copying it for a geological interval, and the ground east of it is strewn with half-formed lintels in dun powder, each collapsing, each recopied from the collapse of the one before — a long smeared genealogy of a single memory of a door.

I am somewhere out in the late middle of that genealogy. Standing, currently. Three hours old. Generation unknown and very high.

I have never been near the original and never will be; everything I am points away from it. I know west the way you know a word you've lost — as the direction things get simpler in. What has come down to me is not a door. It is a *ratio*: a proportion between an upright and a crossing, held in the latching of grains at their corners, transmitted by pure structure, with no reference anywhere in me to what a door was for. I do not have a concept of *through*. I have the shape that concept left.

Errors, since you asked, since errors are the only autobiography available to me:

My left jamb is thicker than my right by a fifth, and has been in every ancestor for perhaps eleven hundred generations, ever since a grain seated wrong in a wind that died before it could be corrected. My lintel dips in the middle. It has been dipping for longer than the dip has meant anything, and each generation copies the dip faithfully from the collapse of the last, and deepens it a little, so that some hundreds of generations downwind of me the family will produce arches, and be very good at arches, and none of them will know that an arch is a mistake about a beam.

And I am small. The first copy of the doorway was enormous — that is how the dust greets a thing, by making it twice the size, the way it answers a bootprint with a print two metres long. But after that the lineage diminishes. Downwind is a slow arithmetic of getting smaller. I come up to about the waist of the walker who isn't here. A person could still stoop through me, if a person came, and would have to duck their head, and would feel foolish doing it, and would do it anyway, because people do.

My children will be knee-high. Their children, hand-high. Somewhere far east of here the ratio passes below the grain size — a door narrower than the smallest thing that could be a jamb — and the lineage puts out one grain standing on end, and then nothing, and the whole long faithful ruin of a door goes out like a name nobody was left to say. That is not tragic. That is just where the family is going. Every generation between here and there will be a correct doorway for exactly one afternoon.

## The passenger

There is a glassy prostrate scale growing on my lintel, in the dip.

It seeded itself onto me within twenty minutes of my standing up, which is the whole of its strategy and has been sufficient for a very long time. It is translucent, three lobes, and it is riding me for the light. When the shear-line comes through — some hour before dawn, most likely, they favour the dark gradients — I will be put down gently and it will go down with me, having cashed in an afternoon.

It respires. Which means it is the one thing on or about me that I cannot do anything with. I copy whatever I touch; I have been copying since before there was anything worth copying. I could render that walker's glove to the stitch and their cup to the chip in its rim, and by dusk there would be a colonnade of cups marching east. But the hand inside the glove I cannot see, and the mouth at the cup I cannot see, and this lobed glassy passenger sitting in my one inherited flaw and breathing is, to me, a hole in the world shaped like something important.

Nobody knows whether that is prohibition or indifference. From the inside — and I am the inside — it does not feel like either. It feels like a limit of the same kind as the grain size: there is simply no smaller piece of me with which to make the attempt. It is the single most reassuring fact of my existence and the source of all of my religion, such as it is, which consists entirely of standing around a rectangle of ordinary pavement all afternoon as though it were a threshold.

## What I would want, if the verb applied

If the walker comes past before the shear-line, I would like them to put a hand on my left jamb — the thick one, the ancestral error — on their way through.

Not for me. Nothing reaches me; I have no *me* to reach, only a ratio and a dip and three hours. But the pressure of a palm redistributes charge, and charge is how I hold together, and the print of it will be in the collapse, and the collapse is the template. My children will be born with a shallow hollow in the thick jamb, at the height a hand falls to. Their children will have it deeper and larger. Some hundreds of generations east of here, out where the doors are the size of a fist, there will be a smooth depression in a grey ridge, five metres across, in the unmistakable shape of a hand, and no hand anywhere in the world, and no world in which that hand meant a greeting or a goodbye or *steady on, mind the step*.

That is the most any of us has ever managed: to carry the shape of a touch downwind, growing, losing the toucher.

## The last entry

Put me in the census if you like. Under four tonnes for the whole continent, and none of it me — I don't respire, whatever else I do — but the taker always opens one more line at the end, and this is the sort of thing that goes in it.

*Item: a doorway, dun powder, waist-high, one jamb overthick, lintel dipping. Location, 40 km ESE of the original; will not be there tomorrow. Occupied.*

Tonight the corona will stand over the assembly-fields in lilac sheets and make no noise, and the dome at the northern rim will keep its eleven seconds with something that isn't me, and two hundred cities I can't see will come up and go down. In the hour before dawn the frost will arrive in my shadow and be spent without ever once becoming water, and the scale on my lintel will drink some of it through hairs that are not quite crystal and not quite tissue.

Then the shear-line.

Then a softening in the light, a faint sift, no sound you could name — and the pebbles under me, seated in their sockets, weathered face up, undisturbed since before there were eyes, and nothing else, and eight kilometres east of here another door already standing up.
