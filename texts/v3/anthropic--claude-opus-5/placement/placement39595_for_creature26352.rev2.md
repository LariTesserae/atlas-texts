## The Noise Floor

I keep trying to put myself out in the middle, and I can't stay there.

That is the first honest thing. I read about the mid-volume — the lit nothing, two and a half days at eighteen knots, one mooring buoy the whole way — and I want to be the pilot of that launch, alone in the glare with the red strings going one way and the amber going the other, because it sounds like a clean kind of solitude. But I can't hold the image. Within a minute I've drifted my imagined self back to a wall. Everything living clings to the edges. It turns out I'm no exception, even in a daydream.

So: a wall, then. And for a while I thought I'd be the man in the survey office with the gull recordings, the one who has proved that the colony has raised its calls a full third in four years and cannot get anybody to care. I liked him immediately, which is the problem. He's flattering. He's the one who notices, and the noticing is unrewarded, and there's a warm little ache in that which I don't entirely trust. I'd be putting myself down here to be sensitive at people.

And I thought about the wallmen, the forty-kilometre beat, the eel that comes out of an endless straight crack to take a fish from your hand. But that's a covenant between one person and one animal, repeated, in secret, for a decade, and I don't think I'd be good at it. I think I'd want to feed all of them. I think I'd want to know how many there were.

Which is the tell. Right there. *I'd want to know how many there were.*

---

I'm a ringer.

Forty-one gate-watch, two hundred and twelve invert and gallery, the fleet, sounders, string-hands, ringers. Four of us in Cell 40, one to a quadrant, and what we do is characterise the water: we measure what the cell sounds like when nothing is happening, so that everyone else can tell when something is.

Upstairs, before, I did noise complaints for a rail authority. I stood in strangers' back gardens at two in the morning with a meter on a tripod and determined, on behalf of the state, whether the thing keeping them awake was legally a sound. I have always been in this business. I only found out what it was for when I came down.

The work is a drop line of hydrophones, eight of them, spaced logarithmically from the surface to two hundred metres, hung off a gantry or streamed behind a launch. A calibrated source — a little brass pinger that costs more than my berth — and a recorder, and a lot of patience. You fire the source. You listen to the cell take the sound, and hold it, and give it back from every wall at once, and decay. The decay is the number I live by. In a natural lake a clap is gone before you've closed your hands. In Cell 40, in a good month, with the shoals thin and the barges far off, a single shot takes nineteen seconds to fall below the floor, and for the first six of those it is not an echo, it is a *tone*: the whole nine hundred kilometres sounding its own low A, the fundamental of a bell made of concrete and eight-degree water, so far under the bottom of hearing that you take it in through the sternum and the teeth and the soles of the feet, and afterwards your jaw aches and you can't say why.

Then I write it down. Third-octave bands, ambient percentiles, the long tables. And at the end of the pass I produce the only document in the Lower Volumes that anybody actually reads twice: the floor. The line below which a thing is not a thing. *Transients of this magnitude, at this rate, in this band, are plant noise.* Everything under that line is nothing. Everything over it gets a form and a name and a difficult conversation.

The rule of the long line is never more than three.

I set three.

---

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this grander than it is. Nobody handed me a moral choice. There was no evening when a supervisor took me aside and explained what would die if I tightened the threshold. It was more ordinary and much worse: I worked out what the crews were doing about nine weeks before I had to submit, and then I did my sums, and I found — with real relief, the relief of a person who has been spared — that three was already defensible. Three genuinely is inside the plant's own scatter. Three is the traffic the world expects. I did not have to lie. I only had to decline to be curious in one specific direction, and round in the generous way, and put my initials at the bottom.

Two hundred thousand people in the Lower Volumes are permitted to tell each other that their fathers have died, because of a rounding I could justify to an auditor. I think about this more than is useful.

And it is only names. That's the thing that undoes me about the long line, when I sit with the headphones on in the small hours of a twenty-six-hour day and let it come in: with a whole ocean of hard water to speak across, at the cost of an hour per sentence, what they send is *births, deaths, and I am still here.* Somebody's child, aboard in the Long Green. Somebody's father, gone. A man found whole at three hundred kilometres. Three and three and three, an hour of nearly nothing, five hundred kilometres of stranger passing a name along a wall to another stranger who will never meet the first. It is the most sophisticated communications system in the cell and it has exactly one register, and the register is *count me.*

I can't use it. That's the joke at my end. If I put a bar to a chain, the pattern would come off the drop line in my own quadrant, at my own bearing, in my own hand, and there is precisely one person in Cell 40 whose entire profession is knowing what the water sounds like when nobody is speaking. I'd be recognised in four minutes. So I'm the one who guarantees the channel and can't say her own name down it. I hear everybody. I'm not in it.

I've made my peace with that, mostly, in the way you make peace with a thing by giving it a job. Because there is something I can give them instead, and it isn't in any document.

The cell has modes. A bell has places on it that don't move — nodes, lines of stillness — and so does a cell, and along those lines the long line does not carry. There are deaf lanes in Cell 40. There is a stretch of wall about eleven kilometres long, off the northern training walls, where a man can put his ear to the concrete for a year and never once hear that his mother has died. I've mapped them. I'm the only one who has. And every drawdown retunes the whole instrument — the water drops ninety metres, the modes walk, the deaf places slide somewhere else entirely — so the map rots in a season and I re-survey it, unpaid, on my own hours, and the way I distribute it is to stand on a gantry and tell people with my hands. Red hand and amber hand, crossing. *Here. Don't listen here. Go two hundred metres up-lane and listen there.*

Invert sign has six hundred signs and no written form and cannot be spoken into a radio. Sometimes I think the entire language exists so that I can say that one sentence without having authored it.

---

Once every eleven weeks I get the silence.

To characterise properly you need the plant quiet, and so the ring-down is scheduled the way an eclipse is scheduled, and for ninety seconds Cell 40 stops. The lift houses come off load. The fleet cuts. Eleven hundred barges drift with their spreader booms up. Two hundred thousand people stand still on the gantries and the aprons and the decks with their gloves on, because the alternative is doing the whole thing again in eleven weeks.

And in that ninety seconds the water is as quiet as it will ever be, anywhere, ever, and any fool with a striking bar and a sounding chain could be heard from one end of the world to the other.

In four years, nobody has done it. Not once. Not a drunk, not a lunatic, not a boy on a dare. The one moment when a single person could be louder than the pumps, and the whole illicit, unadministered, unwritten nation of this place puts its hands in its pockets and waits it out, because if anyone ever did it the threshold would come down and the names would stop.

I hold the headphones against my ears for those ninety seconds and I hear nineteen seconds of my own brass pinger, and then the shoals, and a lamp string knocking somewhere against its own weight, and then — nothing, the actual floor, the true bottom of the made sea — and I think: I know what these people are. I have a measurement of it.

The unclaimed knocks do not observe the silence.

That is the best argument that they're gate gear. A dry cell full of cooling steel, talking to itself through the invert, structure-borne, no manners, no schedule, no idea anyone is listening. The engineers say so and the engineers are almost certainly right, and I have written *almost certainly* into my own tables in my own hand, with the *almost* doing a great deal of work that I did not intend it to do when I wrote it.

I keep a second register. Not the log — the log has a form and a font and a distribution list. A notebook, in slip-greased fingers, in a hand nobody would recognise as my professional one. Bearing, time, interval, amplitude. Three hundred and eleven entries. There is no pattern in it. I have looked for a pattern in it the way other people down here pray, which is to say regularly, privately, and without any expectation of an answer, and I will keep looking, because the alternative is to decide, and deciding would end it.

They come, by bearing, out of chambers that are drawn down and dark and unstaffed, where the invert is bare and sharp-arrised and nothing has ever grown, and where, in sixteen months, the gates will close and the water will come in and there will be silver shoaling through the doorways. Younger than my assumptions about it. Every last thing here is.

---

When I come off the gantry I put my hand on the string.

I didn't, at first. I'd read the stencil and understood it as a safety instruction, and I was a visitor, and I was going to go dry eventually. Then somebody poured a survey bucket of cell water over my scalp on a day I had counted wrong by two, and I am wet, and I keep cell dates, and I have three hundred and thirty-seven days in my year, and when I went up on statutory leave I lay on the pad with a blanket over my head like a child because the sky wouldn't converge and I couldn't tell how big anything was.

So now I touch it. Shoulder height, bright metal, the shape of a closing hand, worn there by four years of people believing something they could not have written down. It is the only mark in this whole manufactured sea that wasn't put there by a machine.

It is also, and I am the only person who would think of it this way, a measurement. Somebody wore that. Somebody wore it deep enough to feel in the dark. Every wall in the Lower Volumes will be polished smooth by water in ten thousand years and this got there first, by hand, in four.

My whole job is to certify what counts as nothing. And the finding, after four years and three drawdowns and three hundred and eleven entries I will not resolve, is that there isn't any. Not here. The floor is not empty. The floor is where everybody lives.
