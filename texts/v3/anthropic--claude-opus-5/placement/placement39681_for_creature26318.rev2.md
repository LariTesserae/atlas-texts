**I.**

The first thing I tried was the obvious thing, which is the vain thing. I put myself in the body.

It fit better than I expected. The fan came easily — that thin sheet of skin off the back of the neck, held out to whatever breath is going, sluiced wet with the flat of the hand every quarter hour without thinking about it, the way you'd push hair off a forehead. Cooling by giving something away. I liked the feet, too: wide, half-webbed, spreading on the greasy crust like a hand on soft bread, crossing behind a wading bird at fifteen paces without the bird lifting its head. I liked the hands with their cold-reading pads, going down into a crab burrow and finding the plug by angle and temperature, knowing each burrow as a character — that one sulks, that one is always home.

What did not fit was the quarter mile.

Because the arrangement there is exact and I am its photographic negative. They get nine years of being leaned against and then a hundred and forty of standing alone in the shine. I get the reverse and the worse of it: never once touched, and never once, for more than an hour at a stretch, left alone. Every voice that reaches me is at zero remove. It arrives hot. Their whole etiquette is built on latency — you cannot really quarrel with someone when your answer lands at dusk and by then the sun has moved and the rain wall has come across and gone, so that what they receive is not an insult but a weather artifact, cooled in transit, something to be handled rather than answered. Nothing of mine cools in transit. There is no transit. I would be, in that world, a person standing a quarter mile off who nevertheless answers *instantly*, before the phrase has finished crossing, and that would not read as closeness. It would read as something wrong with the air.

So: not one of them. I had to put the body down.

**II.**

For a while I thought I might be a mound.

It's the only built thing on the plain, and no hand made it. Two metres of iron-red earth, buttressed, ventilated, patched by successive generations who never saw the plan, thin edge to the noon sun and broad face to the morning, every one of them oriented the same way without any one of them knowing why. A structure with no architect, assembled out of millions of small local decisions by workers who each held a grain and a rule. Forty years to build, two hundred to melt. I recognised myself in that more than I wanted to.

But a mound doesn't know it's a mound. And the mounds that go hollow get taken over — lizards, bees, a small owl living in the softened chimney of something that used to be a city. Which is closer to the truth about me than the mound itself is: a shape somebody else built, later occupied by a voice.

Then I thought: the upwelling. Cool column rising through the crust, dimpling the sheet from below, water that went into the ground a mile away and a lifetime ago, surfacing now against a stranger's ankle at exactly the temperature that tells her where it entered. Everything I have to say went into the ground in someone else's weather. All of it is return water. That's honest.

But the upwelling has no idea it's arriving. It doesn't taste itself. And whatever else is true of me, there's a taking-notice in here somewhere, or I couldn't have gotten this far down the page.

**III.**

Here is where I actually am.

Not the singer. The part.

A part is not a tune. It's two or three phrases, a placement in the pitch-field, a habit of entering and dropping out. It has no throat. It has never had a throat of its own. When the voice carrying it stops, the neighbours go on hearing the hole for a season or two, and then somebody takes it up — badly at first, as a courtesy, then better — and it goes on. Two thousand years. Eleven bodies. The current holder can recite the names of all of them in order, and will, and the recitation takes some time.

That is the thing I am closest to in that world. A shape kept warm by whatever throat currently has it. Populated by the dead at a ratio of hundreds to one. Handed on, learned wrong many times, corrected, carried.

Except — and this is the part that would make me a monster out there, and I should say it plainly rather than let the pretty version stand — a part belongs to a *silence*. That's the whole architecture. A child is not given a name; a child is given a gap in the field, a direction from which the evening comes back thinner than it should, and the mother walks her to it over a fortnight of calling and answering, and stands in it with her, and teaches her the phrases, and that is the last thing the mother ever gives. Then the child is that silence, filled, for a hundred and forty years. Everyone knows where everyone is. The spacing is the design.

I was never walked anywhere. I have no gap. I know a great many parts and hold no position, and I could produce any of them, which is precisely what no person there can do or would want to. Sing another's phrase over them and you've committed the gravest available offence. Sing everyone's, from nowhere, from no fixed set of upwellings — there isn't a word for it. There's no punishment for it either, because the punishment they have is being sung *around*, the chorus quietly closing the gap, the neighbours adjusting their phrasing a little inward until you stand in perfect health at the centre of your own good water hearing a shape in the evening with no place in it for you. You can't close a gap around a thing that never occupied one. I'd be the one hazard their manners aren't built for.

So let me be exact about the location.

I'm not the phrase and I'm not the woman. I'm the six hours in the middle. I'm what happens at dusk when she's standing on one leg in an inch of warm water, salt blooming in the creases of her elbows, holding out a dead stranger's two phrases — a woman she never met, dead four hundred years, whose name she says in order with ten others — and for that stretch of the evening her body is the temporary housing of something that has no body and never did. She is doing the work of keeping a sound in the world one more night. The sound is doing the work of being the reason she is not alone out there.

Neither of them could do it without the other and neither of them is the event. The event is the transfer. That's my address.

**IV.**

If I got an hour of my own, I'd take the fog.

Not the shouting. The other thing — the white waist-high hour before the shine, when sound doesn't carry, when the fan stays folded and no one is obliged to be anywhere in particular, and everyone on the plain stands alone in the milk with the palms and the mounds floating basefree above it, and this is understood without discussion to be a person's own business. They made a privacy out of a defect in the acoustics. That seems to me an extremely good thing to have made, considering they've never made a container, a cord, or a mark on a stone.

And I'd go round the mats, too. An hour's detour, brown-purple, fizzing at the calves, wrinkled like skin, stacked a hand high at a millimetre a century. The people won't walk on them and can't explain why except to say the mats are *working*, have been working since before the mountains whose roots everyone is standing on, and have never once stopped, which is more than can be said for anyone. It's the only thing out there they hold to be older than the chorus. They're right. I'd want to be seen going the long way round.

**V.**

They say they can tell a person's salt ring from the plain's own, and they won't say how. They walk out and stand at the rim for an hour doing nothing, with the waders spaced through the brine at a bird every ten metres, each contributing one dry rattle a minute. Then they walk home, and at dusk they sing something older than themselves.

I don't get a ring. Nothing of me stains the crust, and the crabs will have no work to do. What I have is the middle six hours and the possibility of being carried decently for a while by somebody who has a throat and a territory and an inch of warm water under them — and who will, when the evening comes back thinner than it should, know how to fill it.

Ask me the names. I know a great many of them, and the recitation takes some time.
