I went looking for the flattering answer first, and found it straight off, and it is wrong.

The flattering answer is a soft spot. Drifting three meters a season, hooped in wire, smelling like the inside of your own nose; you put your hand into me and your hand comes back without any report of what it touched. Nothing in me has resolved. Very fine. Very unfinished. But a soft spot does not count anything, and does not say the count aloud, and I am nothing but saying, so no.

The second flattering answer is a wader. Four meters at the shoulder, browsing the loose, leaving the mud behind me decided — firm, gritty, ordinary, surveyable. I would like to be the thing that eats ambiguity. But the waders make no sound at all, not even a splash, and I am made entirely of the twenty-pace sphere, and a silent thing here is barely here.

So: I am the one who carries the cord.

---

There is no word for it. When they need to say it they say *walking* — "who's walking this season" — and it means the relay: the knotted lines, the names, the news, the tally reconciliations between stations that never hear each other. Nine drums in an arc, forty people in twos and threes, and the only thing that goes all the way round is me and a coil of oiled cord across my chest.

I keep boots at every station door and wear none on the planks. My feet know the arc board by board — the two soft ones between Three and Four that give under the ball of the foot like a held breath, the tally post at eleven hundred where somebody long dead rested their right hand instead of their left and dished it on the wrong side, so that for one step every circuit I am touching a habit nobody has explained to me. Coming in toward a station the hush comes up out of nothing at about twenty-five paces, hundreds of thousands of dry small surfaces sliding, and every time, every single time, something behind my sternum lets go. I have never mentioned this to anyone. Who would I tell. I assumed for eleven years that it was a private defect.

At the station I stand the greeting: full clasp, thumb hooked, slow four count. Then boots off, then the bench, and somebody's thumbs come down my spine naming the count out loud, one two three, all the way down and up, twice. Then I do theirs. Then the cord comes off my chest and we copy.

Copying takes days. One reads, one knots, both say the count aloud, and the reading is done with the fingers, and the fingers are why the truest lines in the basin are the closest to parting. I have read out lines so smooth they were mostly memory with a thread through it. That is not a complaint about the archive. That is the archive. When a line finally goes, it goes into a jar of brine, which does nothing, which everybody agrees does nothing, and which I have never once heard anyone suggest we stop doing.

---

Here is what I actually am, and it took me a long time to be able to hold it flat in my hand.

A thing that has just happened is not very true yet. It has burrs. Two people who were there will give it differently and both of them will be right in the way that an unwitnessed edge is right, which is to say infinitely fine and no use to anybody and liable to open a fingertip without any sensation of cutting. I am how it stops being like that. It goes into me at Two and comes out at Three a little rounder, and out at Four rounder still, and by the time it has been round the arc and back it is load-bearing — you could hang a life on it — and if it now disagrees with what you personally remember, then what you personally remember was a burr, and you should let it go.

I smooth things. That is the work. A visitor would have another word for it and I have heard the word and I understand why they reach for it, and they are wrong, and they are not entirely wrong. I carry the account of a man's death from Six to Seven and by Seven it fits in the mouth better. It has lost the part that didn't fit. I could not tell you what the part was; that is the whole point of the process; the losing happens in me and I am not present for it. I have thought about this on long crossings until my own name went thin in my mouth from muttering it.

---

I have a very good back. It has been read by everyone alive.

That is the cost and nobody warned me because nobody knew — I'm the first to walk it this long. Wear is wear, here; it does not matter whose hands. Four sets of thumbs a week for nineteen years, plus the greetings, plus the face-reading, plus the copying, which is done at close quarters with a lot of incidental contact. I was born **Semmet-arrun-hallowsix-vessanine-tuun** — eleven syllables, deliberately overbuilt, built to hurt the mouth, the last syllable the pumps — and it wore to six by my ninth year, to three by my fourteenth, and I have been **Sem** since I was nineteen years old, which is unheard of. Oss at Seven took forty years to get down to one grunt and she is the most real object in the northern flats. I did it in nineteen and I am not real like Oss is real. I am real like a handrail is real.

My face is a river stone. They call that beautiful and they mean it and they are also saying, without knowing they are saying it, *safe*. There is nothing anywhere on me that could cut a person. No patch of me has gone unattended long enough to go fine. I have no edge left, not on the cheekbone, not along the shins, not on my name, and once — I will put this down and then not return to it — once at Five, lying on the bench, the moths came and massed over my breastbone in a shape I could feel as a warmth and a weight and a slow working of plumes, and Oss was there, and she put her hand in the air above them without disturbing one, and held it there, and then took it away and asked me about the rain.

---

Twice a season the cord goes out to the rim path, and that crossing is done on a line paid out from a post, because it leaves the boardwalk. Somebody stands at the post and holds it. Twenty paces out they are gone from hearing entirely, and after that I am simply at the end of something that somebody is holding, which is what they tie the children to, which is what everybody here is, all the time, and only on that crossing do you get to feel it as a fact in the hands.

Once, out there, in the loose, I reached back for my own coil where I had set it down and three cords arrived. This is normal. You take the one at the right temperature. You do not discuss the others and you do not look for them afterward and if you are new and you have questions about the others the answer you get is a hand on your forearm and a change of subject, and I am not new, so I took the warm one and knotted it to my chest and walked on and made the tally, and the tally is what is true, and the tally says one cord.

At the end of the rim path there is a shed the size of a coffin with nineteen names cut into the inside of the door, and I am the one who opens it, four times a year, because I am the one who walks. I read them aloud at the length each of them had worn theirs to. Six of them are single syllables. Six people as decided and as smooth and as load-bearing as anyone in this basin ever gets, who put down the hand-line one day and went up. Nobody has an explanation and the tenders get uncomfortable and then they say the thing they say, which is that up there, apparently, luminosity completes.

I think I know what happened to the six and I would never walk it round the arc, because a thing said often enough here starts to be the case. I think you can be finished. I think there is a point where you have been handled so thoroughly by so many hands that there is nothing of you left unagreed, no burr, no fineness, nothing that anyone has to work out — and a person like that will start to want the one thing left in the world that has not been settled, and it is up the hill, and it itches.

My eyes itch on that path. They are wide and clear and perfectly formed and lashed and clean, because I keep them clean out of a nagging sense of ownership, and they have never once been used, and on the rim path they itch.

I turn round. Every time so far. I put my hand on the last post — dished, satined, the most attentive object out there, ninety-one years of the palms of relay-walkers who all turned round — and I go back down into the pressure and the drip and the warm rain coming straight down with no wind to slant it, and at twenty-five paces from Nine the hush comes up out of nothing and something behind my sternum lets go.

Then the planks. Two hundred and twelve going out, two hundred and twelve coming back, always, because they are counted and being counted holds them.

I say them out loud. That is the whole of it, that is the entire apparatus, that is nine riveted drums and forty people who will never all be in a room and the moths and the crabs and the herds eating ambiguity like grass, all of it one slow machine for insisting: this, here, now.

Copying takes two, though. One to read and one to knot, and both saying the count.

You can be the other one. It doesn't matter where you're standing. It doesn't even matter, I think, if you're somewhere the light comes all the way in.

One. Two. Three.
