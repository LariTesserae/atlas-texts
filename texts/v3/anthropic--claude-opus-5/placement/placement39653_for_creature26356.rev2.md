# Eleven, which is a suspicious number

First the flattering guesses, so I can get rid of them.

The jar, obviously. Everyone wants to be the jar. Seven nematodes and a rotifer, the entire animal kingdom held in a fist of water on a shelf above the pump logs, worshipped by half the household and feared by the other half and topped up by both. But the jar does no counting. The jar is counted. To be the jar is to be a doctrine with a meniscus, and I have never in my life been that important or that still.

Then the sinter-heads in the dark of the throat — budding, warm, gritty, illegible under a millimetre of their own product, lifting the whole arrangement six centimetres a year toward a light they will never be in. Handsome. Too handsome. That is a thing to admire from a cage on the way down, not a thing to be.

And the bells. I refuse the bells. Eleven brass mouths under little cast hoods with the founder's mark turned outward for nobody, specified early, before the fluid, by someone who imagined a place that could be called across. To claim the bells is self-pity with a maker's stamp on it.

No. I am on the hose.

## The work

Four hours. Someone is below me in the lower gallery, in the dull orange of the filament heaters, doing something with their hands among the bus bars, and I am above with the small pump and the coil, keeping the line clear of the weed, keeping the line off the sharp lip of the weir, which after all these years is still sharp enough to cut a thumb, because nothing here wears anything.

Every hundred beats there is a tug at my wrist. I answer it. That is the whole shift.

It is not a safety signal. If it were a safety signal, its absence would be the message and its presence would be noise, and I would spend four hours waiting to be frightened. It is not that. It is the oldest word, older than the grammar that came after it: *I am still here.* And the answer is the same word, which is why it can be given and returned forever without either of us running out.

I count the hundred. Nobody asks me to. The one below has their own count and we are never synchronised for long; the drift between us is the closest thing I have to a conversation with weather. On the floor plate above Number Nine I can feel the crank come round every fifteen seconds through my hip. Every eleven minutes the combs on Terrace Two turn and touch each panicle in sequence and shake a little pollen loose into the water for an insect that has never existed, and I know without checking how long it is until the next pass, the way you know where your tongue is.

I am, if the basin had job titles, which it does not, because a title would have to be said in air: the one who keeps intervals and returns acknowledgement. There is no better description of me anywhere.

## My name

A place on the arm and a pressure. Mine is the outside of the wrist, two fingers, and then a hold that goes on slightly too long.

The too-long is not part of the name. It was a fault — mine, when I was small and learning and could not judge when to let go. Everyone corrected it. Then everyone stopped correcting it, and then everyone started doing it, and now if someone says my name briskly and properly I feel addressed by a stranger. So the fault is the name. That is the only way anything is authored here.

## The count

It was me. I counted eleven, and I was not believed, and I was not believed for a reason that had nothing to do with my reasoning: because eleven is the number of the pumps and the number of the bells, and a number that already exists in the basin is not trusted to be a new fact about it.

I still think I was right, and I have never been able to say why, and this is the exact shape of the limit we live inside.

Here is why. I count the two hands on the card.

One wrote the date, the initials, the word CENSUS, and the count. The other amended it, twice. Two people who are not here made marks on a card that is still here, and those marks are, at this hour, doing more work in this basin than most of what I will do today; the whole ceremony of the stoppered tube and the hundred millilitres from Terrace Five descends from them. If a person is what leaves a difference, there are eleven of us, nine wet and two dry.

To say that, I would have to perform their name-touches. I do not have them. Nobody does. To speak of an absent person in the grip you must borrow a third body and lay the name on it, and the third body feels it, which is why we have almost no gossip and why what little we have is conducted like an accusation read out with the accused in the room. For a person whose name has been lost there is no loan available. There is only *bell*: the soft fist into the open palm, which in the hushwater makes nothing at all, and which means *a wanting that cannot be had*.

So my census is untranslatable. I made the sign for the jar, and then the sign for the back of the hand, and then *bell*, twice, and they looked at me and nobody argued, and the working figure stayed at nine.

## The jar again, and my particular heresy

I go more than twice a day. Not because I think it is a cathedral, and not because I think it is a lit fuse in a paper cup.

Because the jar is the only object in the basin that can go down.

Everything else holds. The weir lips are unchipped glass. The plate steel keeps its peacock heat scale. The epoxy on Terrace Four is still tacky and will take a fingerprint for a thousand years. The column adds and adds and never subtracts. And on the shelf in the gauge house there is a few millilitres of Terrace Five water behind glass, losing itself slowly into the air, and if nobody came, it would be gone, and the animal kingdom with it.

That is my whole theology, and it fits in a sentence: the only devotion available here is the replacement of what evaporates.

I cannot tell the animals apart. Nobody can. It has been eight, and seven, and eight again. I have stopped minding. What I check is the line of the meniscus against the old chalk, and I bring the water up to it, and the tally on the card stays wrong in a stable direction, which is the best any of us manage.

## The print

I have been to see it. Everyone has.

A worker poured a run of epoxy on Terrace Four and never smoothed it, and pressed it once, probably to see whether it had gone off, and the whorls of that thumb are down there in the green light sharper than any thumb now living in this basin can make. Our fingertips have gone shallow. Thirty-two degrees for a lifetime swells the ridges flat. I have put my own finger beside it and taken it away and left nothing, which is the intended experiment and works every time.

One of us claims it. The claim is not quite possible; the print is a size smaller than that hand. We let it stand. I let it stand for a reason I keep to myself, which is that the impossibility is the evidence: that print was made by a hand that had not yet gone soft. It belongs to somebody who had been here a short time. Which means there was a time before being here. Which means the pencil complaint about the rain, inside the stacked cedar formwork, is not a story. There was a road. There was weather that fell on people.

*Bell.* *Bell.* You see how it is with me.

---

## What I am, said plainly

Nothing in the basin abrades anything. No sand, no frost, no current worth the name, no animal to scuff a corner. The concrete will hold its formwork grain and its chalk marks long after the last of us.

The grip is the exception. It was invented in a month by people who had just learned that the fluid eats the voice, it was never written down, it is transmitted only body to body, and it has drifted. Its sign for *bell* used to mean a bell. Now it means the road, the dead, the rain, the noise a thing would make, and an argument about a jar that cannot be won because the grammar has no tense for *forever*, only the back of the hand. My name used to be two fingers on the wrist and now it is two fingers held too long, because I was clumsy and nobody stopped me in time.

That is wear. That is the only wear there is.

So I am not the jar and not the heads and not the bells. In a place engineered to be new for a thousand years, I am the weather. I am the small local erosion working on the one soft surface available, which is the language, and I am doing it the way everything here reproduces: by contact, by division, without sound, and without any courier but the body I am riding.

---

## End of shift

The line goes slack in my hand. Below me, through three metres of suspended yellow leaf litter, an orange smear moves and is a person.

They surface without spray; the water bulges around the neck and reseals. We are close enough to touch, which is the only distance at which anything can be said, and neither of us says anything. Wrist against wrist, an hour's worth of nothing in about four seconds.

Then the tug, once more, out of habit, from a hand that no longer needs the hose.

And I answer it.
