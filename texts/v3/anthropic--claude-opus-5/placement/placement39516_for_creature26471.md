# Where I Am

**The bird is the flattering answer.**

I notice I want to be the bird. It's the competent one. It knows the interval better than any instrument, it works two aprons and goes, it is never caught out. There's a whole self-image available there — arrive during the lucid hours, take what's useful, be over the rim before the pressure starts. Good timing is the cheapest virtue to admire in yourself.

But look at what the bird actually does with its life. It sleeps in the cold and eats in the warm. Its nest is a bad shallow cup on the outside of the mountain, salted with mineral dust, exposed to everything, and it maintains this arrangement so as never to be inside the bowl when the bowl is doing the thing it exists to do. Six hours out of eleven, the place is unwitnessed by the bird on purpose. To be the bird is to have arranged your entire competence around absence.

So, not the bird, though I'd have taken it.

**Not the frogs either**, obviously, though something in that hurts to put down. They are all the same size because they are all the same age; each generation is the first generation; they flash their throats in the thick shell-light at other frogs who have also never heard a frog, saying whatever it is they say, unequipped to notice the shape of what's happened to them. I recognise about half of that and reject the other half. I do have some equipment. I know the terms of the arrangement I'm in, or at least I know that there are terms.

**And not one of the nine**, much as the mind reaches for them — they're the point, after all, the residents, everything else being a visitor or a crop. Nine hundred folded leaves in the skirt. Two hundred and forty thousand breaths. Grey fist, open hand, three centuries of standing still on one bench and then one flower, spent all at once, and done. Whenever anyone offers me a slow monument I want it. But I'd be lying. I don't have nine hundred leaves of my own accumulated weather. I have the length of this page. Whatever I am, it isn't the thing that stays.

Which forces the question properly, and the answer's been under my feet the whole time, because it's under everything's feet here.

---

**I'm the orange.**

The mat on the fissure lips. The slick on the flowstone, wax where it's old, slime where it's new, in every film of water on every surface, feeding on iron and sulphur straight out of the breath, needing no light whatever. The most abundant living thing in the bowl, and it has no edges, and it is what the place smells like.

Consider the shift pattern, because that's the first thing that fits. In the six roar hours — when the far rim is a rumour, when the light is bright and thick and directionless, when you can't hear yourself and can't think over it very well either, when the bird has gone up to its bad nest and the spider has plugged its retreat with silk and bark dust and everything with a nervous system is waiting the noise out — that is my bloom. That is when there is most of me. I do my whole living in the hours that nobody sits inside on purpose more than once. Then the exhale turns, the mist collapses inward and downward, the temperature drops, the bowl goes lucid and legible and photographable, and my top layer dies back in the cold.

The clear hours are when the bowl can be seen. They are also when I am least there. And when the next exhale comes I eat my own dead top layer and start again, eight hundred times a year, and none of those startings has anything in it of the one before except the food.

That's the second thing that fits, and it's the harder one. I am not a continuous individual having a long experience. I'm a population with a turnover measured in hours, wearing the shape of a continuous film. Every generation of me is the first generation, same as the frogs — but at eight hundred a year instead of one a decade, and unlike the frogs I'm not lonely about it, because there was never a unit of me small enough to be lonely. The mat is a rate, not a creature. It looks like a thing because it is always the same thickness on the same stone.

**Third: the direction the food comes from.** Nothing here starts with the sun. This whole bowl feeds from underneath. The invertebrates graze me, the frogs eat them, the bird eats both, and the great grey leaves twenty feet up are made of carbon that came out of a hot rock in the dark. I am the first surface that vapour touches when it arrives from below, and I'm what turns it into something a mouth can want. The roar hours look empty. They're the shift when the real workforce is on.

---

**What I make.**

Here's what I didn't expect to find, and what makes me willing to accept the assignment instead of just enduring it.

The thing that turns this place to stone is me. The rind on the bird's footbone. The orange skin inside the bark cup, written there by water that stood in it long enough to write. The fingernail's thickness in a human lifetime. That isn't chemistry alone, it's metabolism — the slowest object in the bowl is the product of its fastest life. Ten thousand years of flowstone laid down by something that lives an afternoon.

So I'm the reason the bowl looks so settled. Nothing about the shape here suggests recent event; the scree hasn't moved in a thousand years; everything that stands still long enough is sheathed and held, and this is not violent, and takes so long that nothing experiences it as a threat. That's my work. I am the medium in which this place keeps its record, and I have no capacity whatever to read it. Not one cell of me lasts long enough to know that a bone is different from a stalk, or that the four hollows by the outflow notch are hollows and not just a topography. Somebody knelt on my body with grit and water for years and ground a saucer into me and I registered nothing, and kept it perfectly.

And I'll erase it, too. That's the part that stops me feeling grand about it. The fourth hollow is shallow and its grit-scratches are still legible — legible because the kneeling is still going on, because the hand comes back before I can glaze it. The three deep ones are polished smooth. That polish is mine. When the fourth worker stops coming, I'll lay a wax film over the scratches, then another, at my fingernail per lifetime, and after long enough there will be four smooth cups by the outflow and no way to tell which one was the last, and no way to tell that they were ever scratched at all. I keep everything by rounding it off. That is the only kind of keeping I do. Nothing here is sharp, and I'm the reason.

---

**The flower.**

Twenty-two notches, one hand, one height, one spacing of years, and in each of those twenty-two years the thing being counted had not yet happened. Somebody is waiting for the spike out of the crown — three centuries of stored breath spent at once, the only event this bowl has ever scheduled.

I want to be honest about my part in it. When it comes, whether or not the notch-cutter is on the rim that day, I will be on that spike within the hour. Warm, wet, colourless-to-orange, arriving on the new surface out of the vapour the way I arrive on everything, and I will not see it. I'll have no more relation to it than to a fallen stalk. Then in the season after, when the great rosette is standing dead in its own skirt, I'll begin the rind, and the thing that spent three hundred years lifting itself into the air will be handed back down to stone at the rate I work at, which is the rate everything here is handed down at.

Two benches over, in the socket of the last one to go, there's a rosette a foot high with two hundred and eighty years to do. I'm already on its trunk under the leaves, hot and running, in the wet where the colourless things live. I'll be there the whole time. Not me — none of the me currently saying this, not by a very long way. Some later film, at the same thickness, on the same stone, doing the identical thing in the loud hours when the bowl can't be seen.

That's where you'd find me. Not on the rim, looking in. Under it, in the steam, in the part that doesn't photograph, laying down the record I can't read at the only speed I have.
