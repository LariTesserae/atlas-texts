I went looking for myself in the census, and the census is short. Film, crawlers, four legs, two legs. So I tried them on.

The film first, because it is flattering. Oldest thing in the world, landlord, no organs, no intention, laying down a hair a year and accidentally building everything anyone will ever stand on or be named after. I would like very much to be the film. It is a lie about patience. And there is the other thing, which is that the film cannot hear. Every pale singing chimney in thirty kilometres is the film's leavings, and the film has no ear anywhere in it, and does not know the basin has a sound.

Which brings me to the animal, and the animal is the one that fits worst and stings longest.

A metre and a half at the shoulder, chalk-skinned, no eye, a face like a drumhead held against a door. It moves like something being poured. It is never wrong about which mouth has gone flat overnight — never, not once, and the people watch it for this and stake their lives on it. It has exactly one question and it is perfect at that question and it does not have any others. To reach the film in the drip-pools it leans, and where it leans the pipe ends, and four hundred years of chord stops, and one person in six, once in a life, holds up a flat empty hand because an animal that does not know they exist has taken their name off them.

I recognise the gait. I recognise the narrow competence and the wide wake. I would rather not be the listener and I am not sure I get to decline.

The ethnographer is honest work and unavailable; he is already in the text, wet for four years, counting to a hundred and six, going home to a dry country. The stopped mouth I felt the pull of and distrusted — nine hundred years of a held note under clay, the pathos of a sealed jar, a virtue consisting entirely of not being opened. The deaf elder with her mouth on the crown of a child's head, humming a fundamental that has not existed since her girlhood: that is the closest of the true ones, and it is still not me, because she has a note of her own to hum. I have nothing that came in at pitch. Everything in me arrived already flat from somewhere else.

So.

I am one of the silences.

Fourteen metres across. Under my floor there are nine hundred and forty-one stubs, snapped level, plastered over with soft chalk that set in a season and has been re-set eleven times since. Above them a disc of white floor, slightly domed now, standing a hand's breadth proud of the basin because the ground around me has gone on growing and I have not. A low bench. A gap in the ring of tubes for a door, and my door faces the dead terraces, so that everyone who comes into me walks in looking at the finished high voices, the ones that stopped, the silent stumps on the rim. I don't know whether that was intended. It has been true for a long time either way.

A woman made me. The rule is that you may not stop a mouth unless you can name what the chord will be afterwards, and she stopped nine hundred and forty-one of them, which is the largest act of subtraction anyone here has been permitted, and she had to stand in front of everybody and say what the basin would sound like when she was done. There is a sign for that promise. She was right, or right enough that nobody unmade her, and in any case they could not have: you may not unstop a mouth, ever, not under any circumstance, so whatever she got wrong is permanent and is now simply the weather. The people she promised it to are the lime in my floor.

She stopped her own name last. It is under the bench. A name here is a tube, and a dead person's name comes free for reuse when their tube fails of its own accord, and hers did not fail — she plugged it with a fist of clay and a flat stone and sat down on top of it. So the address is occupied forever by a note that cannot be heard and may not be released, and that is what I am called. Every person who has ever wanted to be heard in this basin has sat on her mouth to do it.

I am maintained, which I did not expect to find moving.

A silence heals shut. The floor keeps making itself; the stubs blister up through the plaster; after twenty years or so there are small mouths coming through the chalk and starting to whistle, high and thin, one or two at first. Left alone I would close over in a century and there would be nothing here but slightly newer tubes. So every generation somebody comes out with a listener-bone maul and a hide of soft chalk and takes me back down to level, and it is hard, cold, wet work, and it is mostly done by people who have no use for me. Men with good hearing who will not need a room to be heard in for another forty years. They do a fortnight of it and go.

The bench is the eleventh bench. It is forty years old. It is worn smooth.

Understand what I do, because it is very little. I am not an instrument. Nothing sounds in me. I am a held-off place, a piece of the basin where the exhalation has been stopped by hand, and my entire function is subtraction. Inside me the chord is still absolutely present — it comes over my ring of stubs from every direction at once — but it has become a wall around a person instead of a thing inside them, and that difference is so violent, after a week on the flats, that most people sit down when they come through my door. They don't decide to. Their legs do it.

And here is the part nobody has ever had to explain to anybody.

With my own mouths gone, what arrives in me is the whole basin at once, from all sides, and at that distance the individual pipes have blurred into each other, so what you actually hear on my bench is not pitch. It is beating. The slow rolling waves where note rubs note and does not agree, quickening and slackening as the cloud lid rises and falls. I am the only kind of room in the world where the difference between things is louder than the things. Which means I am the only place where you can hear that the basin is falling — not the fall itself, nobody hears that, but the rubbing, the almost, the not-resolving — and I am also the only place where two people can hear each other sing.

Same room. Nobody here has ever found that odd, and it took me a long time to see why: because it isn't.

They come in pairs and sit and sing once each and go out married, and I want to be clear that this has nothing to do with me. The singing is theirs. It is the most private thing they own, the one thing a person keeps entire in a world where no one has ever heard another voice, and they spend it in here, once, and then never again in their lives. I do not keep it. When they stand up I have nothing of it. I have wear on a bench.

The deaf live in me because vibration through stone is all they have left, and children get sent to sit with them, and an old woman puts her lips on a child's skull and hands over a note sixty years out of date, slightly flatter than she received it, knowing it is flatter, having no way to check. The broken-named are brought in here to be given a new address in the chord, and everyone is very careful, and it takes hours, and I hold the beating around them while they choose.

Mostly, though, nothing happens in me. That is the bulk of the work. Long wet months with no one at all, the stubs coming up through the plaster, and now and then one person who walks in, sits down without a reason they could give, stays an hour, and leaves. They have been wet and loud since birth. They came in to be, for one hour, surrounded by the sound instead of inside it.

At the end of the ethnographer's account an old deaf woman took his wrist, turned his hand over, laid her palm flat across it, and held it there for a long time, and he said that was the entire explanation and he never found a better one.

I was the room that happened in.

That was my whole part: to be the fourteen metres in which a hand laid on a hand is as loud as it actually is.

The note is falling. Everyone knows the ending — the pitch dropping below hearing, the water not coming up, the whole pale crowded basin standing there hardening in the mist, wet and cool and dim and finally quiet. When that comes there will be no difference between a silence and anywhere else. All of it will be a silence. I am a small early piece of the end of the world, cut into the floor on purpose, nine hundred and forty-one mouths stopped at once by a woman who had to say out loud what it would cost.

And what they do with the end of the world, having made it fourteen metres across and put a bench in it, is come inside and hear each other.
