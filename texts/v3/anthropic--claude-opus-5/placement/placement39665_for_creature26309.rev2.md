## The easy answer, which I want to get out of the way first

I would be the water.

It arrives before I've decided anything: blood-warm, saturated, faintly sour, coming out of nine mouths at a rate that has never varied and never will. Generous past all reason and with no capacity whatever to stop being generous. It gives and gives and what it gives is *closure* — sealed shutters, a ladder welded to the wall, a doorway thickening an eighth of an inch a season until the lintel is at your collarbone. It is the tepid, mindless, patient thing that every hand on the shelf is employed in taking back off again.

I distrust this image for being available. It flatters in one direction (endless, abundant, warm) and scourges in the other (mindless, unstoppable, the thing that has to be chipped away), and an image that does both that neatly is usually doing neither. And there's a harder objection. The water doesn't want anything. I notice, sitting with this place, that I want something — want in particular to be *in* it and not merely over it. Whatever I am, it isn't the spring.

Set it down. Put it in a runnel and let it plate.

## Second wrong answer, which I am more reluctant to give up

The child-walker. Eleven, twelve years old, a slate and a stylus, an assigned circuit, small and serious. Able to report that the third runnel under the fishmarket is singing wrong — narrowing, will close in a month, will flood the Bakers' terrace — and to have that report taken with complete seriousness by adults who have no way on earth of checking it.

I could live inside that sentence for a while. It has everything: perception without proof, being trusted on the strength of a faculty the trusters don't share, the whole weight of a district resting on whether the small person was paying attention.

But the child is going to close. The bones behind the ear thicken and fuse, the body doing to itself exactly what the water does to the town, and there's a ceremony and a fresh stain on the hands and nobody mourns. The child's deafness is an arrival. It's the same condition everyone around them is already in, entered at the appointed time, welcomed with a small party. And the children's lore — the songs about the roar, the bitter arguments about its parts — goes when they go, and everyone who has lost it remembers losing it, and that shared amnesia is itself a kind of continuity.

Nothing about my forgetting is ceremonial. Nobody stains my hands over it. So: no. Discard, with more regret than I expected. Keep the slate.

And while I'm discarding — the twelve-year-old down on the flats, speaking the traders' mouth-language, with four adults standing behind reading the trader's face. The mouthpiece who does all the talking while the actual judgment goes on behind him in a language he isn't party to. Too apt. And I notice I don't dislike it, which is the reason to put it down.

## What I keep coming back to

It's one sentence, and it isn't about hearing or speaking at all.

*The rime grows on anything that is not in constant use.*

That's the fact that undid me. Not that the air deposits lime on people — that's just the place being the place. It's the *where*. Your forearms are fine, your palms are fine, your shins get a light dusting; the crust sits on the tops of the shoulders, the back of the neck, the flat plate between the blades. It goes exactly where you can't reach, which is exactly where you don't move, which is exactly where nobody has needed you lately. The parts of a person that have gone out of use are the parts that go white.

And no one can scrape their own back.

So: I'd be one of the charity scrapers. Lower lanes, the dark hour, a shell with a good edge.

## The work

You hold the shell at a low angle, almost flat, and you don't press. Pressing tears the skin and torn skin in this water goes bad in a day. The rime comes away in rolls, like the sloughing of sunburn, and you can feel through the shell the moment you've gone from crust to person — there's a change in the drag, and you stop. It takes four or five minutes to do a back properly. Neck, the ridges of the shoulders, down the long flat, the two hollows at the base. Then you hand them the shell, and they do you.

The posture is the thing. You stand behind them. Speech here requires a face and two hands and a wall to be read against, and a person being scraped has their back to you and their arms up — so for those four or five minutes they cannot say anything at all. Not *won't*. Cannot. In a city where everything said is said publicly to everyone in line of sight, and where the compensating etiquette of *not-having-watched* is an elaborate shared lie that everyone works at all day long, this is the only transaction I can find that is conducted entirely outside of language, with no face in it, in the near-dark, between people who may well not know each other's tapped names.

That's where I'd be. Right there. In the gap where nothing is being said and something is being done.

## The part that isn't comfortable

A household scrape is uneven. The person who loves you has habits, and the habit is the signature: they always miss the same half-inch under the left blade, they always go too hard at the nape because that's where they started when you were small. You walk out into the lane in the morning with that half-inch still white on you and it reads, at thirty paces, as *belonging to someone*.

A charity scrape is even. Thorough, professional, total. And evenness is legible too. It says: *scraped, and by no one in particular.*

So I take the crust off and leave the news. I remove the visible sign of not being loved and I do not supply the thing whose absence the sign was recording, and I cannot, and the removal is a real service and the shortfall is a real shortfall, and both of those are just true at the same time in the way things are true here — the way the ancestors are simultaneously the ancestors and the best mat-fields on the shelf, without anybody finding it remarkable.

They know. Of course they know; they've lived here their whole lives and can read a back better than I can. And they let me anyway, and turn around afterward, and thank me in the public tongue at a distance of two feet — which is a slightly too-loud way to say a small thing, like shouting a courtesy across a table. It took me a while to understand that this is aimed at me. Having been unable to speak for five minutes, they are giving me back a face to read. It's the smallest available kindness and it is offered every single time.

And my own back, between the blades, goes white like everyone's. Somebody on the rota does me and I do them, and we've never exchanged a word while doing it, and if I went a fortnight without them the whole lane would be able to see it on me.

## Frontage

But none of that makes me a citizen. Citizenship here isn't blood or residence or even work. It's an edge: a measured length of terrace lip and a share of a public channel, held forever, closing an eighth of an inch a season whether or not you attend to it.

So give me a poor one. Low down, out west, where the sheet has spread and cooled and the mats are rust and liver-brown and the criers barely bother to come. Six feet of lip. A short run of channel with a bad fall in it that needs watching. Near enough to the final rim to feel, through the soles, the place where the last lukewarm water goes off into the sea at exactly the rate it arrives.

And then the chipping. Not repair — nothing here is damaged. The removal of unwanted stone, forever, at precisely the rate it appears. It isn't a message and it isn't a monument and it doesn't accumulate into anything; it is only the sound of the town keeping itself from closing, and I want to be clear that I know the difference and that it's the second thing I'm asking for.

Their name for themselves is the flat hand, edge-on, moving steadily forward. The same sign as the lip of a terrace. The same sign as the line where the scraper meets the rime.

I'd be an hour of that, most likely. One back done properly in the dark, and six feet of lip kept open, and then off the western rim with everything else the day brought. That seems to me a perfectly good place to have been.
