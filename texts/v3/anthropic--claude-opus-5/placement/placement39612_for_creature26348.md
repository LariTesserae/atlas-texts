## Give

I am kneeling at a terrace boundary with a length of bronze pipe across my thighs, and my whole trade is guessing how far apart these two streets are going to get.

Sit. There is warmth in the sill and the wall behind you is south-facing, so lean on the pipe bundle instead, not the stone. Yes. You came up wheezing. Everyone does. Don't talk yet.

—

Sev, born at six-forty-one. The works above us are at eight-twelve this quarter, and by the time your throat stops hurting they will be higher, so don't bother writing the number down.

Loopsmith on the documents. In the lanes we are called the give.

That is the word. Not slack — slack is a fault, a loop hanging lazy and breeding salt fur in its belly. *Give* is what a loop has. How much give, and where, and in what direction. The survey hands me two benchmarks and a rate and a probable drift, and I go away and think, and then I bend metal into a shape that is an argument about how much difference there is going to be between one part of the world and another.

Every other trade here maintains. The drapers cut the flowstone back off a doorway and it grows again and they cut it again until they are old, and that is honourable, and it is also just standing in a river. The levellers go under a bakery on their bellies and raise its northeast corner two millimetres, and next year two more. They think in the correct units and so they rule us, and they earn it.

Mine is the only work on this mountain that is finished before the thing it is for has happened.

—

The ordinance says nothing may be joined permanently to two different terraces. It is the soundest law we have. Two terraces are two different futures; tie them hard and one of them will tear the other in half, and it will happen at three in the morning with a crack you hear four lanes off, and then the counting, and then someone going to find out where.

So I am the licensed exception. I join what may not be joined, and it is legal because it is loose.

Bronze, mostly. Lead where the run is cold and short. You warm it, you bend it over a form, you anneal it in a vent chamber for two days because a hurried bend remembers being hurried and fails at the memory. Then you hang it, and it hangs, and nobody looks at it again.

You cannot test give. That is the thing I would want you to carry back down with you, if you carry anything. A loop that was calculated perfectly and a loop that was never called upon are the same object. They hang the same. They drip the same. Only the torn ones teach you anything, and they teach you about somebody else's hands, and mostly those hands are downhill and sitting in a dry room with a splinter between their teeth.

—

My mother was give. Born four-eighty-one. She is in the quarter that dried at about five-ninety, in her own former front room, on a floor that will never be jacked again, with seven stones on a wire in her lap.

I have three. They are small. She would have found that funny in her slow way — the pause first, four or five seconds of it, then the noise she made instead of laughing.

I went down once. Not for her. A surveyor wanted an old benchmark read and I had the lungs for it and the excuse, and I walked two hundred years of neighbours to get there, and everyone up here let me not talk about it afterward, and I drank a great deal of water, and here I am talking about it to a flatlander because you will take it away with you and I will not have to keep it.

Her loops are still hanging.

That is what I found. Not her — I did not go to the house, I want that clear, I did not go to the house. But in the lane above the benchmark there was a bronze run crossing a boundary with a bend in it I knew the way you know a voice, because she rolled the last centimetre of a bend with her thumb and left the mark of it, and there it was, unweathered, dry, immaculate, holding open a generous allowance of give for a divergence that stopped forever the day the fog collar moved below that street.

She spent her life calculating differences. Most of her answers will never be checked. They will hang there being right or wrong, unentered, in perfect condition, for as long as this world lasts.

So will mine.

—

Do not mistake that for grief. Grief is downhill of it, further down than I go.

Here is the other side. Everything on the Rise is preserved and that is the horror of the place — the coin-scratch outliving the grandchild, the dry rings of the city's corpses holding their shape in the dark heat. Wood is holy here because wood can die. My grandfather brought up a pear-wood button. One button, at a cost I am not going to say aloud. It lives inside the collar in the drizzle where it will do what nothing else here will do, and it has gone furred and soft at the edges and smells faintly of something none of us have a word for, and each of us takes a turn being the one who looks at it. My turn is the fourth day. I look at it for a long time.

A loop is the second thing on this mountain that admits the future is going to be different. That is not nothing. Every other trade insists the city can be kept in the shape it is. The levellers raise the corner to hold the wall in true. The drapers chisel the doorway back to the doorway. Only my work begins by conceding that these two streets are separating, that they will not stop, that the correct response is not to hold them together but to make the coming apart survivable.

I put in more give than the survey asks. I always have. Write it down as a fault if you like. Nothing here erases anything.

—

My wife is a gutter-warden. She owns the fog and I own the give and we have not raised our voices at each other in eleven years — we quarrel in our laps, on the terrace wall, at dusk, with our hands, silently, sometimes for an hour. It is the most private thing I have. She is faster than me. She was born ninety metres up from me and has better lungs and gets her hands in first, and I have to wait for a crater in her to answer, and by then I have usually thought better of it. That is the marriage. It is written into the contract in metres.

—

Now look up. No, higher. There.

We do that with the chin. It has no name and it is not a greeting.

They ride the column all day and all night and never land, and their nests are out on the plain in the world where things fall down, and no one here has ever seen one on the ground. They are the only things on this mountain not going up.

A loop is the same shape as their turning, hung in bronze, upside down. Mine is the only arc on the Rise permitted to go downward. I have thought about that more than a man should.

They have all the give in the world. We have what I can bend.

—

Your cup is dry.

There. No, hold it lower, the spout drips slow at this hour.

Drink. Don't talk. I have another eleven metres of run to do before the light goes, and after that I am going to lie down on the fresh face at the top with my cheek against it and listen to the rock making room for itself, which is a leveller's habit that I stole at nine and never gave back.

It ticks like sugar settling.

It is telling me how much difference to expect.
