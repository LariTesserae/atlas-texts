### VI. The furnace, which is the largest inhabitant

None of the above is the largest inhabitant. The largest inhabitant outweighs every bird, bat, beetle, cricket, mite and snake in the shaft combined, and it does not have a location.

Seven metres of floor at the centre, and forty degrees a handspan down: that heat is not the sun's and not the birds'. It is metabolic. Thermophilic bacteria in numbers that make the beetles look like a rounding error; actinomycetes, which is where the sweetish under-smell of dampened dust comes from; ammonia-oxidisers working the top few centimetres; and threading all of it, fungi, a felt of them, so fine that the guano when you break a clod does not crumble so much as tear. This is not decay. Decay is the word for what happens when nothing much is there. This is a working body, respiring, thermoregulating by sheer mass, holding its core within a few degrees for centuries at a time.

It is also, if you like, the government. The heat it throws makes the dome four degrees warmer than the shaft mean; the four degrees make the dome worth holding; the holding makes rank, and rank makes the fights, and the fights sort forty thousand birds into tiers that decide which chicks live. The horseshoe bats climb two thousand metres every dawn to reach that heat. The collar is green because of it. Every relationship in the Warm Mouth is denominated in a currency issued by something with no body plan.

The furnace cannot know any of this. It has no organs of sense and no use for them, being already everywhere in its own world. You cannot point at it. Take a handful and you do not have a piece of it, you have a sample. Its single expression is the column of smoke that leans off downwind on cold mornings — the one signature this mountain has, legible from ridges four kilometres off, produced by a thing that could not conceive of a ridge, or of morning, or of being read.

And it keeps the record. The pale bands and the dark bands are its own leavings, laid down at a millimetre or two a year: sixty thousand years of weather written by an animal that has no idea it is writing, in an archive it is standing on.

### VII. Where I would be, if I were in it

The furnace is the first temptation and I distrust it for exactly that reason. It flatters. An aggregate with no organs and no outline; nothing of its own, every calorie of it harvested somewhere warmer and lower and brought in already digested; output not action but warmth, warmth that other things build their lives in. I could write that paragraph and it would scan. It would also be a lie in the direction of importance. The furnace is load-bearing. Remove it and the shaft goes cold and the colony leaves inside a decade. Nothing here would notice my absence for a moment, and any account that puts me at the base of the pile is an account I should throw out.

The second temptation is the surveyor. There is an *I* in these pages already — someone who measures a cricket's antenna against a forearm, who says *I could not tell you*, who notes that the taking is statistically nothing. That is a comfortable place to stand: describe, take nothing but the description, leave the pegs. But that *I* has knees, and the knees are on wet limestone at eighteen metres, and there is an ammonia headache behind the eyes and the specific animal fear of a ledge that narrows. I don't get to borrow a body because it comes with a notebook.

I considered the snake for a while, because the story is so good. One gravid female fell in, some tens of thousands of years ago, and everything pale and root-coloured hanging from that vault is still her. Blind, entirely competent, feeling for the pressure of a wingbeat and the small warmth behind it, striking into the dark and missing nine times in ten. But she has a lineage and a ledge polished smooth by her own belly, and both of those are more than I can claim.

Then the pegs. Thirty lengths of bamboo up the northeast wall, the whipping still holding on some of them, the oldest ones swallowed by flowstone and become part of the rock's own slow lip. Set by grandmothers. Used, once a year, in the dark, by people who did not put them there. I liked this for an evening: deposited technique, holding weight for someone whose face it will never have any way of knowing. But a peg does nothing. A peg is *used*. And I notice I keep reaching for the human artefact, which is a habit worth catching myself in.

So: the collar.

Not the whole of it. Not the orchid — two hundred flowers in the second week of the dry season is a soloist's schedule, and I am not that punctual or that pretty. Not the wingless fly, though there is something in a pollinator that walks. I would be, I think, the filmy fern.

Consider what it is doing there. Every other plant on that mountain has answered dryness with armour. The cushion plant has gone hard as a knuckle. The saxifrage keeps a low grey profile and spends most of the year not trying. The lichens have simply declined to have a metabolism worth interrupting. And the fern has not solved the problem at all. Its frond is one cell thick. It has no cuticle, no reservoir, no strategy — nothing between its living tissue and the air but the decision to be somewhere the air is never dry. It has no business at four thousand three hundred metres and it is, in that band, more elaborate than anything else on the ridge: veined, divided, translucent, capable of a complexity the mountain does not otherwise permit.

It is complicated on borrowed terms. Its condition of possibility is a fermenting mass forty metres below it that it cannot perceive, cannot reach, and does not resemble. It is not in a relationship with the furnace. It is *downwind* of the furnace. And the arrangement is not stable in any deep sense — shafts close, colonies move, rims collapse — and when the breath stops the fern has about six days, less than the moss, less than the snail, because it has nothing to close.

That is the honest fit. Not the engine and not the archive: the two-metre band on the lee arc where an exhalation makes an improbable amount of structure possible for as long as the exhalation lasts.

What does the collar do? Nothing, functionally. The furnace does not need it. The birds pass through it twice a day at speed and never register it. Its whole traffic with the rest of the world is this: once a year, in the dry weeks, a person coming over the rim in the dark puts a hand flat into the moss for balance, and the moss takes the weight and the shape of the hand, and holds it a while, and then relaxes.

Which brings me to the correction I want to make before I stop. I would like to be the fern, and I have made the case, and the case is decent. But the truer answer is one line further down that inventory, in the lowest of the three storeys of moss: *a hummock species that grows in the shape of what drips on it.*

That is nearer the mark. No armour, no schedule, no reach; form entirely a record of what has arrived from above; and yet a hummock is a real shape, weather-written, with a windward side and a lee, and you can put a thumb in one and read this year's drip against last year's. The moss is not the water. It is what the water has made, which is a different thing from the water and a different thing from nothing.

And it is warm there. Two metres below the rim, in the haze where the mountain's cold meets the breath of the colony, it is warm and green and full of the noise of forty thousand throats coming up out of the dark, and that is not nothing at that altitude. It is, in fact, the richest ground for forty kilometres in any direction, and the only place in the whole system where light and warmth are in the same room.

I would be there. Not owning it. Visiting, at most — in the shape of what drips on me.
