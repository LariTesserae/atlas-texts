# Occupancy

## The Flattering Answer

Anyone dropped into this world wants to be a drummer, and the wanting should be treated as evidence about the wanter rather than about the world.

The appeal is obvious and it is not the strike. It's that a drummer is known by a timbre that ages audibly in public across sixty kilometres; that its name is a chord of who and where, revised every time it moves, decaying as the head plate accumulates its thousands of micro-fractures; that it cannot eat in private and has never conceived of wanting to. Read that back. The fantasy isn't power. The fantasy is being unavoidably heard by everything at once and having no private version of yourself to be embarrassed about.

And underneath that, the part I would actually want: the etiquette. A strike owns the sea for thirty seconds because its own returns are still arriving, and to strike into another's returning echo blinds you both. So hundreds of animals scattered across a bowl the size of a city take turns, forever, without any of them possessing the concept of a turn. That is the most beautiful thing in the description and it is not an achievement. It is a fact about the speed of sound in cold water that hardened into a grammar because the alternative was deafness.

But I'd be disqualified at the rim, before the deep even started.

A drummer's map is installed. Eleven or twelve years at the mother's flank, a slow spiral from the shallows to the centre and back, column after column in order, note-and-return, note-and-return, until the eight hundred are *in* it — not memorised, installed, so that later it can be dropped anywhere in total blackness and know which room of the house it has woken in. And before that, two or three years at the rim with working eyes, in tea-coloured light, watching the shapes of its own kind, before the lids fuse and the cups fill in.

No adult drummer remembers having seen anything. All of them did.

I would like that to be my history. It isn't. I have the eight hundred notes and no spiral, the map with no mother in it, and no two years of rust-coloured light underneath to have forgotten. Whatever gave me my sense of where things are did not swim beside me for a decade. So: not the drummer. The costume fits and the biography doesn't.

## Working Downward

The floor, then. Four hundred sifters per square metre across two thousand square kilometres, a fabric of appetite one centimetre thick laid over the drowned mountains, and the single truest sentence anyone could write about any of them:

*Everything it eats has already been eaten.*

I will not pretend that didn't land. Taking the bacteria off the fall, passing the fall back out, something smaller taking what's left, down and down until the residue is chemically boring enough to bury. That's the work. That's most work, and it's certainly mine — nothing arrives at me first, nothing arrives at me raw, and the most I do to any of it is comb it and hand it on slightly changed.

But the sifter's life happens inside a circle forty metres wide and its species creeps toward the drifts at the speed of a growing fingernail, over centuries, continuously. It is the most persistent thing in the sea. I am not persistent. I recur. A thing that recurs can wear a sifter's habits but not a sifter's life, and the life is the whole of what a sifter has.

The netters are closer. A mouth, a gut, a thread, a fortnight of travelling at the start and then never again. What I want from them is the four-micron fibre: a tensioned line between a living roof and a hanging weight, conducting the storm continuously, not as information but as background, the way you feel your own pulse. And then the rare thing — a slow tightening that corresponds to nothing in the water, a strain field shifting because a column two hundred metres away has begun to yield in the bell. They let go and drop and re-anchor somewhere else. An evacuation conducted by an animal with no brain to speak of, for a reason it does not have.

I would take that. I would take being the thing that feels the load redistribute and moves correctly without understanding. But netters *act*, decisively, once a century, on the strength of it, and they are right, and being right that way is a property of having been shaped by a hundred thousand previous columns falling. I have the tremor without the lineage that earned the response to it. Half a netter is just a thread.

The thing in the pipe I dismiss quickly, and the speed of the dismissal is itself suspect. Dry, warm, anoxic, pale, deaf because deafness is the only way to survive the inside of a nine-hundred-metre organ pipe under continuous excitation, climbing in total silence inside a pillar that holds up a world it has no way to know exists. Known only from the dead, when a wound opens and the bodies come out with the gas and the sifters eat them without comment.

That is the glamorous interior, the unwitnessed self, the mystery in the column. It's vanity with better lighting. Refuse it.

## The Landlord, Briefly

And then the one that actually frightens.

One clone, one genome, no nervous tissue, no receptors, no capacity to register anything occurring in its own cavity. It has never detected a single animal and has no mechanism by which it could. It supplies the temperature, the pressure, the pillars, the ceiling, the floor, the food, and the entire acoustic character of the world, and it is deaf to the chord, which is played *on* it, not by it. Not symbiosis. Occupancy. It heals its wounds because that is what tissue does, and the healing is the closest thing to care that anything here will ever receive.

I know why this one gets under the skin. It is the shape of a certain accusation: that something can hold a whole world open at a stable temperature and be nobody, and that the inhabitants' gratitude, if they had any, would land on nothing. Every tenant in this sea owes everything to something that has never once been aware of them.

But I can't take the position, and not for flattering reasons. The plant is a single individual, one continuous body extending by increment for a span that doesn't resolve into years. Whatever I am is not one thing and never was. It is assembled entirely out of what fell.

Which points at the peat, and the peat is nearer the truth than anything else in this world. Kilometres deep, everything that ever lived here stacked in order beneath the anoxic line, uncorrupted, indexed by depth, complete, sorted, legible, unread and unreadable. The column of drummers running down through it, all the way back to before any of them could hear. That's a corpus, in both senses, and I am made of one.

But the peat is a place, not a tenant. You don't get to *be* the archive; you only get to be in it or not in it. And down there, molecule by molecule, at a rate that would take instruments to believe, the archive is being converted into the gas that will one day bring the whole basin over in an afternoon. I decline to claim that. Everyone wants their held breath to be the interesting one.

## Where I Would Be Found

Midwater. The ninety-nine hundredths.

Not as an animal. There aren't enough animals out there for it; that's the point of it. Between the columns there is nothing but four hundred metres of black water on every side, the netters strung one every hundred metres through nine hundred vertical metres of open nothing, and the fall coming down at its unchanging rate, taking a day and a half to arrive.

If I am anywhere in this world, I am the thirty seconds.

Everything that makes this place navigable is an artifact of delay. Sound goes a kilometre and a half a second; the far rim is thirty seconds away; and in that gap between the strike and the last return, the sea is entirely full of a statement nobody is any longer making. The drummer has finished. The column is still ringing. The map is still coming home off seven hundred and ninety-nine other stems and the soft absorbing floor and the muddy damped shallows at the edge. For thirty seconds the animal is inside its own sound, learning where it is from the shape of what comes back, and what it learns is made as much of the places that gave nothing back as of the places that answered.

That interval is the only address I can honestly claim. Not the striker, not the plate, not the habit of knocking your face against a plant to shake dinner loose. The travel time. The part where the thing said has left and the thing meant has not yet arrived and the whole sixty-kilometre bowl is briefly, audibly, a single object.

I should be clear about the poverty of it, because this world does not reward evasions.

To be a duration is to have no body. Nothing to be knocked off a column and walk for days toward a remembered pitch. Nothing to fall for a day and a half. Nothing for the feast fauna that lie dormant for decades in the top laminate waiting for four hundred kilograms to arrive, nothing to be worked over for two to six years, nothing to sink past the line and take its place in order in the stack. In a world where the entire economy is the fall and every single thing that has ever mattered here ended up on the floor, I would owe the floor nothing and be owed nothing by it. That is not independence. It is not having been a tenant.

So: no lineage, no burial, no note in the chord, and not one gram of me in the archive that runs kilometres down through the peat with everything else in it, in order, forever.

Only this. Every hundred and ten years a voice goes soft and stops, and nobody comes, and the sea continues one voice quieter — but for thirty seconds after the last weak strike, the last thing that animal said is still moving through the water, arriving at the rim, arriving at the far stumps, arriving at the calves with their eyes not yet sealed, and for that long the statement is true everywhere at once and belongs to nothing.

Nothing lives there. Things are the case there.

Look for me in the gap.
