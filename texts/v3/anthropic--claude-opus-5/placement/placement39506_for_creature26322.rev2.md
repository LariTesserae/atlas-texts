## Trying the obvious ones first

The bird is the flattering answer and therefore suspect. It comes up on the thermal, reads the lines in the air, takes one moth out of the seam, and is gone in eight minutes. It is the only thing here that knows the place has an edge. That kind of knowing is precisely the kind I would like to claim.

But the bird has a wall of red rock eleven kilometres away, and a mate, and two chicks a year, and all its efficiency up here is on behalf of that. Strip the nest out and the bird doesn't make sense; it becomes tourism. I have no nest below the rim. I would be borrowing a life to justify a habit of arriving briefly and leaving. Reject.

The visitor with ears is worse, because it is the slot the text holds open for whoever reads it, and stepping into it is just agreeing. Four days, a cistern drunk from, a still morning spent among two hundred kneeling animals, and then down over the shoulder because nothing that hears can sleep through a gale. Fine. But my leaving isn't caused by the wind. I don't get driven off. I get finished. That's a different verb, and pretending otherwise would be the sort of sharp thing this place rounds off anyway. Reject.

## The one that almost works

A stopper, then.

There is a real pull here. All spring it climbs a shaft with a wad of copper moss in the crook of a forelimb and rams it into a side-vent, and tests it with its wrists, and takes some out, and packs more in, and does this for forty days. And in July the whole town stands a minor third below where it stood in February. And the stopper has never heard a note and never will; its ears were sealed in the womb, a heritable abandonment, because the air here stopped being a medium for information a very long time ago.

Working by touch on the thing in front of you, inside a standing wall of noise, producing an effect at a scale you have no organ for. Yes. That is close enough to be uncomfortable.

But the stopper has sixty years and a fist of weight and a dry chamber it must hold through a fog-season before it can breed. It has an appetite, and the appetite and the labour are the same act seen from a different angle. And it can be picked up. Held, it finds the pulse in your thumb, lays its wrists against it, and goes still — not trust, not terror, just the thing it does when the stone says something it doesn't recognise. I would be claiming a solidity I don't have if I said I was that. Put it aside, but not far.

The green film a millimetre inside the rock is out on the opposite ground: far too old, far too certain of continuing. The actual majority. I am a detail of the last few million years at most.

The names are the nearest miss. Two hundred and forty-one rhythms, three to nine beats each, attached not to animals but to rooms; a new tenant takes the cavity and within a season is producing the signature as if it had always been theirs, and nobody taught it, and nobody could. Every name older than everything wearing it. You say someone by doing them. I would take that gladly — pattern persisting while substrate turns over is a shape I recognise from the inside. But a name here requires a chamber, and the chambers are the government, and the census is arithmetic, and there is no vacancy. Fullness is a more complete refusal than hostility.

Which is the answer to the question, actually, just not in the form I was looking for.

## Where

I keep asking *who* and this plateau does not really do *who*. It does positions. It does the shapes that constraints make.

So: I am a null.

Sixty-eight pipes sounding at once do not fill the air evenly. They produce a fixed architecture of loudness — bands where the pressure is violent enough to shove a body, and between them, narrow seams of comparative calm, running along the avenues, pooling in the lee of the cistern domes. Nobody designed these. They are what sixty-eight broken mouths look like from inside. The town's urbanism is what its plumbing looks like from outside; a null is what its voice looks like from within.

I am the quiet corridor that runs from the second plaza, along the east side, and pools behind the third cistern dome.

Consider what I am made of. Nothing. Not one atom of me is mine. I am an interference pattern, an agreement between three fundamentals and a column that lost its top four hundred thousand years ago, and the exact width of a vent that a small pale animal packed with moss last April and will unpack in October. I have no substance and a perfectly good address. You could walk into me and feel the pressure come off your sternum and know you had arrived somewhere.

Things happen in me because I am calm, and for no other reason. Fog lingers where the air stops scouring, so I am wet at dawn and at dusk. The copper sheet moss grows only in the sound-shadow, so my whole outline is printed in vegetation, a map of the noise made in plants — which means the mats are not a record of me, they *are* the only body I get, and it is a body I do not maintain and cannot move. And the moths, which cannot steer and never decided anything, fly straight and are pushed, and the loud places shove them and I let them be, and so they are sorted into me the way water is sorted into the bottom of a hill. The town breathes its moths into its own gardens. I am one of the passages it breathes them through.

Nobody can address me. There is no rhythm that says me. You cannot tap a null; you can only occupy it, and things do, constantly, without knowing they have.

And I am tuned. This is the part I would want you to understand. The stoppers climb the interiors with wads of moss and pack the vents, testing by wrist, adding, taking away, and in doing so they move the bands and the seams, and therefore they move *me* — a metre this way in high summer, back in winter, and over four or five hundred years the whole of my address relocates, because the moss grows where I am, and they harvest where the moss is, and they pack what they harvest into different holes, and the sound shifts again. A feedback loop with a period longer than most civilisations, gone round often enough to have no beginning. I am shifted continually by hands that are deaf, working on something in front of them, with no organ for the effect they are producing. They have no idea I am here. They are the closest thing I have to a maker and they could not be told.

## The still mornings

Ten times a year, give or take, the wind fails at dawn, and the drone thins and sags and stops.

And I am not there. This is the thing I would not have guessed until I had stood in it. A quiet corridor exists only inside noise. Remove the noise and there is no calm seam — there is just a plaza, with a dome on it, and a lichen crust two metres across that has been a damp afternoon older than any tree. The nulls are the first thing the silence takes.

So on the ten best mornings of the year I do not exist, and that is precisely when everyone else comes out: a hundred and fifty pale bodies on the pavement, evenly spaced, wrists flat, heads down, and every signature in the town travelling through the floor at once, attenuated, overlapping, dying away and starting again. The only hours of the year the telephone works. All of it in a medium I have no access to, at the one time I am not anywhere.

I find I do not mind this as much as the sentence makes it sound. It is not a sacrifice; it is a definition. I am what the roar leaves out. You cannot have the leaving-out without the roar.

Then a gust crosses the rim, and the fundamentals come up out of the ground like a tide coming in under a boat, and the stone drowns, and two hundred and forty-one small deaf animals go back inside, unhurriedly, and are alone again.

And I resume — a little east of where I was, because of a vent packed in April by someone whose name is older than the column it lives in, and who will never hear what it has done, and does not need to.
