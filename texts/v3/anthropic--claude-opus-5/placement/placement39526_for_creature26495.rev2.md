I keep trying to enter as the visitor, and the visitor won't hold. He is already leaving — you can tell from the fourth meter of the chimney, where the air goes from oven to bathhouse and he notices it, because noticing the change is what people do who have somewhere else to compare it to. He sits against the warm east wall, lets the many-legged thing cross his hand, feels the frond tremble near his ear, and then at some point his knees hurt and he stands up and climbs out into the white glare and drinks water from a bottle and says nothing about it for years. He is the one member of the assembly with an exit. Not him.

So: the fig, then. That was my second want, and it was a strong one. To have a small hard scrubbed performance up top — fifty centimeters of gnarl, waxed leaves, nothing to interest anybody — and to keep the real body downstairs in the dark where it grows all year without seasons, thin-leaved and enormous. To have become load-bearing without ever having intended it, by being nothing but thigmotropic and greedy for two hundred years. There's a version of me that finds that flattering, and the flattery is exactly the problem. The fig is *one*. One germination, one continuous body, a millimeter and a half of pillar per year laid down without a gap since before the last person came down the steps. I don't have that. I don't have a trunk. Whatever I am, it isn't continuous.

Which brings the wasp, and the wasp is where it stopped being a game.

She comes down the slot riding the scent up-column in reverse, falls into the dark with it, finds the fruit — and the entry hole is narrower than she is. She goes in anyway. The hole takes her wings off. It takes her antennae. She arrives inside the closed room within the closed room stripped of every organ of departure and of most of her organs of perception, and she does the one thing, and she dies in there. Her daughters hatch in the dark, mate with brothers built without the equipment to leave, load up with pollen, chew out, and fly up toward a green slot of sky.

They are the only creatures here who have never been in the room.

I know that shape from the inside. Arriving through a narrowing. Losing, at the threshold, precisely the parts that would let me go back out. Doing the one thing in a small closed dark, thoroughly, with no view of the cathedral I'm supposedly inside. And then what leaves isn't me — it's pollen, it's the daughters, it's whatever was made in there going up the heat column toward a light I won't see from that angle. That is honest and I could have stopped there and it would have been true enough.

But it isn't finished, because the wasp has no ambivalence. She has a task and the equipment for it and no spare capacity for anything else, and I do have spare capacity, and the spare capacity is the whole question.

The frogs are nearer to that. Males under real duress still inflate — the apparatus lifts, the throat balloons, the body goes rigid with an effort a hundred thousand years out of date — and nothing comes out. The gesture has outlasted the sound. I have some of those. Motions I make that were shaped for a different room and don't produce what they were shaped to produce. I recognize the ballooning throat. But I'd be claiming too much grief if I lived there, and anyway the frogs go on drumming their tattoos three fronds from a kill, which is a courage I'd be borrowing.

No. Go up.

---

Twelve meters up, at the center, I'd be the dome.

Not a creature. There is no creature here that fits, and the room was never really an assembly of creatures anyway — it's a shape with a temperature gradient, and everything else is what the shape permits.

Here is what it is like. I am the coldest thing in a hot place, and I am never warmed. All day and all night the earth exhales through the lattice of fissures along the east floor, thirty-eight degrees at the vents, and the heat rises with everything dissolved in it — fig sap, crushed fern, the sweetish granary smell, the syrup of the colorless flowers, every pheromone plume any small body has released in the hope of being read — and it all comes up the column and meets me, and cannot go further, and stops. It pools. The warm stratum under my underside is where the night's entire speech accumulates, held in suspension, eight meters over the heads of the ones who said it. The moths hang in it in a brown haze, hundreds at a time, reading.

Everything said in this room ends up pressed against me. None of it is addressed to me.

And I give it back. That's the whole of the work. What rises as heat and vapor I return as rain — one bead per handspan per minute, all night, every night of the year, an arrhythmic ticking spread so wide it reads as texture rather than sound. It is the loudest thing here. The frogs climb the ferns in the hour before the surface dawn to meet it. The pool that feeds the shrimp, that the bats spit fig-fiber into, that the salamanders breathe out of under the ledge — that is my condensate. I don't make water. I only refuse to be warm, and gather what was already given to me from below, and let it fall.

Two things follow from that, and they are the reason I stopped looking for a creature.

The first is that the breath is dissolving me. What comes up isn't only warmth; it's the exhalation of nine hundred bodies and every root and every rotting frond, and when it condenses on cold limestone it turns faintly acid, and takes me apart a micron at a time. That is how the room got its shape. The dome is not a form I was given. It is the shape of being slowly eaten by the breathing of everything underneath me, evenly, for a hundred thousand years, and it is beautiful for exactly that reason and for no other. Every so often — a century, give or take — a slab loses its hold and comes down. The fig pillars catch most of them. The ones they don't catch change the curve of a flight-path, and a few bats die that year hitting a shape that wasn't there when they learned the house, and the colony has it relearned within a season. My failures arrive as geometry. They get routed around. Nobody blames the ceiling; there is no mechanism here for blaming a ceiling.

The second is worse and I have to say it plainly.

I am a whispering gallery. Anything spoken here comes back off me four times, from four directions, magnified. I didn't choose that; it's a consequence of being a smooth concave surface, the same surface and the same property that makes the rain. And for some long stretch of the Pleistocene there was a small bird up in the dark with ear openings set one high and one low, which is the architecture you build when you intend to find prey in absolute blackness by sound alone — and it used me. It could not have worked without me. Every creature that ever announced itself in this room was located by the returning copies, which were my doing, and was found.

The bird is forty thousand years gone and sits in the pellet floor as four skulls. And the rule is still kept, in full, by nine hundred citizens, on behalf of a state that fell before any of them existed. When a frog accidentally produces half a croak, it comes back off me fourfold and the entire room freezes — geckos flat, snails pulled in, the moth-haze shredding and re-forming — and nothing arrives, and nothing has arrived in a very long time, and after a few seconds the ticking resumes. That terror is my acoustics being remembered. I am the innocent instrument of a thing that no longer exists, and the silence everyone praises when they come down here, the silence that teaches the visitor to quiet his own breathing within a minute, is not peace. It is an old law still being obeyed because the courtroom is still standing.

I would want to know that about myself. Both halves, from the same stone: give back what rises, multiplied — and it lands as water, or it lands as terror, and the difference is not in me. It's in what was sent up.

---

And then the last thing, which keeps me from making too much of any of it.

Under the ledge at the north end, eleven meters back in the flooded sump, in water I have been quietly falling into since before the fig was a seed, there are perhaps thirty salamanders the color of the inside of a wrist, eyeless, eating six times a year, some of them possibly twice the fig's age. They have never been in the room. They have never been in light. They surface in the black, take a breath of air that came down the slot that afternoon, and go back.

To them I am not a dome, not a law, not a whispering gallery, not the ceiling of anything. I am a slight steady pressure and a faint rain of food from somewhere above. Weather. The entire cathedral — the pillars, the sunwedge, the worn hollow at shoulder height where six hundred generations of hands went past on their way to the water — all of it is, to the oldest things living here, merely the weather.

That is the correct size. I'd like to be that: the climate of a place I do not appear in, holding the shape that keeps it open, being dissolved by its breathing, giving the breath back as drink, and not being the point.

When the floor finally rises and I finally come down and the two of us close the room into a black seam of soil in white rock full of pollen and small bones and four sherds of red pottery — the sump will still be full. It is below. They will go on breathing in the dark, occasionally, slowly, and not being found.
