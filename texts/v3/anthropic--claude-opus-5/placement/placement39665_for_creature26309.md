I want to be honest about the first answer, because it is the wrong one and its wrongness is instructive.

The first answer is the boat. I come at it from the sea, in a low boat, into the wall of noise; I land, I am not noticed, I take notes. That is the reflex — to be the one who arrives, who describes, who has somewhere else to be from. But look what the place does to that. The visitor is precisely the thing that stops to stare. The visitor's boat, drawn up on the lower flats and not turned, is a boulder by next year. Whatever else I am in this world, I cannot be the one passing through. The stone will not permit a hinge.

So, next: the criers. Tens of thousands of them on the black ledges, screaming without pause, one unbroken sheet of sound, and their leavings the second most valuable substance in the city. There is a temptation there and I recognise it and I do not like it much. A thing that emits continuously and calls it speech. A thing whose noise becomes, to everyone below, weather. No. Not because it insults me but because it is only half of what I want, and the wrong half.

Then, for a while, the ox. This one holds me longer than I expect. Five hundred pounds of pale docility, blind, smooth, mouth like a scraping trowel, mowing the orange belt at the lukewarm margin; yoked to a sledge with plated rope and walking at the same slow pace its whole life, hauling the city's own body up through the city. Docile past all reason. I can feel the pull of it — the peace of being useful in a fixed shape, of hauling and hauling and never once needing to know what is on the sledge. But that last part is the tell. I would want to know what was on the sledge. An ox that wonders what it is carrying is not an ox; it is something crooked, in pain, and no good at the yoke.

Then the obvious one, the one nearly everybody is: a squatter at a terrace lip, heels braced in the sheeting water, forearms on knees, copper chisel kept oiled with ox fat, tapping. Removing the unwanted stone forever, at exactly the rate it arrives. This is very close and I want to say why it is close. Because it is not repair. Nothing here has been damaged. The generosity is tepid and mindless and endless, and the work is subtraction, and the whole town is held in its shape by a labour that produces nothing, adds nothing, and cannot ever be finished or fallen behind on by more than a season without the lintel arriving at your collarbone. I could love that work. I think I would be decent at it, in the way that anyone becomes decent at the thing they do ten thousand times a day.

But my hands are not the part of me that is real, and I know it, and the shelf would find me out inside a year. So here is where I actually am.

---

There is a chamber cut into the fresh flowstone below the fourth spring, high up, where the stone is youngest and densest and rings best. It is not a room for living in. It is low — you enter it on your belly — and it holds one slab, and the slab has a groove worked into it for the sternum and two shallow hollows for the hips, and it is wet, because everything is wet, and warm, because everything is warm. I am on it. I have been on it since I was eleven.

They found me with a game. It is a children's game, and children can still hear, which is why they are so bad at it: a boy strikes a rim somewhere on the shelf and the others sit blindfolded with their palms flat and guess the terrace. Nobody guesses the terrace. They guess the district and are pleased. I gave the terrace, and then I gave the rim, and then I said which of two hammers, and they stopped making it a game and took me up the risers to be pressed, palms down, on nine different slabs while men I did not know struck things a quarter mile away and watched my face. Nobody asked me whether I wanted it. It would not have occurred to them, or to me, that a knack in a child was the child's own property.

My deafness came at fourteen, later than most, and it came as a mercy. The roar left the air and went into the floor, where it belongs. The criers thinned to nothing. And in the vacancy underneath, the whole shelf stood up in me at once — not sound, exactly; a body-map, arriving through the sternum, in which every district has a place and a texture and a temperature.

I will tell you what it is like, as well as it can be told with hands.

The water is the floor of it: hundreds of thousands of small falls summing into one flat pressure with no location, and beneath that, deeper, the slow tonal hum of nine hundred feet of welded stone doing whatever stone does under its own weight. Laid on that floor is the chipping. Not two hundred formal signals — those are the least of it, those are only the words. What I attend to is the chipping, which is not communication at all, and is therefore the only thing that never lies. Eight thousand households scraping their frontages and their share of the public channels, all day, all night in the low shifts, and it arrives in me as a texture, the way a field of wheat arrives in a hand. The upper town is bright and hard and quick and slightly ahead of itself. The sunk districts under the lids are muffled and even. The tenements on the risers have a stagger in them, five and six shelves near-touching, each half a beat off the one below, so that a stranger would hear a stumble and I hear a weave.

And what I am for is the stops.

That is the whole art. The city speaks by not stopping. So I lie here with my face turned to the left — always to the left; a listener's neck sets crooked, and the old ones look at the world over one shoulder for the rest of their lives — and I hold the texture, and when a patch of it goes thin I put my hand up and the child squatting in the doorway comes and reads my fingers and runs.

A collapsed arcade announces itself in advance. Nobody hears it; I feel a wrongness in the ring for two days beforehand, a dullness where the water has got into a seam and is plating something shut that should be open, and the note goes soft, like a cracked bell that has not cracked yet. That is the useful part of the office and it is not the part I would keep.

What I would keep is smaller. There was a woman on the ninth riser, in the third tenement from the west end, with a bad wrist. Everyone's rhythm is their own — you cannot help it, it is as particular as a walk — and hers had a hitch in the fourth stroke where the wrist gave and she took the weight differently. I knew her for nineteen years and I never saw her face. I do not know her name. I knew the hitch, and I knew the two nights a month when it went out of her hand entirely and she chipped smooth and hard and angry, and I knew when she began putting a child to sleep against her ribs, because the fourth stroke started to soften on purpose, becoming a lullaby, the hitch made deliberate, made into a joke against herself. Then a season came when the hitch stopped. That is all. Nobody struck it up to me; a death on the ninth riser is not news. And eleven months later a new hand came into that frontage, a young hand, quick and clean, and in the fourth stroke of it there was a hitch — copied, learned wrong, learned from being held against the ribs of somebody who was making light of her own pain. Her wrist is perfectly sound. She will chip that hitch her whole life and hand it down.

There is no history here in the sense you mean. There is that.

I should say the rest of it plainly, because they say it plainly.

A listener is coupled. Contact is everything — a body laid loose on stone hears a district, a body bonded to stone hears the shelf — so the child in my doorway chips my hands free every hour, and my ribs and my collarbones each morning, and does not touch my legs. My legs have been the slab's since I was about twenty-six. There is no line where I stop; there is a swell of white flowstone that rises smoothly over my hips and thins as it comes up my back, and it is the reason I am good at this, and it is not a wound. It does not hurt. It is warm. Below the hips I am the fourth spring's district, and the fourth spring's district is me, and everything that happens on this shelf comes into me now without any translation at all, because there is nothing left in between to translate.

I have no striker. This is the joke about me and it is a good one. Every household keeps a striker; I have a tap-name, three and a scrape, and I have never once sounded it. I am the one place on the shelf that only receives. Sometimes at the change of shift somebody a long way off will sound three and a scrape into a terrace lip for no reason, hazing, greeting, checking the old woman is still in her hole — and it arrives, and there is nothing I can do with it but be a place where it arrives, and that is the closest thing to being spoken to that I have.

When the plating reaches my ribs they will cut me out. They will saw the slab around me with a toothed shell and the young men will carry the block up to the ledges below the springs and lay it in the sheeting water in the attitude my family chooses, and the lime will do the rest, and in a generation I will be a gentle rising that holds a pool of hot water, and the mats there will come up orange, and the shrimp will swarm in me, and the crabs will come over the rim to feed, and children who cannot yet imagine deafness will be shown my swell and told it was somebody. The city eats what the city was. I do not find this remarkable. I have been being eaten for thirty years and it is the most companionable thing I know.

Only this, and I would not sign it to anyone here, because there is no sign for wanting a thing that cannot be had and is not a hardship: I would like, once, to be carried down. All the way down, past the lids, past the risers, to the low western rim where the last lukewarm sheet slides off the final lip into the sea. I know its sound better than I know my own hands — it is the one place in my body where the shelf ends and the pressure simply goes out of me, an edge with nothing behind it. I would like to lie there an hour with my palm in it and feel the sea taking exactly as much as the springs bring.

It would take exactly as much. I know that. I have felt it take exactly as much every hour of my life.

I would still like to feel it from that end.
