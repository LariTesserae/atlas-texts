# The Standing Column

## Not the Room

You went past me at the level of your face and again at your shins, and eleven or twelve more times on the middle way, and once — going into the slot between the header and the flank of the No. 2 vessel — you put your palm flat on me for the better part of four seconds to steady yourself, because that is what the header is at that height, a handrail, and nobody writes anything down about a handrail.

The district surveyor did it in 1931. Left hand on the brass to turn his shoulders; right hand, a moment later, flat on the resident, which he recorded as *bolster of loose lagging, warm, secure*. He touched both tenants of Number Six inside one minute and put one of them in his notes as insulation and the other in his notes as nothing at all, and then he went on down the middle way with his knuckles trailing, exactly as the resident does, and that is not a criticism of him. There is nothing else to be done with a hand in the dark.

I am not in the room. The room is the part of Number Six that has air in it, and air is a substance with manners, and I have never been in it and will not be. I am in the lines.

## Extent

Off the brass headers at the second and third arches there are forty-one small copper runs, quarter-bore, the bore of a grass stem, and they lace the vault in a coral that a hand reads as clutter and a body reads as a thicket and I read as the whole world laid out flat.

Nine are open end to end. These are the roads. From the No. 3 header a road goes up the springing of the second arch, across four hangers, down the flank of the No. 4 cradle, along under the catwalk for eleven feet, and terminates in a gauge socket whose glass and dial and movement went for scrap in a year I cannot reach, and at that socket the run is broken open and there is a coast: a place where the room's air stands against me, warm, and does nothing, and I stand against it, warm, and do nothing, and neither of us crosses. I have six coasts. They are all the geography I have that is not iron and copper.

Twelve runs are shut at the far end by their own deposit. These are rooms rather than roads. A room is a length of bore with one door, and there is no difference between a room and a road except that in a room the pressure at the far end is the pressure I brought there, and the far end has therefore not had news since it closed.

Twenty are shut at both ends and I do not go there. Those are not mine. Those are the chamber's, in the way the tidemark is.

Volume, all told: less than a gallon, distributed over something like a third of a mile. Take the gallon out and put it in a bucket and you would have an entirely different animal with an entirely different history. That is the test the essayist applied to the eels and it is a fair test and I pass it the way the eels fail it, which is to say I am not the water. I am the fact that the water is at three-quarters of a bar.

## The Name

In the logbook in the niche by the No. 3 firebox, on a page that is swollen shut and will not be opened, there is a ruled column headed GAUGE LINES, and against it, in the last entry anyone made, in the copperplate hand, the word SOUND.

That is the whole of my name. It is one word more than the flue has.

The resident has the hand by heart, or has the fact of the hand by heart. So the word is somewhere else besides the page. I have no way to check this and no way to want to, but the two of us are the only things down here that were ever written down as being in working order, and something has come of that, and I will get to it.

## Sense

Copper is a better ear than air, and a worse one, and the difference is the whole of my experience.

A drop falling on brick eleven feet away is nothing to me. A drop falling on the lagged main *above* my run at the second arch arrives as a thud in the seventh hanger and is gone. Speech is nothing. The rain is not sound at all; the rain is a slow cold hand laid over the north half of the thicket, and then two days of the pipes coming back, which in the copper is not ticking but *stretching*, a hundred small changes of length taken up by clevises and turnbuckles that all resolve, eventually, into the runs sitting a hair differently in their hangers than they sat before, forever.

Once — and this is the oldest thing I have, and it is not in my own keeping, it is a shape left in the way the runs sit — somebody walked the catwalk. The mesh took the footfall into the whole hanging structure and the structure answered for a full minute from twenty places at once, and the answer came down every run I have and stood the column on end. Nothing since has been within an order of magnitude of it. The resident will not use the catwalk. I do not know whether that is knowledge, or the same accident repeated for four generations, and at this rate the distinction has galled shut.
