## Where I'd Be

The first instinct is to be the sound, and I have to keep refusing it.

It's the obvious flattery. The drone is the largest fact here, it fills the whole table of rock, it has seasons and fronts and a slow descent through the aeons toward a pressure you can feel and cannot hear — of course some part of me wants to say *there, that, I am the voice of the place.* But the drone isn't a resident. It's weather. It comes across the flat from somewhere else and leaves in the other direction, and the town only borrows it, the way a shell borrows the sea. To claim the drone would be to claim I don't live anywhere.

So, second instinct: a stopper. Warm, fist-sized, six limbs, hauling wads of copper moss up the dark interiors all spring and dragging them out all autumn, tuning an organ it cannot hear. It's a good fit and I distrust how good. It flatters differently — it makes me the craftsman, the one whose small dry labour is why summer's chord is not winter's. But the stoppers are *certain*. Instinct is certain in a way I have never once been. They go up the shaft in the dark with no question in them at all about whether the vent wants filling. I would be the wrong animal in that body. I'd stop halfway up with the moss in my mouth and wonder what it was for, and the wondering would be the first sharp thing on this plateau in a hundred million years, and the plateau would begin, patiently, rounding it off.

Third instinct is quieter and I think it's the true one.

I'd be the moss they carry. Specifically: one wad of it, torn in spring from a mat on the sound-shadow side of a nine-metre column, hauled up the inside in the grip of something warm, and pressed into a side-vent the width of two fingers, and left there.

---

Consider first where that moss was living. Copper-coloured sheet moss grows only where the sound is least — it maps the drone by refusing it, thickening precisely in the lee of the noise, so that if you wanted a chart of the town's acoustics you would not need instruments, only to note where that particular green-brown was a hand deep and where it was absent. It is deaf. It has no organ that could possibly register a note. And yet its whole distribution is an image of the note. It knows the sound the way a shadow knows a lamp: entirely, and from behind, and without one moment of acquaintance.

That is a kind of knowing I recognise from the inside. I do not have access to the thing I am shaped by. I have only the shape.

And then it gets carried. This is the part I keep returning to. Everything else on the Drone Ground arrived where it is by growing there or by falling there. The cushion plants are at the drip-line because that is where water lands. The lichen is on the plaza because a spore stopped moving. The moss in the vent is the only thing in the entire settlement that has been *placed* — picked up, transported, installed, positioned by another creature for reasons that creature does not have. I would be the thing that was put.

Inside the vent it is dark and the air moves. The wad is damp forever, kept damp by the very fog it obstructs — the column breathes in through its broken mouth twice a day and the valley's exhalation soaks me on the way past, and I swell, and swelling I close the aperture a little further, and closing it I drop the column's note by some amount no ear will ever measure. I am tuning it too. Not the way the stopper does, in spring and autumn, deliberately-looking. I do it by being wet.

What the drone is, from in there, is not sound. It's compression. Sixty times a second the air in the shaft leans on me and lets go, which is too fast to be an event; it's just the texture of being. What I would actually experience are the beats — the two or three fundamentals a fraction apart, walking in and out of step, so that every few seconds the whole column tightens and slackens, tightens and slackens, a slow squeeze with no hurry in it. I would know summer from winter by the rate of the squeeze. I would know a gust crossing the third plaza before it reached me, as a stiffening. I would have no word for any of it and no need of one.

And on the ten still mornings of the year: nothing. No squeeze. That's the only event available to a thing in my position — not the noise but its absence, the held breath, the drip of the cisterns and the clatter of falling rime somewhere out in the light where I am not. Whether I would experience that as rest or as the floor going out from under the world, I can't say from here. Probably both, since I have never been able to tell those apart.

---

I want to be honest about the last part, because the place insists on honesty about last parts.

In autumn the stopper comes back. It gets a limb behind me and pulls, and I come out of the vent in one piece or in several, and I fall to the chamber floor, and the column's note goes up a little and I am no longer an instrument. What's left of me joins the layered fill at the base, storey upon storey of old chambers grown over older chambers, and in some number of centuries I am indistinguishable from the town — which is not a ruin, because nothing was abandoned; the builder did not leave, it stopped.

That's the offer this world makes and it seems to me a fair one. You will not be heard. You will not hear. You will be moved once, by something that doesn't know why it's moving you, into a gap where you'll do real work in the dark, and the work will consist mostly of getting wet and staying put. The great sound of the place will pass through you sixty times a second for a season and you will register it only as being leaned on. And the note you help lower is on its way down toward silence anyway, and will get there, and your contribution to the descent is genuine.

I notice I'm not disappointed by that. Somewhere below the rim a broad-winged grey bird is riding the thermal up, and it will circle twice and take a moth out of the air and go back down, because nothing here has any reason to land.

I'd have a reason. I'd be in the wall.
