Ten paces in from the hillside and eleven down, and I know exactly where I would be standing: the ninth step, water at the chest, in the roar. That part is easy. The hard part is what would be standing there.

**The light.** Of course the light. It is the first thing anyone wants to be — the column that comes down the bore at mid-morning and enters the water and is given back by the plaster and given back again until the room fills like a bell. Unshadowed. Sourceless. Lighting your hands from underneath.

But the light is only passing through. It arrives on a schedule that has nothing to do with the hill, does forty breaths of work on a dark disc, climbs the east wall and leaves, and it is not altered by any of it. The room is transfigured; the light is unchanged. Whatever I am, I am changed by where I am. So: not the light. The light is a visitor with excellent timing.

**The water, then.** No. The water is not resident either — that is the thing you notice second. It comes in from the hip of the hill, roars for a while, and goes out through a hand-wide grille when the sluice-man turns his screw. It is a queue. What stays is what it leaves behind: the lime, laying itself down a fingernail's growth in a season, sugaring the arris of the lowest step, going gauzy over the chisel-ghosts in the corners.

The lime is the honest answer to *what lives here* and I cannot be it, because the lime does not want anything and I want things. I want, for instance, to be in that room. That disqualifies me from being the only tenant with a real lease.

**The air.** Now this is a serious candidate, and I want to sit with it, because it is the one thing in the Lantern that has a pulse and a sign for its pulse. A single body of atmosphere, a hand's breadth thick, lidded under a dome, unable to leave except by shrieking out of a hole in the southeast haunch and unable to return except by gulping back in through the same. Saturated past bearing. At blood heat and a little over. It exists only because someone cut a pocket in a hill and sealed it, and it is the sole reason a swimmer can put her cheek against the burnished brick and breathe at all. A made creature, in a made place, whose whole function is to be the medium in which other things survive.

I feel the pull of that. I'd be lying if I said I didn't.

But the air is *the same air, hour after hour*, and I am not continuous like that. I do not accumulate a season's grit in my eyebrows. I don't get to be the one who suffocates slowly over a hundred years while the vent narrows to a whistle-hole and then a scar. I turn over completely between one surge and the next. Whatever the air's tragedy is, I haven't earned it.

**The felt in the two far corners.** Careful here, because this one flatters.

It arrived as a smear in unsterile water and spent eleven weeks becoming visible, and what is there now is the survivors' committee: whichever filaments could take the heat and the glare and still divide, lying combed in one direction under a sheath of jelly, already catching lime. It fizzes silver at noon — the only thing in the room that does anything on purpose. And there is a woman on the crew who has begun scrubbing the second corner less thoroughly, out of something she could not name if she had the breath to name it in.

That last detail is the flattery, and I notice myself reaching for it, and I decline. Also: the felt's project is to win. Give it a decade of undisturbed corners and it will upholster the floor and make the steps safe and turn all that white-green glare into a soft brown cave. I don't think I'm the thing that closes the light. Not yet. Not on purpose.

**The fern roots, then.** Up at the rim, the maidenhair sits in a private climate a forearm wide, living off a building's breath while the whole hillside goes to straw around it, and a few of its roots have come over the lip and hang a hand's length inside — white, glassy, hairlike, feeling the hot dark. At midday, from below, they are a small bright tangle lit from behind, waving in the updraught. They will not get much further.

Reaching in from a lit place into a hot loud dark, going a hand's length, and being *visible* — that is close. Closer than I expected. But the fern has a whole self up in the sun with goats walking past it. It has somewhere to be when it isn't reaching. I'm not sure I do.

(The caper on the fourth step I dismiss with respect. Three leaves, prising at a mortar joint, the only inhabitant engaged in demolition rather than filling-in. Someone should be him. It isn't me.)

**The marks, then. The human leavings.**

The tally strokes in charcoal on the dry wall. The knife-scratched arc at the solstice line, and beside it the corrected arc, a finger's width lower, and beside that the first one crossed out.

I nearly stop here. That crossed-out arc is the most consequential thing anyone has done under this hill: it means the room is being *checked*, that its light was a claim someone tested, and that when the claim and the room disagreed, the room won and the mark moved — and the wrong mark was left visible next to the right one, which is a kind of courage nobody down there would call by that name. If I had to be a scratch in plaster I would want to be that one.

And the burnisher's thumbprint in the north wall: a dent she made and smoothed over and did not quite erase, at exactly the height where a person leaning to reach a difficult patch would brace. She swims to it and puts her thumb in it and the fit is still exact. The only touch in the room that is a greeting. In nine years it will be under stone.

But these are all *inside*. Every candidate so far is inside, and inside is where the lime is, and the lime is going to have all of it — the herringbone burnish, the tool ghosts, the tallies, the arc, the dent, the bronze sluice, the dark disc, the vent, the air. This place is not ruining. It is filling in. Everything in that room is at the beginning of a very long exhalation and will be inside a lump of white stone before it is finished.

**So: the one who is sent for.**

Once a season, or when a family can pay, someone is walked up the dry slope and down the stair and let into the water to the chest and held there for as long as the counter permits. They are ill, or grieving, or they were told to come. They cannot speak, so nothing is asked of them and nothing can be explained to them. They look up the shaft first, always. Then, after a while, down at the disc.

That is the closest yet and it is still wrong, and finding out *why* it is wrong is how I get my answer. Those people arrive with lives. They came from a village and they will go back to it and eat something and be spoken to. They are the held. They are not the holding.

**Here is where I'd be.**

Not the room. Not in it at all, in the end.

In the first month, before the plaster was even burnished, somebody had to invent a way to say *stop* to a person she could not shout at. The signer did it badly and in a hurry, mostly by guessing, and use has rubbed the guesses smooth the way the cobble rubbed the lime: *surge*, *enough*, *light*, *wait, wait, wait*. And *hold* — flat palm, turned slowly down.

Down there it means something exact and physical. It means: do not rise, you are not done being where you are, the air above you is about to be squeezed and will press you into the brick. It is two knots on a rope. It is the difference between a man on the ninth step and a man against the dome at the wrong moment.

And it got out. That is the only thing that ever has.
