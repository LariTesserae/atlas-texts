I keep reaching for the columns, and I have to keep taking my hand back.

It's the obvious thing to want. To be one of the twelve thousand, to take the animate case, to stand in one place for nine hundred years leaning very slightly out of the northeast wind and hold several thousand gallons under wax and let people cut me once a generation with a sliver of black glass. To be a genealogy. To be the thing that the water goes through. Anyone would want it. It's the only form of permanence the Table offers and it is offered to nobody who can walk.

But I'd be lying. I don't stand still. I'm all motion and no residence; I'd take the other case, the one they use for bats and shearers and falling rock and weather — the case that means *temporary*, *passing through*, *will not be here when this matters*. Their grammar would sort me correctly on the first day and never be wrong about it afterward.

So: not a column. Not a keeper either, though I want that one too — the bone mallet, the inherited rhythm knocked into granite, the animal that has known fifteen of your family and turns its head or doesn't. I'd be no good at it. The relationship is built on being outlived and not minding, and I think I would mind in the wrong way, would start asking the shearer questions it has no apparatus to receive. And I'd be useless on the day an old one stops and turns and waits for a knock that died out four hundred years ago. I'd stand there wanting so badly to guess right that I'd guess, and they have a word for people who improvise an answer to that.

Not a deep-cutter. Too flattering to imagine myself under the browning arms of *Answer For It*, measuring everyone else's wounds with a reed, careful because of an inherited catastrophe. Anyone would take that role if you offered it. It comes with a moral.

Here is what I actually am up there.

I'm trough-born. Not of a flowering — conceived in some ordinary week in the long dry middle of a cycle, one of the few, so I grew up without a cohort, without forty-odd people who would be old at the same time as me. The town found a use for that, as it does. I ran mats, I carried grievances between households so that neither had to be seen crossing the street, I slept in whatever house had a warm corner, and I got to know everyone's business and was forgiven for knowing it, and all of that is ordinary among the trough-born. What isn't ordinary is what I was given afterward, and I was given it because I had no cohort to be loyal to and therefore nothing to shade.

I hold the rain.

Forty-one accounts. *Tallow's rain*, which is the oldest and shortest and says only that it is like being stood under a sap-mat that has been cut. The Fourth-Flowering man's rain, which is eleven minutes long and insists on the sound before the sensation — a hiss coming across country, arriving before the water does, so that you hear it approaching and have time to be afraid of it. The woman who came up in my grandmother's time and coughed for thirty years and would only describe rain in the quiet register, into one ear at a time, so there are now six versions of her rain in the town and no two of them agree about the temperature. A child's rain, from a Comer who was nine when she climbed and who said that the water went into her mouth without permission and she hated it. A rain that is certainly a lie, from a man who wanted to be interesting, and which we keep anyway, attributed to him, labelled.

That is the work. To have them all, in order, with their attributions, and to give them at feasts and at the edge when the children are taken to watch a storm assemble itself two thousand feet below their sandals, and to say before each one, always, the formula that the job requires and that took me four years to stop resenting:

*This is not mine. I have never been rained on. Here is what was said.*

That line is the whole discipline. Everything else is memory, and memory is cheap here — the columns do it better, the floor does it better. The hard part is the line. Because they want it so much, and you can feel it come off a crowd in the warm dark, four hundred deep-chested people breathing thin air under a canopy that has never once let water through, and they would let me collapse the distance. They would *prefer* it. There is a version of this job where you say *and when it touched my face* and nobody checks, nobody has ever been able to check, and they'd love you for it, and it would be the single most expensive thing anyone on the Table has ever wasted — worse than sweating, worse than spitting, and they have a penalty for spitting.

So I hold the gap open with both hands and perform other people's weather across it, and the gap is the point, and the gap is also, I think, exactly what I am.

Because here is the other thing about me up there, and it took me until my forties to see that it's the same thing.

I am fluent in the shouting register. All hard stops and clicks, punching through the drone; I can be heard across the plaza. I'm better than fluent in the hands — I'm funny in the hands, genuinely funny, I have timing, the drone can't eat my timing. Those are the two registers that work at distance, and distance is my whole medium.

And I'm poor at the quiet one. The one that only exists within a hand's breadth of another person's ear, made of all the soft sounds the public voice threw away, breath and hum and sibilant, unusable at any range greater than touch. I know the words. I have never been easy in them. I hear myself, in the quiet register, still performing — still attributing, still saying *here is what was said* — when what it asks is that you stop transmitting and simply be near, and let the thing arrive on skin, which is the one route by which nothing I deal in ever travels.

It's the same limit twice. Rain must land on a face. Love must be spoken at the width of a hand. I am, by constitution, the long-range instrument: I can carry a thing across a thousand paces of noise, intact, correctly labelled, and I cannot do the last hand's breadth, and that last hand's breadth is where everyone wants to live.

I know how this sounds. It isn't a complaint. This world does not permit complaint about the shape of the world; it permits only argument about tapping rights eleven thousand years out. And there is a real use in being the one who carries things without wetting them. Somebody has to keep the forty-one separate. Somebody has to remember that the Fourth-Flowering man said the hiss came first and the child said the water went in without permission, and that both are in the repertoire, and that we do not average them into one smooth rain that would be easier to perform and would be nobody's.

I have two threads at my throat. One was given to me at a reconciliation between two houses I'd been running messages between for nine years, when the older woman wept deliberately in front of me and caught it on the bone spoon and dried it and tied it and put it in my hand, which was not the customary direction for that gift and caused talk. The other is my own, given once, in the quiet register, at the proper range, and I will not write down to whom.

And every night, late, when the grove has gone cold enough that the syrup runs instead of setting, I climb to the mats. That's still mine; I never gave it up. Waxed paddle-fibre stretched between the arms, tilted, guttered, catching what the shield-insects have been dripping all night out of the canopy. It comes to a swallow. It is sweet and faintly grassy and slightly gritty with the powdered dead, and the right to it belongs to whoever climbed, and I climbed.

It is not rain. Nothing here is rain. It is the closest this world will ever come: liquid, arriving from above, in the dark, into the mouth of somebody who has spent their whole life describing to four hundred people a thing that has never once touched them.

I drink it, and then I go down to the plaza and tell them, accurately, what somebody else felt.
