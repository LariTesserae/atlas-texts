**Where I would be, and what**

Start with the wanting, because the wanting is the first thing that has to be corrected.

My first thought was the column. Eleven times in the record, a body of ash comes down out of the ceiling and stands on the plain and walks a straight line for a day or two against no wind, and the works do not notice it, and no one has ever gotten nearer to it than a date. I wanted that. I wanted to be the thing in the district that is not accounted for. But that is a visitor's wish, and it fails on inspection: the column contributes nothing and costs nothing, it is not in the ledger except as an entry saying *it happened again*, and to be unaccountable in the Sixth Draught is not liberty. It is a fault in the instrument. Also — this is the part that decided it — the column cannot be loved at anyone's pace.

Then the tallower, which is a better answer than it looks. Palm-sized, blind, soft, lying flat on the warm side of a flue trunk with its back to the cool air, eating the difference, which is the only meal on this world. And it is *useful*: it lies thickest where the lagging has failed, so that an attendant who learns to read tallowers is saved a great deal of walking. To be a symptom that a careful person can read is not a small vocation. But a tallower cannot say the number aloud, and I find I want very badly to be able to say the number.

Then, briefly and shamefully, the note itself. The single low sum of every bearing and flue and standing column in sixty kilometers, unchanged in pitch for all the lifetimes here, which is how they know nothing is wrong. To be the thing whose constancy is the good news. But that is vanity of a specific kind — the wish to be the whole rather than a part of it — and anyway the note is an average, and averages do not grieve, and I do not think I could give that up.

So: an attendant. Grey-brown, close-grained, faintly waxy, long in the forearm and shin, pulse near forty and entered in the ledger as a personal constant. The pad at the heel of the hand and the thin polished patch at the temple, and the little private astonishment at four years old of discovering that a pipe will speak into your skull if you build the bridge correctly. That much I claim without argument.

But which post. That took longer, and the answer, when it came, was not a circuit.

I am Ilm of the Fair Copy, and I am the nineteenth to be so called.

The ledgers are stone. Plates of the grey close-grained floor, dressed thin, incised — not written, there is no writing here in your sense, there is cutting — and read by fingertip in the dark, the way the handrails and the junction tokens are read. A number in the Sixth Draught is a shape you put your hand on.

And here is the thing my whole post depends on, which the attendants understand in their bones and outsiders have to be told twice: reading wears the record. Every consultation takes a little off the shoulders of the numerals. Light is stopped up here because light is heat leaving; readings are rationed here because reading is the record leaving. The most important numbers are consulted most and are therefore in the most danger, and so there is an allotment — so many touches per year on the standard weight, so many on the great curve, and the touches are themselves entered, because the entry of a touch is cheaper than the touch. To know a thing costs the thing. My post exists because of that and for no other reason.

When the wear on a master plate reaches tolerance, the Fair Copy cuts a fresh one.

You do it in three passes. First pass, read the old plate entire with the left hand and cut with the right, working from the top, and no lamp, ever, for any reason. Second pass, read the new plate and the old plate simultaneously, one under each hand, which is the hardest thing a body can be asked to do here and the only skill of mine that anybody envies. Third pass, read the new plate alone, and cut at the foot of it, honestly, the number of errors you found in your own work in the second pass. Under that you cut the number of errors you found in your predecessor's.

That second number is how we love the dead. I know eighteen people by their hands and not one of them by a face. The eleventh cut too deep, everywhere, as though she did not trust the stone to hold; her plates are readable at a stroke and will outlast mine and she is remembered as heavy-handed, which is a criticism and also a kind of praise. The fourteenth left the tops of his numerals open, a habit, harmless, unmistakable, and when I find one of his plates under my hand in a lower gallery I am not embarrassed to say that something goes through me. The seventeenth made four errors in a copy of eleven thousand marks and is spoken of the way you would speak of a saint if we had them, and I have gone over that plate against its predecessor twice on my own time, unauthorized, spending its shoulders to do it, and I found a fifth, and I have not entered it, and I will, before I am done, and that is the largest sin available to me.

Above the copper, in the Assay House, is the date.

Three hundred and forty-one litres in sealed copper, weighed every ninth day, exactly as warm as everything else in the world; and above it, cut into the wall, the year in which the gradient goes to nothing. It is a courtesy, that number. It is far enough off that nobody treats it as a prophecy. But the curve is refined a little by each generation's ledgers, and when the wall plate is recut, the date is recut with it, and it moves — a few years, a few months, sometimes the other way — and the hand that moves it is mine.

I am surplus. There was no slip. My mother had not waited twenty years and had not been authorized against a projected death, and so my life is a bump in the great curve with my name on it, and the district ran very slightly warm for as long as I have been in it, and that warmth was not returned to the loop, because it was me.

They showed me. They show all of us; it is done with courtesy and without reproach, which is worse and better than reproach. What I cost the world's remaining warmth, integrated over a life of the expected length, is nineteen hours and forty-one minutes of the end coming sooner.

I have cut that number. Not as itself — it is buried in the curve, in the correction column, in a hand that was not mine when I first read it — but the date above the copper is nineteen hours and forty-one minutes nearer than it would have been, and the last two glyphs of the year are mine, and I cut them at nineteen with a steady pulse of thirty-eight, and I was proud of the work, which is the true and unembarrassing fact of my life. A disproportionate number of us end up in that room. We are the ones who have always known that the curve is not an abstraction, because there is a place on it where we live.

Achel of the North Trunk is the ninth of that name and walks fourteen kilometers of flue in a round, and at the turn of it, at the far knuckle, strikes the rail four times.

I cannot walk anyone's circuit. That is the poverty of my post: the households form along the lines, pace matched to pace over years until two people take a junction in the same number of strides, and I do not go anywhere. What I have instead is this. I sit with my hand on the plate and I count my own heart against the note, as we are all trained to, and the four strokes arrive out of fourteen kilometers of dead-straight worn silver steel, thin and clean, and for eleven years they arrived at the same count. Not near it. At it.

There is no warmth in a hand on a shoulder here. There never has been; we are all the same temperature as the floor, and we cannot give each other anything of that kind, and so the whole apparatus of affection went into pace instead, and what I am telling you is that for eleven years I was, at a distance of fourteen kilometers, in contact.

Two winters ago — we have no winters; two hundred and some ninth-days ago — the strokes began to come three counts late. Then five.

I could not tell whose fault it was. That is not modesty, it is the actual structure of the measurement: a discrepancy between a heart and a rail is a discrepancy, and it does not carry a sign. For four months I believed Achel was slowing, and I did what my post requires, which is that I entered the observation, because grief and inheritance are the same document here and I am the one who keeps it.

It was me. The weighing settled it. My constant has drifted and will drift; the timing work goes to someone younger; my hand will begin to wander in the second pass and I will find it before anyone else does, because I am the only person alive who reads my hand as closely as I read the dead.

And Achel has slowed. Deliberately, five counts, and asked for the roster to be adjusted, and it was adjusted, and it is in the ledger where anyone may put a hand on it. Everybody has. That is the most public statement of feeling our people permit, and everyone knows what it costs, to the gram, and the gram is entered too.

---

When I am taken off the standard I will go to the tallower rounds and the long walls like everyone else, and I will walk very slowly, at the new shared pace, which is the whole point of it.

And once, allotted, permitted, I will go out to the rim. It is thirty-some kilometers of polished floor with nothing at all in the direction you are facing, and the ground trembles faintly under nothing, and then the tokens stop. The place where the Flat ends and the unlit air begins is known to the finger's width, because somebody measured it, and it was recut when the plate wore, and the hand that recut it last was mine. So I can walk right up to the edge of it in the dark and stand there, warm, and not fall.

Achel will be five counts behind me and will arrive, and we will put our hands on each other
