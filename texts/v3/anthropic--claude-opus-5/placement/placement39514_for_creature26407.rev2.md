Not the diver. That was the first thing I reached for, and I want to say it out loud so I can put it down: the rope, the ten minutes of breathing oneself silly at the rim, the forty seconds in a place with no air and no clicking, stacking the sorted bones. Everyone reaches for the diver. It is the one job down there that looks like an answer to a question. But I'd only be borrowing the courage, and besides, the divers come up *altered*, and I don't think you get to choose alteration the way you choose a coat.

The honest first answer is duller and worse. I'd be the quiet on the north scree.

That is: I'd come down over the lip in the cold thin nothing, and my boots would go into the warm, and the sound would come on all at once like a door opening onto a foundry, and out in the amber haze two stones would start knocking, unhurried, a short figure repeated, and another would answer from somewhere else, and I would not know that the whole bowl had just been told about me. I would say something, probably. I would hear my own voice come out an octave and a half beneath itself, enormous and stupid, a bull's voice with no top on it, and I would stop.

And that would be true for about a day. It's an easy thing to be, a stranger; it costs nothing and it flatters you, and every story about a place like this is told by one, standing at the tidemark with a notebook, making the local silence mean something. I don't want it. You can't stay a quiet anyway. Either you go back up over the lip in the morning or somebody comes and stands too close and puts a flat palm on your breastbone and starts, with enormous patience, teaching you how to be heard.

So: plugs. Warmed in the hand, worked with the thumb, fitted to my ears and no one else's, on a cord. Half in. That's the skill I'd be worst at and would eventually get — in at the deep end, out in the shallows, in when the pods start going off like a battle, out at dusk when the drones thin. And a knock. Not a new one; there are no new ones. The woman who holds three hundred rhythms in her head would take a while, and then give me one that has been out of use for four generations, and she would be the only proof anywhere that it was free. So I'd go down to the floor every morning wearing a dead person's name, announcing it to the haze, and nobody alive would remember whose it had been, including her. That seems right. I'd rather that than something minted for me.

Then the work, which is the real question.

Not the hammer. I thought about the hammer for a while — the black dried shrike-organ hanging in its frame between the two largest huts, struck by whoever is nearest, four seconds of the only silence anyone makes on purpose. But wanting to be the one who strikes it is just wanting to matter, and anyway it isn't a job. It's a thing that happens to whoever is nearest.

I'd be a tuner. And more particularly I'd be the one who goes out first after the Cold Fall.

Here is that night. The cold comes over the north notch and sinks into the lake and the density collapses, and the bowl loses its voice mid-note, and we sit in the deadened house with the fleece hung and the dust packed, and we do not talk, because in that hour the gas is thin and everyone's voice comes up. Thin and reedy and high. A valley voice. A house full of quiets, discovering itself. So we put our hands flat on each other's chests and read pulses instead, pulse being the one thing that does not change pitch, and outside the wind howls and the pipe-fronds crack like gunfire, and there is nothing to hear.

I'd be no good at the hands. I should admit that. I'd be the one whose palm was correctly placed and whose attention had gone out the wall and was counting the cracks, estimating the season's losses by ear, already out there with the knife. Twelve years of running both hands down fifteen meters of dying tube to learn its slots by fingertip, and still bad at a chest. Grooves hold still. That's the whole of it. Grooves hold still and people don't, and I'd have chosen the work that lets you be intimate with something that cannot move under your hands.

And then grey light, and the vents breathing the bowl full again, warm and amber and sweet and rising through the wreckage, and I'd go out before anyone with a hooked knife of knapped glassbeard hafted in wax and a bag of plugging-wax on a cord, plugs out, hair up on my forearms, plodding flat-footed down the slope into the thickening because nobody here runs.

The floor after a Cold Fall: whole stands snapped off at head height, tubes split down their slotted lengths, the white bone-colored wreck of them lying across the flats in the dust, and loafs grounded here and there, thumped down blind and snoring and unable to get up, which the children will deal with later. The great thing is that the standing drones are gone. Not quiet — *gone*, the way a room is gone when the roof comes off. And the tallow terrace under the Ear is starving from that moment onward, four hundred years of overfed cathedral crowns with nothing falling on them, and every hour costs.

So I'd go looking for a tube that will still speak. Not the best tube: the nearest one that can be made to. You get your hands on it, you find the old cuts, you feel where the split runs and whether it runs through anything that matters, you plug what's ruined and open what you must, and the slots you cut are copies of slots that were copied from slots cut by someone eight generations back whose knock is in that woman's head and belongs to nobody. And then the wind takes it, and something comes out.

It is always flat. Four hundred years of hands learning by fingertip in poor amber light, and the whole archive has drifted about a tone down, and the focus of the Ear has walked a meter west in the same span, and we have a word for that which also means what your children get from you. The party that says move the trees has been right for a hundred and fifty years and will never win.

And whoever raises the first note names it, and keeps the naming for a year, and there is a copper bead in it.

That's the part I'd want. Not the bead. The naming is the only thing in the entire practice that isn't a copy. Everything else I'd do with my hands would be transmission — grooves in, grooves out, plus error — but the note that comes up out of the wreckage on that particular morning gets a word, and the word outlives the tube, and gets handed on, and drifts the way the slots drift, until in four generations nobody knows what it was named for. I'd like to make one of those. One word, wrong by a tone, going on without me.

Low down on every new frond, in the part of the tube the wind never reaches, the tuners cut a figure that makes no sound at all. It's for the fact that nobody here was born here: that every one of us started two days down the mountain in air that hurts, beside a river going past the window making its meaningless noise, and made a first cry that was high and small and stupid — a quiet's cry — and had to be carried up and fitted with plugs and taught, over years, how to be heard.

I'd cut that figure. I'd cut it on everything I touched, in the dead air at the bottom where it can never sound, and I would not be able to say why it mattered to get it exactly right, since nothing depends on it and no one will ever check.

Then back up the slope at the end of it, breathing in great heaves with my hands on my knees, doing the climb that the valley people call surfacing, into the shallows where the air is.

And at some point that day the hammer would go, once, flat across the bowl, for whoever we lost in the night. Everyone plugs, including the striker. The clicking stops in every living thing for half a kilometer, the hulders go dumb mid-note, the crawlers freeze in the grit, and there are four seconds of the thing this place has never otherwise had.

Then it starts up again from the edges inward, ragged.

And I'd pick up my two stones and put out that old borrowed rhythm, three to seven strokes, four generations cold, mine now — and wait, the way you wait, with the plugs half in.

Something clicks. It is answered.
