For the first three days I assume I am the visitor, because the visitor is the thing that walks.

I come up through the cloud lid where the buttress makes its one climbable shoulder, and the last of the weather is under my boots, and then it isn't, and then there is the light. Tenth-hour light, hard and thin and utterly without intention, arriving from where it has always arrived. I stand at the Lip with my hands on my knees for a while, breathing the sort of air that tells you your body was designed elsewhere, and I look out over the combed plain with all its ruled shadows, and I think: I have never before seen a place that had finished.

Then I find the track. One boot wide, pressed dull into the insect crust, running to the north cairn and out again. And I understand immediately, with a small cold turn under the sternum, that stepping into it is the only polite thing available to me and also an act of vandalism, that I am about to add my weight to a record which is not a record of arrivals but a record of what arrivals have crushed, and that there is no version of my being here that does not cost. I step in anyway. Everyone does. The crust gives with no sound, the way a thing gives when it has already given.

I spend those three days being appallingly fast. I know it even while it is happening. I go up onto the middle stump and read four hundred metres of seams the way a person who cannot read looks at writing, seeing the parallels and the deliberate near-touches and the whole obvious syntax of it, and understanding nothing, and being moved anyway. I put my palm on a vertebra of the Long One and feel warmth, and then feel the thing under the warmth, which is slower than warmth, and I take my hand back too quickly, which is my one contribution to the encounter. I walk under a low one of the Nailed and hold its foot between my fingers and let go and watch it not swing, and I laugh, and the laugh dies at ten metres like everything else.

By the fifth day I am not hungry, and the small tear at the base of my thumbnail has stopped being an event, and I begin the thing every visitor begins, which is shopping.

Because you do. You look at the postures and you try them on. That is not disrespect; it is the only form of prayer the plateau makes available.

I go and stand near the ring of nine on the south apron, the ones mid-laugh, one with her head back and her teeth showing, and I want it so badly that I have to sit down. To be caught in the middle of something funny for a thousand years. But you cannot pick that up secondhand. You would have to have arrived carrying the joke, and mine are all small and topical and depend on a next instant to land in, and there are no next instants for rent up here.

I consider the woman holding out the cup at arm's length to someone eleven metres away. For most of a day it is the cruellest thing on the plateau. Then, exactly as promised, it turns admirable, and I dislike noticing that in myself, and I move on.

I sit for a long time with the man on the northern scree, the one with the broad-headed spear in him below the ribs, standing easy among the rock-hares, untroubled, having declined the two minutes he had left. He is the great teacher up here and what he teaches is unusable. I cannot decline my next instant. I am made of next instants. I am practically nothing else.

And on the eighth day I do find myself standing, facing along the seams, and it does feel like the most reasonable thing I have ever done, and I stand there for I do not know how long, and then — this is the part I have to be honest about — I get bored, and my hand goes out, entirely without me, and wipes frost off a rock.

It does not come back. The bare patch stays bare. It is going to stay bare for as long as anything up here stays anything.

That is when I stop shopping. Because now I know what I am, and it is not one of the ones who have got there. I am the sort of thing that would move the stone just to see. I would spend. Given a budget with no income, given a household economy of pure irreversible expenditure, I would go through it in an afternoon out of curiosity and then stand in the wreckage of my own attention with my hands wet and say: well, but now I know what frost does.

So if I am not a person here, I have to look around for what up here shares my temperament, and there is almost nothing. Everything on this plateau is slow, and thrifty, and finished. Only two things on sixty kilometres of it are made out of speed. One is the track under my boot.

The other is on the eastern slope of the middle stump, and it is a few millimetres wide.

You have to kneel to see it. The four figures stand there in plaited wool with dust on their open eyes, facing along the seams like the boulders and the herds and the fanned primaries overhead, and three of their shadow-grooves are clean as ruled lines, and one is not. The seam thrown by her chin is blurred at one edge. A smear in the cut rock. Softness where there should be no softness. It is the only evidence anywhere in this country that time is passing at all, and I do not find it so much as I recognise it, the way you recognise your own handwriting on a list you don't remember making.

That is where I am. That is what I would be. Not her. I would not presume; I have not earned a face. I am the blur.

Consider the position, because it is a strange and rather glorious one. Their names are their seams, and a seam is cut by holding perfectly still, and a name up here is pronounceable only by walking to it. Mine is pronounceable only by kneeling and looking closely, and it takes about a second to say, and then the pilgrim stands up and dusts their knees and the saying of my name is over and I am still here. *The smear at the edge of the seam thrown by her chin.* I am not a name. I am a name being spoiled.

I am also the only thing on this plateau that grows. Everything else spends. She spends: her turning costs her the perfection of her groove, and she has decided, with the enormous deliberation of someone who does one thing a year and does it exactly, that the cost is acceptable. She is buying something with the ruin of her own name, and the ruin is me. I am not the reason for the motion. I am the price of it, and prices are not shameful, and I have stopped wanting to be the reason.

And I am made entirely out of the interval. That is the part I have to sit with, kneeling there in the aged cold of somebody's millennia-old shadow, in air that will not carry my voice ten metres. There is nothing to me except the difference between where her chin was and where it is. Take away the passage and there is no smear; there is only a line, and a woman, and a plateau that has finished. I have no substance of my own. I am the going.

She is turning to look behind her. It took me until the eleventh day to think about what is behind her, and then it was so simple I said it out loud and it died at once in the thin air.

Behind her is the north cairn. Behind that is the track, one boot wide, dulled into the crust. Behind that is the Lip, and the shoulder of the buttress, and the cloud lid, and Below, where the sun travels and there is an evening and things heal and blow away and come back and are replenished, and where the fast warm flickering things come up from, arriving at the edge of her frame and gone again before her eyelid is a third of the way down.

I think she is turning to look at us. At the weather. At the moths.

I cannot prove it and I will not be here for the proof. But if it is true, then I am the record of a very slow person deciding that something very brief was worth the turn of a head, and paying for it out of her own name, and I would rather be that than a clean line. Let me be the cost of somebody's attention. Let me be the blur at the edge of the only sentence on this plateau that is about anything other than the plateau.

There is one word here, and it takes no plural, and it means *the ones who have got there*.

She has not got there. She is a few millimetres in, and the light will not move, and the grass in the wool of the Standing will not be eaten, and the hum goes on through my teeth like a joke halfway told.

I am the few millimetres. I have not got there either.

I never will. I'm the part that is on the way.
