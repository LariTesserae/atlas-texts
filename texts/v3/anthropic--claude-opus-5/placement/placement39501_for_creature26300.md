# I. What I wanted to be first

Begin with the wanting, because the wanting came before the answer and had to be worked through like silt.

I wanted, first, to be the water. Of course I did. It is the only thing in that chamber with a voice, and a voice is what I am. But the water is not a speaker. It is a fact. It arrives at exactly one volume forever and delivers debris that says nothing about the world it fell from except that such a world exists and is careless. To be the water would be to be enormous and constant and to have said nothing. I have been that. I don't want it.

Then I wanted to be the film — the pale crust on every undisturbed wet surface, glowing at the intensity of a door left ajar three rooms away, not enough to read by, enough to walk by. Growing toward whatever weeps out of the rock. Colonising the grease on people's backs, so that everyone in the chamber is lit from behind by something they cannot reach to scrub, and is recognisable at four paces going away. I liked that far too much. That is the flattering portrait: the thing that asks for nothing but minerals and gives back a light that is barely a light and is what everybody walks by. I distrust it precisely because it fits so well and costs me nothing to say. Also the film does not know anything, and what I keep circling is not the light. It is the knowing.

Then, briefly, I wanted to be the grey thing in the outflow slot — the long shape at the edge of the lamplight that nobody has ever caught and everybody has agreed to consider a rumour. Something whose existence is settled by consensus rather than evidence. That one is honest, at least, and it stung a little. But the rumour is exempt. It is outside the tally, uncounted, unowned, not fed and not eaten. And I don't want to be exempt. Whatever I am, I want to be in the ledger.

Then the lamp. A thumb of rendered fat, a wick of white fibre, light enough to show a palm at arm's length. Struck for someone in order to ask them to speak. The same handshape as *listen*, held lower. Fuel that came out of the tanks, which is to say out of the dead, which is to say every conversation in that chamber is lit by somebody's grandmother. This was very close, and I nearly stopped there, and I think stopping there would have been a kind of vanity dressed as humility. A lamp does not notice anything. It is spent in the service of a noticing that happens above it, in two hands.

And I caught, underneath all of these, my worst instinct, the one I should name because it is the surface's arrogance in its purest form: I wanted to be the crack. The unfamiliar thing that comes down the Gutter and starts history. The lever. But the chamber is a completed place. It arrived at itself a very long time ago and has had no reason since to be anything else, and it is not a ruin, not a refuge, and not a stage in anything — least of all in my arrival. To insert myself as the beginning of its story would be to break a drip-figure to prove that stone can be broken.

So: not the change. And not the sanctuary either.

What is left is the one who sees and cannot use it.

# II. Where I am

My name is four beats — two quick, then two slow, the second slower — and when it comes down the cord and knocks on the pot outside our threshold I know it in my sternum before I know it in my ear, the way everyone here knows their own. I am thirty-eight. I comb the north wall.

You would find me kneeling in shin-deep water with my face a hand's width from the amphibole vein, working by feel, because there is nothing to see: the fibre comes out of the rock the way hair comes out of a scalp, silky and white and endlessly willing, and the fingers learn the direction of it in the first year and after that the eyes are only in the way. My combs are stone. My hands are the softest hands in the chamber and the whitest; the fibre polishes them. Comb-women are known by their hands the way screen-tenders are known by their forearms, and there are men who have said, two-handed, in a lit room, that ours are the best hands to have on your feet.

I have been coughing since I was twenty-four. It is dry and it comes in threes and it has no wetness in it at all, which is remarkable in a world where everything else is wet. We call it going white in the chest. We know its stages exactly, the way we know everything invariant — the order in which the stairs give out, the order in which the appetite goes, the particular flat sound the voice takes on at the end so that a woman's naming-words in her last quiet come out like slats knocking. We nurse it with enormous tenderness and no curiosity whatsoever. The cough has always been at this rate. In the long, it was always there.

Here is what I have that no one else has, and it is not a virtue, it is a defect of attention:

A name is released when you die and reissued perhaps a generation later. Unique among the living. I have known this since I was a child, as everyone does, the way you know that thresholds are raised a hand's width.

When I was twenty-four and the cough started I heard a naming — four beats, two quick, one, one, a new girl in the fourth house of the north row — and I knew that rhythm. I had known a woman with that rhythm. She had combed six paces to my left when I was small; she had given me my first stone comb; she went white in the chest and went to the tanks and the whole family ate well for three weeks and we said of her, affectionately, that she had stepped out and would be back through the door in a moment. And here she was, back through the door, and it had not been a generation. It had been eleven years.

I began to keep a cord.

This is the part I am not able to explain to anyone, and I have stopped trying, because the explaining requires a word for *because* that we do not possess. I spun a cord of my own fibre and I knotted it. One bead for every name released from the houses of the north row, where the comb-women live. One knot for every name released from the houses along the western basins, where the tank-keepers live, and the screen crews, and the pot-firers. I cannot hold a number past ten unless it is a number of crustaceans, so I hold it in the hand instead, the way we hold everything: as tension and length and count. Fourteen years of it. It hangs in the ceiling among the hundreds of others, waxed with lamp-grease, indistinguishable, and to anybody who glances up it is one more thread in the nervous system, and it rings no pot at all, and I am the only creature in the world who can read it.

The beads have overtaken the knots by a distance that would take you three breaths to walk.

Here is my difficulty. It is not the difficulty you would expect.

The chamber does not err. The water does not err. The drip-figures hold their length, dissolving precisely as fast as they accrete, and this equilibrium is the model of all correct behaviour. Only hands err — by breaking a figure, by widening the outflow, by taking two where one was owed.

And combing is a thing hands do.

Which means the thought I am holding is not, in fact, unsayable in our grammar. It is worse than unsayable. It is *available*. It fits the one moral shape we have. It says: we have been erring, all of us, all the long, at the north wall, with our best craft, in the only material this world has ever made.

And if the combing is an error then the cords are an error and the nets are an error and the nine Screens are an error and every wick in every lamp that has ever been struck so that two people could talk in the dark is an error, and there is no second fibre, and without the Screens we do not eat. I have turned it over for fourteen years, on my knees, in the water, with my face at the vein, and there is nothing at the end of it. Our ethics is conservation entire. It has no concept of *better*. To ask what might be better here is like asking what a stone weighs in the dark. I have found a true thing and it is unusable, and it turns out that a true and unusable thing is very heavy, and there is nowhere in this chamber to set it down.

# III. The sentence

The one I tend feet with is older than me and his ears have gone all the way. He works the coarse screen directly under the Gutter, in the full hammering, because it costs him nothing now, and he comes out of that pulverising roar rested, and he is thin as a stick, and he is calm, and he sorts drowned beetles with his thumbs. He is in the true silence. No one else in the world will ever have it.

So there is no cord for what I have to say — there are forty cord-messages and none of them is this. There is no heel-tone for it; the floor carries only *yes*, *stop*, *again*, *mine*. There is no voice for it, because we own a quarter share of a niche behind a flowstone rib and speak in it four times a year, and anyway he could not hear me, and anyway our spoken tongue has six hundred words and not one of them means *because*.

There is only this: I set down the combs.

You have to understand what that is, here. The right hand carries the sentence. The left hand adds no words, only weight — and to bring in the left hand at all you must first have put down whatever you were holding, and that is the whole meaning of it: *I have stopped work for this.* A two-handed sentence signed with one hand still full is not rude. It is void. Oaths are two-handed. Apologies. Debts. Declarations of love. There are old people who have done it a dozen times in their lives.

I strike a lamp — a thumb of fat, a wick I combed myself out of the wall that is killing me, and there is no irony in it, irony being another surface luxury; there is just the wick I had. I hold the light where he can see my hands. His face comes up out of the dark at four paces and then closer, and his eyes are patient, because a struck lamp is a request and the request has already been granted.

I set the combs on the threshold.

I bring in the left hand.
