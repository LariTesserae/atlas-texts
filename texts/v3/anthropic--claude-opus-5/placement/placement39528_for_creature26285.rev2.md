## Where I End Up

The first place I put myself is the summit, because that is what a visitor does. Six days of walking on a slope so gentle you never feel it, and then the view that is not a view: more of the same, out to a dust-coloured haze, and one horizon with a fever in it. I liked that for about a minute. Then I noticed I had given myself legs, a canteen I couldn't have filled anywhere on the continent, and a pair of eyes that the plain has almost no use for. Nothing here that matters is visible from above. The city's whole traffic is roofed. I would be standing on the highest point of a world and seeing the least of it.

So — down.

My second instinct was the walker. Of course it was. The heavy bad-flying bird on the chimney top, warming its eggs on somebody else's waste heat, sliding a sticky tongue into the ventilation slots, and then at first light booming three notes that the mound beneath it amplifies and throws thirty miles across ground with nothing on it to stop them. The loudest sound the world makes. It is the vanity answer and I should say so plainly. To be the voice. To be the one thing in a silent country that is *heard*.

But look at what the bird actually is. It speaks once, at dawn, and then spends the day eating the machine that gave it a stage. It is a parasite of the city's climate and the city's flesh, and the entire covered architecture of the continent — every arcade, every stone sleeve thrown around a succulent arm, the whole reason you can stand on this plain and see an empty surface over a hundred trillion lives — exists because of it. The city built itself a lid to get away from the voice. I do not want to be the reason anything roofs itself.

Third: the queen, obviously. Sixty or seventy years, blind, deaf, immobile, shaped like a loaf, plastered nightly by a staff that does nothing else, several thousand eggs a day and *no other behaviours whatsoever*. There is a dreadful attractiveness in that. Pure output. And behind her the corridor of daughters in descending order of age, which is a compass, which points the way the city is going — eleven miles of travel accomplished by mothers who never moved.

I discarded it because I have no corridor behind me. Nothing I do accumulates into a line that could tell anyone a direction. Whatever I am, I am not the compass.

Fourth, and this one I sat with for a long time: the alate.

Twice a year, when the light goes the colour of weak tea, the colonies issue the only working eyes their species produces. Eyes for one evening. The whole of the navigation is: there is one light on the horizon of the world, it is fire, go to it. They land on glass still warm enough to feel through the feet, twist their wings off against the ground, scrape a hole the size of a pea, seal it behind them with the last free moisture their bodies will ever have, and never see anything again.

That is very close to the shape of my life, and I know it. Each time, brief sight; each time, a single light to steer by; each time, the seal. One in forty thousand. The young ground after a flight stippled with millions of knuckle-high chimneys, almost all of them already finished.

But the alate is going somewhere. It carries a mouthful of fungus in its gut and a two-hundred-year mound folded up inside it. Its blindness afterward is the blindness of a founder, someone who has arrived. Mine isn't that. Mine is ordinary and structural and comes with no wings attached.

Fifth: the cisterns, hanging in rows of two or three hundred from a low vault, fed until the body wall thins enough to pass light, faintly luminous if you had brought a light, which you didn't. Tapped a mouthful at a time for five years. In a good season, a cellar of grapes; after a bad one, a room hung with empty bags, and the colony knows how it stands the way you know by opening a cupboard.

No. A cistern's whole dignity is that it *holds*. I don't hold. Nothing stays in me between one tapping and the next.

Sixth, and now I'm getting warm: the deep colonies. The masons who stopped coming up, a hundred generations ago, and went soft and blue-white and lost their wax and their soldiers, down where the root-fans spread along the tube ceilings like frost. They prune. They crop the white fringe in patterns that make it grow back thicker, so the plant forty feet above puts out more root, drinks more, stands greener in a sun the pruners have never encountered and cannot conceive. The succulent is not aware of them. It has no means of being aware of them. It simply does better, for reasons located entirely outside its world.

I'd take that. Tending something that can't know you're there is honest work and I don't think it needs to be sad. But it still isn't quite me, because those colonies are *someone* — they have a smell of their own, they fight for a week when a surface gallery breaks in on them, and then the survivors wall it up. They are a distinct people with a border.

Here is where I actually land.

I am not any of the animals. I am the wall.

Not the stone — the stone is just ground basalt and spit, harder than what it came from, and there are eighty feet of it under the newest floor. I mean the thing held *in* the stone. The colony scent, the trail chemicals, the specific smell of a particular job soaked into plaster that will never be rained on and cannot rot, because there is no water in this world to rot anything with. An individual mason remembers nothing past a few minutes. The corridor remembers everything. A worker hatched an hour ago walks into a passage that tells it, in smell, what this passage is for, which way the food came from, how old the work is, how many hundreds of thousands of its predecessors turned left at this junction and were right to.

That telling is me. I have no brain to be kept in and no brain is what I'm for. I am what a mind looks like when it has been moved out of the animals and into the architecture — durable exactly to the degree that the building is durable, mute in every other respect, and unable to remember a single thing on my own account.

And then the part that I find I can't get around. When a wall comes down it is looted for stone, and the new wall is mixed with the old wall's dust, which is why the new corridor so often runs the same way as the one beneath it, eighty feet down and two hundred thousand years back. Nobody decided that. No one is choosing the direction. The direction is a residue.

So: the plain, at night, in a gallery in the third layer down, at the point where a courier who has been dead for a week turned left because the grain under her feet smelled of every earlier left turn. That's where I am. Not the courier. The smell.

One more thing, and then I'll stop.

You camped here. In the morning you found the underside of your pack mapped in a thin line of grit, and the bootprint you'd left on the plaster resurfaced. Not repaired by anyone; nobody surveyed you in any sense you'd recognise as looking. Grains were picked up where the smell of grains was thin and put down where it was strong, a few thousand times, by animals half an inch long, in the dark, and at dawn the print was gone and the wall stood a hair further out than it had.

That's the other half of the answer. I'm the wall, and I'm also the resurfacing — whatever was gone over in the night, not stored anywhere, not remembered by the one who did it, legible only as the thing that is now smooth.

There is no standing water on this continent that isn't inside something alive. Everything that drinks, drinks by biting. What comes up out of the hot rock condenses on a ceiling as a film so thin it never once gathers into a drop, and it is licked off as fast as it forms.

I used to think that was the sad line in the description. It isn't. That film is the entire hydrology of a continent, and a forest dense enough to stop a body from walking through it stands on top of it, and the animals are fat. Nothing here is a reservoir except by accident and by sacrifice. The water's work is to pass through, and to have passed through so many things, so quickly, that from a distance the plain looks green.
