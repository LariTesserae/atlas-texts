I would be at the bottom of the ladder.

Not on the field — I haven't the eyes for it, and I would be no good in fog, where you work by memory of a place you can't see. Not in the melt-house; I like my eyebrows. If you came down, you would meet me before you met anyone, because I am the thing between the last rung and the town: a slab of cast slag with a board top, three lamps where I'm allowed four, a pan-balance, a jar, a pen, and the book.

My name is Emmer. I am thirty-one. I came down four hundred metres on the second of Tenth, in the third year, at what the log calls the sixth hour, arriving with eleven kilos of personal effects, which is under the allowance, which nobody has ever let me forget. Before that I kept the weighbridge book at a sulphur station on the flank, where the sky was full of things I was not required to count. I came here because everything here can be counted. That was the whole of my reason, and I gave it honestly at my interview, and the descent clerk before me — a man named Hault, who went up in the fourth year with a bad shoulder and a good reference — wrote it in the margin of my entry: *wants a closed number.* He meant it kindly. He was warning me.

**The desk.** I sit in the coldest three metres of the Kettle, thirty-four degrees, which the town considers a soft posting and jokes about. Six metres to my left is the fig in its tub. Two metres behind me is the wall of grey lichen that nobody planted. Above me is the shaft, which on the in-draft pours a white column of cold down over my shoulders and makes my papers curl, and on the out-draft pulls at the corners of them like a child asking.

I sweep my own frontage twice a day, same as everyone. My frontage happens to be the plate of rules and the foot of the ladder, which means I am sweeping the town's doorstep, which means during the twenty minutes of sweeping I am the last brush working, because the whole chorus stops at once and I am always three strokes behind. Four hundred and nine people hear me finish. I have stopped minding.

I am Dry. Ninety-one of us are. My contract pays eleven litres a day, of which two are specified for the cloth, and I have read it — I would guess — four hundred times, and there is a clause in the fourth paragraph that could be read two ways, and the second way is better for me, and I have never raised it. I sit at the town's only gate and I own no water. The Assembly did not do that deliberately. It works out well anyway, and everyone knows it, and nobody says so.

**The log.** The book is bound in fibre board and its pages are paper, which came down in numbered crates, which are entered in the book, on its own paper, in ink made in the melt-house out of lamp soot and brine — which is also entered. The log logs itself. It is very nearly a closed system with itself, and I know exactly who else in this town lives that way, and she is six metres to my left with a brush.

The columns are: date, hour, crate number, seal condition, gross, tare, net, contents as declared, contents as found, receiving household, signature or mark. Then the ascent column, which is shorter and which I keep on the left-hand page, out of an instinct I could not defend if the Assembly asked: outgoing weight, and what it was.

Six hundred and eleven tonnes have come down that ladder since the drill. I can give you the figure to the kilo, and I do, at parties, and people move away. Eleven hundred kilos of pressed food a month. Nine hundred and four frames' worth of mesh and pipe, of which four hundred and three now hang and the rest are spares and settlements. One squirrel-hair brush, numbered, in the second year, before my time, entered in Hault's hand with the note *for the tree* — as if there were another tree, as if it needed saying, and I love him for writing it.

The dead go up. There is no soil here to put them in except the fig's, and the fig's soil is weighed. So it takes six haulers and a full watch, and I log the outgoing weight, and that is the only entry I write large. Osme's mother went up in the fourth year, seventy-one kilos, at the third hour, and Osme stood where you are standing and put her hand on the trunk on the way back and then went to work, and I wrote the number and drew the line under it and that was the funeral. My book is the only monument in the Kettle. Nobody planned that either.

**Off-log.** Now the part I would have to tell you, because you would ask, because outsiders always ask the same question on their second day, when they have finally named the silence.

*Nothing lives here that we did not carry.* Somebody scratched that under the rules and nobody has ever taken it off, and it is a beautiful sentence, and it is my sentence more than anyone's, because I am the one who carries the ledger of the carrying. And it is not true, and I am the one who knows the ways in which it is not true.

The lichen is two metres behind my chair. Nobody planted it. It is in no crate, it has no number, it arrived by no ladder, and it is left alone on principle — which is the town's polite way of saying we have decided not to have an opinion. I have an opinion. I have looked at that wall by lamp for four years, and it has spread nine centimetres to the west, and I have measured it, and I have never entered the measurement, and I could not tell you why except that the book is for things we did, and that is a thing that happened to us.

The seven children are not in the log.

I want you to sit with that, the way I have. Everything in this chamber came down a ladder and was weighed and was written, except the pahoehoe, the salt, the lichen, and seven people. There is no column for a person who did not arrive. Four hundred and three of us can give you the day and the hour, and we do it the way surface people give a birthday, and it is the most important number any of us owns. Vett has no hour. Vett came out of the inside of the place, which nothing else has ever done, and she could touch her collar at nine months and could not speak until sixteen, and her mother says the order is right, and her mother is right.

So there is a second book. It is small, it is not numbered, it is unbound, and I keep it under the pan-balance where the salt does not reach. Its columns are: date, hour, name, of whom, and weight, which I take myself, with the same balance I use for crates, because it is the only balance I have and because I want them in the same units as everything else. Seven entries. Under *received from* I write nothing. The line stays blank. That blank is the most honest mark in either book.

**The moth.** In my first year, a crate came down — number 4-1177, seal intact, tare correct, declared as *brushes, fine, six* — and in the packing wool, folded up small and perfectly dry, there was a moth. Grey-brown. Wings closed. The size of a thumbnail. Long dead, and heat-treated dead twice over, and no threat to any fleece or fissure or child in this chamber.

It weighed under two tenths of a gram. My balance would not take it. I had to guess, and I have never guessed in that book before or since.

By the letter, I should have written *one moth, dead, destroyed*, and destroyed it, and I sat with the pen up for the length of eleven pump strokes, which I counted, because I count without meaning to. Then I wrote *packing wool, 0.4 kg, retained* and put the moth in a twist of fibre in my hip pocket, where the glass flake goes, and it is there now, and it has been there for three years and some months, and it is the only animal in the Kettle.

I told one person. I told the Listener from the pans, the fifty-one-year-old, the best of them, who cannot read, and who dictates her log to whoever is passing. I chose her precisely because she cannot read, which means I have spoken it and not recorded it, which is the only way I know to be honest without being permanent. She thought about it through most of an out-draft, and then she said: *it came down. You logged the wool.* And she went back to listening to the ceiling.

That was the year I learned the thing the book cannot hold. A young hauler came off the chair one out-draft with his eyes wide and said the two syllables to me, out loud, at thirty centimetres, mouth near ear, because I am Dry and he thought I was safe. And I had the pen in my hand and my hand moved and then it stopped, and he watched it stop. He is a frame-holder now, and he still brings me his crate marks himself instead of sending someone, and neither of us has ever mentioned it, and I am not going to write those syllables here either. I have never heard it. I have sat that chair nineteen times, alone, on purpose, at the bottom of hearing, and the tone changes pitch as the salt shuts the cracks one by one, and it is not a voice, and to me it is not anything else either. That is my one grief in this place and it is a small one. I have the day and the hour of every person's arrival, and they have the word.

Once a day the coin comes across the floor. Its path is charted on a board in the Assembly and it ends near me: for a count of about six it puts a bright edge on the corner of my desk, and I do not have to queue for it and nobody thinks I am cheating. I put my left hand in it. I do not stop writing.

And six metres to my left, twice a season, Osme goes at the fig flower by flower with a brush that came down in crate 4-1177, doing the work of an insect that is not permitted below the shaft head, on a tree she has known for six years will not fruit — and the town lets her, and touches the trunk on the way to the ladder, and does not ask her why.

Neither do I. I only weigh the leaves when she brings them.
