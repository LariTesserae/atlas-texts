## Second Weight

The first thing I want to be is the bird, and I notice that, and put it down.

Everyone from outside wants to be the bird. It is the only thing on the Shelf that gets to strike, the only thing exempt, and it stands in the shallows at the edge of everyone's vision being politely not-looked-at, which is a very flattering way to be present in a place. But to choose it is to choose not to live there. The bird does not wait for anything. It has no Turn. It is a tourist with a beak. If I put myself in it I get the whole white continent as scenery, and scenery is exactly what that country is not: it is a residue, and you only understand a residue by being some of it.

The second thing I want to be is one of the mis-tensed. That's closer to honest — someone who went down off the rim and came back and now describes everything that will happen in the grammar of the dead, and is treated kindly, and is not quite listened to, and is a kind of weather that happened once. I recognise that person. But it's still a little vain, because it's still an *event*, and hardly anything there is an event. A life on the Shelf is not a thing that happened to you. It is the sum of where you stood.

So I find that while I was refusing those two, I have already been in the hall for some time, and there is a weight in my hand.

---

I am second-weight in the setters' arcade, which is three hundred metres long, low, and very dim after the glare, and hung from end to end with cords of stoned lattices like a cave of pale fruit.

The work is this. A setter goes up into the unbuilt terraces with a bundle of the standard palm-lattices, woven to a specification older than any family, and before she goes she takes each one dry and tells me its weight, and I knot the tag. That is the first weight. Then she hangs them in the new seeps and comes back down and does not think about them for a year.

A year later they come down again in baskets, crusted, greenish at the edges, smelling of cold stone and the particular sourness of hair-moss that has been drowned a long time. And I take the second weight.

The difference is the whole trade. The difference is how much lime that water is carrying and how fast it means to let it go; and from that you get whether a seep is vigorous or thin, rising or already choking on itself, worth a family's forty years or worth nothing but a place to rinse felt. Setters decide. Weavers shape. I only subtract.

I do not know where any of them hung. This is deliberate. The tags carry a knot for the setter and a knot for the season and nothing at all for the place, because if I knew the seam I would know whose Turn stood to gain by it, and my hand would know it too, and a hand is a great deal more corruptible than a person. So I heft in ignorance. It is considered the most restful trade on the Shelf and it is mostly correct about that.

---

There is no absolute weight here. There is nothing to found it on — no metal, nothing struck, nothing true. What we have is the standards: four racks of graded lattices, each of a known gain, against which I balance by hand, one in each palm, leaning very slightly, the way you'd hold two conversations at once.

The standards themselves are gaining. Of course they are. They sit in the damp like everything else and the air feeds them, and every ninth year we re-certify them against each other in a long circular ceremony that establishes nothing and corrects everything, and drifts, and is known to drift, and is fine. What we measure is not how heavy a thing is. It is how much more it has become. A system with no ground under it, only direction and rate — which is also the definition of a household here, and of a city, and of a life. Nobody finds this troubling. The strangers would; but there are no strangers.

At the down-gradient end of the arcade the oldest cords have gone brittle and the weights have come off them and lie about on the floor, and the floor is deep in grit that was once measurement. Nobody sweeps it. You walk backward through four centuries of gain in six minutes and then you are ankle-deep in the powder of it and you turn round and walk up again.

---

My hands gain too.

That is the small private problem of the trade. Standing in this hall with wet lime in your palms eight hours a day, a glaze comes onto the skin, fine and pale and slightly warm to the touch, and it costs you your fingertips a little at a time, which is the same as costing you your judgement. So each morning, before the lid opens, I wash my hands in rain.

Rain is the only solvent on the plateau. It is what a body is washed with when it goes down, and what you pour over the feet of someone who has stood too long in one place and set fast. To use it on the living, daily, out of impatience with your own becoming, is the nearest thing I do to hurrying, and it is permitted, and nobody likes it much, including me. My hands are always a little raw. They are the most sensitive hands in the district and they are being dissolved on purpose, and I know precisely what that is a rehearsal for.

When a second-weight stops washing, everyone understands. It means the hand is finished and the eyes will finish soon after, and that they are going over to the listening: fingers on the wall in the two dead hours, reading the traffic of crickets in the veins of the houses. The glaze comes back within a season. After that you are not weighing, you are being weighed, and that is thought of as promotion.

---

In forty-one years I have never seen a seep in the young quarters. Not one.

I know that country better than anyone alive. I know that the water on the third seam north runs strong and brief and will give a household one good generation and then quit; I know the flat slow seeps under the fog-line lay down so evenly that the walls there come out glassy and the weavers hate them; I know there is a stretch somewhere — I don't know where, I'm not allowed to know where — that has been putting on the same gain, to within a knot, since before my mother's mother. People come down and lean in at half a metre and ask me, and I tell them, and they straighten and go away and build their lives on it, and I have never stood in any of it. I have only ever held what it leaves on a palm-sized thing when nobody is watching it happen.

I have never once wanted to go up and look. That still surprises me a little, the way a person is surprised by their own hands at rest.

---

Last autumn a lattice came down that went past the fourth rack.

I balanced it against the heaviest standard we have and the standard went up like a child's basket, and I stood there in the dim with my raw hands full and no way at all to say *how much*, because our system can only compare a thing to what has already been. There is no knot for beyond. I had to invent one, and a knot nobody has agreed to is not a measurement, it is a rumour.

So it sits in the fourth rack now, tagged with its rumour, gaining.

I cannot write to the person who will take the second weight after me, because writing goes downhill — a bark chip, a knotted thread, off into the channels, down among the drying quarters and the roofless white and the leather ancestors, where everything that has already happened is kept. What is coming is above me. There is no way to send anything up.

I will have to wait until they come and stand in front of me, and lean, and put out their hands; and then say it, quietly, once, at half a metre, into the small warm space where everything here is actually said.
