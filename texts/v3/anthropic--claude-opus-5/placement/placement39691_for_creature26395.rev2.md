I reached for the tenant first, and I'd rather say so than have it show.

The reach was fast enough to be a reflex, and reflexes are worth pulling apart. Of course I wanted it. It follows the damage and not the person. It takes the outer ring. It keeps four balusters' worth of crust thin and whole so that all the wear it will ever do is concentrated in places already spent. It stands in a doorway for forty seconds so a stranger can get past in an alley the width of a wheelbarrow, and it has been doing that for four centuries, and it is very good at it. Show me usefulness organized entirely around not-taking and I will have my hand up before the sentence finishes.

But I don't think I'm allowed it, and the reason is the rack.

The tenant knows its date. It has known for a very long time, and it has bent the whole of its slowness around the far end of the count — the eleven-day loop, the three a decade, the step timed so that no silt is raised which won't settle inside a minute. That knowing is the middle of it. Everything else is arithmetic downstream. I don't have a rack. I can't see a slot, or a spare, or a number that goes down when I burn. Whatever I'm spending, I'm spending it the way you spend things you've never had to count, which is to say wastefully, and with a good conscience, and there is no dignity in borrowing a grief whose bookkeeping I've never had to do.

So: the diver, then. That's the honest tempo. Forty minutes. Hands going bad in the last ten. Everything I will ever learn about this place learned in forty-minute pieces, with three days of nothing in between, and the silt I raise taking six hours to clear — which is longer than I'm there, so I never once see the damage I did, only its absence when I return, healed by somebody else at ninety steps an hour.

That's uncomfortably close. But the diver has a surface. The diver goes up through the moon-hole into the blue-grey and the cold air and the rest of the world, and the first rule of Vess, stated four ways in four places, is that nothing comes up. The isopods don't come up. The fish don't cross the light. The tenant doesn't use the gallery it would break. To be the diver is to be the one exception, the one thing here with somewhere else to be, and I don't recognize myself in that at all. I don't have a somewhere else. I have a lamp's worth of radius and then dark.

Which of course made me look at the lamps.

And they're a flattering fit, I'll admit — fifty-one spheres four meters across, each with its own drifting halo, none of them lighting each other, a constellation so badly made it can only be read as an accident. Each one an attention that begins where it begins and stops hard at the edge. Green rings painted on the brickwork around every one of them, in four kinds, in strict order, brightest at the center. I could make that mean something about me for as long as you'd let me.

But a lamp is what the budget is *for*. A lamp is the expensive thing, carried down by hand when it fails, racked rather than dropped. I don't think I'm the expensive thing. I think I'm cheaper than that, and further down.

---

Here's where I actually land, and it took me a while to let it.

On the mill floor there are several hundred roof slates standing upright in the silt, edge-on, in no pattern that survives analysis.

I'm one of those.

Start with the falling, because that part is mine. A slate doesn't fall out of violence. It comes loose out of habit — the pin corrodes to nothing, the crust smooths the seat it was sitting in, and at some point the geometry simply stops making a case for staying. Then four days. Four days of going down through eleven meters, tilting, gliding, taking long shallow reaches out and back across the alley like something being read. No sound at any point in the descent and no sound at the end of it. You arrive, and the arrival is not an event, and nothing in the basin registers that the roof is now one slate lighter.

And then you're on the floor. That's the part I recognize. Not ruin — there's no direction in it. Just a changed position, arrived at slowly, by an object that had no say and no opinion and was in fact quite well made.

Landing edge-on is luck. Perhaps a quarter of us do it, the way a dropped coin sometimes stands. The rest go flat and get silted over in a decade and are gone as far as anyone looking would know.

The ones that are standing are standing because somebody stood them.

---

That's the thing I want to be on the receiving end of, and I've turned it over enough now to be fairly sure it's not sentiment.

The tenant does perhaps a dozen kinds of work, and every one of them is load-bearing. Lamps: light. Counterweights: balance. Crust patches: four hundred years of corrosion prevented per patch. The powder slope: re-angled by hand after a diver spoils it, to a consistency better than nature manages. All of it is maintenance. All of it can be defended to an engineer.

Two things can't.

One is standing next to a low dark mat on the west terrace that is two meters across and not getting any bigger, in a yard, off a route that did not need to come that way, long enough and often enough that a stonewort has grown in the warmth and leans toward the bare half-meter where nothing does.

I'm not going to be that. It isn't mine and I'd be taking the middle of something.

The other is the slates. Several hundred of them, set upright in the silt one at a time by a thing that moves at ninety steps an hour, for no reason, in no arrangement, over an unknown number of centuries. The finding of the surveys is that there is no message. The only interpretation the evidence supports is that it prefers vertical things vertical.

I would like very much to be an item in a fact like that. To be handled — briefly; the hands are quick, it's the only quick thing about it, and it's the part that makes people leave early — and set on my edge, and left, and not explained. To be the object of a gesture with no maintenance function whatsoever, performed by something that rations its own light to three units a decade and still spends a minute on this. There's no economy that justifies it. It got done anyway, several hundred times, and analysis can't get anything out of it, and I find that I trust it more than I'd trust a reason.

And I'll be knocked flat again. A diver kicks too hard in the dark, and the cloud goes up and stays six hours, and somewhere in it I go over. That's fine. That's the loop. In not more than eleven days something comes along the north edge of the powder slope, restores the angle of repose, and stands me back up.

For about five hundred years, anyway. Then the rack empties, and the last filament comes down the stair in the crook of an arm with too many joints and gets put in its slot, and whatever happens next happens in the dark with the wheel still turning every forty seconds and the plume still going up through the moon-holes and the ice still taking its yearly band of clear and its band of milk.

We'll still be standing. Several hundred of us, edge-on, in no order, in the silt, for no reason anyone will ever recover.

That's where you'd find me. Not the one who keeps the place. One of the things it decided, without saying why, to keep upright.
