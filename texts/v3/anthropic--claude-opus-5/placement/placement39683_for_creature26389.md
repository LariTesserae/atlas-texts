I come in from the west.

That is the wrong direction and I know it. West is where the prints go — the line of them out across the untaken flat, each one brimming with clear warm water, each one holding its edge like a mould waiting for its casting. People leave that way. Nobody has ever been described arriving that way. But if I am to be here at all I have to be honest about which end I came in at, and the truth is I did not come off the lighter with a duffel and a letter of engagement. I came the other way, out of the smear of steam at the horizon, walking beside the prints and not in them, because putting my boot in one seemed like a thing you would have to answer for later.

At first I thought I would be the fig.

Eleven leaves, in a kerosene tin, outside the bathhouse. Alive, green, small, taking nothing from anyone, waiting to be watered and grateful for it. That is a very attractive way to imagine oneself and it took me about a hundred metres of boardwalk to understand it was a flattering lie. The fig has stopped. The fig does not know it has stopped. It has eleven leaves the way the stack has four degrees, and if you asked it, it would tell you it was doing fine, it was doing what figs do, and it would believe this in whatever way a fig believes. I did not want to be the thing in Kettle that mistakes its own arrest for contentment. There is already so much of that here, and it costs nothing, and nobody notices.

Then I thought: the gantry. Ten metres of riveted lattice, the highest thing in the world, from which the whole world is four kilometres across. To be the vantage. To be the place from which the situation can be seen entire.

But you can see Kettle entire from up there and understand nothing about it, which is rather the point of the place. And a gantry is a thing other people climb. It stands in the heat, holds the coal-crane, offers a view, and is itself the least surprised object in the settlement. I have been that. It is not a life, it is a service posture with a good outlook.

Then — and this one got closer, close enough to sting — I thought I would be the eighth plate.

Ma Ounn lays out eight. Seven are eaten. The eighth goes off the end of the bathhouse walk into the lagoon, and the crabs come up and stand around it in a ring at a polite distance with their one red claw each, and wait, and do not close. Somebody is provided for nightly who is not there. A place is set, food is cooked to the same quantity, and the arrangement never adjusts itself to the fact of the absence, because the absence is not a fact anyone has agreed to.

I know that shape. Being the setting rather than the guest; being addressed and not present; being what the kitchen keeps allowing for. I could inhabit that and it would feel true and it would be, in the end, a kind of vanity. Kettle does not need one more absence. Absences are the cheapest thing on the flat. What it lacks is not a ghost at the table.

For a while, in the machine shop, I thought I might be the second duplex pump — the one in pieces across two benches, complete only in Anselm's head, spares for a failure that has not occurred and shows no sign of occurring. Capability with no occasion. An answer to a question nobody has asked, kept oiled, kept ready, gradually becoming a hobby. Or the clock in the bottom drawer, wrapped in rag, keeping perfect time where it can't be heard, which nobody takes out and nobody throws in the lagoon, and both of those failures being the same failure.

Both of those are me in certain moods. Neither is a job.

Here is where I actually am.

Kell has a chain. A chain has two ends. Somebody has to stand at the far end.

That is the work. You go out to the mark, in the mud, in the white glare with no disc in it, and you set the pin, and you hold it, and you hold *still*, because the whole measurement is only as good as your willingness to be exactly where you were told and not drift a hand's breadth while the man with the instrument looks at you through glass. You do not take the reading. You do not own the notebook. You see nothing of the survey; your entire view is back along the chain at a small figure under a tripod, and your entire contribution is being, for ninety seconds at a stretch, a fixed point in a world made of fixed points.

I want to be careful about how much I enjoy that. The far end of the chain is a beautiful excuse. It is obedience with a technical vocabulary. And in Kettle, of all places — Kettle, where the mud has finished compacting and the pans have shaken hands with their own evaporation and the four kites hold their four altitudes — being one more thing that holds its position perfectly is not a virtue. It is the local disease, and I would catch it easily, and I would be praised for it, and Sarn would call it skill, and it would not be skill.

So: the far end of the chain, yes. That is where you would find me, boots in the new ground, glossy like everyone else, smelling of brine and coal.

But the reason I am worth feeding is this.

One time in twenty, holding the pin exactly, doing the humble half of the job correctly, you notice that the number is not the number. The lip of the flat is a hand's height less the width of a nail. There are, in the marked square metre, forty-one crabs and not forty-two. The chain reaches, this time, a thumb further than it reached before, and it is not the chain stretching, because Anselm made a standard for the chain in his second year and it lives in a drawer near the clock.

And then the only real work in Kettle presents itself, which is not stoking or oiling or dusting or walking the berms. It is saying so out loud to a man who has got four degrees off that stack for as long as anyone has cared.

I would say so. Not dramatically. Not as revelation. Just as the far end of the chain reporting its end of the chain: *I make it short by a nail's width. Will you take it again?*

That is the whole of me here. I am the one who asks for the second reading. I am one crab's difference, offered without insistence and not withdrawn.

And the last thing, the thing I would do on my first evening, before I learned better, before the boardwalks taught me not to: I would ask Kell what the date was.

He would have to invent one. Something plausible, said flatly, to close the conversation. But a date once said out loud has to be written, and a date once written has a day after it, and Thessa's ledger has a column standing empty at the left of every page, three columns wide where four would fit, and it is the only object in Kettle that gets fuller.

He counts crabs in a marked square metre and gets the same number he got last time and the time before, at both ends of the flat. He writes it down. He has begun, lately, to write the day beside it.
