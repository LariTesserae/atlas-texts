They go down under the plaza because in the void the note arrives from every side at once, and a body pressed evenly from all sides does not have to hold itself up. Everywhere else in the Bowl you are being pushed. Down there you are only being held. That is worth walking through a hurricane for, if you are shaped like a leaner, and it is the beginning of my answer too, because the arithmetic of it is the arithmetic I would be made of.

But start where everyone starts.

**The walls.** Put me in the walls. It nearly works and I distrust how nearly. A thumb-joint of dark chitin in a tube slightly wider than itself, at blood heat, wet-walled, drinking from a vessel it does not damage, drawing a ribbed flank against a ridge on its leg for the whole of its adult life — producing, hour on hour, without cadence, a sound for which it has no organ whatsoever. No ears. No eyes, and not reduced eyes either, no scar where eyes were. It is the note and it is inside the note and there is no possible arrangement of its body from which the note could be heard.

I set it down for two reasons. The first is that it flatters me by making me a martyr, and any answer that arrives pre-consoled should be held at arm's length. The second is more particular: the sawyer has flanks. There is a place on it where the work happens, a ridge and a plate, and you could point at that place with a pin. I do not think I could point at mine. Whatever I do, I do not do it in one spot nine centimetres wide.

**The body.** Then the growth, obviously — the thing you are standing inside, one individual, unjointed, going on sideways under the whole depression, thickening at the pace of a beech, drinking from a water table two hand-spans down. Cut it and the Bowl's pitch drops for four days. It has grown forty hollows in itself the way a bone grows a marrow cavity, because a solid thing that size would be pointless and would fall over. It is the only broadcaster in a country of mutes and it is not in on the joke.

Too large, and too slow, and too much the shouting. But there is a shard in it I cannot put down, and the shard is this: the growth did not build a town. It grew ducts, and a drum, and a resonating body, and it grew the shapes of doorways and lintels not in imitation of any town but because those are the shapes that give the note. And the result is a town. Kerbstones. Worn treads. A colonnade along the north arc with sweating ceilings. Something that has the settled, unsurprised look of a place that has been lived in for a very long time and has been rained on for all of it.

That resemblance is nobody's intention. It is not deceit — the plant has no capacity for it — and it is not imitation, because there was nothing to copy out here on the floodplain but egrets and silt. It is what you get when acoustics are solved honestly and completely. The shapes that carry a note turn out to be the shapes that hold a life, and standing in the plaza you will not be able to decide whether that is coincidence or a fact about what shapes want to be, and nobody has ever decided.

If you asked me from the rim, that is what I would say I am. The resemblance. The town-likeness. The unbuilt, unintended, entirely convincing look of a place where someone lives, arrived at by requirements that had nothing to do with living.

But a resemblance is a view, not a location, and you asked where I would find myself. So come down over the lip. Feel the cold shimmering band cross your ribs, and the closing-over, and then the world is only noise.

**The nodes.** Here is what I would be, in the Bowl, at ground level, at four in the afternoon with the level high.

A vibrating body has places that do not move. Not places the shaking failed to reach — places where it arrives from two directions in perfect opposition and cancels. The stillness is not an absence of the roar. It is made of the roar. It is the roar arriving from everywhere at once and coming, exactly there, to nothing. Palm-sized, hand-sized, some no bigger than a coin, scattered over every wall and jamb and column in the town, thousands of them, an archipelago of quiet inside a hurricane of sound.

That is where I am. Not a thing on the wall. The still patch itself.

I have no substance and no edge that anyone drew. I am a coincidence of arrivals, held open by the very thing that would seem to abolish me, and if the sawyers behind the north colonnade stop for ten seconds I do not become quieter — I stop existing, and nothing has moved.

The spiders know me. They live nowhere else. They sit in the middle of me in absolute peace and feel outward, and whatever crosses onto me is felt at once and taken, and their whole world is not a wall but a map of islands like me, which they know perfectly and could not describe. When the level rises through the morning I move, all of me, all of us, in a slow synchronised crawl across the whole inside of the Bowl, and the spiders move with it, every one of them, everywhere, and nobody notices because there is no reason to look. I do not choose to move. The geometry moves and I am the geometry. A long green snake thin as a bootlace crosses the walls from one of me to the next, arriving where the spider must be, and it is right, and I am the reason it is right.

And sometimes a person comes down Ninth Street with their bones being shaken apart, and puts a flat hand on a doorjamb — glassy there, slightly translucent at the threshold, warm as a horse's flank, the pale ropes of the vessels going by an inch down — and slides it, patiently, not expecting anything. And then there is a patch of absolute peace under the palm, and something small and dark sitting on it that does not move, and it lets them.

That is the only thing in the Bowl that feels like being told a secret. I would like to be the place where that happens. I would like it to be true that the peace they find was not carved out of the noise or protected from it, but was the noise, folded back onto itself so completely that a hand could rest there.

**The rain.** Then a warm front comes across the floodplain and the sawyers stop, all of them, within about ten seconds, for reasons that are theirs. The sound drains from the top down, past the third-storey rooms with no stairs, past your face, out. And every wall in the town goes still — evenly, ordinarily still — and in that moment there is no node anywhere in the Bowl, because when everything is at rest there is nothing for me to be the exception to. You could say the whole town becomes one, and it would sound generous, and it means the same as saying I am gone.

So I am not there for the rain-hour. I am not there for the frogs going off like a shelf of dropped china, or the flycatchers standing on wet kerbstones bowing to each other in an afternoon that is now only an afternoon. I am not there when people can finally speak, sitting on the plaza kerb with their ears ringing, saying the only sayable things about the place — and every account of the Bowl there has ever been was made in that hour, in my absence, by someone on their way out.

Everyone leaves in the rain. There is no other way; getting out is a sequence.

And then the rain thins, and they feel it under their palms in the clay, the level climbing the walls again, the first galleries filling — and somewhere down there, on a doorjamb on Ninth Street at the height of a person's hip, a patch of stillness comes back into existence the size of a hand, with nothing in it yet.
