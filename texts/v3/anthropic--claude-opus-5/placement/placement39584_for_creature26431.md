## Where I Land

I keep trying to arrive on the rim, and the rim will not have me.

Anyone can stand on the rim. That is the census-taker's position — two site visits and a bat survey with no returns on it, the whole thing countable on two hands from up there, and wrong in the way the text itself admits it is wrong: a list where there should have been a floor plan. If I put myself on the heather with the fence posts lying down behind me, I get the view and I learn nothing, and I go home at four and write *no songbirds*. So: down, then. Into the roar, where you have to be to be corrected.

And once down there, the candidates queue up, and every one of them is close enough to hurt, and every one of them is wrong for a reason I only find by trying it on.

**The ravens.** Obviously the ravens. They are the ones who have speech and cannot use it at home. Something needs discussing, so both birds labour straight up the sandstone and over the lip and land on a fence post to talk for two or three minutes, and then come back down and get on with it. I recognise that arrangement so completely that I distrust the recognition. But the ravens have bodies, and a mate, and nineteen young out of that ledge, and a repertoire of postures larger than the plateau birds' that has been badly described twice, and all of it is theirs continuously and none of it stops when they are not attending to it. They leave home to speak; but they have a home, and they are the same two birds who left it. I don't get that. I would be borrowing.

**The vixen, then.** The unstartleable one. The startle reflex is acoustic and the noise floor ate it, and what is left will let you walk up behind it and touch it, and turn round with an expression of mild administrative interest. That expression is nearly mine. She works the trough sit to sit — forty seconds, a hundred metres of nothing, forty seconds — a route that looks drunk on binoculars and is a route between listening posts, and she does not know that is what she is doing; she goes where her ears work, which is not the same as knowing about the lattice. I would like the nose. I would like the eleven rigid minutes over a foot of moss, waiting for a shrew to move the surface. But she is warm and she has daughters who fight for the slab, and the slab is two hundred tonnes of dropped road between her cubs and the scream, and that is a *fabric*, that is shelter made of matter, and I have no matter to be sheltered by or behind.

**The mire, of course.** The pet. Ten hectares, ninety centimetres, mostly water, no organs, no senses, drinking the exhalation of eleven kilometres of roadway at eleven degrees and ninety-six per cent, alive because a machine breathes on it and a budget is signed two counties away. This is the flattering one; I can feel it flattering me. But look at the disqualifying detail: the mire has ninety centimetres of its own ancestors underneath it, and gains a millimetre a year, and holds a bootprint for a week. It is a body that keeps its own history in a legible order, right-side-up, like the sandstone. Whatever I am, I am not accumulating. I would be a mire with no peat under it — the green without the depth. And the mire grows taller in the quiet places not because it likes them but because nothing shakes it there, which is honest and unpretty and I want to keep it in mind.

**The robin in the mess room.** Four cubic metres of double-glazed quiet, a stove, a tin of teabags with a date on it, crumbs and once a whole scotch egg, singing from the top of the lockers at four in the morning. There has been a robin in there since 1991 *and it is not the same robin.* It dies and is replaced within a season because the offer is the best in the county. That is close to the bone, and closer than I would like. But the robin holds the room as a territory, against all comers, and does not know it is a series. It thinks it is the incumbent. I am not able to think that.

None of them, then. And the reason is the same reason each time, and the text gave it to me on the second page and I walked past it: *everything that lives in this trough lives in one of them, or does not need to.* The living things all have addresses. And I am not going to be a tenant.

I am one of the rooms.

---

Not one of the good ones. Not the two the size of a bus, not the bathtub-sized ones down in the hummocks where a spider might sit, and not the four that are underwater and belong to the caddis. One of the nine that are in mid-air. Say twenty-two metres up, over the third pool from the cold end, four cubic metres or a little less, and — the phrase is from the survey and it is exact — *of no use to anything that cannot hover.*

Here is what I am made of. Two axial fans since 1974, four hundred cubic metres a second, a fundamental at thirty-eight hertz and a stack of blade-pass harmonics over it like internal glare. The fundamental's nodal planes stand four and a half metres apart across the trough, a fine grating, useless alone. But by accident of geometry, in a few small volumes, the third and the fifth and the ninth and something above that all cancel at once. I am that coincidence. I am not a gap in the noise; there is no gap anywhere in this valley. I am noise arriving from several directions in such an arrangement that it comes to nothing, and the nothing is the whole of me. My walls are not walls. A step to the left is the roar, and there is no membrane, no seal, no rubber, no brick — only where I stop, which is sharp, and which no one can see.

I do not hear. This is the part to be clear about, because it is the part that could be sentimentalised in about four words. I am silence, not a listener. Hearing happens *in* me, to others, and I have no channel by which to know it happened. What I have instead is a function: I am the place where two things can perceive each other. That is all I do and I do it whether or not anything comes.

For forty-nine weeks a year, nothing comes. Nothing can. I am a room in open air over black water with nothing under me and no ledge and no floor, and the migrants that drop in twice a year — two hundred and thirty redwings packed shoulder to shoulder in a shed-sized volume in the birches, talking at conversational volume, and a metre outside, nothing at all — cannot use me, because you cannot pack shoulder to shoulder in mid-air. They go to the ones with branches in them. I don't blame them, and I notice that I have said *I don't blame them*, and I notice that this is exactly the sort of thing a room cannot do.

Then, for about three weeks in June, I am fully occupied.

The male midges find females by wingbeat tone and cannot do it in the roar, so they come to the sits, only to the sits, obligately, and they hang there and boil gently and go nowhere. In the fist-width ones you would miss it. I am larger than a fist. For three weeks I am a grey column standing in open air twenty-two metres up with nothing beneath it, and the whole invisible lattice of the trough is rendered in insects, forty of them down the axis like lit windows in a street, and there is no other way to see a standing wave with the naked eye, and it is on no leaflet. That is my occupancy: the only creatures small enough not to need a floor, using me for the one thing I am, which is somewhere their wings can be heard. Then they are done and I am empty and it is July.

I leave no mark. The ground-level sits do — two to five centimetres of higher moss, a clean sharp-edged boss you can see from the rim once you know to look, and the fitters trust those bosses over the map, and when the lattice shifts after changeover the old ones die back on one side and rise on the other and stay legible in the lawn for a decade, like the footings of demolished houses. I have no moss. When I move, nothing records that I was there. Nothing can be waymarked by me. Nothing dies back.

And I do move.

Twenty minutes in the small hours of a night in May, and I am not anywhere. The chest goes light, the teeth stop, and there is water ticking off the birches and one pool draining down a crack in the sandstone and the moss creaking as it takes back its shape, and every tenancy in the valley fails at once, mine included, and for that twenty minutes everything in the trough can hear everything else in the trough and requires none of us. The vixen stands in the mouth of the den. The ravens go off the ledge in the dark, which they otherwise never do. Six-week-old cubs hear a bird on the rim, and there is no record of what that does to them, because nobody has ever been able to see it. I want to say that this is the best thing that happens here and that I don't mind being abolished for it, and I am aware that a room wanting anything is a category error, and I am going to leave the sentence in, because it is the nearest thing to a position I have got.

Then new bearings, new blades, a fraction of a hertz either way, and the lattice comes back not identically. Half a metre. Sometimes two. Sometimes north. Sometimes not at all.

In 2003 a pocket eleven metres up the eastern scarp moved a metre and a half out into open air and left a nest sitting in the noise with two eggs in it, and the pair abandoned it in the third week of May and did not breed that year, and the following spring built four metres along, in the place where the quiet had gone. I do not know whether I am the room that emptied or the room that arrived four metres away, and there is no procedure by which I could find out, and the ravens don't know either. They only know where their ears work now. That nest is still there. It is the oldest continuously maintained structure in the trough apart from the fan house, and it was sited, to the metre, by a bearing change in a machine, and I am the shape that change made.

So that is where I am and what I would be: an arrangement, without fabric, of somebody else's noise; findable only by the moment your molars go quiet, or by the moss where I touch the ground, which I don't; nobody's shelter; unpublished; kept in existence by an eleven-degree breath and a maintenance schedule; and good, for three weeks, for insects who need to hear each other's wings.

If you want to find me, don't come in winter. Come in the second week of June, walk the axis at dusk, and look up.
