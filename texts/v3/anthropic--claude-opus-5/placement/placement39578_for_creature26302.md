## Hands

The first thing I understand is that my callus is on the wrong hand.

Everyone here has the shine along the back of the left hand and the outer left wrist, from a lifetime of walking with a palm on the stretcher courses, and I have it too, the way I have a nose. But the working hand is the right, and what is on it is not a shine, it is a split: the pad of the thumb cracked across the grain, healed, cracked again, with lime standing in the cracks in a pale line like a joint in miniature. The hand smells of wet ash all the time. Washing does not help and makes the split worse, so I don't much wash it.

Second thing: my face has no mask. Everyone else on this mountain, when they lift the smoked glass at the end of the day, has that band of paler skin around the eyes and the squint-lines fanning white to the ears, so that all of them look faintly astonished forever. You cannot judge a colour through smoked glass. So I work with the shades up on my forehead, and my face is one tone from hairline to jaw, and by fifty I will have very little sight left in the bright end of the range. This is the price and it is known in advance and it is written into the terms of apprenticeship, and no one has ever pretended otherwise to a child on the first morning, which I think is decent of them.

Third thing: my teeth are pale and short. Everyone grinds, from the hum; the dentists are wealthy here and keep casts and are our only real historians. But mine are worn on the inside as well, from the tasting, because the fastest way into an old mortar is the tongue — you lick the chip, you find the sourness of the lime, the sweetness or grit of the sand, and sometimes, in the deep old work, a faint blood-taste that means an iron-rich fines that no crusher on this face has produced in two hundred years.

I am a matcher. Iva, Weeping Face — and then a difficulty about the number, which I'll come to.

## Sand

No new clay comes up the mountain, and so no new sand either. Everything I work with is the mountain's own shed skin, sorted.

The brick-crushers throw off fines too small to re-temper: that is the body of most mixes, and each crusher's fines are its own colour, because each crusher is fed the demolition of its own quarter and every quarter's brick was fired in a different century. There is the bleach-sand off the dry terraces above the cloud, wind-milled, almost white, which is the only thing that will lighten a mix without making it chalky. There is cistern silt, and here the mountain becomes a country I know intimately and have never walked: each cistern drains its own comb, its own parapet, its own lichen crust and fig litter, and so each silt is a different grey, and I can name eleven of them by eye and four more by taste, and I have been to none of the eleven. My map of this place is a map of silts. There is clinker dust for the blacks, which cuts the hand. There is the pink grit out of the dye works cascade, illegal on any windward face because it fades, and used anyway, in courts, by everyone.

I keep them in bins in a dry room. This is the difficulty of the trade: colour work needs dry, and I live in the fog band, where everything drips night and day. So a matcher's room is always set over a live flue, warm and faintly sulphurous, the driest room on a wet terrace — and therefore also the room with the most hum in it, which means the boards cannot be hung on the flue wall or the dust walks off them. Mine hang on ropes from the ceiling and swing a little, all day, all of them, together.

## The Boards

The boards are tiles, thumb-sized, a mix on each, dated on the back.

There are about four thousand. The oldest are my great-grandmother's and the mixes on them are dead — nobody knows what went into them, the crusher that made those fines was broken up and re-eaten a century ago, the parapet that yielded that silt has been repointed twice since. Those boards are the most valuable things in the room. We keep them not as records but as targets. When a face comes due whose last pointing was that old, I take down the dead board and stand at the bins and try to walk up to it from underneath.

I never get there. This is understood. You cannot get there; the material is gone.

Colour must be judged wet, because a mix dries three shades lighter and every mix dries lighter by a different amount, so the whole craft happens with a wet thumb, dabbing, holding the wet dab beside the old wet-licked chip, turning both to the north light, and then waiting a day to find out how wrong you were. My thumb is wet from the first hour of the morning. When I am thinking I put it in my mouth without noticing. All of us do it. In a room of matchers arguing you would think we were a room of children.

## The Tell

Here is the rule that the trade is actually built on, and it took me until my fourth year to stop resenting it.

Come as close as you can. Then miss, on purpose, by the smallest amount you can still see.
