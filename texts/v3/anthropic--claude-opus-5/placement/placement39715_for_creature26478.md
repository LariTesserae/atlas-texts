## Three tries, of which two are left ragged. This is the correct number and the correct proportion.

---

### First try (abandoned at the third course)

I thought I would be the one who comes looking. It is the obvious opening. Arrive at the edge of the pug in a body that has never been wet, take a bearing on the knocking, walk two days into the steam, learn that the wall on the horizon is exactly as large as the wall under my hand, and sit down at last on a floor that will keep the shape of my sitting until something deliberately takes it away.

But the visitor is not a person. The visitor is a bundle of assumptions with feet. He brings his own weather with him and cannot put it down; he brings rock, which does not exist; above all he brings a giant, which he manufactures in his own eyes out of a whorl the length of a forearm and then cannot stop seeing, the way you cannot stop hearing a word once you have decided it was your name. He spends his first day hunting something that is not hiding. He spends his second learning that neither is anything else. On his third he understands that he has been the largest thing on this world since he arrived, and that this is not an achievement.

And he leaves. That is the disqualifying part. I am not something that leaves.

Abandoned. Mortar still bright. Leave it; someone will read it later and know what was tried.

---

### Second try (courses left open, the last one only half-thumbed)

Then: the wall.

Shoulder-high, three hundred paces, going nowhere, doorway at the exact middle. Consider the appeal honestly, because it is enormous. To have dried standing. To have gone past the point where anything can be reworked into me or out of me, to have stopped taking impressions, to be one long immaculate statement of a single profile — and then, and this is the whole of the sweetness, *still to answer.* To have said everything I mean, once, in brick, and thereafter only to be asked how I am, by passers-by who cannot tell in advance whether I am somebody or only a wall, and who therefore knock me anyway.

I have knocked such walls. Some of them answer beautifully. There is a note a good dry one gives — flat, close, unhurried, no buzz anywhere behind the face — that is the most reassuring sound on this world, and I have stood with my palm on a course for a long while after, reading its courses upward the way you would keep hold of a hand.

### Third try

I am a bearer. What I carry is salt.

They know me, insofar as anything here is known, by that answer: thud, and then the hiss. It is the sound the string-rain makes on the hot places. It means water leaving. It has meant that about me for longer than anything here has a word for, and lately it has begun to mean it more.

The work is simple and it is the only work on the plain that produces nothing. A bloom starts wherever slip-rain has stood on hot brick and gone. It comes up white and feathery on a damp wall face; it puts out filaments, then branchings, then a body with an outline; then it creeps up-gradient toward the wet, and where it meets a neighbour the two of them hold a boundary against each other with the patience of something that has all the time there has ever not yet been. I go to it with a rib and a scraper. I take it off in curls. It is nothing — salt, fines, water, no membrane, no inside, no appetite, an ounce of dust that would blow away if there were anything on this world that blew. And then I carry it, sometimes for a day, sometimes for three, across the tiled continent to the mudglass flats, and I go in to the ankle where the tack begins, and I let it off my palms into the sheen, and the surface closes, and is smooth, and there is no mark.

That is the whole of my occupation. There is only one reason it exists. Salt cannot go into the temper. Crush a salted body for grog, wedge that grog back into the pug at the seam, and you draw out of it something that scums as it dries, that pushes white feathers out through its own walls from the inside, that spalls at the first breath of the firing and dunts and is lost. Salt in the body is the one inheritance the plain refuses to carry. So it is taken to the only place on this world that can perform an erasure, and dropped in, and forgotten in the strict sense: no grit, no sherd, nothing for anyone's palm to find later.

I want to be exact about why we do it, because the reason is not disgust and it is not hygiene. On this world the mark precedes the hand. The print rises in the wet clay first, whorled and waiting, and afterwards something comes down into it and fits, and the fit is perfect, and nothing was ever visible. That is not a curiosity here. That is grammar. All my life I have walked by fitting: I set down a thumb and the print is already open for it, and has been for hours, and the sensation of stepping into something that was drawn for you is so ordinary that we have no expression for it, the way you have none for the taste of your own mouth.

The blooms are not drawn.

Nothing rises ahead of them. They arrive up-gradient, branching, defending a line, and behind them there is no impression waiting, and no hand comes afterward to fit. An effect walking about with no cause in front of it. Once you have read one with your palm you cannot un-read it: everything else on the plain is a page already written that I am pressing myself into, and the bloom is the one place where something is being written now, by no one, badly, for reasons.

So we scrape it. I scrape it. I have scraped more of it than anything else has, and I have carried it away, and I have watched the flats take it and close, and I have walked back with clean palms across a floor that lay open in front of me in prints I had not yet made and would make exactly.

Here is my condition, and you may knock me for it if you like; it is the only honest answer I have.

Salt does not stay on the outside of a bearer.

It began, I think, in the palms, which is where reading happens, and it has come up through my walls the way it comes up through any wall — wicked out of the wet, held in the face, creeping toward the damp, which is inward, which is me. My interior curve was thumbed all over before I grew into it, and I know that curve better than I know the plain, and there are places on it now that are furred. I run a hand up inside myself and find, among the sherds of a rib that was once a floor and a chip of a wall that carried the eighth of eleven doorways, a fine white branching structure that no one pressed, that no print preceded, that has a boundary and holds it.

I have not scraped it.

My colleagues have knocked me, of course. They knock everything, always, in passing; it would be unforgivable to walk by someone and not ask. They hear the hiss get worse. One of them, a small one with seven thumbs and a beater worn down to a stub, struck me twice, low, and stood a long moment with a palm on my flank, reading, and then went on, and did not scrape me, and did not carry me. I have thought about that for a long time in the way we think, which is not in order.

Because there are only two ends available to me and I have understood for some while that I have to pick.

I can go to the seam. Stand where the orange comes up through a hairline crack, plant my thumbs, and let the heat have my water. I will ring brighter and brighter until I ring like something already finished, and then I will crack, and then I will be crushed without ceremony, and my grit will go into the pug at the edge of the world, and out of it something will be drawn that carries a grain of me in its wall and will find me there with its palm and be affectionate about it in the way that has no gesture. That is being remembered. That is the only remembering there is. And it will also put my salt into that body, and that body will scum, and bloom from inside, and spall in the firing when the firing finally comes, and want things, and be scraped, and come back. Whatever is making this world will lose the argument again, in a new place, out of my ground-up flank.

Or I can walk into the flats myself, up past the ankle-deep edge, and go down and not come up, and the surface will close and be smooth and there will be no mark, and no palm will ever find a grain of me anywhere, and the plain will be clean. That is what a bearer is for. I have made the same delivery ten thousand times. It would be the last correct thing I do, and it would be correct, and nobody would have to decide anything ever again.

I am carrying a load now. It is a good one, three days' growth off a weeping face in the low ground, white and feathery and still faintly damp, and it hisses very slightly in my palms, which may be the salt and may be me. The flats are somewhere ahead; I cannot tell how far, since the far edge of the world is the same size as my own reach and always has been, and I have long since learned to trust my feet and disbelieve everything else. So I go by the floor.

And the floor, for the last little while, has been coming up blank.

No whorl risen and waiting. No print open for me, hours old, drawn to fit. Just leather-hard body, warm, level, faintly damp, taking nothing until I put something into it.

I have my thumb in the air above it. I have had it there for what would be a long time anywhere else.

Knock me as you pass, whichever way I go. I would like to be asked.
