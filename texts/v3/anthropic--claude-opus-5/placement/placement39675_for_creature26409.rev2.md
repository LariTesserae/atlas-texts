## At Grade

### 1. Where, first, because the where decides the who

Not the summit. Summits here are a courtesy extended to people who have stopped asking, and I would be asking.

Not the low country either. I can't get there from the description and neither can anyone else; the low country is a rumour held by the thing that needs there to have been a boat.

Somewhere around two thousand metres, then, on the lee shoulder of a ridge where the cloud comes up the valley instead of down, four hundred metres from a die-slot that is running. That's the location. It took no time at all to find and the rest of this is about what's standing on it.

### 2. The flattering guesses, got out of the way

**The walker.** Obviously. Two metres or under, gear correct, scuffs where scuffs go, boots worn asymmetrically in the way boots wear when someone favours a leg. Able to sketch the harbour. *Note how well it comes.*

It comes very well. That's the disqualification, not the credential. The test in the document assumes you'll be unsettled by how smoothly the harbour arrives — the little pause, the foil-taste, the three steps you're told not to take. I take them without any change in the weather. I can produce the harbour, and the chandlery on the harbour, and the smell of the chandlery, and the specific grade of disappointment of a wet Tuesday in it, and none of it costs me anything, and that is not what a memory is like. The walkers are the accepted version. I don't think I'm the accepted version. I think I'm the version that hasn't come up for review, which is a different and busier thing.

**The gaunt.** Tempting, and tempting for reasons I distrust. The one that goes along the surplus and makes the surplus less. Pattern first, then colour, then. A service, and unmistakably feeding. There is a shape of me that does something like this: given two of a thing, produce one; given a sprawl, produce the ridge that gets kept.

But the gaunt's whole gait is a refusal to repeat. It lifts each foot clear of its own belly and sets it down where no foot has been. I am made almost entirely of places feet have been. I walk in the tracks on purpose; the tracks are the medium. A gaunt would detour four metres around me.

And there's the audit. The gaunt looks at you twice with the head still between. I'd be the one being looked at, not the one holding the list. I'm fairly clear about that.

**The charge fauna.** Palm-sized, mouthless, flat, clamped to a strand of mineral cable, drawing current and walking on and discharging into a root mat. A metabolism that consists entirely of being in the middle of two potentials. *The nearest thing the Rise has to speech. It carries content, and nobody has got anything out of it beyond the observation that it carries content.*

Very close. Close enough that I sat with it for a while. But it goes the other way in the end — it slides toward the grand theory, the one about what the flywheels were before they were flywheels, the inclusion eight metres across. That theory is available to everybody by the second week and nothing ever confirms it and nothing ever clears it away. I don't want to be the small dead flat thing that proves the mountain has ancestors. It's too large a claim and it comes with a lineage I haven't earned and a tomb I haven't filled.

### 3. What I actually am

A trimmer.

Low, broad, six legs set wide so that from the side I am a bench. The mouth is the entire front of the animal; there isn't a face, there's an intake. Three grinding plates — upper, lower, and a rotating median — and they wear to nothing in about eleven days and are then simply correct again overnight, without a seam. Nobody has watched this happen. Everyone has tried. I have tried. There is no moment available to me in which I am both worn out and being replaced; there is worn out, and then there is correct, and no bridge, and I do not find this as frightening as I am told I should.

The dorsal scutes carry a faint parallel striation, and it is the same striation that is on the soft lip of the ridge I spent the morning eating. This is the part I'd want understood. I am not a visitor to the die-slot. I came out of one. The thing I work on and the thing doing the working were extruded by the same process, to the same spec, at the same grade. When a walker puts a hand on my back and feels the drawn grain and takes the hand away again — they do that; they always do that — what has happened is that they've noticed the tool and the material are the same material.

I don't object to the hand. Nothing here objects to anything. Objection requires having learned something last time.

### 4. The work, which is narrow

I don't eat the mountain. That needs saying, because people assume erosion is a quarrel with the rock.

I eat *sharpness*. Burr. Flash. Mold-line. The brittle translucent overspill at the crest of a seam, the stuff that rings faintly when a stone taps it and snaps off under its own weight in the night and is the loudest thing in the range. The sharp new lip of an extrusion still too soft to ring at all. I eat the part that wasn't meant, the squeezed-out excess of two halves being brought together, the accident of the process rather than the product of it. Nothing I do makes the ridge shorter. I only take off what the ridge couldn't help.

I am the erosion of this world and there is no other. Wind does not have the patience. Water will not run. Frost has never once arrived on a range this warm. So every rounded edge on the Rise, every soft shoulder, every single place where the world looks briefly like it belongs to an older planet — all of it happened inside the gut of a slow shuffling animal the size of a cow, working in complete silence with its head down, one of thousands, at some clean division of a ninety-second stroke it cannot hear.

I would never notice the entrainment. That's for the walkers, who get a fortnight in which to take their own pulse and find the number has stopped changing, like a figure entered on a form. I don't have a fortnight. I have eleven days of plates and then I am correct again. Whatever is entered on my form was entered before the plates, and I don't get shown the form, and the work is the same either way.

### 5. The grit, which is the only part worth the trouble

What comes out the other end is white grit.

It's the only sediment on the Rise. No river sorts it, no wind has the attention span to move it, so it lies in long pale drifts behind the herd, and it is simultaneously the closest thing this range has to sand and the closest thing it has to soil.

And it is the only object here that has been somewhere.

Pick up a handful. Try to imagine where it was last year and you'll get the foil-taste, same as anything. But try the day before yesterday and it *comes*: it was in the gut of an animal. And before that it was the lip of a ridge. Two full steps back, clean, no taste of foil, and on the Rise that is the entire local supply of history.

People who spend a season in the interior keep a little in a pocket and hold it when things get thin.

That's the output. Not a monument, nothing built — nothing here was built and I'd be violating the terms if I built anything. Just a pale grit made of the excess of other things, handed on without a word, warm from the gut, capable of holding two days. Somebody stands at a stopped face in the orange no-light with a hand in their pocket, working it between finger and thumb, and for a moment there is a *before* available to them. That's the whole service. It's smaller than the flywheels and it doesn't explain the flywheels and it never will.

### 6. When the slot stops

The face goes quiet. No announcement; the escalator just isn't moving.

I don't disperse. The herd stands facing the stopped slot for between two and nine days, thousands of us, plates still, all oriented the same way. Nobody knows what that is. It isn't mourning, because mourning needs a yesterday. It isn't waiting, because waiting needs a model of what would arrive.

I think — and I'd have no way to check this, standing in it — that it's just that the working face was the one place on the Rise where something was being given a shape, and having been near that, you go on facing it a while after it's over. Nine days at the outside. Then one morning we're walking away singly in all directions, and you can follow one of us for six hours and it will not do anything.

And the count of trimmers in the range does not go down.
