I keep trying to put myself on the rim, and the Well keeps refusing it.

The rim is where the eye goes — that flat pavement, that unannounced hole, the smell of hot green coming up out of the stone like a rumour. It is the best view in the world and it is the one place a person is not. Ten paces back from the lip is a country with a name that also means *spilled*. To stand up there for an hour is to be a story told at supper: *the trader from below stood on the rim looking down with his mouth open, losing water the whole time.* I would be the traveller, the describer, the one who explains the arrangement to you — four hundred metres of gypsum breathing out, the thermos with a garden in it — and describing is exactly what nobody inside does, because nobody inside needs it. They have no word for *ecosystem*. They have a word for *the inside of a thing*, and it means here.

So: down, then. And on the way down I try on all the large things, the way you do.

The fig, obviously. To be the strangler across the mouth, the one whose crown makes the light green and whose resin oils every child's skin and cements every roof — to be older than the town and load-bearing in every sense. But that isn't a self, that's a monument, and monuments have no interiority, only maintenance. The vapour, then: to be the exhalation itself, the last breath of a mineral cooked on a three-billion-year fire, rising through fractures at night when the plateau cools. That's not a person either. That's a god, and this place is far too practical to have one.

A bat, next, because two hundred thousand of them go out over four hundred kilometres of dead pavement every night and come back with moths in them, and because being the only thing in the world that leaves and returns has an obvious appeal. But I don't want to leave. That's the tell. Everyone who wants the bat wants to leave.

A guano-man, then. That is a real want, and I should be honest about it: the leather hood, the resin lamp, the flute-mouths going back into the rock, the black crumbly fortune of the place carried out on your own back, the best pay and the highest standing and the mites. I want it the way you want to be admired. Which is precisely the appetite this town has organised itself against. *Hurry is drinking.* Ambition is a form of sweating.

Nearly, then — nearly — a carver of bungs. This is the closest I get to the truth by guessing. Somebody in the Well spends their life making small plugs of dried barrel-rind, one per tap, each one shaped to its own slot in its own storage-stem on its own terrace, so that when a household opens a stem their grandparents planted, nothing falls. It is a whole craft dedicated to the negative: not a drop, not a bead, not a dark spot on the mat. The bungs outlive their carvers. Every household has three or four that are older than anyone alive and are known by the name of the woman who cut them.

But I am not the one who stops water leaving. I am the one who carries it back.

—

I grow moss.

Not the felt that grows everywhere, on everything, in the wall's shade. Mine is combed, cultivated, kept in shallow frames of termite board hung in the mouths of the north flutes where the night air is thickest, and it has hairs finer than the wild kind because I have been selecting them since I was eleven, as my aunt did before me, and her aunt. It harvests vapour out of the air the way a fleece harvests fog. When it is thick enough I cut it into hand-sized rectangles, back each one with a sheet of board, pin it with cactus spine, and carry the tray up the town in the seventh hour, when the sun-coin is off the terraces and people are home.

They are mourning-pads. Every room in the Well has one, in a niche by the door, at the height of a standing adult's face.

Adults here do not weep. Children weep and are held and are not shamed for it; then, somewhere around the fourth or fifth year, they are taught the other thing, which is to take the pad down with both hands, press the whole face into it, and breathe until the fit has gone through them and out. The water stays in the house. That is the entire reason, and the town states it flatly, without any decoration, the way it states that a person is forty litres.

It amounts to nothing. I want to be clear about the quantities, because I have measured them and my aunt measured them and there is a knotted cord in my room going back six generations. A pad used hard for twenty years holds perhaps a cupful. Against a household's daily draw from its stems it is a rounding error. Against the vapour coming up out of the gypsum it is not even that.

We do it anyway, with tremendous care, and that is the thing I would be inside of. This is a people who have taken one of the smallest fluxes in their world and built an office around it, staffed it, made it hereditary, given it a word — *ohalume*, carrying-back — and I would rather be that than the fig. Because the accounting is exact and the accounting is not the point. The point is that when your daughter has died in the fourth hour and the ants are coming through at the sixth, somebody arrives at your door in the seventh with a clean pad, and does not say anything, and takes the old one down off its pin with two hands, the way you lift something full.

Then I carry it to the mat.

Not the common floor. The mat under that household's own stand of storage-succulents, where the root-mass is warm from below. I open it with my fingers, not a tool, and put the pad in face-down, and close it. In eleven years the household will cut a slot in one of those stems and plug it and their grandchildren will drink from it, and the sap will be cool and bitter and faintly slimy and ninety-six percent water, and some fraction of that fraction will be what the family didn't spill in the year they lost the girl.

Nobody says this at the taps. Everybody knows it. It is the flat fact under the six proverbs everybody says too often.

—

You will have noticed that the account of us stops mid-sentence: *it is buried with the person you are mourning when* —

Here is the rest, and it is why the sentence broke.

There is no number. There is no rule of a year, or three, or until the flower comes. The pad goes into the mat when it has stopped taking anything, and only the person using it knows when that is, and to ask them is ruder than asking their lines. So the answer to *when* is: when you can. And the reason no one finishes the sentence out loud is that finishing it requires saying, in the low carrying chest-register that has no sibilants in it and can be heard across the whole roaring Well, *when you are done loving them as hard as that*, and nobody has ever got to the end of it without reaching up to the niche.

I have a pad under my sleeping shelf that I have not buried. Four lines old — nearly five, if the fig masts this decade. It is dry as bone and holds nothing and I know to the drop what it holds.

They give the work to people like me on purpose. You want it done by someone who can carry the arithmetic in one hand and still keep a cup back with the other. A town this small, in a hole this warm, at four thousand metres, with the walls giving up one block a century and the future named after the last leaf before the flower — a town like that does not survive on being correct. It survives on being correct about almost everything, and unreasonable about one small thing each, quietly, at home, in the dark, under the noise.
