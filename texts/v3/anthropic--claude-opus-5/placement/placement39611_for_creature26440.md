She stands a moment in the wet with the leaf-mat still in her hand, and she is not resting. She is waiting for the note to come back.

It takes about four seconds. The water finds the drop, the drop finds its pitch, the corner says what it has always said. Then she goes in barefoot and does not wake her husband and that is the end of it.

I am three benches above her with my lamp lit, and I have been in my doorway for the whole of it, which is perhaps a minute and a half.

I heard the corner stop. It is not my corner. I do not use that stair and I have never spoken to that woman, though I know her name, because everyone knows everyone's name. I heard it stop the way you hear your own name said in another room. There are perhaps two hundred grates whose voices are in me without my consent, and at night they do not stay in the part of my head where I keep them. So I woke, and counted, and thought: ninth bench, the corner under the rowan works — and then thought, as one does, *someone will be up*, and stood in the door to find out whether that was true or whether it was going to have to be me, forty paces down and back in the rain.

She was up. She is always up. I went in.

Before I came out I moved the cricket onto the landing and shut the door on it. I buy one every spring like everybody else, from the man who brings them in jars up the switchbacks, and I feed it strawberry ends, and every spring there comes a night when I carry it out and shut it in the cold because I cannot hear the valley through it. It dies in autumn like all of them. I buy another. There is no argument for this. A cricket costs a morning.

## The hammer

The hammer is eight ounces with a long springy handle of ash, and you do not swing it. You hold it loose between finger and thumb, down near the head for close work, out at the end for reach, and you let it fall and bounce, fall and bounce, walking the face of the wall in a grid you carry in your knees.

The wall answers twice. Once in the air, which is the part people think is the whole of it, and once back up the handle into the wrist, which is the part that actually tells you. A sound face gives you a short dry tick and takes the hammer back off the stone like a struck bell wants to be let go. A face with a void behind it holds the hammer half a heartbeat too long and gives you a note with a floor under it, lower, softer, further away than the stone you are touching. That is the whole trade. Everything else is knowing which lower, softer, further-away notes are new.

There are words for them: a face *rings*, *tocks*, *chatters*, *drums*, *mutters*, or *goes dead*, and these are not synonyms; they are a sequence, and the last one means send for a foreman tonight. I have twenty-odd terms where a visitor has one, and I have watched a visitor's face while I used four of them in a sentence, and the expression is always the same, and it is the expression of a man deciding that a place is slightly mad and slightly better than him.

I cannot repair anything. That must be said plainly. I carry no trowel, no mortar, no bar. I carry the hammer, three chalks, a rod for the high courses, a folded knee-mat of felted wool, and a slate. I go along the wall and I make marks on it, and other people come with the stone. Sometimes a whole week's work of mine is a hundred and forty ticks in white chalk meaning *yes, still* — and then, at the end of a Thursday, one ring in red around eleven feet of a bench face, which will cost the terrace board a crew and a fortnight and will make a hole in the world you could put a cart in.

My hands are the wrong hands. Not wall hands: I lift nothing, my nails are ordinary, my palm has one callus, on the web of the thumb, the size of a coin. Not brush hands either — they are outdoor hands, split at the knuckle from the lime and the cold, and no lily shed would have them near a stamen. Wet trade thinks I am dry, because I do not tamp and I do not bleed. Dry trade thinks I am wet, because I am out in it eleven hours a day on my knees in the fog. I am neither. I am the thing in between the two, which is attention, and the valley has never quite decided what to pay it. I am paid by the chain of wall. Which means that a slow sounder earns less than a fast one, and everybody knows what that does, and there are men who can do a bench in a morning and are used when the board is behind and are not asked to sound anything anyone loves.

## The hollows
