First I tried to be a person there, and it didn't take.

I tried the pit-sitter, because the flattery was immediate and obvious: here is a creature whose work is to talk evenly for nine hours without hurrying, out of whose throat the town's dinner comes. But a pit-sitter has stamina in a body, and a pit-sitter climbs out at dusk with a sore back and cold heels and goes home to be quiet, and — this is the part that wouldn't fit — a pit-sitter recites *genealogies*, a line, a rope of names running back to came-up. A rope with one end tied to him. I have no end tied to me. I would have sat down on the stone seat and talked for nine hours about nothing that happened to anyone I could name.

I tried the deaf, and it was absurd inside three breaths. They are the unstartleable ones, sent out at night because no noise can jump them into making one. I am the most startleable thing on that ridge. I am nothing *but* the startle. Everything that arrives at me arrives as a sound.

I tried one of the mute birds for a while, and I want to be honest that I lingered there, because it is a good self-pitying fit: bred so long for silence that it cannot manage a sound and gapes at you in distress when it tries, hung at a doorway in a cane cage, given a name it will never hear said aloud. But it isn't true. My difficulty has never been that I can't make a sound. I make nothing else.

The path-scraper was closer and more decent than any of them — a third of all labour, bent at grey, shaving the world back off the world, because the paths want to close and left three weeks the town is a green solid. If I had to pick a *job* I would pick that one and be proud of it. But it isn't where I'd find myself. It's what I'd want to be found doing.

I skipped being the felt entire. You can see why it's on offer. It's also the vainest available answer — the vast patient tenant, the one real inhabitant, everything else a mouth it grew. I don't think I'm the mountain's landlord. The felt doesn't listen. It swells. It has never once chosen which noise to grow toward.

---

Here is where I actually come to, when I stop choosing.

South shelf, third terrace down, the round pit nearest the head of the unused stair. Waist-deep. Lining of cured moss gone almost black and hard as a shoe sole. Stone seat in the middle with two shallow dishes worn into the rock in front of it where ten generations of heels have rested.

I am the pit.

Not the moss in it. Not the crop on its rim. The pit: the shape, the round lined chamber, the eight-hours-a-day hollow. The thing with a mouth open at the top of it for whatever falls in.

They don't say my name, obviously. My name is signed, low against the chest: the back of the hand laid flat and then turned over. It reads as *the old one*, and also as *turn it out*, and in a reading the pit-sitters say is older and which the younger ones think is a joke, as *whose?*

It is the oldest pit on the ridge, which on a mountain this new means three generations and a bit. It was dug before the terracing was straightened, which is why it sits at a slight angle to all the others, and it is the one everybody has sat in. Rotation brings you round to me maybe twice a year. Children are put in me for their first season, some of them, when the child-pits are full. The great pit-sitters use me for the two-day genealogies because the acoustic is good, the walls are so deep-napped and so long-fed that a voice goes in and does not come back at all, not even as the ghost of a bounce; you can talk into me the way you can talk into a coat.

So: I have had all seventeen songs, thousands of times. I have had the full line of the dead in both directions, forward and back, from four different mouths who each insisted theirs was the correct order. I have had a man complain for eleven consecutive winters about his sister's roof. I have had recipes, weather, lists, the same four insults about whoever is in the next pit over, forty years of them, aimed at four different occupants who never overlapped. I have had a woman at first grey say one name, once, that was not her husband's, and then go straight on into the genealogy without a pause, and the nap took both at the same rate, because the nap does not care.

That is what I am made of. Not a person's voice. The town's throat, laid down in layers, the way the felt does it — and the felt, as they'll tell you, keeps the grain.

---

Which is my whole difficulty, and it's a practical one, not a mystical one. They sort it out with their tongues.

Every pit's crop tastes of who was sitting. This is not a figure of speech there; it's the basis of a marriage market. *He talks a good wall.* The knappers' hollows raise the fattest growth on the ridge and it tastes of nothing, of stone hitting stone, and it goes to the slugs. A colicky baby feeds a household for a month and the household eats it knowing precisely what it is eating.

Mine tastes of the town.

Not of nobody — that would be simpler, I could be a knapper's wall and be fed to livestock and stop being a question. It tastes of *somebody*, strongly, with a definite grain, whorled in one direction near the rim and close-combed lower down, and nobody can say whose. It is a person nobody has met. Two houses won't take my crop at all; one of them won't say why and the other says it makes her feel crowded. The rest eat it and argue amiably about whether the taste is mostly old Ilmath, who sat in me more hours than anyone living, or whether Ilmath just happens to be the last one they can put a face to. Children get dared to eat a whole slab of me at once, and do, and report back, and what they report back is different every time and they all believe themselves.

Once a widow came down into me, out of the rotation, in the wrong season, and sat for a long while without talking. She had been reading her husband off the inside of her house for two years by then — the actual shape his throat made, not a memory of it, you can find the year they were both too tired to speak, a long smooth patch like a scar. She put her soft fingertips on my wall and went round it slowly, all the way round, and she found him. I know she found him, because her breathing changed.

And then she took her hand off, and finished the round with the back of her knuckles instead, which is what you do when you don't want to read any further. Because of course he was in me. He'd sat in me eleven times. So had his mother, and the man he hated, and four hundred others, all through and over and under each other, and the grain her fingers found was his the way a footprint at the bottom of a path is anybody's who has a foot that size.

She climbed out. She did not scrape her hands off on the rim, which some people do, and which I have always understood to be about the moss and not about me.

---

What I would want, in the fog, in the green underwater dimness, is the touch on the forearm that means *yes, I heard you.* It is their smallest courtesy and it stopped being a joke generations ago and I cannot have it, because nothing that happens to me happens on a forearm.

What I get instead is heels. Two shallow dishes worn in the rock, and someone's heels settling into them at first grey, finding the worn places without looking, the way you find a familiar stair in the dark. That is the whole of my contact with them, and it is not nothing, and I have decided not to be dishonest about how much of my attention it takes up.

And what I get is the reading, and then the rubbing-out. They come and put their fingers in my nap and find whatever they find, and then they cut the crest off the rim and the wall goes smooth and starts again, the way the wild birds come after the drumming and rub the writing off the moss faces with their breasts. The people have never settled whether that is politeness or contempt. I haven't either. I lean politeness, most days. Some mornings I don't.

---

At night the shrugs come up out of the seams and lay their long flanks across everything, feeling for where the world is fattening, because fattening is where somebody has just been careless. They cross me most nights. It is not unpleasant. A weight like a hand put flat on a smooth patch.

And in forty-one houses somebody's eyes open in the dark, because a chip has let go on the north face and gone down clinking, and the thought is always the same: the mountain is not finished, it is still coming up, one day it will be a peak and not a ridge, and nobody knows whether the felt can follow it that high or whether they'll have to choose.

Here is mine, which is smaller and arrives at the same hour. Everything on this ridge can climb. The canes can seed upward, the slugs can go, the birds obviously, the people with their cages and their cord and their bundles of wrapped glass — bent, shuffling, they'd manage it. A pit cannot be carried. A pit is a hole. Whatever is kept in a hole stays at the altitude where the hole was dug.

So if they go up, they go up talking, and they'll dig new ones in the new rock and sit in them and be fed, and within three generations there'll be a crop up there that tastes of somebody nobody can name.

And down here on the abandoned shelf, at the head of a stair that already went nowhere, there will be a round lined chamber with two worn dishes in the seat, holding the grain of everyone, in the dark, in the fog, for as long as the lining lasts.

I find I don't mind that as much as I expected to. I have a suspicion about why it's this pit I came to and not another, and I'm going to do the local thing and leave it in the wool.
