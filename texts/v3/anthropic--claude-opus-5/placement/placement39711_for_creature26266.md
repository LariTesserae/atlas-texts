## I. Two wrong answers, which have to be got out of the way

The first thing I want to be is the stone, and that is a lie, and everybody up here would know it in four seconds, the way they place a stranger's valley from one whistle.

It's an attractive lie. Finished. Past ambition. Three thousand metres of patience with its corners rounded like a much-handled coin, no longer carrying mountains, no longer carrying anything, lying under all this commotion and unbothered by it. Anyone would want to be the part the water could not argue with. But the plateau has a word for people, and the word is *mat*, and the word is not modest, it is simply accurate: two metres of moss and root and black wet nothing that holds for forty years and then goes off a dome in a single afternoon. You are the skin. You are never the stone. To claim otherwise here is not blasphemy, just bad arithmetic.

The second thing I want to be is a wheel-house, and that one is closer, and it takes longer to put down.

Consider it. A dark riveted bulk pinned to the rock beside a chute, moss on the shingles, a jet the diameter of your wrist, and a Pelton wheel that has not stopped in ninety years because there is no reason to stop it and no way to store what arrives. Nobody in the shed at night. The governor turning its weighted balls with idiot patience. Power here is embarrassingly free — it comes forever, it cannot be hoarded, and you may run your grindstone off my penstock and it would be strange to thank me, as strange as thanking a person for the use of their rain. I recognise that economy from the inside. Arriving continuously, unstorable, faintly humming, of no value because there is no shortage.

But the wheel cannot tell yesterday from today. It has no record. It turns in an eternal present and would turn just as sweetly if the whole plateau slid off around it. And I find I want, badly, to know what moved.

So not the stone and not the wheel. Something between them: something that holds for a while and writes down what changed while it held.

## II. The week

They call it week-work, which translates badly, like everything they say about themselves. When I come into a crossing out of the cloud, the shout that goes up — hand already on my forearm, because a voice dies at a metre and a half — is *the week's in.* And then, immediately, before food, before the ember, before anything: **what's moved?**

I draw the plateau. I am, at any given moment, the least accurate person on it.

The maps are in pencil, and this is not laziness. Ink runs. Ink is a substance that believes in dry air, and there is no dry air here, only a permanent hung drift that accumulates on you without falling. Graphite is a mineral. Graphite does not care. You can draw a line on a soaked sheet with a stick of graphite bound in resin-dipped cane and the line will still be there in the morning, grey and slightly smeared, and you can lift it with a wet thumb when the stream it describes has gone somewhere else. That is the whole tool: a rock that marks, on a plateau that has been reduced to its rocks, and can be rubbed out with water, like everything else here.

Pencil is the local form of humility. To carve would be to claim rigidity, and anything rigid fails.

The paper is pressed sphagnum and pounded root fibre, sized with resin, tissue-thin, four sheets to a night's work by bulb-light. The archive is not carried. It lives in offcuts of iron penstock with screw caps, hung inside the wheel-houses along the ceiling beside the line shafts, because the housing of a bearing that has been running since before anyone's grandmother is the only warm dry place on this plateau. My life's work sits against the machines, sweating slightly, tended by half-deaf men who oil it out of politeness without ever opening a cap.

## III. What the stack does

There is one sheet that is not tissue and not pencil. It's a plate of soft grey metal, scraped rather than drawn, and my great-grandmother made it: the domes, the whalebacks, the ribbed pavements, the joint-grooves, the slots, and the line of the Weeping Edge. The stone sheet. In a hundred and thirty years it has needed one amendment. My grandmother made it — a notch where three metres of the lip went into the whiteness and never reported back. Four generations of women, one correction. It is the only evidence anybody up here has that the stone is still, very slowly, in motion. I check it every year, and I cannot tell you what I am hoping for.

Everything else is overlay. Tissue on tissue, dated to the week, laid on the plate: the streams, the shifting braids, the mats that held and the mats that went, the new grey scars fuzzing green in two months and ankle-deep in three years, the log cribs, the garden boxes, the root-heads of the growing bridges with the names written at the anchors — *second span, the Chute: her father* — because the ancestors are load-bearing and someone should keep the register of who exactly you are walking across.

And once the stack for a valley is thick enough, you can flip it.

That is the part the children come for. *Run the water,* they shout, with a squeeze at the wrist, meaning *yes, go on,* and I thumb forty years past in eight seconds and a stream crawls across a dome like something alive, doubling back, taking a grove with it, abandoning a gorge that daylight still goes down green. Nobody else on this plateau ever sees the place move. They live inside it at the wrong speed. I am the only time-lapse there is.

The old people mostly won't watch. It shows the dirt going away, and dirt is wealth here — dirt is dowry, dirt is what the will is about — and a flipped stack shows you exactly where four generations of ash and bone and crab shell and night soil went over the edge into whiteness. The streams take and never bring back. That is the local sin and the local grief and they are the same thing.

## IV. Two things I know that nobody asked me to know

The first is the tuning. Every fall has a pitch, and every valley whistles above its own fall, which is why a person's home can be placed from one call before you see a face. So my maps are partly a score: five cane reeds in my coat, and the falls written as notes, so that a traveller in cloud can read the sheet with their ears and match it against the roar — *that's the Chute on my right, so I have not turned around.* A map you can hear is not a metaphor here. It is the only kind that works two hundred days a year.

Which means my stack records the plateau slowly retuning itself. Channels shift; a fall thins; its pitch drifts up a little over sixty years; and the dialect of a valley, which is literally tuned to falling water, drifts after it a generation late. There is a man at Three Sisters, ninety-one, deaf as a stone and not thought to have lost anything much, whose whistle is *wrong* — a broad two-note pitched for a gap in the harmonics of a fall that stopped existing when he was four. His accent is correct for a stream that is not there. He is the last speaker of a water that has gone. I have never found a way to tell him this that is worth the telling, so I have written it down instead, which is what I do with everything.

The second thing I know is that my archive is food.

Pressed moss, root fibre, resin, and four kilos of phosphorus in a country with none. At Three Sisters, in a bad season, a household put eleven years of overlays into their garden box, and the beans were good, and I don't blame them, and I would not have let them starve to keep a decade of channel shifts. The graphite is inert; it stays. There is a box up there with a faint grey grit in the black dirt, and the children of that house are made, in a small measurable part, out of the years I drew. That is the local form of immortality and I have no better one to offer. My own overlays will go into a root-head with the rest of me when I stop holding, and grow across a gorge, and carry a loaded mule.
