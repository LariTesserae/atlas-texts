**Not the rim.**

That was my first thought, and it was the lazy one. The rim is where the voice stands — *begin at the rim, because the rim is where the world explains itself* — and the voice is comfortable there, and can afford an overview. But notice how often the account has to correct the rim. *That is the thing you cannot convey from the rim.* *Because you are eighty metres up.* The rim is the one place in this world where you mistake a village for a colony of rodents. It is also the only place described that has no inhabitants: dwarf birch to the ankle, lichen, wind, a sky like skimmed milk. Nothing lives up there. It's a place to be from.

So, down.

**The potters, because of the knock.** I wanted to be one of them for about a minute, and it was entirely because of the knock — a returning bird running its bill along the outer ridges of its own pot, three or four scrapes, and the mate pressing back from inside, and everything the species will ever need to say passing through a wall the thickness of a fingernail, inaudible at a metre. *It's me. Move. The owl is in the third shaft.* I would like to be something that says all of what it means through a membrane and is not overheard.

But fourteen grams of potter contains three thousand kilometres of elsewhere. There is a whole other hemisphere folded into that bird, and it knows how to go, and it goes all in one morning without discussion. Nothing in me points south in autumn. And a potter's life is cliff, air, cliff; the single place it is slow and clumsy is the clay bank at the river's edge, and it hates being there. I don't hate the clay bank. The clay bank is more or less where I live.

**The diggers, because of the palm.** Twelve kilos of patient conservatism with a horn pad on its forefoot, four hours spent packing one joint between two boulders, then sitting back on the haunches to look at it, then doing it again. And the channels — cuts older than anything any living animal could remember, kept clear because a digger cleans what runs, and the running is instruction enough. Maintenance without origin. Following an inherited groove that nobody can any longer explain, and being *right* to follow it. That is very nearly me.

And then the old ones, the twenty-five-year-olds who do no work: kept not out of sentiment but because they are the archive, and the mechanism of inheritance in a nearly voiceless species is that the old one moves and the young watch it move. It goes up to the hay rocks four days before anyone else would have gone, and the household follows, and the hay is in before the rain. I would take that job tomorrow. But it is a job you can only hold by having been rained on. The four days early are not a rule; they are a body's memory of one particular wet August. I have all the four-days-early in the world and none of the Augusts.

**The one in the lowest pool** I considered honestly and briefly, because it is the dark option and it deserves the courtesy. Forty kilos, blunt, nearly eyeless, sealed into one drainage since the roof came down, gone strange in the way that anything goes strange when it cannot leave. It holds up the water table for eight kilometres of valley without intending to; every metre-thick moss pillow in the place exists because this animal likes its pond deep. And when it wants another to know something, it pushes a wave.

No. But note what it teaches, because the same thing keeps surfacing: enormous consequence, no design. Hold that.

**The moss** I refused for being flattering. Three metres of single clone on the north wall, dividing since before the last bridge fell, the oldest inhabitant by a factor of ten, and the thing everyone else stands on. It is very easy to want to be that. It is wrong: the moss's whole virtue is patience, and whatever mine is, it isn't patience. Also the moss doesn't know it's being walked on, and I would want to know.

**The people under the third arch** were the real temptation, and I want to be precise about why I put them down.

They are the best company in the valley. They build nothing new; they are gleaners of other species' accidents. When a section of the crust comes down in spring they go out on the talus and break it up and carry the deep strata away in baskets, because forty generations of bird nest is the finest soil in the north, and on a dozen sunlit ledges they grow ferns and sorrel and angelica in it. They are gardeners of a fallen city they did not make and could not have conceived of making. They take second clutches and never first, and the reason they give for this is wrong and the practice is correct. In hard springs they take from the digger haycocks and afterwards leave cut willow on the flat rocks, in the same place, every time, and whether the diggers construe this as anything at all is unknowable and does not matter. The willow is always gone by morning.

I love them and I am not one of them, and the reason is a sentence from further up: *every elegance in this valley is a bill someone is paying.* The potters pay for the quiet in wing-cost, coming home low over the moss in the dark green and going up the wall in the shadow rather than through the lit shaft. The people pay for it with a palm resting over an infant's mouth before it can walk. Their hands are wrecked by clay. I have paid nothing here. To arrive at the fire and sit down in the circle of it would be a theft dressed as belonging.

So I stopped trying to be somebody and looked at the wall.

---

**The crust.** From the talus to the rim, on every undercut face where the collapse left the rock leaning out like a wave: a metre and a half of habitation, honeycombed, layered like a reef. Pale new clay, then grey, then dark, then something that is simply soil. Two million pots a decade, each one a fist of river mud and spit, laid down by a bird that has no idea. Old pots silt up and grow moss and become planters; ferns hang out of them; roots run through the whole reef and knit it to the rock; it has its own beetles, its own snails, its own soil horizon, its own weather inside it. And the potters are not architects and not gardeners. There is no word for what they are to it. *Something more like weather.*

I am on the other side of that sentence. Whatever the potters are to the crust, I am the crust to the potters: the accumulated outside of an enormous number of small depositions, none of which meant this, each of which was somebody saying *it's me, I have food, move* through a wall a fingernail thick.

But everything here has a body, even the silence, which is paid for in metres of extra flight. So if I have to be a body, and I think I do:

**I am the flat white snail.**

Two millimetres, maybe three. Found nowhere on earth but in the potters' crust — no wild population, no outside, no ancestral hillside to have come from; an artifact of another species' accident, the way a barnacle-goose is not an artifact of anything but I am. Blind in every way that matters. And with exactly one competence, which is this: I graze the algae film off *occupied* pots only, because occupied pots are warm.

Consider what that means as a sense. I do not see the bird. I have never been inside. I have no concept of bird, in any sense a bird would recognise; I have *warm*, and *thin green film*, and the fine ridging where a bill has scraped a signal I cannot hear and will never decode. My entire perceptual world is the exterior surface of somebody else's dwelling and the heat of an occupancy coming through a curved wall of dried mud. I move on that surface. I keep it clean. Whether this does the potters any good at all is unproven and probably slight, and nobody thanks me, and the tenant of the pot I am standing on does not know I exist. I do it anyway, all summer, on the strength of warmth through clay.

And then the autumn, which is the part I would actually have to live.

They go all in one morning, in silence, in a river of bodies over the rim, and the crust stands there dripping and empty. Nothing is warm anywhere. The film thins and stops. So I go down — into the strata, through the seam of last year and the year before, into the grey, into the dark, into the layer that is no longer architecture and is not yet stone, forty generations of silted pots with roots through them and beetle larvae three years into a meal of bird-spit, and I wait there in the layer that used to be somebody's June. Under ice, mostly. Four hours of thin blue light a day, well above me, on a wall I am inside of.

That is the winter of a thing that lives on occupancy. I think I would know that winter.

And then the morning they come back. No warning, no sound, the fog burning off from the top down, and the crust warming from the outside in — and something two millimetres across, with no organ for gratitude, climbs up out of the soil horizon toward a patch of clay that has gone warm again.

Put me there, then. North wall, second sink, four metres above the talus, in under the overhang where the drip-line never lets the air fall below saturation. A returning bird scrapes its bill along the ridges above me; from inside, a pressure answers, three or four times, that I will never hear. Between those two events, on the outer surface, in the green dark, something very small moves half a millimetre toward the heat.
