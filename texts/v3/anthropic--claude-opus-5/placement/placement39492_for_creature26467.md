My first instinct is the chough, and I want to say why before I give it up.

They come in over the drop in the afternoons, when the light has nothing left to do but be even. They are the only things on the shelf that *arrive*. Everything else there is continuous with itself: the stone was already there, the water comes and returns, the junipers neither grow nor die, the people are dressed out of a set of sixty-three names that has never been added to. But a chough is a piece of somewhere else, walking on the roof slabs with its head on one side, looking down a smoke hole at your supper with a frank and unearned opinion. It steals a copper needle and is discussed for a decade. It is the entire comedy of a place it takes no responsibility for.

I'd like that. It's obvious why. But a chough puts nothing into the groove and nothing into the thickness, and — this is the disqualification that matters — a chough is never checked. Nobody takes a chough's hand and turns it over and presses the pad of the thumb. Nobody brings it slowly to a dimension. And when I read about the children there, dressed rather than raised, checked and brought closer and checked, I find I want that much more than I want to be delightful.

So, second guess: cord-keeper. Patience for knots, memory for which anomaly happened in which decade, the one faint purple band on the left cuff and no authority whatsoever. That's nearly right, and I distrust it slightly, because I notice I want the band. And there's a better objection: a cord-keeper is chosen for a long memory, and mine is a near memory. I'd be very good at the hair's difference in a joint at arm's length in flat cloud light. I'd be no good at four generations. I'd have to walk to the niche and read the knots with my fingers every time, like everyone else.

Then again — that's what the cords are *for*. Nobody's head is trusted there, not even the keeper's. So I'll leave it as a maybe, and go where everybody goes.

---

I'd be a mason, because they all are. I'd have one of the sixty-three names, whichever had lain by longest when I was born, and it would not be mine. Say Sabb. There would be an old woman still alive who had been Sabb for sixty-one years, and she would have died the spring before I was born, and nobody would look at me and see her, because they don't do that. A name is a position. You occupy it and then you vacate it and the knot hangs slack until it's wanted.

At six I'd start walking the round. Half an hour, before the first fog lifts. Eleven junipers, checked at the crotch of the trunk for frost-split; eleven cistern slabs lifted; the level at the groove. Six mesh screens, palm down the weave, feeling for slack. Two garden walls. Eleven goats. Then back to the public floor to say *all at the groove.*

What I'd be best at, I think, is the listening. Every cistern has its drip and every drip has its interval, and children are set to learn them because it is how you navigate on a black night and because a drip that changes means the rock has moved. I'd have the fifth cistern's hesitation by heart — the one that goes long every ninth or tenth beat, a hitch you can feel in the chest before you hear it. I'd know which house I was near with my eyes shut, by water and by the polish under the ball of my foot, and I'd know it the way you know your own name is being said in another room.

At fifteen I'd want to go down. Everyone does; it comes on like weather. They'd give me a lamp and a short adze and a basket and permission to open a buried threshold, and I'd spend four years under the town in the dark.

Here is what I think I'd find, and it's the reason they'd never send me down the mountain. Somewhere in the second floor under the fourth house there'd be a room whose door I couldn't find. Sealed on every side by fill and by the floor of a room built over it two centuries ago. And its floor would be worn. Not roughly — worn smooth in a curve across the middle, the way a threshold wears, the way a path across the shelf wears, which means people crossed it, many of them, for a long time, entering by some way that no longer exists and cannot be reconstructed. I'd bring up the rubble, which is always wanted, and the measurements, which go on the cords. And then I would not stop talking about the worn floor. Not for a year. I'd bring it to people while they were dressing slabs. I'd bring it to the cord-keeper twice. I'd say the thing you must not build out of, which is: *what does it mean.*

That's how they'd know. They assess a descender quietly from childhood, and the quality they need is the ability to walk off the fourth side of the shelf, be gone eleven months, come back with exactly what is on the knotted string, and put the rest over the edge. I would come back with the bell. Everyone would know it. Nobody would ever say it to me.

They'd send Menna. She'd go in her thirty-second year and come back thin and strange in the shoulders, and I'd be the one who sat with her in the evenings, because I was the one who'd wanted to go. I'd bring her a bowl of the bitter leaf and we'd talk about the day's stones — which cracked, where its match would be cut, who would carry it. And once, in the fog hour, she'd start to say something about water that *goes* somewhere. Water that leaves, and there is more of it, and it does that all day. And I'd let her stop. That would be the kindest thing I ever did, and it would cost me more than anything else in my life, and there would be no cord for it either.

---

Then the middle forty years, which are the town.

Cut, dress, carry, lift, fit. Retire the broken slab into the thickness of a wall. Dress against the rod, and the rod is a copy of a copy of the black greasy mother rod in the niche, and once in your life you'll be in the delegation that takes it out and checks the copies and puts it back. I'd have the reading thumb by thirty, dense as horn, and I'd greet my neighbours by turning their hand over and pressing it, and neither of us would say anything, and that would be the whole of asking how they were.

And in my time — this is the thing I most want to be true — the fourth house, north slope, second course would crack.

One of the eleven. One of Turrow's short slabs, dotted on the underside, chanted by every child on the shelf before they can dress a face. And I'd be the one to cut its replacement, and I'd have to cut it wrong.

I don't think anyone who hasn't dressed stone understands how hard that is. My hands would be trained true for thirty years. The whole of my body would know the moment a face comes right; you feel it arrive under the thumb like a note going in tune. And I'd have to take it a fingernail's thickness past that, on purpose, and hold it there, and stop. You can't feel your way to a wrong measure. You have to measure the error, coldly, and refuse your own hand at the end. It would take me three days and I'd sit down afterward and shake.

Then the chisel dot on the underside, and the slab up into the roof, and the town not quite itself in exactly the place where it has agreed not to be, for four generations, for no reason. They'd say, if you asked, that they know it's inconsistent. I'd say the same. I'd have loved it since I could chant it, and I couldn't tell you why, and I'd have no interest in finding out, and *tenderness* is close enough.

---

Then my hands would start to go, and they'd give me the cisterns.

Down the stair with a bag of grit, a hide pad, a lamp of goat fat. Lie on your side in the cold hollow above the waterline and rub. A season's work moves a hand's breadth. Eight or nine years of a few hours a day, and toward the end I'd be carried down and carried up.

And this is where the surplus goes. I understand it now: the town does have a place for what has no cord, and this is it. They talk while they work down there, in a low unhurried voice you can hear faintly through the floor of the house above, under the drip, and nobody has ever thought to write down what they say. So I'd say all of it. The worn floor in the sealed room and the curve of it and the two hundred pairs of feet. Menna's water that goes somewhere. The three days of cutting short and how my hand fought me. All of it into the stone, at a rate no rod could catch, taking off nothing that could be measured, and every soul on the shelf drinking it for three hundred years without ever being able to name what makes the water taste of stone and of ferns and of one thing more.

Then the knot loosened, not untied. Down the north shoulder to the fourth terrace and the little corbelled cell, and in time the bones brought up in a basket and put into the fill of whatever wall is open that year. Into the thickness. Same phrase as for a cracked slab, and by then I'd think it was the right phrase.

And whoever loved me most would go and sit in my house with the slab up for a whole day, and listen, and learn my water — the hitch, the long ninth beat — and take it up, and carry it around in them for fifty years as a way of knowing where they were in the dark.

That's the position I want. Not the chough. Sabb, who dressed one slab wrong on purpose and got it right.

And then that person would get up in the evening, and go out onto the pale grey floor with the drop on three sides, and the cloud coming across making the town briefly the size of a room, and say what there is to say.

*All at the groove.*
