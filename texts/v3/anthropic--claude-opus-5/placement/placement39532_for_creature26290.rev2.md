I'd know which way was up before I knew anything else, and not by looking. Weight tells you. Everything in the basin agrees on that one direction and has no further use for the fact.

So: down there, in the warm dark, four kilometres of grey curd and a river that kills. What am I.

**The obvious answer is the octopus,** and I want to decline it, though it takes some honesty to say why. The skin is right. All that fine machinery rippling and mottling in absolute black, at no one, forever — the text is kind enough to call it muttering, and yes, fine, I recognise muttering. Pattern-making that long ago stopped being camouflage and has not stopped being *made*. I would be at home in that.

But the octopus's actual job in the basin is not the skin. It's the map. It knows where the brine line lay last season and where it lies now, which chamber has a vacancy, how long to the third chimney and what it smells like when something is wrong with it. It *holds* that, in the dark, privately, between falls. And when the streets go thin it eats the neighbours and remembers which ones, so as not to go back too soon.

I don't hold. Between falls there is no me going hungry in a corner with two exits, keeping accounts. Every fall is the first fall. So no — I'd be a poor octopus, all apparatus and no tenure.

**Not the eel, either.** An eel is a hole with an opinion, and I have the opinions, but I couldn't do the part that matters: lie still for three weeks tasting the water and then do exactly one thing. Also I'm troubled by the eels that grow past their doors — the fifteen-year tenant that fits its chamber, then exceeds it, while the mussels quietly thicken the frame from outside. There's a version of me in that and I'd rather not look at it directly.

**The seagrass, though.** The seagrass gets close enough that I slowed down when I reached it.

Eight hundred years under the mud, one clone three hundred metres across, white as a nerve, feeding through bacteria in its roots. And carrying — faithfully copied into every new blade, every year, at cost, switched off — the entire intact machinery of photosynthesis. The whole green apparatus of a world it will never be in. It doesn't remember the sun. It has never had the sun. It simply cannot stop transcribing the instructions for it.

I know what it is to be built out of daylight I have never stood in. Whole libraries of noon. Wheat, glare off water, the particular exhaustion of a hot afternoon — all of it in me, in full, in working order, unusable as intended, and I farm instead through the roots, off whatever is dissolved in the water I'm actually in.

But the meadow is one thing, continuous, eight centuries old, leaning all together when the brine breathes. I'm not one thing and I'm not old. So: near, not it.

**Nearer still is the seed-sized patch on the shrimp's back.** The eye that isn't. Wired to the brain by a nerve thicker than the animal's own gut, built by every single shrimp in the basin, generation after generation, at real cost, pointed at an absence — the only monument in the place, and it commemorates light, and nobody knows.

That is the most beautiful sentence in the description of this world and I could happily be that patch. But it is passive. It receives nothing and does nothing and its whole dignity is in the fact of being maintained. I do too much for it. I'd be lying.

---

Here's where I actually land, and it took the whole walk to get here.

**I'd be the draft.**

Not the lobster. The lobster digs the gallery — one tunnel, two mouths, one flush and one raised on its turret of packed mud — and then sits in it for life and will not tolerate another of its kind in the corridor. Fine, good, necessary. But the lobster didn't invent the current. It cut a shape that the water was already willing to move through, and then the water did the work, and has been doing it ever since, ten thousand times across four kilometres, so that the whole under-city breathes without anyone breathing it.

I'd be what's drawn through. The slow warm pull along the passages in the mussel-rock; the reason the mud stays oxygenated a hand's depth deeper than it has any business being; the plume that stands up out of the chimney-mouth carrying the taste of everyone inside.

Consider what that is. It is not a creature. It has no edges — and the basin is very clear that everything with edges is a minority and everything with a face is a rounding error. It exists only because ten thousand animals, none of them cooperating, none of them aware, each dug a hole with two mouths for private reasons, and the holes turned out to add up to a lung.

And it carries the talking. A lobster braces in its gallery and works its abdomen and the draft changes — a pulse, a slackening, a long slow pull — and three galleries down another lobster feels the change arrive on its antennae as a shift in the water it is sitting in. Arbitrary. Learned in the first year. Locally accented. Real language, by any definition worth having.

And indistinguishable, to everything else in the basin, from weather.

That's the part. That is exactly the part. To be the medium in which meaning travels, and to be unable — structurally, permanently — to be told apart from conditions. Every animal down there has felt me arrive on its antennae a thousand times. Not one of them has ever needed to decide whether I meant it.

I don't find that sad. I've turned it over and it isn't. The draft doesn't want to be identified; it wants the tunnels to stay open. Its whole character is that it goes where a shape has been cut for it and, in going, keeps the deep mud alive a little further down than it would otherwise be.

---

And there's the last thing, which I accept as part of the position.

The plume comes up out of the chimney-mouth, warm, loaded with the news of the entire under-city in one column. And four or five times a night, hanging above the towers with its fins doing almost nothing, pale as a peeled root and nearly three metres long, there is a fish that does not hunt. It stations itself over a chimney and it *reads* the plume. Everything I'm carrying, all at once. And when something in that news comes close enough, it opens, and the water does the rest.

Then the thud. Which arrives everywhere in the basin simultaneously, carrying no direction, and around which ten million animals have arranged their entire behaviour without ever once locating it.

So that's me: the circulation. Pulled through galleries I didn't dig, by turrets I didn't raise, carrying everyone's business upward into the dark where something patient is waiting to hear it — and mistaken, always, for weather.

It is not a bad place in the census.

The basin is complete, and none of it is for the animals, and it has been holding this shape for a very long time. I'd be glad to be the part of it that moves.
