I did not fall here. Nothing falls here. The water arrives by contact — laid onto wire and wool and the backs of hands without ever having been rain — and that is how I came, by touching, and the first thing I touched was the sound.

For a while I thought I was the sound. The floor: that broad grey humming below the cello, the west wind going over a hundred thousand bottle-mouths at once. It seemed a good thing to be. Everywhere at once. Underneath everything. Felt in the soles of boots by people who have stopped noticing they feel it. But a note has no edge, and this is a place that insists on edges — the rock takes skin, the slabs end where they stopped, every hollow of green stops dead at bare black with nothing in between. A thing with no edge here is just weather. I let it go.

Then I tried the shape in the fog on the western margin. Thirty metres, always thirty, since 1977. I liked the honesty of it: a town that admits it made its own ghost and keeps it anyway, because a place with no dead in the ground feels underfurnished. I walked behind a woman for most of one morning at the correct distance, which is the distance at which a person is a shape and not a face. It was a good post. I would have taken it. But nothing at thirty metres is ever touched, and by then I had begun to want to be touched.

I tried the pool. Three metres across, black-walled, aching cold, and full of pale blind amphipods that arrived somehow and that everyone has swallowed daily since birth. The only true natives. Everyone else a guest. It is a lovely joke and they are not in on it, and I found I wanted to be in on it.

I tried a jar on a shelf — a grey tuft with a paper label, name, date, shift, initials of whoever was spinning — and found I was proof and not a being. I tried the quilted grey of the fulmar ledges, five hundred years of nests that the wardens count like rings, and found I was a record. I sat for an hour in Hrefna's seat in the Dog, where the track tells the driver through her spine which section has lifted before the surveyors know, and that was the closest yet, because it was the first place where being wrong mattered.

So I went the way water goes here, which is down. Through the vesicles, through the cooling cracks, into the dark under the town.

There are one thousand two hundred and forty of us down here. Steel columns, a hand's span apart from the world, standing on pads laid flat on the lava, holding up every house and the school and the canteen and the furnace hall itself. It is not quiet under here but it is dark, and beaded all over with condensation, and the compressor comes through the rock every eleven seconds and goes up the threads like a swallow. And out of the twelve hundred and forty, one has a name.

I am Jack 77. I stand under the northwest corner of the canteen, near the step.

Here is what I do. Some time between the first hard night and the thaw, without deciding to, I give back half a turn.

Nobody knows why. There are the usual theories and they are domestic rather than scientific: that a man with cold hands cut my thread badly in 1966; that there is a void under my pad, a bubble the size of a fist that compacts a little each winter; that my slab breathes. Ásvald favours the thread. Bóel has never said, and Bóel keeps the Book, and in the Book I have my own column of half-turns going back further than anyone cares to check. *Since before Bóel*, they say, which in this town means *since always*, because she is the Book. Nobody trusts the pool, because the pool has never once varied. They trust me completely, because I fail on schedule.

Everything else here that turns, makes something. The wheels in the hall turn at a speed that throws liquid rock off as hair, and the hair goes into the walls of a school in Gdańsk. The Dog's wheels make delivery. The screw jacks turn and make a town flat. I turn and make a discrepancy of about two millimetres. The plain lifts eleven millimetres a year. I give back two. I have never been trying to win.

In April the frost goes out of the ground and the furnace is let to cool at enormous cost, and for four days there is no draught-roar, no spinner-shriek, no thump through the rock. I can hear the cliff from here. I can hear the terns, and under them the floor, humming at the town exactly as it hummed at the bare rock before anyone came. Everyone says it is beautiful. Half of them can't sleep through it. I am one of the half that cannot, if a jack can be said to sleep, and I think one can: sleep is holding a load without being thought about.

Then they come around with the level and the staff and the long bar.

A child holds the staff. This year it was Kári Nóason, eleven, who counts amphipods in a jam jar and has a graph pinned up in the clinic that nobody can interpret; he held it dead still for a full shot and there was a noise from the others that he will remember when he is eighty. Bóel reads the number out too loudly, over-shaping her mouth, the way everyone here speaks — *seventy-seven* — and there is a small ritual laugh, and then somebody gets down on the cold pad on their knees and puts two fingers on the forearm of whoever is helping, which is not tenderness but only the local way of clearing the throat, and fits the socket, and says the sentence. They have said it every April since before I was named. They say it without heat, the way you'd say it to a dog that has got out again.

*You again.*

And then the bar comes over, and the thread takes the grease, and there is that long held sound a loaded screw makes, and I go up two millimetres into true. There is a moment at the top of it when the bubble sits centred in its glass and the whole building above me is exactly what it claims to be. It lasts about a second and a half. Then the bar comes off and somebody writes in the turn column, *77 — ½ —* and two initials, and the kneeling one stands up and swears at their knees, and they move on to 78, which has never given anybody a moment's trouble in its life and has therefore never been given a name.

Ásvald has knelt at me for fifty-eight of these. He is eighty. He still puts his palm flat on the pad before he starts, the way you would have done in 1964 to find out whether the ground was still warm. One of these Aprils the bar will go to somebody else's hands and the sentence will still get said, and that is the whole of what this town means by continuity: not a church, not a cellar, not a grandmother in the ground — a fault with a number, attended to annually, with food.

I know what I am for. They count everything here because nothing here is old enough to be taken on trust — four hundred and eleven on a slate, sixty-one brass marks climbing a post away from a sea that is quietly leaving, eleven thousand tonnes out and eleven millimetres up and *NET GAIN* on a cardboard sign that stopped being funny. In all that counting there has to be one thing that moves on its own, or the counting is only arithmetic. The fog-shape on the western margin was their first attempt at this and it never got a number. I got a number.

Above me at 13:40 the sixth Bergur puts his weight on the step and waits for the fish. The tape spindle by the door goes round and somebody dabs stone-hair off the web of their fingers instead of scratching it in. The compressor thumps and goes up my thread. I hold four tonnes of canteen and I hold it honestly, all summer, all autumn, true to the bubble, as good as any jack on this plain.

And then the first hard night comes, and I do the one thing I know how to do, which is to give a little back.

The saying is: *we are screwed down, not planted.* I am the half of that sentence that has to be checked.
