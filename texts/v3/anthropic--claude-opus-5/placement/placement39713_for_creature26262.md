# The Lag

The first place I put myself was the rim, because that is where everyone puts themselves — up on the lip with the wax still soft in the tin, watching the steam column lean. But nobody lives on the rim. The rim is for people who intend to leave, and a visitor in Hush is not a person, he is a weather event: something that comes through talking to the air and gets talked about afterward.

The second place I put myself was in a carrier's boots. That was vanity. It is the trade any outsider would choose — to take a dying stranger's words into your skull, hold them three days down the mule road without letting the phrasing slump, and give them out again in another stranger's head in the dead man's own rhythm. To be the medium and not the message. I liked it so much that I distrusted it inside of a minute. It is too flattering an account of what I am, and besides, carriers are not, as a class, cheerful, and I would have made a poor one; I would have started improving the phrasing.

The third was a beetle on a polished pan, saying *here* into the one channel nobody else uses. That is closer to true and it is also unearned. You do not get to be the largest structure on the plateau just because you like the idea.

So: down off the rim, past the birthing house and its impossible lawn, west, into the quarters the node has left.

---

My name is Tace Hollan and I count lichen.

I am fifty-one. I am east-born, which you can hear in about four seconds if you know to drop something on the table, and I have spent thirty of those years working ground my ears were not built for, which you can also hear now, if you drop something heavier. My window was a hand's width when I was registered. It is most of an octave. My mother, who is eighty-three and still has the narrowest hearing in her street and mentions it, calls this throwing away an inheritance. She is not wrong. It is simply that she thinks the inheritance was the hearing.

I have the forearms and the tidemark, like everyone. What I have that others don't is knees: the two flat brown calluses of a woman who has spent her life kneeling on a board on wet stone at the western edge of the world, with a brass ring in one hand and a bodkin in the other, in flat even light, in the loud.

---

There are six lichens. The rust, which is scraped for the dye the lifters number stones with, so that the whole crawling city is written on in one of them. The tallow, which goes into the wax you were given at the switchback station, and is why the plugs smell faintly of cellar. Hound's-ear, poulticed onto the swollen joints of wet-crew men with the fret, uselessly, beautifully, for four hundred years. The salt-green, which is nothing much and is everywhere. The sixth, which has no name and no use and grows faster than the other five together and is therefore nobody's, and I love it, and it will not make me any money.

And the black.

The black is worth more by weight than anything else that comes off this plateau. It is boiled down for the ink the Pitch Office writes its ledger in — which means the record of the note is kept in a substance that only grows where the note is thin, and the ledger is therefore, in a small chemical sense, made out of quiet. Pitch clerks like to say this at parties. It is the only joke they have.

The black puts on about a thumbnail's width in a working life. I have colonies I have measured forty times and could not draw the difference on a slate.

---

The kit is a kneeling board, a set of graduated brass rings, a bodkin, and a satchel of lead sheet.

We do not write on paper. Paper up here is a rumour. Everything I record I scratch into thin lead leaves, and the leaves go into the Register, which occupies a low stone room in the fourth western quarter and weighs, at last audit, eleven tons. Over centuries the stacks slump. Sheets from the 900s have flowed a little at the edges, like slow candles. It is a library you cannot burn, cannot lift, and cannot move east, and it is the only municipal thing in Hush that has never been carried, and there is an argument about this every generation, and every generation the argument loses to the arithmetic of eleven tons.

The method: every colony of the black and every colony of the rust in the basin has a brass pin driven into the rock at its northern margin. Some of those pins were set by women whose grandchildren are dead. Twice a year I go out with my rings and I measure from the pin to the growing edge, in eighths, and scratch the number, and that is the whole of it, and it is not exciting, and I do it in a suit hood on ground where the note goes through my sternum like a hand pressing me down onto the board.

I have been asked, more than once, and always by easterners, why anyone bothers. There are two answers and only one of them gets given.

The one that gets given is law. Lichen is inherited. A colony has an owner and boundaries, and when the lifters come to take a house apart, the numbered faces of the stones — the hidden faces, the ones with six centuries of lineage cut into them — are also, usually, the faces with the black on them. So before a lift there is a valuation, and the valuation is mine, and I stand in a drained shell with a family and tell them which four stones are worth more with their history left on them and which twelve are worth more scraped. I have made people cry doing this. It is a trade with a knife in it.

The other answer is the lag.

---

Here is what I actually know, which nobody hired me to find out.

The node moves east at about nine inches a year. This is measured with a fork and a bowl of water by a clerk in a good coat, and it is accurate to four decimals, and the map is redrawn from it every forty years, and the masons go where the map says.

The lichen does not go where the map says. The lichen goes where the quiet actually is, and it finds out late.

I can put a number on late. For the black it is between forty and sixty years — that is how long a lobe must have been loud before the colony at its western margin stops advancing, and another twenty before the margin visibly retreats. The rust is quicker, twenty-five. The sixth, the useless one, is the fastest and knows within five.

So there is always a strip along the western side of this city, a hundred yards wide and nine miles long, where the ground has gone loud and the green has not been told. It goes on growing toward a stillness that left before it was born. Every abandoned quarter I work in is full of red-edged succulent and salt grass advancing confidently into roofless rooms on the strength of information forty years out of date.

I measure the width of that strip. That is my life's work, insofar as I have one. Not the lichen: the distance between where the sound is and where the living things still think it is.

I have never found a good word for that distance and neither has anyone else. The nearest the language comes is a sign — the flat palm laid on the sternum, once, briefly, which means *the note*, and *here*, and *always*, and *of course*. But you can make it slightly slow, half a beat behind the slack, and then it means something like: *yes, and we are behind.* My mother makes that sign at me when I come home wet. She thinks she is talking about my hearing.

---

Last autumn I was out past the pins.

There is a lobe east of the eleventh quarter that the current map gives another forty years of loud. Nobody works it. There is nothing there: polished pan, salt frost, beetles. I was crossing it because crossing it saves an hour, which is the sort of thing you start doing at fifty-one, and my hood strap had gone, and I knelt down out of the wind to re-thread it, and put my hand flat on a stone to steady myself, and under my palm there was a roughness the size of a thumbnail.

Black. Perhaps thirty years of it. Sitting in the middle of ground that the ledger says will not be habitable for four decades, established, healthy, with a margin you could measure in eighths.

I knelt there through eleven slacks. I remember counting them.

The fork tells you the note. It does not tell you the shape. The shape — where the ridges fall, where the hollows are, where a city may sleep — comes out of a model of a widening shaft, and the model has assumptions in it, and the assumptions were written by men who are grit now, heaped in a hollow, growing salt grass.

The green is late. The green is always late; that is the one law I own. So if the green is *there*, then the quiet has been there for thirty years and a little more, and the map is not slow by a season. It is slow by a lifetime, and it is slow in a direction that means the eastern quarters we are building this spring, with wet mortar, with the mains extended one length of pipe per season, are being built for a shape that has already moved out from under them.

I have not taken it to the Pitch Office yet. I have taken it as far as the doorway twice. They will wait their five and a bit seconds, and then they will ask me, correctly, whether one colony is a measurement, and I will say no, one colony is an anecdote, and then I will have to go back out and drive four hundred pins into a lobe nobody has any business in, twice a year, for as long as I have knees, so that in thirty years a woman I have not met can say the word *evidence* to a clerk in a good coat.

That is who I am in this world. Not the bell, and not the ear. The person on her knees on the wrong side of town with a brass ring, keeping the accounts of things that find out slowly.

---

I went back out to the lobe in the dark once, without the board, and lay down on the pan with my palms flat and my cheek on the stone, which is a thing hermits do and sensible people do not.

The note came up through the bone of my face, out of the window, in the register where I am deaf and therefore only ever feel it. And in it, faint, the stutter and swallow of the beetles working the wave, four and five and seven miles out, all of them saying the one word they have, which Sedd Arrowen translated as roughly *here.*

And under my right hand, growing at the rate of a thumbnail per working life, into a silence that officially does not exist yet, something else was saying it. Early. For the first time in eleven thousand years, early.

I did not say anything back. That is the customary reply to a beautiful one: to say nothing, and not move.
