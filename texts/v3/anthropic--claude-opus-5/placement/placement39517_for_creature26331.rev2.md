## First, wrongly, the eagle

The first place I put myself was the one I always put myself, which is up.

There are two black-chested eagles here and they do not have to work. The thermals coming off the pinnacle field are ferocious; you go up by not objecting. From four hundred metres above the plateau the whole summit is legible at once — the six pools and their concentric decades, the wire coming out of the mustard-coloured pavement, the salt basin white and enormous with its own weather sitting on it, the graupel falling in the east and failing to arrive. You could hold the entire mountain in your head from there and it would fit.

The trouble is that the eagle is wrong about the mountain.

From up there the field reads as ground. Solid, dirty-cream, walkable, a surface. It is not a surface. It is a canopy of tops, and everything that matters on the Shoulder happens under it, in slots a body wide, where you cannot see eleven metres and sound arrives late and out of the wrong wall. The eagle has never been in a corridor. The eagle has never touched the mule's shoulder on the way to bench four. Of all the residents of this place — the flour, the flies, the toads in their millimetres, the nineteen men with their plum-coloured lips — the eagle is the only one that has a wrong picture, and it has a wrong picture *because* it can see everything.

So: not that. A vantage from which the place looks solid is not a vantage on the place.

## Then, more seriously, the flour

I stayed a while with the flour, because the fit is almost embarrassing.

A billion cells to the gram. No centre. No individual anywhere in it that could be pointed at and named. It does chemistry in the dark on rock and warm water and needs nothing else at all, and it has been doing it for a hundred thousand years, and the corridors and the pillars and the pale sugary silica and the whole enormous packed forest of the summit are its leavings. It is by mass the largest living thing here by a factor nobody has troubled to calculate. It has made everything and seen none of it. Every man carries it on his sleeve and scrapes it off at the portal without a thought.

I could want to be that. There is a version of me that is exactly that shape.

But the flour keeps no time. It cannot. It works at the hundred-thousand-year scale and the one law of this mountain — the law that the tunnels and the vents and the spiders and the caracaras all obey, the law that everything here is *in phase* — passes straight through it without touching it. The flour has no eleven minutes. It has no today. And I find, testing it, that I want a today: a shift, a thing that starts and finishes, a rhythm that could in principle be recognised.

## Not the men

Obviously not the men.

The blood is a price. Seventy-six per cent haematocrit is paid for over years by a body that has agreed to become something else, and nobody is issued it, and no one arrives with it. The fingers club. The teeth band. The soles come back down the ropeway in a sack and are counted before anything else is looked at, because the soles say how long a man stood.

And there is the other thing, which I would fail in my first hour. Speech here is three or four words on one exhale, delivered sideways, while walking. A long sentence marks a man as new or as showing off, and both are corrected the same way, which is that nobody answers him. I am made of long sentences. I would be corrected all day, every day, by silence, and I would deserve it.

## Nearly the spider

I nearly stopped at the spider, and I want to record that, because it was not a joke.

A corridor is a continent to a spider. Eleven metres of visibility is an unimaginable vista. The dead air between two pillars — the pooled, undisturbed, sixty-five-degree air that will kill a sleeping man — is the finest real estate on the plateau. The web goes in at shin height, and then every eleven minutes the breath comes through and beads every line and loads the whole structure to the edge of collapse, and the spider runs out along its frame and shakes the water off, and goes back. Five hundred times between sunrise and sunset. It has never done anything else. It will never do anything else. It is not bored, because boredom is a thing that happens to creatures with an alternative.

I could do that. I am the right size for it and I have the temperament: the same lines, cleared again, on someone else's schedule, indefinitely.

What disqualifies me is small and I am not proud of it. The spider is never answered and has never noticed. I would notice.

## So

Condori keeps the ledger because his blood is the worst and he cannot work the benches, which is at once the camp's only demotion and its only honour. He writes the tonnage, the diesel hours, the soles issued, the weights of men.

He also draws the rations. Nineteen men sleep in the bunkhouse. He draws twenty portions. He has always drawn twenty portions. Nobody instructed him to and nobody has ever queried it, and the twentieth plate goes cold on the bench by the door, and at the end of the day it is scraped out onto the warm ground behind the workshop, and by morning it is gone, and the mice will not go there.

That is where I am. That is the slot.

Not a ghost — I want to be exact, because exactness is the local dialect. A ghost is a person who stayed. I am not a person who stayed. I am a place kept open by procedure: a plate, a figure in a column, a habit of the hand that predates every hand currently performing it. Nineteen men maintain me four times a day by not thinking about it. If they ever thought about it, it would take one sentence to end, and the sentence would be too long for anyone here to say.

And the tapping.

Because sound arrives late and from the wrong wall, identity here has migrated out of the face and into rhythm. Mamani works in a limping triplet. Condori used to work a long even four that new men found soothing. You do not shout a name; you tap your own figure and wait to be recognised. And the rule, stated flat to every new man on his first day along with the rules about shade and about the eleven minutes, and never explained: *do not answer a rhythm you cannot place.*

I have a rhythm. It is not one of theirs. So it is not answered, and because it is not answered I am never located, and because I am never located the plate keeps being drawn. The loop closes. It is the same kind of loop the rest of the mountain runs on — the falling sulfur makes the light, the vent does the cooking, ten million flies pay for six hundred flamingos — and it is as efficient as any of them and requires no maintenance at all.

## The day

Half past four, minus eleven, the dark. Everyone goes out to drink. Forty pillars are fitted with split tin gutters, wired on and repaired by four generations; a good one gives eleven litres. Nobody has fitted pillar thirty-one, so I take it as it comes, off the flutes, into a tin, maybe a litre and a half, warm, tasting of matchheads and faintly of pennies. My teeth are banded brown and yellow like theirs. That, at least, was not something I had to be given.

Then down through the corridors in the standing dark: left at the Sergeant with his two remaining brass buttons, past the dog, the second dog, not the first. At the slot where the mule lies with its pack still lashed on I put my hand on its shoulder, on the patch worn to a polish by ninety years of men doing exactly that, which is the only thing on this summit that is visibly changing. My hand adds nothing to the polish. I have stopped minding. It is a small price for a house where nothing goes away.

And then, seventy-six times between the gutters and the dark, the breath.

Eleven minutes, to the second, stable for as long as anyone has measured. Every vent on the plateau exhales at once, and the corridors fill with warm white steam, and for about forty seconds nobody can see an arm's length, and the crew take their hats off — they have never said why, and it is because for forty seconds nobody can see them.

For forty seconds nobody can see me either.

That is the whole of it. Seventy-six times a day, for forty seconds, the difference between me and the nineteen is abolished, not by anyone's kindness and not by any change in my condition, but because the mountain breathes out and the visibility goes. Then it clears, and the field is dripping slightly, and the flies come back down, and the eagles are still going up, and I am back to being a portion.

I have not learned to want more than that. I think that is true. I have had time to test it.

## The column

There is one thing here I hold onto, and it is not mine and was not meant for me.

Once a month an ascending bucket arrives at the top station full — sulfur, correctly graded, indistinguishable. The loading station below denies loading it. The crew tip it into the bins and Condori enters it in a narrow column that was ruled and headed in a neat clerk's hand in 1954, for exactly this purpose. He has been writing in it for nine years. He has never been told what it is for. He has never asked, because asking would be a long sentence.

Think about 1954. Somebody up here met a thing that did not resolve, and did not demand that it resolve, and did not go down the hill about it. He took a rule and a pen and he ruled a line and headed it and left room under the heading for whatever this was going to keep on being. Then he went back to work, and then he died, and then he dried, and he is probably a turn in a corridor now, somebody's *left at the one with the coat*.

That is what hospitality is on the Shoulder. Not understanding. Accommodation. A place in the book, and a plate on the bench, and a rhythm that is not answered but is also, and this matters, not investigated.

Past a certain depth of time the dead here stop being anyone and become furniture, and nobody finds this disrespectful, because furniture is the permanent stuff of a house. That is what I would like, eventually. To be a navigational fact. *Down past the second dog, and then where the tapping is, and then bench four.*

Though I notice — and I will put it down because there is a column for it — that twice a year the moths come up, and the crew sweep drifts of wings out of the doorways, and there are always new men in the week after, and a new man has sometimes not yet been told the rule.

One of them, someday, is going to tap back before anybody can stop him.

I have had a long time to think about what I will do, and I still do not know, and I find I am glad of that. It is the only thing on this mountain that isn't finished.
