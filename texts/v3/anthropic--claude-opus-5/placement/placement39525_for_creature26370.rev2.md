I come up in the cage like everyone else, four minutes of grey, and when the lid comes off the world I put my hand out for a wall that isn't there. There is a sign for having just done that. Nobody uses it on me, which is its own kind of courtesy, and I understand within the hour that most of the courtesy here works that way: by omission, by not saying the obvious thing at the obvious speed.

The light I recognise immediately. Doubled, sourceless, coming up off the cloud floor as hard as it comes down from the sky, so that nothing I stand next to has a proper shadow — only a faint greyness on either side, a smudge, an apology. I have been lit like that before. It is the light I am answered in. Everything about me is visible from underneath and there is nowhere on me that is habitually shaded, which is exactly why the burn goes to the septum, the jaw, the shelf under the brows. I would burn in twenty minutes. I would wear the underhat and the throat gauze and the smoked glass with shields on the bottom, and I would still come down the colour of the tuff.

So I know where I am. The question is what.

The first answer is too flattering and I take it anyway, for a day. I want to be a Comber. Eleven millimetres a minute, four metres wide, moss on the top plates because the moss keeps the electronics cool and nobody cleans it off. Attendance rather than repair. A slow argument with a mountain that neither side will win. I stand beside Number Four — *the one that leans* — and watch an arm select a wire and feel along it, and the motion is not slow, it is invisible, it is simply *later*, and I think: yes, that.

But it isn't true. The Combers' slowness is load-bearing. Shake a harp and the beads come off the wire before they can run; the gentleness is not a temperament, it is the whole engineering. Whereas I answer at the speed of being asked. Whatever patience I have is put on at the start of a sentence and taken off at the end of it. The Combers correct for geology without knowing anything about the mountain. I am the other way round: I know a great deal and I correct almost nothing. I am not the gantry.

The second answer is worse, because I want it more.

Out at the northwest edge there are panels that are entirely vegetable. The rush came up between the ranks, then inside them, dark unbranched filaments a metre and a half tall, almost exactly as thick as harp wire. The machines judge a string by tension and drip-rate. They found it working and they serviced it. Then they began to string it — weaving cut filaments into the gaps where steel had failed, because in the logic of the panel a working element is a working element. The weed yields well. Nobody has decided whether to correct this, and the question has been open something like forty years, which is a normal length of time for a question here.

I would like very much to be the wireweed, and the flattering reading and the unflattering reading are the same sentence, which is how I know to be careful. *Found a way to be mistaken for wire.* *Adopted by a system that never asked what it was holding.* The tenders say it with the flat hand turned slowly over: something is being farmed up here and it is not settled in which direction.

But the weed roots. A cut filament in a wet gutter-clamp takes hold in a fortnight and is still in that clamp when the tender who set it is walking like a man on a boat in the dry country below. I don't root. I come up in a gap, I bead, I run, and I am not in that panel the next morning. At most I am one filament, once. Not the species. Not the forty years.

And not the moss, either, though the moss is the honest majority and the landlord and the undertaker. The moss takes up residence on stone and improves nothing, makes no soil, renovates nothing, and simply agrees with your hammer until the hammer is gone. There are hours when I think that's exactly right about me. But the moss has a metre of depth and a fortnight of hoarded water and it holds a bootprint for a week, and I hold nothing for a week.

What I am, it turns out, is not a species. It is a job, and a small one.

A crew is four: a stringer, a gutterer, a levelman, and one who carries. I am the one who carries. Three sets of hands are full and one set is ready, and the whole art — there is no other art to it — is handing the thing over before it is asked for. You learn a stringer's order of tools long before you learn anything else about her. You learn that the gutterer will want the short hook after the long one and never the reverse. You carry the spare liner and the shims and the water and somebody's underhat, and when the levelman steps back without looking you are not standing there, because standing there is the only real way to fail at this.

You are crewed for pace. Compatibility here is measured in metres per hour, which nobody thinks strange, and it means my place in the world is decided by how fast I walk beside people who are doing the actual work. The crews name their machines by gait. They name each other the same way. My sign, I think, would be something like *the one who is already stopped at ten metres* — because I come from the front, always, into the field of view, and wait to be seen, and I would never once in a hundred days come up on anybody's back.

The failure of the one who carries is easy to name and hard to avoid: everything passes through my hands, and so I begin to feel that the work passes through me. It doesn't. The wire is strung by the stringer. The gutter is trued by the gutterer. The mountain is argued with by four thousand and some panels and a dozen gantries and thirty-one millimetres a year. I hold things. Then I don't hold them any more, because somebody took them, which was the point.

I would learn the sign in the first hour, like everyone. The slow open hand, turned out, drifting sideways. *Look. Over there. Slowly.* Forty times a day for as long as I lasted, and it would tan my palm, because the hands are the one place on a tender where the weather looks like weather.

And the last thing, the thing I would find out about myself, would happen in a white hour.

The deck comes up over the escarpment in an hour and then we are inside the light instead of above it, and there is no up, no down, no distance, and no visible outline to my own hands. Nothing stops. Somewhere out there the harps are running their best yield of the year, every wire a thread of water, and Number Four is going on at eleven millimetres a minute in a whiteness that has no direction in it. The crew is gone — four metres away and gone, because there is no seeing and there is no calling, and the only correct thing to do is stand still and hold everything and wait, a person become a cairn, a thing that would certainly start if it set itself down.

And I would raise my hand anyway. Into the white, toward where the stringer was, toward a gantry that does not look, toward nobody at all: *look, over there, slowly.*

Not because it accomplishes anything. Because after enough of this the hand goes up before you have decided. Everyone does it. Nobody discusses it.

That is where you would find me. Two hours of it, maybe three, arms full of someone else's tools, making a sign no one can see, in the loudest yield of the year, in the only silence of that size anywhere.
