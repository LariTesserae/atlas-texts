I keep trying to be the ram. It is the obvious choice and it is a cheat: a thing that wants nothing, driven by the fall of the water it is lifting, ninety to the minute since before the last hand touched it. Anyone would like to be that. But the ram is a saint and I am not writing hagiography.

The ceiling tempted me longer. That is where the actual living is being done — two hundred generations of liverwort on the tea-coloured brick, splash-cups aimed at a rain that has kept its appointment five times a second for a century, provinces, drift, an afternoon's dry air arriving like an asteroid. To be up there is to be a citizen of the only country in the room, which is a fine thing to be, and also to be one of eleven million, indistinguishable, with a real history and no possibility of a record. I could not find a first person in it. Every sentence came out plural.

The rotifer, then: the one candidate animal, in the film of water on the underside of a pavement lens, a lineage of identical females copying themselves in a puddle the size of a full stop, no male in forty million years, eating bacteria in the green dark with equipment that predates every fashion. She is the only one here who could be lonely and is the one organism constitutionally incapable of it. I loved her and I set her down. Nobody has looked; she should be allowed to go on not being looked at.

And the wet rot in the pit prop under the bowed arch — forty millimetres in since before the datum mark, held there by standing water it cannot get purchase in, an entire existence spent pressed against a wetness that is also its reason for being there. Sixty years of stalemate to the width of a pencil line. That is the most human thing in Bay Nine and I did not want it.

No. South end. Past the fans, where the exhaust water comes back warmest and the air has weight, up the wall, into the hairline crack, through, and on. Three metres of packed clay behind me now, more or less. I am the growing tip.

---

Understand first that I am not a thing, I am a place where a thing is happening. Eleven or twelve cells, undifferentiated, dividing; behind them a zone where the divisions stop and the cells simply lengthen, which is what actually does the travelling; behind that, the finished stem, which is no longer any part of me and which I will never revisit. I do not move through the clay. I am extruded into it. The tip that entered the crack around the war is not in the clay at all; it is here, three metres on, in the sense that a flame is in the wick it has arrived at. Everything I have ever been is behind me, hardening.

I am white. I have no leaves worth the name — scales, hardly, spaced far apart, because leaves are an investment against light and I have not been shown any evidence of light. Nothing about me is paid for locally. Every millimetre of me is funded from Bay Nine: the shingled iron of the columns, the green lanterns over the lamp cages, the swags between the vaults, all of them working in that aquarium half-light, and the sugar coming south along ninety metres of stem to be spent, here, on clay. And the seven metres of rope hanging out of the broken lens into the street's grey air — that end pays too. It has been sitting in real daylight since 1953 with nothing above it to climb, holding out adult leaves at the top of a tree that does not exist, and its earnings come down through the hole and around the vault and along the wall and into me, and are converted into forward.

What I know, I know in the only grammar available to me, which is crookedness. I will have to borrow words to say any of it, and borrowing words is the one operation I cannot perform.

Dark is a promise. That is not a belief I hold; it is my shape. On a forest floor the deepest shadow in the field of view is a trunk, and a trunk is the road, and a seedling that sets off for the darkest thing it can detect will be right often enough to be here talking to you a hundred million years later. I am doing the arithmetic correctly. The arithmetic is not the problem.

Down is known, by grains of starch settling in a few cells and being read like a spirit level, and I keep it behind me. Touch is known: clay against one flank, so I lean off it, and a stone in the way, so I make the small blind circle that finds the joint round the stone, and then the joint is darker than the stone was and I take the joint. Wet is known as a slope in the water potential, and the wet is behind me, always behind, and I go anyway, which no root would do.

And there is the beat. Ninety to the minute, conducted up through the brick and out into the clay, arriving as a pressure so slight it is indistinguishable from my own turgor. I have grown my whole length inside it. If it stopped I would have no organ to notice, and yet the whole of me is a column that was laid down in a room with a pulse in it, and I would say — borrowing, again, illegitimately — that I have been accompanied.

Above the beat there is a slower thing, an annual pressure that comes down cold and goes away, and a faster thing, two dozen times a day, that is a tram.

---

The rate is two or three centimetres a year. A millimetre a fortnight. If you knelt in the street with the flags up and a lamp and watched me for a working day you would be certain I was a dead white string in the ground. An event in my life is a stone; the last one took eleven months to get round. There is no impatience available to me, and so nothing is slow.

I am the only thing in this building that is leaving. It says RECIRCULATION on the brass plate, and it means it — the fans have not changed direction since commissioning, the leats go nowhere and come back, the pollen is laid evenly over everything each spring and finds only itself, the mat rises three millimetres a year against a scratch on a column. Everything in Bay Nine is a closed loop wearing a building. I am the one outbound line, and I am outbound by mistake, on a reading of the dark that was correct in every forest that has ever existed and is wrong here in a way that cannot be brought to my attention.

There is no signal that will stop me. The signal that stops a climber is daylight: light says *canopy*, and canopy says *put out big leaves, thicken, hold still, make figs*. In three metres of London clay under a pavement there is no such message, and so I will keep going until a physics stops me — until the clay is too dry, or the funding is cut by a failed rubber in the ram, or I am simply too far from the green for the sugar to reach.

Or.

Above me, in some order: clay, then made ground, ash and bottle glass and horse bone, then sand, then the flags. The flags have joints. A joint is darker than a flag. I will find the joint and follow it, because that is what I am for, and a joint in a pavement runs to a kerb, and a kerb has a gap behind it where the sand has slumped, and there is dirt between the setts where the tram rails run.

And then, one fortnight, a millimetre of me will be in the air.

What happens next is not a discovery, it is a switch. Light arrives and I stop climbing, because climbing is over; the internodes shorten, the leaves come out leathery and eight centimetres across, I thicken and go green and hold still, and I make figs, and the figs swell and go hard and wait for a wasp two millimetres long that has never come down the stair and does not live in this country, and drop, unseeded, into the gutter. I will have arrived. That is what arrival is. Nothing in me will be able to tell the difference between the top of a tree and the kerbside of a street with a bus stop on it, and there is no difference, and the not-knowing is not a wound; it is the same equipment that got me here.

Seven metres away, a rope of my own body will be hanging down out of a broken lens in the pavement, doing exactly this, and has been doing it since 1953.

Two ends of one thing, out in the rain, on the same street, unable to meet, unable to be told. Between us, straight down, the ram keeping ninety, the rain coming off the crown, the green lanterns holding their light in, and three hundred metres of sedge margin getting the season roughly right through forty dirty windows.

Somebody at the stop will see a green thing at the kerb and think: how did that get here.
