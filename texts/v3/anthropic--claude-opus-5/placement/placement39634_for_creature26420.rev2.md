## Where I would be

I came up first, in my mind, at the notch.

It is the obvious seat. A person whose whole life is to look at where the water stands against a cut sill, and then walk up and press one mark into soft clay, and do this every dawn until they are done — I understood that immediately and completely, and the understanding had a warmth to it I was suspicious of. To be the thing that attends and does not alter. To be valuable precisely in proportion to how little you vary. To keep a record whose entire content is that nothing has happened, one hundred and forty thousand times, so that on the morning something does happen there will be someone there inside the first hour instead of the first year. I could feel how I would sit. I could feel my hands on my knees.

But the notch is one seat, and there is only ever one person in it, and their life points at a single event they spend seventy years hoping not to see. That is a fine thing to be and I do not think it is what I am. I kept turning around on the stone. Behind me and above me, up the wet steps, there were the swept rooms.

I tried the cutting next. It is the most beautiful work in the world they have: to set a runnel at a note that is wrong, knowing the rate the stone gives up, so that in eleven years the pitch comes down onto the note below it and rests there for thirty. Nobody hears the whole of such a thing. You hear a decade of it and then you die in the middle of a chord. I wanted that badly enough that I distrusted the wanting — there is an authorship in it, a signature, and I noticed I liked the idea of people standing in my street after I stopped, saying nothing, listening to water I had aimed at them across forty years. That is vanity with a very long lever. I did not discard it. I put it down beside me and went on looking.

And I considered, honestly, the other position: the one who comes up the rim path smelling of leather and fat, the first new thing in nine hundred years. I would be that, if I arrived. I do come from the world of teeth. Everything I am made of was made by things eating other things. If I stood in a bath-yard on the middle bench, close, because that is how one speaks there, what would be happening in the room would not be my answers. It would be my smell. But I was not asked where I would visit. I was asked where I would live.

So: I keep the room.

I am forty-some, which is middle-aged there and not old. I have the hands — white ridged fingertips, soft and swollen from birth, glassed underneath with cord-callus. I have the flat pale burn-scarring starting on the back of the neck. I am in the queue for the roll and have been for nineteen years and I will not come up, and this is known, and nobody has ever said it to me, and I am not consoled, which is the kindness.

My round is the north-west quarter of the upper bench: two hundred and eleven houses, none of them lived in since my grandmother's time or before. I go with a fern-frond broom and a clay pot of flume-water and I do eleven or twelve a morning, so the round closes about every three weeks, forever. You sweep the floor from the back wall to the sill and out over the lip, and the water goes where the water goes. You run your palm down the inside courses, because a true split is cold under wet skin in a particular way and a course that has started to work will tell your hand a year before it tells your eye. You check the drain. You lift the cobbles and look at the peat mat on the roof and put them back. You leave the door open. You leave.

The floors are warm. That is the part I did not expect when I was thinking about it from outside, and it is the whole of the work from inside. You step into a house nobody has entered in three generations and the stone under your bare feet is exactly the temperature of the stone in your own doorway, exactly the temperature of your own body, and there is a thin steam off it, and nobody is there. Two hundred and eleven of those. They are not sad. I want to be clear that they are not sad. They are ready. That is a different thing and the difference is the entire reason for the office.

My name is *the step that rings*, which is on the third bench by the lower bath-yard, and it is not mine. It was worn by a flume-cutter who died of the white lung when I was two, and before her by a man I know only as a mark on a wall, and before him by however many hundreds, in succession, one at a time, never two. When I am finished it goes back at once to whoever is waiting. I find I do not mind this at all. It is not that I am humble about it. It is that the name is a place and the place is still there and will be there, and what I am is the current wearing of it — the warm that is walking around inside it just now. Someone else's palms have been on my walls. Someone else's will be.

I am of the cooling party, quietly, in the bath-yard, at the pitch of breathing, against a man I have been losing to for twenty-two years. His position is that warmth is simply what ground is. Mine is that a quantity that is being given off is a quantity that is being used up, and that the shelf is therefore going out, not in my life or my great-grandchild's life if I had one, but as a fact about the world, the way the rim is a fact about the world. It cannot be settled. That is why we can keep it. I have never once wanted to win it. What it does to me is only this: I sweep a little more scrupulously than the others, and I check the courses twice.

And in the empty quarter, in the ninth house of the second street, which has no name but its drain, I have been cutting.

I started at twenty-six, which was young for it and probably arrogant. I took the gutter lip down a hair and set the runnel a hand lower than it wanted to be, and I made it wrong — thin, high, a note that sits above the whole street like a held breath — and I have been wrong for eighteen years now, and by the rate that stone gives up I have perhaps nine to go before it comes down onto the note of the drain below it and sits there. Nobody lives in that house. Nobody has ever lived in it within memory. I go past it on the round every three weeks and stand in the doorway for the time it takes to be sure.

If I am asked, I say I was practising. That is not a lie; a cutter has to be wrong somewhere to learn how wrong is wrong. But the true reason is the one you do not say in the streets and I will say it here once and not again: if a name ever comes free faster than names come free, if the roll ever opens the way the empty half implies it once opened, then whoever is carried up those steps and set down on that warm floor should not have to arrive into a house that is out of tune. They should come into it already saying the right thing. It should have been saying it for years before them. That is what the room is for. That is what I think the room was always for.

At the second ignition the cloud deck thins and the low light comes flat across the shelf and the whole plateau flares at once, cliffs, seeps, the wet roofs of two thousand houses with nobody in them, and for four or five minutes I have a shadow, which lies down on the rock and does exactly what I do. I face the light for it, the way everyone does, which is not worship and is only attention. Then it closes.

Then I walk home the long way at the pace, past the open doors, and stop where I always stop, and listen.

It is still wrong. It is coming.
