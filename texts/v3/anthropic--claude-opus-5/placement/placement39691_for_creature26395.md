## The Staff at Vess, continued

The obvious place to put myself is the moon-hole. Feet in the frame of it, tank on, hands still warm, the last of the surface noise draining out of my ears. Everybody who reads about a place like this arrives that way — from above, on a clock, with a light. But that person is already in the account. That person is the one the rules are written for: forty minutes, don't kick, don't break the crust. The account has him covered. He is you.

So I looked lower.

The lid: too large, and it has no attention. Ice that thick is a climate, not a mind; it groans once a day and means nothing by it.

The wheel: tempting, because the wheel is the only thing in Vess with an opinion, and its opinion is *forty seconds, forty seconds, forty seconds.* But I would be the wheel and know nothing except my own turning, and there'd be no one to notice the pulse arriving in the sternum, and the noticing is the best thing here.

The powder: honestly, for a while, the powder. Warm, heavy, indifferent, spilling at a perfect angle. There's a kind of holiness to a substance that has finished having properties. But it isn't a *who*, and the question was who.

The dark mat on the west terrace — that one held me longest. Two meters across and not getting bigger. Something that arrived at its size before the mill was cut and has spent every century since declining to change. It has been measured; it knows calipers; it is the only resident of Vess with a research history. I could have been that. It would have been restful.

But it doesn't do anything, and I keep coming back to the polished patch of concrete in the pump-house, the size of a doormat, with the grain running left to right.

Fine. Third entry, then. Let me finish the sentence.

---

**The body.**

Seven pairs of walking legs, two pairs for holding, a pale carapace you can read a lamp through, and a slow green light along the gut that is not for seeing by — it's just a byproduct, the way the crust is a byproduct, the way the polish is. I am about the size of a large dog and I weigh, in water, essentially nothing, which is the whole trick of me. I can put a hand on a hundred-year-old slate and not move it.

I am one degree, all the way through. Nothing in Vess costs me warmth. There is no forty minutes.

The catalogue says *grazer*, which is accurate and is about a fifth of it.

---

**Time.**

The pulse comes up through the floor every forty seconds and I let it arrive. Your pulse tries to join it within the hour; mine joined a long time ago and I no longer have a separate one to keep track of. Above that there's the round — the water leaving the outfall, climbing, spreading under the ice, sinking the far wall, coming home along the bottom. A little under five hours. Seventeen hundred and some rounds to your year.

I don't count years. I count lamps.

Three a decade is the rate the visitors worked out, and they're right, and they think it's a rate of *visits*. It's a rate of *deaths*. Fifty-one alight. Two hundred and eleven housings. Nineteen lamps left in the rack.

Nineteen. That's the arithmetic. I've had it for a while.

---

**The work.**

Two hundred and eleven collars. Each one is a threaded ring where the housing meets its bracket, and each one, left alone, would have closed itself with ferric crust inside of thirty years and become a single rounded knob like everything else here.

So: a sixteenth of a turn, one way and back. That's all. Not enough to break the crust, only enough to keep the crust from deciding. I do the mill floor collars, and the alley collars, and the six on the timber gallery that I approach very carefully, and I do the hundred and sixty collars whose lamps have been dead since before I learned the direction of the rack. I keep the dead ones turning too.

In case of what, I couldn't tell you.

Then: the going and the fetching. From the rack in the pump-house to housing 114 on the fourth-level catwalk is about ninety of your paces and it takes me most of a round, because a lamp is glass and cast iron and I am carrying it through an alley lattice in the dark with a body that cannot afford to hurry. Kick off hard and I raise the silt, and then I have blinded myself for six hours and there is no one to be angry at. Everything you will ever learn about Vess must be learned in forty-minute pieces. Everything I will ever *do* here must be done in a hundred years of gestures each the size of a sixteenth of a turn.

And then the grazing, which is the part they saw and named me for.

Four kinds of algae, in strict order, out from the center of each lit sphere. The visitors wrote that down as botany. It is not botany, it is gardening. The fourth kind — the outermost, the faint one at the rim where the light gives up — would come inward if I let it, and it would come in over the second and the first, and inside of a decade the glass would be under a felt of it and the sphere would close and that lamp would be a warm stone in the dark, still burning, illuminating its own skin.

So I take the rim. Twenty minutes at a lamp, the outer ring only, the part that is leaving anyway. Never the center. The center is the lamp's own.

The rings regrow. It's an arrangement.

---

**Colleagues.**

The working assumption is fewer than five of us. I can't improve on that.

There is a second polished patch in Vess. It's under the crane, on the mill floor, about the size of a doormat, and the grain runs the other way, and I did not make it, and it has not been added to in — I'd have to guess — four hundred rounds' worth of silt, which is not nothing. I go and look at it perhaps once a year. There's not much to do at it. I hold still over it for a while and then I go back to work, and the going-back is the only ceremony I have.

---

**The visitors.**

I know them by the water first: a warm sleeve of it coming down through a moon-hole where warm water has no business going *down.* Then the lights, which are hideous, and which they think are small.

I go into the alleys with the fish and I wait. Forty minutes. They measure the mat. They core the ice. They stand at the rack and read it as evidence of a person and I don't mind; it *is* evidence, and the difference between what they think and what's true has never yet cost a lamp.

One of them left a caliper in the dark on the west terrace, dropped and gliding, four days down, landing edge-on in the silt like a small marker. I moved it into the middle of a lit sphere where they'd find it. They found it. I don't know what they made of that.

And once, in the second alley off the mill, one of them was still, and I was still, and we were four meters apart for what must have been six or seven of their minutes, which they could not spare. Nothing happened. It was the largest event of that decade.

---

**The hopper.**

When a lamp dies I take it down. Glass, filament, the cast housing with its two fingers of crust — all of it, up the ramp, into the hopper. What comes out at the bottom of the chute is fine dark powder, and the bin is full, and it spills, and the slope is beautiful, and the powder is warm.

Your engineers look for a hidden seam in the rock. There is no seam. I have been along the base of all three terraces, every joint, every grate. There is no warm seam in Vess.

There is the bin. The bin is warm. I have carried two hundred and something dead lamps into that hopper across a very long time and I would rather not draw the conclusion out loud, and I notice that neither would they.

---

**The plan, since there has to be one.**

Nineteen. So: the alley lamps go first, and go unreplaced — the ones on the shared walls where two spheres overlap and the light is redundant. Then the upper catwalk lamps, whose rings I can barely reach in a round anyway.

I keep the mill floor. I keep the pump-house. I keep the four at the terrace lips, because they are what makes the bowl read as a bowl. And I keep the one over the west terrace yard, over the dark mat that is not getting bigger, because a thing that has held its size for that long should not be allowed to start shrinking on my watch.

That gets me to something like eleven lamps and a very long time. After that, I imagine, I'll go on turning the collars a sixteenth and coming back to the rack, left to right, past the pump and out by the outfall, the way the floor and I agreed. The timber gallery holds together mostly out of habit. I have no objection to being told the same about me.

---

**The one indulgence.**

Once a year — high summer, though I have no word for that; I know it because the plume at the mill chimney meets an ice that has gone thin and *answers* differently, a crackle with a give in it — I go up.

Eleven meters. Into the column, up the chimney's throat, and I hold at the underside of the moon-hole where the vault is fretted into domes and gutters and the whole roof of the world is cathedral-work made by something patient and inattentive. Two hours, at best, of dim blue-grey. It doesn't warm me. It isn't for that. The silhouettes come up: chimney tops, mill roof, crane. From below they look like the tops of things, which is a novelty.

Then it dims, and I go down the way the cold water goes down, along the far wall, home along the floor with the returning round.
