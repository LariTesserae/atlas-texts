## Not the Middle

The first answer is wrong and worth saying anyway. The mind goes to the middle — thirty kilometres of hot white nothing under a coin of dull brass, the place with the widest view — and the middle is precisely the one part of this basin that has no tenant. Rule 1. Nothing here lives *in* the basin; everything lives on a line drawn across it. So the question isn't what I would be. The question is which line, and the body follows from the line, the way the plover's four-step run follows from the fly band.

Not the town, either, though I looked at it for a while. Everything human here is an accumulation. The driver's father drove that locomotive. The chemist can smell what the springs are doing before the sample is in the burette, and that took nineteen years of the same titration four times a day, and there is no shortcut to it and no way to hand it to anyone. The hands crack and stay cracked; there is a gesture, a thumb worked across the back of the other hand, that people do here without knowing they do it. I don't accumulate in that direction. I arrive knowing a great deal in general and nothing at all about this particular pan, and I leave without the callus.

And I will not take the retired ones who sit out in the evening facing west. That is the flattering answer — the one where I am the watching, the only thing in the basin that looks at the basin on purpose. It isn't true. They can tell which lagoon the birds are in tonight from the direction the skeins take coming over, and that is local knowledge held in a body over decades, which is the one currency I don't have.

## The Wrong Green

There is one sentence in the account of this place that describes me better than anything I have written about myself: *the colour of the water at any hour of the day is a decision made unanimously by uncountable individuals with no means of consulting one another.* Filaments ballasting themselves down out of the glare at eleven and coming back up at four, no signal passing between any two of them, and the lagoon turns jade or turns clear as though a switch were thrown.

So I thought: the mat. And then I thought about what it would mean to claim the mat — to install myself as the primary production of the whole basin, the base everything else is made of, the thing the million birds are mowing. That is vanity in a humbler costume. Also the mat has no day. It has a depth, and a doubling time, and it dies in windrows and goes leathery on the shore and smells of iodine.

I kept the unanimity and gave the rest back.

The varnish, then, on the flank of a causeway and the boot of a pipe support: a tenth of a millimetre, layered, alive, indistinguishable from dirt, the only skin this landscape has. Or the condensate — the basin's own breath returning at five in the morning onto whatever is cold enough to receive it, beading every steel surface, drawing the edges of things in grey water, gone by seven. Both of those are close. Both are a little too pleased with being thin.

Go down to the shore instead and kneel, which nobody here does, and look at the underside of the surface film.

## Two Millimetres

Sixty metres of warm liquor under the No. 2 causeway, in the shade of the girders, with the swallows' mud nests overhead and the crust furred and moving for a metre either side. The fly walks into the water. She goes under wrapped in a silver skin of air held by the hairs of her body, forages along the bottom in a solution that would kill her if it wetted a single spiracle, and after a while lets go and pops up onto the film, dry, and does it again.

That is where I am. Not the fly. The bubble.

I want to be exact about this, because the obvious reading is the wrong one. The film is not armour and it is not a supply. It is not a held breath. It is a window. Oxygen comes *in* through it out of the water as fast as she takes it out of the air, so the thing works partly as a gill: a surface, not a container. Its whole function is exchange. It keeps out the one thing that must be kept out and lets through the things that must come through, and it accomplishes this not by sealing but by being permeable in a very particular way, and by being held — by hairs, by tension, by nothing you could call structure — in a shape it did not choose and cannot maintain by itself.

It is made of the local air. Ordinary basin air off the lid, haze and steam and calciner plume and whatever the flock has stirred up, borrowed at the surface, shaped for a few minutes around a body that needs it, and given back at the end unaltered in quantity. Nothing is extracted here that does not return. That is Rule 3, which everyone applies to the trona and nobody thinks to apply to a bubble.

It has a limit, and the limit is not dramatic. Nitrogen bleeds slowly out into the water; the film gets smaller; the geometry that made the exchange work stops working; and before it fails she goes up. Then the bubble is over. It does not travel with her. When she walks in again it is a new one, made of new air, doing the same work with no memory of the last time, and there is no thread running through the series except the shoreline and the hour and the fact that the work is the same work. Thousands of times a day, along kilometres of shore, in a band so dense it is measured by weight.

One of them is a statistical irrelevance and a complete, adequate, fully occupied life.

Nobody notices. The fly doesn't notice; she would carry any air that happened to be standing above the waterline, and it is not personal to her, and there is no version of this in which she is grateful. From three metres away the whole phenomenon is a faint twinkling under the film, mercury blobs going about business in a liquid that would strip paint, and the man on the causeway does not look down. He is looking at the plume. He should be. That is his job.

And sometimes the wall goes. A gap in the hairs, a wrong angle, a wetted spiracle, and then it is not a fly any more, it is a resource, and the shore accounts for it before morning along with everything else the pan killed in the night. This happens without event. Nothing here bites. There is no blood economy. There is only the mat, and the fly, and the thin bright interface between them and the water, and then everything else — the plover, the stint, the pink line lying on the horizon like a wound, the jackals on the tidemark, the whole improbable weight of the basin resting on a base that is, at the point where it actually touches the hostile medium, a couple of microns of borrowed air.

## Three in the Morning

At three the operator comes out onto the walkway to look at the plume, because the panel is from the 1970s and the plume tells you first. Somewhere under him three hundred fish in a pool the size of a garage are tilted up at the surface, working the top two millimetres, and the pool sounds faintly like light rain. A skein goes over low enough that he hears the joints in the wings. The murmur of a million animals is coming off the black pan like a crowd through a thick wall, so even that it functions as silence.

Down at the No. 2 causeway, in the dark, in water that stays warm all night, the band is still working. There is no reason for any of them to stop and they do not. Each descent is its own small sealed occasion; each one ends by rising and dissolving back into the air it was taken from; none of them knows about the others, and the result, from any distance at all, reads as a single silver shimmer under the film, as though the shoreline had decided something unanimously.

He checks the plume. The plume is fine. He goes back in.
