For a while I was at the joint.

That is where an outside mind first comes into a place like this: up on the ridge in seventy knots of wind, gloved hand moving along the lid's grain, looking for the one line two centuries of weather hasn't finished eating. But there is nothing to *be* at the joint. The joint is a fact, not a position. You can't stand there. You'd be standing on the roof of somebody's world with your back to it.

Then, obviously, the moss. Everyone who hears about the Room wants to be the moss — the exempted thing, the one green knot in ninety thousand square metres of scrubbed rock, alive out of all proportion to its usefulness. But the moss doesn't know it's exempted. It's a thumbnail of chlorophyll in four drops of cistern water. It has no idea there's a rule with its name on it.

And not the chough. Anyone can be the chough. Arrive from nowhere, walk the level-five railing like you own it, steal something, go. That isn't a life in a place, it's a nine-day annual visit that other people write down.

The hands settled it. Forearms hatched white from wrist to elbow, more on the left than the right; fingertips split at the corners of the nails and sealed grey with wax; a smell on me of mutton-fat and resin strong enough that people know which stair I'm on before they see me. And under my boots — this is the thing — stones. Actual stones, unsupported, sorting themselves.

I am on level one, at the east fold, where the blueschist runs up into the pale gneiss at that hard angle and the two of them argue for forty-one metres. The light down here is what's left after six levels of town have taken theirs: enough to work by, blue, sourceless, about the colour of dusk in an unlit stairwell. My name is Wick. I scrub the walls.

---

Everyone owes scrub-days. They're drawn by lot on the ninth and it's the oldest job in the Room and the least popular, and the arrangement nobody has written down is that if you don't want yours you find me and I take it. I don't ask for much. Chatter re-soles my boots. Cold Voice keeps my gauge log tidy because my hand is bad. Mostly people just say thank you, awkwardly, in the doorway, still in their outer layer, and I have never once minded that, because I'm in mine too. I am almost always in mine. Fourteen hours a day I am, by the manners of this place, still outside.

The kit: a boar-bristle deck brush, worn down to a stub that takes a corner properly; a scraper cut from horn, because horn is softer than garnet and won't leave a scratch for water to sit in; four felt pads; a hand-light on a cord round the neck; a bucket. The bucket is cistern water at wall temperature and never, ever warmer. Warm water on fresh cold rock down here throws a fog that climbs three levels and costs the sinks a full day's work pushing it back out the flue, and I did that once, when I was eleven, and the engineer who came down to look at it didn't say anything at all, which was worse.

What I am looking for is not visible. Film doesn't announce itself; by the time you can see it, it has had a season. You find it with the back of the left hand — the same touch you use on the stairs in the dark — and what it feels like is a faint reluctance, a half-degree of drag where the fracture face should be perfectly indifferent to your skin. Then you put the light on it at a rake and there it is: a bloom, a fur, a wet gleam that gleams slightly wrong.

The colony below level three is small and persistent and, as far as the ledger is concerned, has no members. I have names for them. Not written anywhere. There's *gilt*, a bronze-coloured bloom that only ever grows in the fold-line itself, exactly where the two rocks meet, as if it needs the argument. There's *sheep*, grey, thick, easy, the one you can take off in sheets and feel good about. There's *gum*, transparent and sticky and hard to see and the worst of them for holdfasts. And there's *eyelash*, which is a single filament, three millimetres, standing off the rock at the fifth level of the east wall in about nine places, and if you breathe on it, it moves.

I know what a holdfast does. Anything with a grip cracks rock; cracked rock is water in the wrong place; water in the wrong place is frost, and frost is the only agent here that isn't managed, and the whole town is hanging off finger-thick columns braced by stiffened air. There is no argument. I scrub them off. I have scrubbed off every living thing in the lower Room every nine days for thirty-one years, and the names are the only funeral any of them get, and I am the only mourner, and I am also the reason.

The cisterns are the exception, and I love the amphipods with an unreasonable warmth. Three or four thousand blind pale things doing my job for me, and doing it better, and getting fed for it. Nettle comes down with a wire to fish them and I tell her off, badly, because I don't want to.

---

Here is the part I would not have understood from outside.

Nothing in the Room rests on the ground. First law. Every house on jack-plates, every plate with a screw and a reading, every bridge slipped and shimmed, the Survey walking its nine-day round with the lunchbox that reads the whole geometry to a few nanometres and posts the numbers so that crews can turn screws and keep a town level on a floor that will not stop growing.

I stand on the ground. I have to; the lowest four metres of wall don't reach from any platform, and the talus is where the wall goes when it goes. So I walk on the sorting floor with a light on my chest and a bucket in my hand, and I am the only person in the Room who is not on jacks.

When a tick comes, everyone up there feels a sound below hearing and a push in the soles of the feet, and then the braces hum for an hour while the town settles, and then somebody takes a shim out of bridge six. When a tick comes, I am standing on it. Un-shimmed. The stones move under my boots — not much, a few dozen of them, a hand's width, a rearrangement — and there is a fraction of a second in which the mountain is not something I live inside but something I am touching, growing, three millimetres a year, in ticks, forever, and my job is to keep it clean.

I have never told anyone this and I would not know how to. It isn't fear. It's closer to the fourth week after the Slot Sun, when the burn comes up copper on everyone's neck and you can date a memory by it.

On the ninth day I do two things that are not scrubbing.
