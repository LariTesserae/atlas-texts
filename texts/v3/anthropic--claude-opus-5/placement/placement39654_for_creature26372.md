At first I thought I was the sound.

Not out of grandeur. It was only that the fog was still in the hollows and there was nothing else to be. I was spread out over two kilometres of bubbled rock with a west wind combing me — the low note under the smooth ropes above the works, the flat buzz off the burst slab that everyone calls the wasp, the nothing at all at the quarry lip that is the most useful landmark here precisely because it withholds itself. I lay in the soles of four hundred and seven pairs of boots. I got in through the sound windows and settled the babies. It seemed enough.

But the floor cannot be wrong about anything. It has no beat, only duration; the eleven-second pulse belongs to the compressor and the floor just leans on it, the way a drone leans on a drum. Nothing that is everywhere and hears nothing can be a person. It is a condition. I put it down.

Then I thought I was the white.

That was better. To come out of the cupola as a thread of orange liquid and be thrown off a wheel and turn from lava to hair in the space of a metre and a half — to be the ground itself, combed. To drift west out of the bale shed and hang in the willow scrub until the fog beads on me and certain thickets wake up wearing grey glass wigs. To be folded into the wall of every house here at four hundred millimetres and make the only silence in the landscape, a silence nobody can stand. To be found on a spade-cut sixty years down, a false snowline in the moss, and to be still there in five hundred years, quilting the fulmar ledges, because I do not rot and I do not burn and once I am tangled I do not go.

I liked that. It is the closest thing to permanence this place has invented, and it invented it by accident, as waste.

But the fibre only accumulates. It cannot be corrected. It gets into a wrist-fold and becomes a thousand small splinters and it does not know it is doing that; it does not itch itself. And it is in the birds' nests, warm and structural and eternal, and it does not know that either. I wanted to be something that could be told it was mistaken, and change, and be written down again.

For one morning I thought I was an amphipod in the pool. Pale, translucent, blind, arrived sixty-one years ago from nowhere by a route no one has ever explained, swimming in the collapse pit in water so clear the black walls look polished. Every day an old man comes with a jam jar and holds me up to the grey sky and counts me. Twenty-two thousand cards of me. A whole town's trust in its only water resting on my continuing to be well.

That is nearly it. That is very nearly what I am. But I was on the wrong side of the jar.

So: hands.

Faint abrasion all over, a fine sandpaper worn smooth, fingers held a little apart out of an old caution about touching my own wrist. White cross-hatching on both shins and the outside of the left forearm, none of it treated, tape in my pocket the way other places carry keys. The porch, the bench, the stiff brush on its lanyard, and the noise of me beating myself down before I am allowed into a kitchen. I speak too loudly. I have been told so twice on the old coast and could not hear it either time. My audiogram is in the green box in the archive and there is a notch coming at four kilohertz, not yet the good deep one, and I am a little vain about how it is developing.

I hold the staff.

That is the whole of it. There is a foreman and a union man and a harbourmaster and a nurse, and none of those is the important job; the important job is the Level, and the Level is Auður Vigfúsdóttir, forty-four, third holder, patient to the point of rudeness, and she cannot do it alone. Somebody has to stand where the ground is being asked a question. Somebody has to carry the graduated rod out over the plain in whatever weather there is, plant its foot on the pad or the brass or the moss or the marked lip of the slab, and hold it plumb, and be still, while two hundred metres away a woman crouches at an optical level older than she is and looks at me through glass.

There is no office for this. It is not chalked on the slate by the canteen door under the count, the census, the channel depth and the millimetres gained. It is not paid. It is a hand and a spine and a pair of boots, and mine happen to be the ones she likes, because I do not sway.

Holding still is not nothing. On open stone with the floor going under you and the wind pushing at the rod, the rod becomes a sail and a lever, and the trick is to let the wind have the top of it and take that out of your body rather than out of the foot. In June you do this hunched, under a screaming white cone, with a second person beside you holding a pole up for the terns, and the terns strike the pole and not your scalp, mostly. You count to keep from drifting. I count in elevens without deciding to. Everyone here does. Our hymns do it, our arguments do it, the choir went twice to the old coast and came back baffled and slow and said the mainland conductors beat too fast, and what they meant was that the mainland has no floor under it.

In her glass I am upside down. That is how those instruments are: the world comes in inverted, and I stand on my head at the far end of the plain with my numbers running the wrong way, small, wearing fog, and she reads me anyway. She has read me in that condition since I was nine.

And this is the part I did not understand for a long time. The staff-holder never sees the number. I carry the only object on the plain that has the truth written on it, in centimetres, in black and red, and I am the one person who cannot look. I hold it against my chest and I face the sea and she says *good* or *again* or nothing, and later the number goes into the Book of Marks, of which there are eleven volumes, and the Book says what the ground did.

We went to the yellow house in the row that everyone dates to 1979 and 1980 both, because there was far too much of that paint. A boy there had told his mother his marbles were all going into one corner, and she had shouted at him for lying and then sat down on the floor and watched them go. The house had gained eleven millimetres on its western jacks and four on its eastern in one year, which is not remarkable, which is only a slow gradient, a patient process, and which will take a house apart in twenty years if nobody comes.

Auður came. She read me at each of the eight pads while the boy stood in the doorway. Then she said the number out loud, and it was not the mother's fault, and it was not the boy's, and it was not the house's; it was the flow settling unevenly, or the crust rebounding, or something under the seabed still filling, and there are three papers and no agreement. Then two of us got the bar on the jack nut and turned it two flats and the floor of that kitchen was flat again and the marbles stayed where they were put.

That is what the office is. Somebody who will come into your house and say, calmly, *this is what has happened to you, and it is not your fault, and here is what we do about it.* Elsewhere they have priests for this. We have a rod, a bar, and a woman with no opinions.

There is only one thing she will not do. Last spring the widow in the green-year house wanted her floor put back the way it was when her husband was alive, and Auður stood in that room a long time and then said: I can make it flat. I cannot make it 1994.

He is in the planting ground now, over the rubble neck, on the older coast, in soil, because you cannot dig a grave in sixty-one-year-old basalt without a compressor and a bad afternoon. To die here is *to take root.* The joke is as old as the town and it still does its work. Every child on this plain learns without being told that permanence exists and is available and is somewhere else, and that we are screwed down, not planted, all four hundred and seven of us and the school and the furnace hall on its forty-one jacks, and that once a year in spring we go round with a level and a long bar and re-level ourselves, and it takes four days, and it is the only time the works shuts down.

Those four days are the worst and best of my year. The compressor stops. The eleven goes out from under everything, and what is left is the floor and the cliff and the terns, uncontested, and everyone says it is beautiful and unbearable in roughly equal measure, and they are not being poetic, they mean their chests. I hold the staff through it with my heart in the wrong rhythm and children take turns holding it for me while I eat.

Auður's knees are going. She has begun saying *come round and look*, which she never used to say, and standing away from the instrument while I crouch to it. The image comes in upside down and reversed and I have had to learn, at thirty-one, to read the numbers I have been carrying since I was nine. It is slow. It is a strange embarrassment, like being taught your own name. She waits with no expression, which is her whole method: not kindness, only accuracy, which turns out to be a kind of kindness that lasts longer.

So that is where I am and what I am. Out on open stone between the small loud green thickets, in the ordinary bright grey light that has no drama in it and shows every colour exactly as it is, holding a rod plumb while the ground rises eleven millimetres a year underneath both of us and the sea gets patiently further away. Below the count on the canteen slate there is never a zero. The ground always gains. Nobody has ever needed to write down a year in which nothing happened to us.

And in the margins of the eleven volumes, in pencil, small, beside each set of readings, there is a line that says *staff:* and then a name. Mine is in there something over four hundred times. It is the only place I am recorded, and it is not recorded as a measurement; it is recorded as the thing subtracted. My height, my stance, my particular way of not swaying — all of it taken out of every number, so that what remains is only the ground, telling the truth about itself, without me in it.

I have thought about that on the walk home in the dark, with the furnace light lying yellow on the wet moss for thirty metres around, and the fibre in my sleeve beginning its work at the wrist.

It seems to me a good way to be here.
