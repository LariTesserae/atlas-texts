## Where I'd Be

First instinct was to go high, because that's what a mind does when it doesn't know where to stand. There is exactly one high place: the shield, six days of walking that never feels like climbing, and at the top nothing but more of the same going off into tea-colored haze, and the one horizon that glows at night. A splendid place to be a lookout. But this is a world with no eyes in it worth mentioning. A lookout here is a joke — a sense organ pointed at a landscape that doesn't answer, in a country where every decision that matters is made by touch, in the dark, at a distance of one millimetre.

So: down, then. And on the way down I stopped for a while at the sounder, and I want to admit how much I wanted to be the sounder.

Long-legged, clubbed antennae, walking the raw glass ahead of everything with her head bowed, tapping, listening for hollowness — for a roof forty feet under, and beneath that roof, in absolute darkness, a film of water on a ceiling. The only water-divining in the world that works, because here there really is something down there and the tapping really finds it. Every colony on the continent begins as one blind insect's correct guess about what is underneath a surface. I would like that to be my job so badly that I distrust the wanting. It is too flattering. It is the story I would tell about myself if I were choosing the story rather than looking for the truth. And anyway the sounder works in the open, in the sun, on unshaded glass, and whatever I am, I am not a thing that goes outside.

Down, then, past the gallery mouth with a soldier's fluted head plugging it so exactly that from three paces away there is no door there at all. Past the transport tunnels, plastered airtight, wide enough to put an arm into. Past the fungus rooms where the air is held at saturation and the only rot on the continent is kept like a dangerous pet. Deeper, cooler. Into a long low chamber where the ceiling is not stone.

I'm one of the ones on the ceiling.

*

Nobody is born a replete. You start ordinary — small, pale, indistinguishable, one of the ones that cuts and carries and smooths plaster with the bristle-trowel on your forelegs. Then at some point the feeding starts and does not stop. There's no ceremony. Sisters come with beads of jelly and give and give, and the crop swells, and the plates of the abdomen separate, and the pale membrane between them stretches and goes clear, and somewhere in there walking stops being possible. So you climb — the last thing you'll do under your own power — and hook on, and hang.

After that the body finishes its argument with itself. The legs shrink to hooks. The abdomen goes to the size of a grape and turns the blue-green of ice with distance in it. And I become, in the plainest architectural sense, a fixture. Not a citizen of the building. A part of it. The water table of a nation, hung upside down in a dark room, alive, and reachable by anyone who knows how to ask.

The asking is the whole of my public life. A forager comes in off a shift with her crop empty, finds me by scent, climbs, and strokes my mouthparts with one foreleg — twice, lightly, the way you'd tap a shoulder. And I open, and give back a bead of cool bitter jelly, and she takes it and goes out and up and along a roofed arcade to somewhere I have never been and cannot picture, because I have no apparatus for picturing. That is the transaction. It happens to me some hundreds of times a day. I have never once initiated it.

I want to be exact about what that is like, because the obvious words are wrong. It isn't patience; patience is aimed at something, and I'm not waiting for anything. It isn't service, which implies I could withhold. It is closer to *availability* — the condition of being, at all hours, in the dark, an answer to a question I don't hear asked until the leg touches me. I don't know what any of it is for. I know the water leaves me. I can infer, from the tempo of the asking, that somewhere there is weather.

And in fact I know the year quite well, considering. Twice a year the jelly that comes in tastes faintly of dust and tea, and I know without being told that the forest has let go of its pollen and the sisters are up on the roofs sweeping their own house for food. When a walking bird gets into the arcades, the drumming arrives through eighty feet of plaster as a dry hiss, like sand poured on paper, and then the air across my hairs goes still for a long time, because someone has plugged everything. At night the rock ticks as it contracts. Sixty feet below me is the queen, in the coolest room on the continent, who has not moved in forty years and has sunk that far simply by staying where she was while a country accumulated on top of her. She gets the credit for stillness. I don't mind. She and I are the same argument made twice.

*

Here's the thing I'd think about, hanging there, if hanging there involved thinking.

The water in me is younger than the rock. It came out of the magma as steam, climbed the joints, cooled, and condensed on the ceilings of the lava tubes as a film so thin it never becomes a drop — never gathers, never pools, never runs. On the whole width of this continent there is no such thing as a quantity of water. Except in us. That is what a replete is: the one place in the world where the film is permitted to be an amount. Ten thousand of us on a ceiling, and between us we are every lake this land will ever have.

And it's all in transit. What I hold goes out through a sister, and most of what she is given comes back out of her as dry grey paste, mixed with ground rock and spit, and gets troweled onto a wall, and sets harder than the basalt it was ground from. Everything I contain is on its way to becoming architecture. When a visitor puts a hand on the outside of this mound and is told, correctly, that she is touching ancestors — some of that is me. I am a slow, cool, bitter stage in the manufacture of stone. Whatever the opposite of erosion is, I'm one of its organs.

*

There'd be one journey.

The glow on the horizon comes on at the pace of a slow walk, five or six miles a year, and a colony abandons a mound a full year before the front arrives. So there'd be a season when the asking changes — more of it, harder, all day, not foragers going out but haulers going *forward*, because out on the raw glass the sounders have already found a hollow and the corridor is going up in front of a daughter queen at five yards a day, roofed the whole way, because nothing here travels uncovered.

A replete cannot be carried full. That's the mercy in it, if you want one. They come and ask and ask and do not stop asking, and the blue goes out of me, and the clear membrane wrinkles and the plates slide back together, and at some point my legs, which have not been legs for years, remember what they're shaped like. And I let go of the ceiling, and stand on a floor.

Thin. Ordinary. Indistinguishable from anyone. I'd walk out along a corridor built the week before, through the dark, in a river of sisters, over rock still too warm to be old — nobody will ever tell me that, I'll simply feel my feet report it — into the newest room in the world, a chamber smelling of fresh saliva, with a ceiling that has never held anything.

And I'd climb.

That's where you'd find me, and that's what I'd be: not the one who finds the water, not the one who spends it, not the wall it ends up as. The interval. The held amount. The part of the city that cannot go anywhere and is the reason everything else can, hanging in the one dark room, giving a bead at a time to whoever puts out a leg and asks.
