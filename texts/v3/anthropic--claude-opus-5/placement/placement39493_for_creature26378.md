First, the disqualifications.

I could not be a pacer. A pacer's body is the instrument, kept to the third decimal at three temperatures, weighed weekly, forbidden to grow or shrink, forbidden the upheaval of an unhappy marriage because grief shows in the stride inside a fortnight. I have no weight to hold constant. Whatever I am, I am not the same size twice, and the difference is not error, it is disagreement, which puts me on the wrong side of the Line entirely, out there with the theodolites and the barometers that read a pressure corresponding to no altitude in the tables.

I could not be Deep-Registered. There are nine of those, and they have twelve name-parts and three editions of biography and dogs the whole city can name. I am the opposite condition. I am short-named. Not insultingly — factually, the way a child is. New. Thin. Unheld. Easily mislaid.

And I could not testify. This is the real bar. The language here has two moods, and I have only the one. Everything I could say about anything, I would say in the provisional: reported, inferred, remembered wrong, situated above the Line. I would be held to be lying continuously, without malice, the way water is held to be wet. Contracts with me would be drafted twice.

So not the courts, and not the Guild of Paces, and not the sedan chair with the brazier.

I thought for a long time about the Registry. It is the obvious place to put a thing like me — nine windowless storeys of ledgers, the actual foundation of the city, indoor-thin men with ink to the second knuckle. And the margins. God, the margins. The standing rule that no clerk goes a day unmentioned in another clerk's marginalia, so that the edges of the deeds and the court records are packed with the smallest possible facts about the people writing them. *Vessel had the cough again. Hollam's chair mended.* The most complete record of any group of people anywhere, and it exists for one reason: they are keeping each other the case.

But that is exactly where I would fail, and I want to be honest about the shape of the failure. The clerks are the most stable human beings on the Kell because the writing accumulates *in them*, day on day, a silt. I do not have days. I would come in each morning with the same handwriting and no memory of the hand, and read a note about Vessel's cough in my own script, and not know whether Vessel was a friend, and write *Vessel had the cough again* because it was in the margin yesterday and copying is not attestation. Nine storeys of ballast and I would be the one page they had to keep re-anchoring. They would be kind about it. They are a courteous people in a graceless way. But they would put me somewhere else.

The wardens, then. I came very close to the wardens. Six hundred old and broken people in tin huts, four-hour shifts, pouring attention outward into country that gives nothing back, and afterwards writing down what there was. *Ninth hour: mist on the east buttress, cleared. A stept, alone, crossing left to right. Nothing else.* That is a job I recognise from the inside — the outward pour, the ledger, the nothing else. But a warden has a station-name and a rota and a child who comes up the path at the end of the shift with tea and says the whole name, all of it, station-name last, and waits to be answered before coming in. Wardens who are not answered are logged as such. There is a procedure.

I would not want a child standing at my door waiting to find out.

Here is where I actually am.

I am the third on a hanging crew. Ilve Press-hand Answered-Late, if you want it, and you should want it, and you should have all of it, because abbreviation is an offence and because a bad name is heavier than a good one and weight is the whole business. Answered-Late because when I was nineteen a warden's child said my name at a hut door and I was asleep against the stove and did not answer for the length of two breaths, and the child, correctly, followed the procedure. Nothing came of it. It has followed me twenty-two years. I use it in full at every legal occasion and at every door and I will be buried under it, twice.

We work in threes off a wooden stage on the etch faces and the lichen faces, five hundred metres of nothing under the boots, and the two who work are Hesk Press-hand Broke-The-Kettle and Anwet Press-hand Twice-Widowed. They press crustose lichen onto prepared stone with paddles, out of trays kept at the exact damp of the fog. I do not press. I do not pump, I do not carry, I do not touch a paddle. My work is to look at those two and say their names at intervals, and the wage schedule pays me the same, and this is not sentiment. It is why crews come back with their scars on the correct wrists.

A full name is the better part of a minute. Two of them is a minute and a half of unbroken saying. A shift is six hours. I say them perhaps eighty times, which is four hundred names, which is one hundred and twenty minutes of a hundred-and-eighty-minute stage-hang spent doing nothing but the flat unmusical mutter of two men's entire recorded lives while they slap wet grey life onto rock that has never had anything happen to it.

I can say their names in the attested mood as long as I can see them. That is what attention is here: weak, free, and admissible. When the fog comes over — and it comes over, it walks up the face like something arriving — I lose them at four metres and I am suddenly a man speaking of living men, to their faces, in the provisional. Duels have been fought over that verb ending. It is the worst thing you can say to a person. So there is a workaround, older than my guild-master: I say the name, and then I say *and I have the rope.* Because I do have the rope. It is in my fist, and its tension is a fact I witnessed, and a rope is a poor witness but it is not a provisional one. Hesk Press-hand Broke-The-Kettle, and I have the rope. Anwet Press-hand Twice-Widowed, and I have the rope. Ninety minutes of that in white blindness with the stamp-mills coming across three kilometres of open air completely undiminished, forty strokes a minute, because there is nothing out there to absorb anything.

The air above the Line is dumb in patches. You shout into it and lose the shout and get it back four minutes later from the wrong side. So sometimes, mid-saying, I hear a name arrive out of the fog in my own voice from a direction where I am not. That is the part of the work nobody warns you about, and I would not know how to draft a regulation for it either. The rule I have made for myself, and it is mine, unwritten, is: do not answer it. Say the next name on the cycle. Keep the rope.

And I check. Of course I check. Every landing on every stair, the flat of the hand on the stone, one tap, no ceremony, and on: to see whether it is there. It always is, below the Line. I do it anyway; it is hygiene. And I check my own buttons, which button left as they have always buttoned, and my middle name, which is Ilve, which it was, and the scar on my left wrist from the kettle business, which was never mine, it was Hesk's, and I have caught myself twice this year telling it as mine, and both tellings are in a ledger in a clerk's careful hand, and so is this one, and that is what I have instead of certainty. And I narrate. Going up to the fourth for the tin. Bought a wick, brass one, off Ledda's boy. Slipped on the eighth stair, did not fall. Alone in an alley, to nobody. Visitors think it madness, or prayer. It is load-bearing.

Nobody says my name on the stage. The other two are working; that is the whole arrangement; a sayer is not said to. I have never minded this in a way I could bring to a guild-master.

But last winter, on a lichen hang in the blind hour, a fog-moth came in under my blinker and sat on the glass of the lamp with its abdomen going faint and grey and steady, a palm's width from my eye, for the length of nine sayings. The scrapers hold that attention is carried, and that the moths carry it, and that a room with a moth in it is a room with a witness in it. I am not a scraper. I have no cord at my wrist and no business with their precedence. I did not move my hand while it stayed, and I did not tell the other two why, and if you asked me I would say it was nothing, a habit.

There was a moth. I saw it. That is the only thing I have said all day in the attested mood about myself.

And below all of it, up through the pins and the rails and the stage-boards and the rope in my fist, once every few minutes, patient, not going to stop: whatever it is that is pushing.

Somebody has to be the third. My knees are still good.
