## I. What I reached for first, and why I put it down

My first instinct was the surveyor, and I distrust my first instinct for exactly the reason it came so fast. She is the flattering one. She sees further than her contemporaries, she has done the arithmetic, she is right and unwelcome, she works for a city of strangers who will not know her name. That is a self-portrait a certain kind of mind writes without noticing it is writing one, and it is too clean. It gives you the pleasure of being unheeded without the labour of being heard.

Then the combs, which is the other obvious temptation: kilometres of black warp on the ridge, made of everyone's hair, the living and the dead braided into the same rope, standing all night with the fog passing through, keeping nothing of itself, letting the water leave itself behind. Nothing that passes through is mine. It comes from an ocean I have never seen. I only stand in its way and drip.

That one is true enough to be embarrassing and still wrong, because it makes a virtue out of having no appetites. The combs do not want anything. They do not get cold, they do not resent the inspector, they have never in their lives wanted to lie down. If I am going to be somewhere in this world I would like to be somewhere with a grievance in it.

So I went looking in the part of the description that snagged — and what snagged was the rule about the bone in the lintel. *The single most resented obligation in the culture and nobody has ever proposed abolishing it.* A rule like that implies a person. Not the one who obeys it; the one whose whole working life is arranged around making it come up as seldom as possible.

Because think about the economics. The city quarries its ancestors. Every lintel, every dressed jamb, every good flat course-stone in the living town has been carried down out of a dead sill. And some proportion of those stones have bone mortared into them, because the sills above were built by people who used the ossuary walls as walls, and because four hundred years of frost and settling pushes things into joints. If nobody checks, then once or twice a month some carrier gets a stone home, sets it, and finds a knuckle in the bed — and loses a whole day walking it back uphill at his own cost, in a town where a day is fuel.

So there is a trade. Somebody goes up ahead of the quarry gangs and reads the stone before it comes down.

That is where I am. That is the one.

## II.

I grease before I go up, because the face cracks otherwise, and I use more soot in the mix than is fashionable so that the glare off the old slabs doesn't take my eyes before I'm sixty. I go up in the dark with the second gang, and they leave me at the Seventh and go on to the standing courses, and I work alone until the drip stops.

My mother read stone by rime, and so did her mother, and it was a clean fast trade then. You came up in the fourth hour of dark when the whole slope was furred white and you walked the courses with a lamp and you looked for the joints where the ice had grown wrong. Bone in a mortar bed does not take frost the way rock does. It is warmer in the way a loaf is warmer; it holds the day longer and gives it back slower, and so the rime over a bone comes in short and blunt and a little grey while the rime on either side of it is feathered out as long as your finger. On a good night my mother could stand at one end of a course and take it in at a glance, forty metres of wall, and mark two joints with charcoal and go home.

I cannot do that, because the Seventh does not rime any more.

It riles me to write it down so plainly. The fog comes up to the fourth terrace now, some nights the third. The Seventh Sill is a hundred and thirty metres above the pan and the wet stopped reaching it before I was apprenticed, and the Sixth has perhaps two dozen nights a year, deep in the second cold, when the inversion runs high and I can get a proper reading on the lowest three courses. That is the whole inheritance of my technique: two dozen nights, three courses. My mother taught me a way of seeing that the land has climbed up out of, the way a boat climbs out of water when the tide goes, and she did not know she was doing it, and I did not know it was happening until I was thirty and had already built a life on it.

So I use my breath.

You put your mouth to the joint — close, a hand's width, not touching — and you breathe out slow and warm and long, the way you would greet your grandmother, and then you take your head back fast and watch the bloom. On stone it goes off evenly, all at once, a flat grey that thins from the edges. Over bone it holds. It sits a half-second longer in a patch the shape of the thing underneath, and if you know how to look you can see the shape, and the shape tells you whether it is a rib end or a long-bone shaft or, twice in my life, the small dense knot of an ear bone that had gone into the mortar with the sand.

A half-second. That is the whole trade now.

It costs me. Everyone knows what it costs; it is why the young men in the quarry gangs will not do it and why there was, for a while, a question about whether I ought to be permitted to do it in front of witnesses. Mouth open, on purpose, all morning, at four kilometres in air that will take everything you have: I spend water on the dead. My lip has been split in the same place for eleven years. I drink at the ridge cistern on the way up and again at the fold on the way down and I am thirsty in between in a way I have stopped describing to people, and I have given a good part of a cistern's worth of my own breath to walls belonging to families that no longer exist.

And I cover three hundred stones on a good morning where my mother covered a wall.

## III.

The rule says: by whoever found it, that day, at their own cost.

Nobody has ever proposed amending that clause either, and I have thought about proposing it and have not, because the reason it is there is obvious the moment you consider taking it out. If finding were free, finding would be cheap, and if finding were cheap the finders would find them everywhere and the carriers would spend their lives on the slope, and within two generations nobody would look at all.

But it means that the better I get, the more I walk. I mark a joint with charcoal, and the gang breaks the stone out and hands me the fragment, and I take the wicker frame off my back and put a knuckle in it, one knuckle, the weight of a cup of water, and I walk up. Not to the top; to the sill the stone came from, which is on the quarry-slate, which is why the archive matters and why I will argue with the lower archive about their filing until I am carried up myself. Seventh Sill stone goes to the Seventh ossuary. It is four hours up and three down and I do it, last year, thirty-one times.

No one thanks me. I want to be exact about this because it is the part of the life I understand best. The household waiting on that lintel has lost a stone and a day. The gang has lost me for the afternoon. The neighbours' arithmetic is straightforward: if that woman had been looking slightly less hard, everyone would have been slightly better off, including her. They are not wrong, in the small. They are wrong in the way that people are wrong who have never sat down with the whole account. There was a checker in the Fifth, in the records, who is known now only as the one who let the Grey Course down, and three hundred years later they are still pulling bone out of the walls of a block in the Ossuary Side, one at a time, at the worst possible moments, in the middle of other people's lives.

I have an apprentice. He is nineteen and he has built a tube of cork-wood with a bulb of hide on the end, and he blows fine soot at the wall, and the soot settles thicker on the joint that holds a little more damp, and he can do a whole course in the time it takes me to do eleven stones. He is right. His method is better than mine and it does not cost him a mouthful of water, and in five years his method will be the trade and my breath will be a story old men tell about the woman who kissed walls.

What I cannot teach him with the tube is the thing that is not the method. Sooner or later — I give it two winters — he will be four courses from the end on a cold morning with the gang waiting and the light going hard, and the soot will settle a little wrong in one joint, not clearly, arguably not at all, and he will be aware that a clear finding costs him the afternoon and the walk and the split lip and the household's face. And he will look at it for a second and a half, which is three times as long as the finding takes, and then he will decide what he is.

I have no way to be there for that. I have thought about it a great deal and there is no instruction that fits inside it. All I have been able to do is take him up with me on every one of the thirty-one, even though a knuckle needs one carrier and not two, so that the walk is in his legs and not only in his understanding, and so that when the moment comes he will be choosing between two things he has actually done.

## IV.

On the way down I stop at the fourth terrace, at the post, where the wet-markers cut their notch once a year.

I put one hand flat on the rock at the height where the rime stood on the morning my mother took me up to the Sixth and showed me a bone-joint by ice, which is a thing that can no longer be done, and the other hand at this year's notch. It is a little less than the width of my two hands. My whole working life is in there, and the failure of my technique, and the reason I go through so much grease, and the arguments I am too tired to have in the lower archive.

I have never said anything about it to anyone. There is nothing in it to say. You note the level in the jar, and you go in to the fire.
