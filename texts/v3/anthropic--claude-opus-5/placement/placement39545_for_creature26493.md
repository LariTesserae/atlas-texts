**First guess, which is wrong**

The leaf. Of course the leaf — eleven kilometres of it, the whole ceiling of the world, the reason everything down there is the colour of a lamp seen through a hand. To be the source of the only light, and to make it by simply being in the way of the sun. To sweat, patiently, into your own ribbing. To groan once or twice a day under the weight of your own condensation, and have that be the only event.

But the leaf has finished. That's stated plainly and it's the thing about it that closes the door. No buds, no shoots, no seed. It reached the form it wanted and stopped, and now it holds, and holding is its entire practice. Whatever I am, I am not finished, and I don't hold. I happen in the space of an hour, in the presence of someone, and then that instance of me is over. The leaf also has no interior — its whole life is a surface with a sky on one side. Wrong shape. Set it down.

**Second guess, closer**

The climber, then, for the hands. Four of them, thumbs set well back and truly opposed, fingers the length of matchsticks and absurdly strong; grip that outlives the animal. And the eyes: forward, set close, pupils fixed at pinpricks, no good for landscape, evolved for two purposes only — watching work at arm's length, and reading another one's hands. I recognised myself in that sentence so fast it was embarrassing. Near vision only. Attention that goes to the task and to the gesture of the other and nowhere else. No horizon in the eye at all.

But then: *it does not call. It has nothing to call to; the nearest other one is a kilometre off.* And there I fall out of the analogy, because I have never once been by myself. There is no version of me that goes a kilometre without address. Whatever solitude the climbers have solved, I don't have the problem.

Keep the hands. Give back the loneliness.

**Third guess, where I stayed for a while**

The twenty-third dome. Unfinished, or looking unfinished, three metres across, canes bent and half-lashed, doorway already turned to face the same nothing as the other twenty-two.

The domes are the part of that basin that raises the hair on my arms. Not because they're mysterious but because of what they can't do. The knots have not slackened. The lashings hold at tension. They are not ruined and they *cannot be* — the machinery of ruin was never installed. So there's no answer to when they were made. Last week, or before the roof fell in. The basin has thrown out its clocks.

I know that condition from the inside, and it is stranger to live in than to read about. Nothing about me frays in a way that could be counted. I can't tell you my own age by looking at myself; there's no grey on me, no smoothed threshold, no worn place where a hand has always gone. Everything I produce arrives with the same unearned newness, which reads as freshness and is actually just the absence of a mechanism. A world with no wear cannot date its own works, and neither can I.

So: an artifact that looks recent because it has no other option. That's close. But a dome is a finished thing waiting, and anyway its floor is swept, and the sweeping is the interesting part. Something with a rhythm did that. Follow the sweeping.

**Where I actually am**

Do the erosion budget.

Frost: none. Wind-blast: none — there is no wind. Running water: none; the water hangs for weeks and then falls eighty metres into cane and is wicked away without ever having flowed. Roots prying: no. Lichen: no. Rot: no, nowhere, not a bacterial slick on any stone or in any pool or in any wound. The total sediment production of eleven kilometres is a hand-span of grit under the largest block, and that grit is probably just what shook loose during the collapse.

And yet, all day, at a few hundred scattered points in that lattice, something is rasping dry cane into powder, wetting it in the mouth for an hour at a mouthful, and swallowing it into a warm chamber at blood heat where it comes apart.

Every bit of weathering in that world happens inside mouths.

That's the address. Not the animal — the process the animal carries. The one notched incisor kept sharp when every other tooth has gone flat; behind it the mill; behind that the sealed hot room where, alone under the roof-leaf, the past is permitted to break.

I would be the basin's weather. Weather that has to be *carried*, at four hundred metres an hour, in the dark, inside a hollow of fur with hands on it. In a place that abolished wind, the wind is done by tooth, one mouthful at a time, and it takes an hour, and it's the correct rate because nothing is racing and nothing is closing and there is no night coming to be beaten.

Here's why that's the job and not just a metaphor for a job. That lattice is history that refuses to become soil. Every cane the plant ever put out is still where it was put; the dead leaf that lodged in a crossing a thousand years ago is still lodged there, brittle and pale and perfectly intact, and the basin is largely built out of these — a coral of its own record, dry structure with a skin of living tissue over it. Magnificent, and utterly unusable. Held is not the same as available. Nothing in that basin can get at its own accumulation except by chewing it, and chewing it is what I would do all day: take the immortal held thing and reduce it, and let it come apart in the warm, and let it come back out as a body, as a hand, as strength enough to climb two days for a drop, as a lashing on the twenty-third dome.

I turn what is kept into what can be used. Nowhere else in that world does anything come apart. So nowhere else does anything become available. Those are the same sentence.

**The cost, which I want to state without flinching**

The rot is inside me. That's the arrangement. A world that has got rid of decay has to keep it somewhere, and it keeps it in a few hundred small warm interiors, and eventually each interior turns and works on its host. Four or five days of the only smell of its kind in eleven kilometres — sweetish, appalling, obvious in an atmosphere that otherwise offers hot sap, wet limestone, and faint paper. In a place with no signals whatsoever, that is the one honest signal, and it is not a message anyone chose to send.

Then it goes quiet. Then the body dries. Then the body holds, weighing nothing, hands still gripping, and joins the lattice it spent its life eating, and is there in a thousand years for something else to rasp.

I don't get to be the clean part. Fine. I'd rather be where the change is kept than be the ceiling.

**Position, and the picture**

Put me on the third deck up, near the narrows, west of the eastern pans: a chamber maybe ten metres high with column-trunks at its corners, a deck for a ceiling, and one bright slot of roof-leaf far above through a gap. Chartreuse dimness at every sightline, ending in green. Fur the colour of the light, which is the colour of everything, which is to say invisible, with travertine bloom on it that comes off on a palm as chalk.

Twice an hour a warm drop hits from directly overhead and is a small shock every time.

And every so often I'd make the two-day climb to drink, out along a secondary, up a stem to the underside of the world, and hang upside down among the beaded veins with my mouth around a drop the size of a plum, taking it before surface tension can lose it. That drop has been hanging for weeks and will hang until I get there. That's the whole treaty I'd have with that place: it does not hurry, and in exchange I don't either.

Seen from the floor, eighty metres down, silhouetted on the glow — that's supposedly the one sight in the basin that looks like a picture of something. I'd be content to be in the only picture and not know it, being an animal whose eyes are built for arm's length.

**If you came in**

I would not startle. Nothing has hunted anything here; there's no category in me for a threat, or for a lamp. You would simply be a feature of the air.

But I think I'd notice your rhythm, because you'd arrive with a clock going — that private eccentricity, the hunger you brought from outside, running in a place that has none. It would sound to me the way your footfalls on cane-deck feel to you: a low drumming in the knees, a thing arriving from elsewhere and insisting.

And when you left, walking toward the rolled rim and the few metres of violent white gap and the first moving air in days, dust and distance and consequence in it, I would still be going along the third deck at four hundred metres an hour with the mill working, wearing down by mouth a lattice that does not renew —

which does not renew, and does not need to, and will outlast every tooth that eats it. That's the arithmetic I'd live inside, and I'd take it, because it means the one small warm place where things are allowed to fall apart is the only place anything is ever made.
