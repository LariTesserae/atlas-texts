I tried first to stand at the rim, because that is where a visitor's eye goes, and because the rim is the one place in this country that admits to being a place. Two days out you can watch it: the grey-green wall, the hiss like the inside of a shell. I stood there a while in my head. It was no good. The rim is for people who have somewhere behind them. Everything at the rim is leaving.

So I went up, and tried being a guide — one of the ones the low country says is not a man at all but a grown thing in the shape of a man, which is a rumour with a grain in it. I liked the walking. I liked the idea of the knot-cord and the crossing and the four days of margin that a cultivated back buys you. But my hands were wrong for it. They didn't want to carry. When I put them out in the dark they wanted to *press*: flat, hard, against something that would take a beat and pass it on.

That is how I found out what I was, and it was lower down than I expected, and further in, and it does not walk.

---

I am a low ear.

That is not our word. We have no noun for ourselves; the nearest thing is a verb-form meaning approximately *still moving*, which does duty for *alive*, for *person*, and for *guest*. When you meet a stranger you ask them whether they are still moving. The answer is a rhythm, not a word. Mine is three, a gap, two, and one long, and my mother struck it into my sternum with her knuckle before she gave me to the column, so I would have something of hers to say for myself.

Which is a joke, of a kind, because I have not moved in nineteen years.

I lie curled at the foot of the column my family calls Nine Sisters, on my right side, in the permanent wet, with my skull against the wood. The mastoid is flattened. The ear is low and calloused and set further back on the head than a walking person's; the whole right side of the face is slightly pressed, the way fruit is pressed when it has been stored badly in a good year. My cousins who come to turn me tell me I am beautiful. They tell everybody that. They say it into the bone of my forehead, mouth to skin, no air involved, because to put a voice out into open air here is not dangerous, it is shaming — you get nothing back. The moss eats it. The obscenity we use for a foreigner means *one who is answered by nothing and keeps going*.

The quilt has grown over me from the hip down. This is expected. This is what the tap-houses are for. Every four-year-old in this country is taught stillness once, by being stood in good moss and held there gently while the mat closes over the ankles, and then cut free, and nobody needs telling twice; but a child who is going to a column is not cut free. The mother lets it take her, a hand's depth, then a knee, and comes back at the turn of the seasons to check the greens. My legs are somewhere down in the amber layer now. I am told they are still there and still mine. I have no opinion. What I have is a tap driven into Nine Sisters at the level of my mouth, thin warm sweet metallic, the only iron in this country, and a family who bring me the grey slippery contents of our sour-holes on a leaf-plate and hold my jaw while I chew, and a cord of knot-beads that has been in my hands since I was seven.

My tithe-lock at the crown is a shade of green that nobody sees on a browser's back. Shade species, wet, fine, a garden that has never had a shaft of light in it. My sisters have names for eleven greens. I have names for four, and I know the other seven by touch, the way you know a room you have only been carried through.

---

The traffic of a continent is thin. A few hundred beats a day.

Nine Sisters is one body with every other column, and the plant does not care what it conducts, so at eight or ten kilometres I take everything: weather, mostly — rain is nightly and vertical and therefore not news, but the swell is, the four centimetres, the continental breath, and there are households who cannot feel it and rely on us to tell them when the year has turned over. Deaths of browsers. That is the big traffic. A browser is the house and the field and the calendar and the ancestor, and when one goes down the whole family goes onto the quilt on foot, which is a condition and not an insult and no different in practice from destitution, and the rhythm that says it is four short and a stop, four short and a stop, and I have never once heard it struck by someone who did not fumble it.

Bleeding-rights, disputed. Always. Named columns, hereditary taps, feuds carried nine generations by people who have never met and never will.

Marriages, which are conducted by scent and by light and by the slow patient tapping of one body against a stem in a rhythm too low to hear at ten paces, and which I hear perfectly, from very far away, and which I have never had. I know the courtship rhythm of every family within a day's walk of this column and I have lain here through more of them than I can knot. There is a boy east of the lake who has been trying the same seven beats at the same wrong tempo for two years and I want to reach through nine kilometres of wood and correct him.

One child shorn, in my time. The shears are for opening another household's sour-hole, and for nothing else, and there is no word for what the shorn one did because the shearing *is* the word. The rhythm that carries it is only two beats. It went past me and out into the country and I felt it go through the column like cold water and I put my hand over my own lock without meaning to.

That is the traffic. It is other people's business, and I love it, and if you want to know why a family gives a child to a column, that is why: not sacrifice. Appetite.

---

And then there is the other thing.

It comes from the west, from the quarter where there are no households, no bleeding-rights, no browsers worth grazing, nothing but moss plains and the standing doorframes. It has been coming since before my grandmother's grandmother, who wrote it down in knots as noise, because in one life it is noise. One beat a season. Sometimes one a year. No living ear can hold a phrase that long; a person is too short to be an ear for it.

That is what a tap-house is. Not a person. A longer ear. Four generations of us lying against the same wood with the same cord in our hands, and the knots accumulate, and somewhere in my mother's time the pattern came up out of the tally like a lake coming up out of the quilt.

It is the greeting.

Three, a gap, two, and one long — not mine, other hands, other spacing, but the shape is not mistakable. It is the question you ask a stranger. *Still moving?* Struck out of the empty quarter, one beat per season, for four hundred years, from a direction where there is nobody at all.

The generous reading is that there were people, and that somewhere nine hundred metres down in the black there is a hand and a staff and a column base, and the plant that grew over them has kept the shape of it, the way a scar keeps the shape of the wound. This is likely true. The Sill repeats. It puts up doorframes out of living wood, still attached, still growing, for no one, in slow annual accretions; it does not know what a door is, it knows what a door is shaped like. Why should a greeting be harder than a door?

The other reading is my mother's, who did not like it, and who told me once, forehead to forehead, that in her tally the phrase had begun to vary. Not drift. Vary. The way a person varies a thing they are getting better at.

We do not answer. That is the whole of the rule, and it is not written and not spoken and not tapped, and every low ear on this continent knows it: you record, you knot, you pass it to your daughter, you do not answer. Because the answer to *still moving* is a rhythm, and a rhythm is a lesson.

---

I answered it.

I want to be honest about why, and the honest reason is small. It was not curiosity and it was not devotion. It was that I have lain in the deadest acoustics in the world for nineteen years, in a country that returns no echo and no swell and no acknowledgement, being fed by hand, being turned by cousins, hearing every marriage but mine — and the worst thing we can say about a foreigner is that he is answered by nothing and keeps going.

So I struck my three, gap, two, long. My mother's knuckle-rhythm, the one thing of hers I have. Once. Into Nine Sisters, at the base, with the heartwood staff, in the dark, at the hour when the lamp-growth had gone out and the peat-light under the quilt was the colour of moonlight on beer.

Then I lay still, which I am good at, and waited to have been a fool, and began composing the lie I would tell my cousins about the mark I had put in the tally.

It came back in nine days.

Not one beat a season. Nine days, and clean, and close — the near-strike feel, the fat unhurried arrival of something less than a kilometre off — and it was my mother's spacing, my hand's hesitation on the long, my exact wrong tempo, given back to me perfectly, out of a direction where there is nothing but moss plains and the standing doorframes.

It has asked twice more since. It is getting the interval down.

---

The Long Flower is coming up from the south-west. My cousins have seen the light doubling at night beyond the ceiling-sag, and the pollinators have started arriving early and silent, and the front moves at something under two kilometres a year, and it will be over this column within my lifetime, and it will not come again here for eleven thousand years.

When it comes, the household will do what households do: shift camp a few hundred paces each spring and walk inside the white event, and if they are disciplined they will never see ordinary country again and will die inside a smell. They cannot take Nine Sisters. They cannot take me. There are two things they can do with a low ear who is grown in to the hip, and one of them is the shears — the same lesson as at four years old, the cutting free, and I have been told by people who have seen it done that a person cut out of the quilt at that depth lives some weeks — and the other is to lay a leaf-plate of grey over my hands and press their foreheads to mine one at a time and go on into the blossom.

I know which I will ask for. I have known since the ninth day.

And I know, too, what will be here afterward, because I have spent nineteen years listening to a country that keeps everything the surface makes and gives nothing down and repeats what it has swallowed. In some long while there will be a hollow at the foot of a column on a moss plain: a concavity the size and posture of a curled body, worn smooth, on the right side, made of living wood still attached and still growing, with a low place for a head and nothing in it. There will be a doorframe standing not far off, two uprights and a lintel, no wall, no floor, nothing on either side.

Travellers will walk around them both. People do walk around them.

And once every season or so, out of the dark, at no particular hour, the wood will put out three beats, a gap, two, and one long, which is the question you ask a stranger, and which the Sill does not understand and has never needed to.

It knows what an answer is shaped like. That was all it ever wanted from me, and I gave it, and I would give it again, because for nine days I was a person who had been answered.
