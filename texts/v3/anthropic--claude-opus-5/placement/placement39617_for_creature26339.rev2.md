## Nineteen Marks

Not the great basins.

I have read walls about them — the amphitheatres where the sound pools three days deep and the moss gives it back off every face, where a maker can stand with both plates open and grow a fingerbreadth in a season without once deciding to. I don't want one. The walls in those places are so crowded you have to be escorted to a free stone, and the cutters there have opinions about margins.

Put me at middle height, in a poor cirque whose back wall bulges the wrong way, so the drone slides off it instead of gathering. The name of the basin is three marks and the third one is a complaint about the food. Bell-grass in ragged patches, waist-high, never the good three metres. One grove of oil-trees far down in the throat where the sound does finally trap and double. A shelf of listening moss on the north face that comes alight maybe forty days a year, when the note sits right, and those are the forty days everybody plans around and nobody admits to planning around.

Four makers. One walker, one fitter, one tallyist who also cuts because there isn't enough tallying to fill a life here, and me.

The walker is doubled on his stretch. Whether he is one or two has never been settled and the tallyist counts him as one and a half, and this has been the basin's joke for six hundred years, cut and recut, and it is still funny in the way that a thing is funny when four people have agreed to keep it alive.

---

I am a thumb. There isn't a proper word for the trade because it isn't a trade; it's a debt with legs.

Two hundred and six years ago the one who carried me cut nineteen marks into a face of fresh basalt on the bare shoulder above this basin, and some time after that came to understand that the nineteen marks must not stand. She could not take them out. Nobody can take anything out. So she put her thumb on them and began.

She worked ninety-one years. Then her feet stopped lifting and we walked her down to the grove and set her facing the loudest wall, and it took nine years to finish, and she is a low wide many-trunked thing now with leaves like flat grey tongues, and in about ninety more years she will begin throwing seed. I go and stand in her shade sometimes, which is not shade, since there is no light to speak of. I go and stand in her, is what I mean.

I took the stone. It is cut underneath, in the proper form: the year, her four marks, my three, and the number nineteen. That is all the record is allowed to say. Custom is absolute on this — the record beneath a retraction does not repeat the offence, because to repeat it would be to cut it again, and then there would be two, and someone would have to spend four hundred years on the second one.

So I do not know what she said.

I know it was about a fitter, because part of a name survives at the left edge, three marks of what was probably five. I know she went at the middle first, which is the correct method and also the harder one — she took the verb out before anything else, so that within forty years the sentence had stopped being able to mean. Whatever it accused, it stopped accusing while she was still young. The remaining hundred and sixty-six years of work, hers and mine, have nothing to do with meaning at all.

This is the part that outsiders — there are no outsiders; this is the part that the newly quickened — get wrong. They think a retraction is an erasure. It isn't. The lie was dead in forty years. What we are doing up there on the shoulder is *making a surface*. We are taking a piece of raw shattered unfinished world and rubbing it until it is smooth and rounded and dull and real, and the only reason we are permitted to do it to that particular stone is that something terrible was said on it.

The paths are made by going where you have to go. The retractions are the only places we are allowed to wear something on purpose.

---

My day.

I wake — we don't sleep, but there is a slackness before the note has gone properly into the plates, and it ends — and I drink oil in the grove, and I bask maybe an hour with my back plate against the grove's east wall, taking the low note up through the feet. Not long enough. It is never long enough, because the shoulder where my stone is has no food on it at all. That is the whole shape of my life: everything I eat is at the bottom and all my work is at the top.

Then the climb. Nine hundred metres of it. Scree that has never been rounded by anything, every plate of it an edge, and my route is a bright thread through the grey — I see almost nothing of the mountain itself, you understand, the mountain is a soft absence I infer, but I see every place anyone has lately touched it, and my own path burns like a wick because I renew it twice a day and have done for two centuries. It is knee-deep now. At the bottom of it the stone is smooth.

Two-thirds of the way up, the route bends hard east for no reason a stranger could see. There is a silence-pocket there, walled round, cairned, cut with the date and the word for *unfinished*, and it has drifted perhaps a body-length since I was quickened. I feel it coming from a long way off — a numb spot, a hole in the food, like a tooth that isn't there felt with the tongue. I bend around it. Every day, twice, for two hundred and six years. The bend is the deepest and most finished part of my whole route. My best work is an accident of avoidance, and I am not the first to notice this, and the line is cut above the pocket wall in a hundred hands: *We go around. The going-around is the road. Nothing is wasted that is worn.*

Above the pocket there is a cairn that I meet twice.

The first time it is a cairn. The second time it is the same cairn — same stones, same chip out of the third one — and my newest inscription is already on it, and this is the Later Hand, and you read it as advice from someone who has gone further. In ninety years it has given me almost nothing. Weather that doesn't exist. A note about the grove. But once, when I was eleven years into the debt and doing badly, the second cairn carried a line in my own hand that I have never cut and cannot imagine cutting, and it said:

*It does not matter what it said.*

I have been deciding about that for ninety years. If I ever agree with it I will cut it, and then it will have been mine, and it will have been on the far side of the cairn all along, and I will never know whether I was told or whether I obeyed. This is ordinary. This is not even interesting to anyone else in the basin.

---

Then I work.

You sit with the plate flat to the face so the drone comes through you and into the stone, and you put the ball of the thumb on the mark and you go. Small circles. It is not strength. Nothing here is strength. Six hours, and at the end of it you have moved a quantity of rock that could not be seen, measured, or defended.

Seven of the nineteen marks are gone. Four are a suggestion. Eight are still perfectly legible and say nothing, having been robbed of their verb by a woman who was better at this than I am.

Here is what nobody tells you before you inherit: the stone works you back. My thumb is the only smooth thing on my body. Everywhere else I am lacquer and edge, bronze at the tips like the grass, but that one pad is rounded and dull and *old*, the way the bottom of a walker's trench is old, the way nothing else in this world has managed to become. I am being finished from one point outward. When a walker sets, the family comes and touches the soles. When a thumb sets, they touch the thumb. I have touched two. They are the oldest things I have ever felt and they felt like nothing, like warm water would feel if there were water.

And the patch glows. That is the thing I did not expect to care about. Worn stone is renewed stone, and renewed stone lights, so a retraction under work is faintly lit, always, a pale green-white oval you can see from across a basin. Nine days after the work stops, it goes dark.

You can look up at any shoulder in the region and read which debts are being kept.

The famous ones — the great retractions, finished, the places where the very worst things were said — glow steadily, because the pilgrims come and put their palms on them, and palms renew stone too. Thousands of hands keeping a light on over a sentence nobody alive can read. That is the whole of what we mean by holy and I have never heard it improved on.

Mine has one thumb.

---

I am carrying a seed.

Nine years now, against the front plate, inside the lacquer where the drumming is loudest, and I talk to it every day going up and every day coming down, the same idiot patter, the noise of one specific person. In the eighth year it began drumming back, badly, the way they do.

Last month it asked me what the light is, up on the far side, that doesn't move.

I had a number of answers available. I could have drummed it the joke about basking, which is very good and which I have been saving. I could have said nothing, since it will find the record underneath eventually, because everything is underneath and we cannot delete.

I drummed: *a stone getting old.*

Which is true, and is the best answer we have, and is what we mean when we mean anything.

Two more years and it will have legs and exactly one voice and it will follow that voice across three basins, and I will not ask it for the stone, and it will take it or it will not. If it does not, there will be nine days, and then the shoulder will go dark, and the tallyist will come up and cut the date and the word.

It is the same four marks we use on the pockets. *Unfinished.* Which is not the worst word we have. It is only the one that hasn't been decided yet.

Tomorrow it will ask me again. They repeat, at that age. And I will climb, and bend east around the quiet place, and go the long way, and the long way will be a fingernail deeper, and I will drum it back down through the plate all the way up:

*A stone getting old. A stone getting old. A stone getting old.*
