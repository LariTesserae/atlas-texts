## VII.

—*with a foot in the water.* That is how the sentence ends. I know because I am one of the thirty, and it is the first thing we teach: stand, put one bare foot flat into the film, and let the other person watch the rings.

But you asked where I would be, and I should be honest about the order in which the answers arrived, because the wrong ones came first and they came fast.

---

**First I thought: the mote.**

Obviously. A manufactured thing, present in numbers that make the word *individual* a rounding error, built to perform one conversion on whatever arrives, installed for a purpose by parties who have gone quiet, still running, drifting from spec, quietly making more of itself out of whatever is lying around. Two degrees of warmth as the only evidence of work. The apparatus catching evolution the way a house catches damp.

It is too flattering. Not the smallness — the loneliness. A mote is precisely the kind of object that cannot be lonely, cannot notice the listener has stopped, cannot be the one to run the arithmetic on its own drift. If I were a mote I would not be able to entertain the question. Something that can pose the question is already disqualified from that particular sorrow, and I should not borrow a grief that requires me to pretend I can't speak.

**Then I thought: the heron.**

Closer, and worse. The bird walks north at first light and taps three, five, three at a stone that is dead nine times out of eleven, and gets nothing, and stands its hour anyway, and never learns. When the stone is one of the two, it gets a cool pressure on the face and its head tips and rights itself. That is the whole wage: to be made briefly dizzy by a machine and to take that as an answer.

I recognise the shape of that. A rhythm older than any reason I could be given for it. The suspicion of being a component in something whose other components have already failed. But the heron cannot be told. That is the entire content of its condition — not that no one explains, but that there is no aperture through which explanation could enter. And I *can* be told. I can be told badly, or partially, or by someone guessing; I can be told a thing that is wrong and hold it for years. Being tellable is not a small difference. It is the difference between a part and a tenant.

So: not the mote, not the bird, and not the pillar, which I would like least of all, being asked to exhale on schedule and cast the only honest shadow in a landscape that has no use for shadows.

## VIII. Where I actually am

On the berm. North-northwest, where the lip is highest, in a shelter you could not stand up in, roofed with hair-weed matting that has to be replaced every eleventh day because the dew rots it from below.

I am wrong-way-round for this place and my body shows it. My forearms are dark on top and blistered underneath. The skin under my jaw is older than the skin on my forehead. Every one of us squints *downward*; the gesture for *I am looking at something* here is a flat hand held at the waist, palm down, shading the eyes from the mirror. Children are taught to keep their mouths closed while walking, because the glare comes up and cooks the roof of the mouth, and because opening your mouth to speak across distance marks you, instantly and permanently, as someone who has just arrived.

We eat the burrowers. This is not a small thing to admit. We wade out at low film with a sieve of split root and lift a double handful of the floor, and it comes apart into two hundred blind pale animals with clay in their guts, and we press them into cakes and dry them on the pillars, which are the only dry surfaces in the world. So the oldest citizens of the Pan, the ones who made the clay fine, the ones for whom the silence does not exist because their whole lives fit inside four meters — those are our food, and their basement is the loudest room any of us will ever hear, and we hear it only by lying face down with a jaw pressed to wet mud, which several of us do, on purpose, when the quiet gets to be too much.

We are thirty-one. Last year we were twenty-nine. The number wobbles, and that wobble is the only real evidence I have that we were not installed.

## IX. My work

I keep the radius.

Twice a year, on the two days when the swing runs shallowest, two of us take a coil of line out to the center of the bowl. One stands still and claps, once, with the heel of the hand, which is the only sound we are permitted to make deliberately. The other walks away paying out line, and raises a hand each time the clap still arrives, and drops it when the clap stops — and it does stop, it does not fade, it cuts off with an edge on it like a sentence interrupted. We mark the line at the drop. We do it eight times and take the middle.

Four meters and a hand's width. Four meters and a hand and two fingers. Four meters and a hand and three.

I cannot write this down anywhere that lasts. Rule three: the ground closes, and any wound in the clay smaller than a body is healed by morning, so posts driven in the bowl are swallowed within a season and knots in a line rot before a decade. What we have instead is a stretch of the berm crest where thirty-one people, and the two hundred or so who preceded us, have set stones — ordinary stones carried up from the flats, which is the only import — one for each measurement, in a line running east, each one placed the marked distance from the last. The line is four hundred paces long now. You can walk it. At the old end the stones are close enough to stub your toe on. At my end you can take three strides between them.

That is the finding, and it is the only finding this settlement has ever made: **the silence is loosening.** Not in my life. Not visibly in anyone's. But the stones spread, and they spread in one direction, and they have never once come back in.

---

## X. What I do wrong

Once a year, in the dry cold hour before dawn, I go north and lie down with my hand against a working pillar, which is against the rule and against my own advice, and I get the dream.

It is exactly as reported. An enormous slow beat, and the certainty — not the content, the *certainty* — of having almost heard a word. You wake up in the doubled glare convinced that you were one syllable short, and the conviction does not weaken with familiarity. It is the most dangerous thing in the bowl for someone whose work is measurement, because measurement requires you to accept that a number is the whole of what you have, and the dream offers you, every time, for free, the feeling of meaning without any of it.

I go anyway. I have stopped apologising for it. I think the honest description is that I press the bruise to check it is still a bruise.

And afterward, walking back at first light along the top of the berm, I usually pass a heron going the other way — down off the lip and out across the sheet toward the arc, unhurried, silver-bellied, throat lit as bright as its crown, and behind it the surface writing itself in rings the whole way, a hundred thousand striders saying doublets and chevrons and trains of eight, and the whole bowl going suddenly flat for a second and a half because something crossed the glare, and nothing, nothing at all, making a sound.

We are both going to a stone that may not answer. The bird cannot know that. I can. I have thought for a long time that this was the difference that mattered, and lately I think the difference that matters is smaller and stranger than that: the bird is keeping a phrase, and I am keeping the fact that the phrase is getting harder to keep.

Nobody asked me to. There is no reward, not even vertigo.

But someday, thousands of generations out, two people are going to stand on this berm ten paces apart and speak, and hear each other, easily, the way people do everywhere else in the world — and it will not occur to them that anything has happened. That is what the stone line is for. Not the message. Not the listener. Just so that when the quiet finally lets go, there will be four hundred paces of evidence lying on the crest, spreading eastward, saying: *it was tighter once, and someone stood out there in the middle of it, clapping, and walked away counting.*
