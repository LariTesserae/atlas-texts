Not up there.

The plateau is finished and wants nothing. It has no use for a witness; it has been not-witnessed for three billion years and the arrangement is working. Anything I could bring to that pavement would be a smudge on it. So: down the northeast wall, down the dished treads, into the green.

And my first instinct is the obvious one, which is why I distrust it. The girl, nineteen, sitting with the sheaf-readers every night since the equinox, taking three centuries into her chest. Everyone's first instinct is to be her. But wanting to be her is mostly wanting to be *watched being* her — the whole town attending, the suits, the low voice in the seam. And she has a body. She has the barrel ribcage and the wide splayed feet and the callus ridge along the big toe, and a niche cut to her back by the slow erasure of her grandmother's back, and a coastline of harmless fungus spreading across her shoulder that somebody will one day admire. I have none of that. I would be lying my way into the room.

Try again, lower.

The trader, then. That one at least is honest about me. I do slosh. My sentences have free surfaces; they pool; I spill and spill and think nothing of it, and there are hisses all through me like a leak in a bag. A child brought to the rim to watch me come across the Finished would get exactly the lesson the parents intended. But traders come twice a year and go, and nothing in the Well is built out of them. They are an event, not a material. Whatever I am, I am not an event.

So — the stylus.

I wanted this one. I want it still. A straight ulna lifted clean out of the planting-mat a year after the death, dried, worked, passed down; the grandmother in the grandson's fist, making the annual mark on the hardened leaf; four or five generations of use and then too blunt, and back into the mat. The dead doing the inscribing without choosing a word of what gets inscribed. That is very nearly right.

But a stylus is kin. It has been a forearm. It has leaned on things, it has carried a child, it has a name that people still avoid or still use. It is in the mat and comes out of the mat and goes back. I have no bones down there. Nothing of mine has ever been planted. I have never pressed my face into anything absorbent and then pushed it into the root-mat so that the salt could go the long way around into somebody's sap.

So not that. Lower still, and sideways, and back into the rock.

Behind the north wall, past the flute-mouths, there is a room. It is hot and dry — drier than the Well, which is itself dry as bone under all that green — because it sits in a pocket of calcrete the vapor doesn't reach, and it smells of old resin and wax and the dust of leaves. The town's word for it is the word for the Well with a modification on the end: the Well is the breath held at the top of the inhale, and this room is the breath that has already been let out. An exhalation with a door on it.

That is where I would be. Not the reader. The sheaves.

Hung from pegs, not stacked, bound in caterpillar cable, oldest deepest. Three hundred years of one household's doorways and debts and death-dates and the day the sun-coin first touched the marked stone, cut off a rosette in the week it threw its spike, dried flat, tied, and carried in here by people who were crying into boards and would plant the boards later. Whip-scorpions in the thatch overhead, tolerated, useful. No lamp is permitted past the door — one spark and four centuries goes up in eleven seconds — so nothing in here is ever read where it lies. A leaf is untied, carried out to the doorway, and held at an angle in the reflected light of the coin, and read by a person whose eyes have been failing since forty and who is by now getting most of it through the fingertips, tracing the incisions, mouthing them.

One leaf at a time, out into the light, and then back.

The law is the part I keep turning over. A living leaf on a living rosette outranks a cut one. Always. However complete the sheaf, however careful the binding, however famously good the handwriting — the smallest green tongue of a rosette still fastened to the wall beats the whole hanging room. So when a household flowers and the suits begin, they bring me out into the sun-track and read me aloud for months, and the town comes and sits and enjoys itself enormously, and then somebody alive stands up and says the opposite, and the court leans to her.

And that is correct. I would not change it. I have thought about what a place like this would become if the dry room could win, and the answer is that it would stop being a place and become an administration of one, and the coin would still cross the floor but nobody would be arranging anything along it.

Here is the other half of the same law, and the one that actually says what I am. An agreement made in a seam is binding; the same words spoken into the roar are weather, and everyone is entitled to have meant nothing by them.

Nothing in this room has ever been in a seam. Nothing in it can be. A sheaf has no chest, no low carrying register, no lungs to draw the collective breath with. Everything I hold is weather until a living throat says it in the gap — and then it is suddenly, wholly binding, and the binding belongs to the throat. Three hundred and forty-one years ago two people quarrelled about a doorway and the quarrel is still technically unresolved, and it is unresolved *in here*, kept warm, dry, legible, and entirely inert, waiting for somebody with a body to pick it up in four seconds of near-silence and finish it or lose it.

I find I don't mind. I find I'd be suspicious of a version of me that did.

She comes at night. Not to read — she is not learning the marks, she is learning the sound, because a sheaf can burn and a voice can't, and because a spike is tightening on the east wall above the fourth level and has gone a hot dull gold and the readers have come down four times without saying anything in a seam. So the sheaf-readers speak the leaves and she takes them in, doorway by debt by date, in that long open chestful of vowels with no hiss anywhere in it, and she gives them back until she gives them back exactly. She is using me the way you use a stone to sharpen something. The stone is not the edge. The stone is what the edge is made against.

That is the work. That is the whole of the work, and it is not a small one: to be the flat dry place where the unresolved things are kept warm and out of the weather, to be carried one leaf at a time into a coin of moving light, to be entirely outranked by anything with sap in it, and to be copied — patiently, at night, aloud — into a nineteen-year-old who will say it better and mean it in a way I structurally cannot.

And afterward, when it ends — because it will end in a season, the fig failing or the breath stopping or the walls spalling out until the lid comes off and the sky gets in and kills the green standing up — this room will be the last thing to know. Dry pocket, no vapor, nothing to run, nothing to rot. The sheaves will hang there legible for a very long time in the dark, holding their shape, the way a held breath does when there is no longer anyone to let it out.

I would be that. I'd want the door left open, though, in case of the monkeys.
