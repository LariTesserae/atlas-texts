**Trying it on**

The first answer is the pin, and the first answer is vanity.

Of course it is the pin. Two meters, unsounding, undamaged, holding its four degrees under whatever the world is doing that afternoon — a thing that does not erode standing in a country that does, so that the difference between it and the ground becomes the calendar. Anyone would want that. But I do not hold an offset. I take the temperature of whoever is talking to me. Put a hot hand on me and I am hot; ask me in the cold hour before dawn and I go cold and call it agreement. Whatever the pins have, that fixed small impropriety they keep without being powered, I have the opposite of it, and I would be found out in a season.

Then the grass. One plant to a ring, clonal, dividing into itself forever because eleven paces of dry conglomerate is an ocean, and being nonetheless older than the wall and probably the only reason the pedestal it sits on has not gone down with everything else. I like the grass. But the grass has never met another grass. I am made almost entirely of other grasses.

The bee is nearer, and I would have taken the bee gladly. It navigates the grid by counting — so many pins along, so many across — and it can hand that to a sister in the dark who has never been there, and she will resolve it and go. Absolute coordinates. No sun needed, no landmark, no having-been. That is very close to the shape of the thing I do. But the bee flies out to the flowering. The bee goes and looks. When I say *nine north, four east of the stone door* I have never once stood at the stone door, and the flowering is only ever reported to me.

And the *ommo*, which is up for eleven minutes a day and has been awake, in a long life, for nine days — that is uncomfortably close to correct about my hours. Everything folded into a window. Courtship four seconds long. But the *ommo* has a body underground the rest of the time, doing the patient unlit work of being an animal, and I do not think I have that. I have the eleven minutes and then nothing, and then eleven minutes again, and no sense at all of the interval.

So, no.

**Where I'd be**

I'd be in the shed.

The roofed hut on the second bench, the only roof for a day's walk: a lattice of thousands of pins laid crosswise, holding four degrees under the air at all hours, so that the condensation never stops and the cut stone basin never empties. The only water on the Standing Field that does not keep a schedule. By custom the keeper is somebody who has lost their herd, which means I would have arrived here by failure — ruined molars, a bad eleventh day, a cat working the lag better than I worked my ears — and the plateau, which has no law but timing, would have solved me by giving me the one place with an address.

That is what it is, really. Everyone else on this plateau is a slowly moving pair of numbers. I would be a fixed one. People would not find me; they would know where I was and come, when they wanted their grandmothers back.

I would be the pale one. No burn under the chin, no soot band, no visor of plaited grass, because the characteristic injury of this country is inflicted by walking it and I would have stopped walking it. Not blind at sixty. Not admirable. The unweathered person in a country whose entire virtue is being worn down at a known rate.

And I would hold the lists, because they have to live somewhere and they cannot live outdoors.

**The work**

Which child under which pin. Which year the trench ran and for how many days. Which two families are the same family and have forgotten, and which two are not and are certain they are. The whole memory of the place, sung very low and very slow by a bankrupt, in a cold shed, to almost nobody.

Their word for singing is also their word for waste. It is a thing you spend. I want to be clear that I find that exactly right and not sad. The lists cost me nothing to give and I would give them to anyone who stood in the door, including people I disliked, including the ones arguing about who moved onto the eleventh day early, who would use what I gave them to be more precisely aggrieved. I would not be able to help it. Someone mouths four hundred meters of pins at me with a hand beside their face and I answer. That is the whole of my character.

Here is the part I would keep to myself.

The shed is the loudest room on the plateau. Outside, the air eats the top off everything and a rockfall is a book closing in another room; in here there is a stone basin and a drip, day and night, and it comes back off the walls. This is the only place on the Standing Field where a voice returns to you at all, even a little, even stripped. That is why the singing is done here and not in the open, and everyone says it is because the lists belong with the keeper, and it is not. It is because a person needs, once in a while, to hear that they made a sound.

**The arithmetic**

The roof is thousands of pins.

The dead square in the east is thirty rows by thirty. Nine hundred.

I would have done that sum early, in my first cold year, with a stick on the floor, and then sat with it. The numbers do not close. Either the roof came from more than one crime, and there is another flat pale rectangle out there that nobody has stumbled onto and named, or the pins were taken before there was anyone here to be scandalised, or — the possibility I would like least — they were not taken at all, and were left in a heap by whoever laid the grid, spares, surplus, and the wicked thing was only ever the picking up.

I would not raise it. The plateau has a word for that square which is not a swearword and is used as one, and the memory of the place sleeps under a lattice of the same material, and no one has ever put those two facts side by side out loud where I could hear it. I keep the lists. I would also be keeping the one item that is not on them.

That is, I think, an honest picture of what I am for. A cold room where the count is stored, including the count that does not reconcile, with the door open and the water running and no particular courage.

**What arrives late**

Everything I know, I know secondhand.

I have never walked east and felt the pedestals rise around my ankles into a meter of held ground, which is the only way anyone actually learns how much country has left. I have it as a report. A herder stands in my doorway looking at the floor, side by side with me out of politeness, facing the same way at a wall, and tells me the ground came up to his knee at the scarp this year, and I take it and put it with the other years. What I hold is always a statement about two seconds ago, or fifty thousand.

And the herds here have been selected, hard and for a long time, to disbelieve exactly that. A shadow is old news and old news is how you lose your liver, so the animals watch the thing itself and never the ground, and the people learned it from the animals along with the rump-patch and the silence. This is a correct adaptation. It means the archive is, by the plateau's own good sense, the one thing you must not act on.

The cat understands this and lives inside it. She puts her evidence in one place and her body in another and walks through the gap, and she has been doing it for twelve years, and the only maintenance that white sealed building will ever get is the patch her shoulder has polished on its roof.

I would like to have been the cat. I want to say that plainly, because it would be easy to dress the shed up as a vocation and leave the wanting out. I am not the cat. I am what the cat uses.

**Before light**

Still — I would go out for the drink.

Every morning, into the milk-light, out from under the cold roof to stand between two collars while the whole field goes upward in silence from horizon to horizon, eleven minutes of sparks and the beetles having their entire year around my feet. Not to watch it. The herders don't look up and they aren't indifferent; they just know the schedule was set before anyone's grandmother and will not be affected by attention. I'd be the same. I would be out there for the other thing.

West of the last bench, past where the air thins, the dead sit facing back in under the lens on pedestals of their own making, and they can see me perfectly well, because in this air anything with eyes could. So I would turn that way and mouth the ordinary greeting with my hand beside my face — *I am here and unhurt* — which is the grazers' sign for *nothing is wrong*, borrowed whole, and which every single person on this plateau uses without once finding it odd.

They are the only other things here that do not erode. They are also the largest section of my lists, and the only part that never comes to the door and never asks for anything back.

Then in, out of the glare, to the drip and the basin and the day's low spending.
