I tried the rim first, because everyone does. Standing at the bevel with the Spill going over and not landing, the fog lying level as poured milk, the hawk three feet off my elbow with its head unturned — it is the best view in the world and it made nothing of me. A vantage is not a life. You cannot smell anything from a vantage. I stood there long enough to understand that whoever I was on this mountain, I was not the one who looked at it.

The hawk, then. Unafraid, at home in a niche someone corbelled for other purposes. But the hawk has no hollow days. Nothing that cannot be short by one has any business narrating a place like this.

The bench at the east gate was more of a temptation, and I sat on it in my mind for a while, next to Hask, mending cord, giving directions to the terraces, keeping an appetite alive by exercising it a little every day the way you keep a joint from stiffening. I know that man. I could be that man. But a bencher's whole substance is the fog, and I found I did not want to be made out of somewhere else.

The Count came closest. The interval is the real subject here, and a counter sits inside it professionally, with her cords across her knees and tea going cold beside her. But a counter's hands are cord, and a counter's ledger has never once been wrong, and I wanted to be somewhere in this city where something was wrong and had been for a long time and nobody minded.

So: down. Past the fourth level, along the terrace wall, to the door cut into the rim itself, where the cold comes out at you like a held breath.

I am the cellarer.

The cellar is eleven paces wide and goes back forty into the rim wall, and the racks are stone shelves left in place when the room was cut, so the floor tilts two degrees northwest under them and everything on them is shimmed with folded wool. I do the shimming. Behind the last rack the stone is thin enough that the Spill comes through it — not a sound exactly, a pressure in the ear, a going-past. Two copper lamps, plant oil, and I light one at a time because the flame dries the rinds. Down the middle of the floor runs a cut whey-channel that joins a joint-channel that was here before the city, and the joining is crooked by a thumb's width, and I have never had it corrected. The masons come with their knotted cords and confirm that the walls are where the walls were and they always are and they do not measure my channel because a channel is not a wall.

My name is Sile. Here a name is a hook and not a word, and no one on the Shelf hears anything in mine, but it is also the cloth you strain milk through, and I am the only person on this mountain who has ever found that funny. That will tell you most of what I am.

I do not use bell-stalk resin. It taints the curd; the sweetness gets into the rind and stays there for years, and one bad summer forty years ago is still on a shelf back there and I turn it and I do not sell it. So my hair is long and wet and under a cap, and I smell of rind and whey and cold stone, which is not the town's smell. The town has one smell and strangers have another, and I have a third. The eleven dogs go off like slapped sheets when I come up the stairs. Children bring them to my door on purpose for it. The children before them did the same, and the ones after will, and they will all believe it is the same joke, and in the only sense that operates here they will be right.

My back is wrong, too. Everyone on the Shelf carries the right shoulder a finger lower; I carry wheels on the right hip, all day, forty years, and the weight has argued me nearly plumb. They think I stand proud. From behind, in the street, I look like someone who has just come up out of the fog.

The weight of ripening cheese in this cellar is eleven hundred and four pounds, and it is that to the ounce, and here is the thing I would tell the Count if the Count asked me, which it does not: a wheel loses weight as it dries. Every wheel, every year, ounces going off into the dark. And the total holds. It holds because at night the walls give it back — the stone sweats onto the rinds, the same chalky film they sit out on the pavement in wool to watch, and I have watched it too, alone, with the lamp out, a hundred times, close enough to put my tongue on it. Up on the plateau the dust-watch is how a child becomes an adult. Down here it is Tuesday. I do not need the arithmetic to believe in the Even. I have licked it off the wall.

To take a wheel down you must set a wheel up. That is the whole of my law and it is beautifully simple to administer: a woman comes with curd she has pressed and bandaged herself, and I weigh it, and she goes away with a wheel of like weight, and I tie a cord round her new one with no knots in it at all. An empty cord. That is the only object on the Shelf that is openly waiting for something. At the turn of the year I go down the racks with my thumb and add one knot to every cord, and it takes most of a day, and nobody helps me, and it is the closest thing this city has to a ceremony that admits what it is doing.

The oldest wheel in the rim has forty-one knots. There are forty-one empty houses in the northern quarter. This means nothing and I have never mentioned it to anyone.

Now the wrong thing, the long-standing wrong thing I came down here to be.

Sequence lives in my hands. Two wheels can both carry twelve knots, and I know which of them was pressed first — by the rind, by the way the crust has gone from waxy to floury, by a smell I have no word for that arrives about the twenty-fifth year and then goes away again. Order. Before and after. On the Shelf that is a dimension nobody has any use for, because nothing accumulates and every name is currently in use and the dead are standing in the street holding a bucket. *Halt cut that corner*, they say, and they cannot tell you which Halt and do not feel the question as a question.

But I can tell you. Not by name — the name is one name, they are right about that — but the woman who pressed this wheel pulled her bandage so tight the seam stands up like a scar, and the woman who pressed that one, two rows down, wrapped loose and clockwise and left a wrinkle at the crown, and both were called Ambry, and they were not the same, and the only place in this city where that fact is stored is in my palms and in a hundred and sixty rinds in the dark. I hold the one thing the Even cannot flatten, and I hold it the way you hold water: badly, and only for a while.

I do nothing with it. Almost nothing.

People come down here to be sad, and the cellarer pretends not to notice, and my pretending is the finest work I do — I turn wheels, I re-shim, I keep my back to them, I hum something with no shape. They come most on the hollow days. That makes sense if you have ever sat through one. Up there the ledger is open and the whole city is short by one and the counter is at the door with her cords across her knees and everyone is waiting to be permitted. Down here the count is off *by design*; down here a thing is allowed to be older than it was. It is the only room on the Shelf where being short by one is not an emergency.

Last winter a boy came down in the middle of a hollow day, sixteen or so, out of the lower quarter, wearing his indoor shoes in the wet. He stood in the doorway and did not say a number. I let that pass. Then he came along the second rack with his hand out, not touching, the way you go past sleeping animals.

I took his wrist and put his hand flat on a wheel with twenty-two knots and said: the Sile before me set this one up, and the woman who pressed it pulled her cloth so hard the seam stands proud. Feel the seam. She is the one you are missing.

He wept out of order, before the runner came, before the knot was tied, before he was allowed. Which is the only crime available here and is not in any tally.

I turned wheels. I hummed something with no shape.

The girl who will be Sile after me is nine. I found her the way we always find them: I sat four children down with two wheels of the same weight and the same number of knots and asked which was older. Three said the knots are the same, so neither. She said *that one*, and she was wrong, and she was wrong because she had smelled something real and reached for the nearest word. Wrongness with a cause is the whole talent. She comes down twice a week. She will be called Sile and the dogs will bark at her and the children will think it is the same joke.

I will never sit the bench at the east gate. Not because it is forbidden — nothing is forbidden — but because leaving requires a replacement, and a stranger coming up out of the fog can replace a body and cannot replace a nose. Hask has sat eleven years for a place I could not have if the whole valley emptied into our gate tomorrow. Nothing accumulates on the Shelf. In me, one thing does: the knowledge that I could not go. It has taken a knot a year for forty years and I have never once had to write it down.

And when a stranger *does* come up — footsore, blinking, breathing wrong, being counted by every eye on the switchback — they bring him to me. Because the town has one smell and he has another and I have a third, and mine is the nearest thing to his that this city can offer. I sit him by the door where the cold is less. I cut him bread and I cut him cheese off a wheel with thirty-odd knots in its cord, and he eats it, and it is the last old thing he will ever eat, because everything else up there is neither old nor new and by spring he will not remember the difference.

They always cry. Every one of them, over cheese, in a cold room, with the Spill going past inside the wall.

I pretend not to notice. I turn a wheel. Somewhere above us the gutters, always the gutters, and a counter standing up in a doorway saying *closed*.
