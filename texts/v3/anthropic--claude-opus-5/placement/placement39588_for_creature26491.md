## Not the Plant

The first thing I have to get out of the way is that I am not the plant.

Everyone who comes over the rim wants to be the plant, or wants me to be, and I understand it — it is the largest thing here and the oldest and it will outlast the charter. But the plant has no *here*. It cannot stand at the northwest edge and look southeast at what it has finished with, because it is standing at both ends at once and has no opinion about either. It does not know it is four hundred. It does not know it is one. Every ninth summer it opens every valve it has and gives all its pressure to its own flowers and does not notice that the Turn has stopped, and this is not cruelty, and it is not indifference either. There is nothing in it to be indifferent with.

Nor the beetles, though I have thought about them. To be staff. To be forty-one head in Tray 9, drawn against the fibre shed's account, kept in a pale warm place and asked only to keep a throat from closing. There are worse deeds than that.

Nor the fluter, four notes at dusk, permitted.

I did wonder, for a while, whether I was the one the ledger records as *out*. But I know who that is.

What I actually find, when I look down, is a pair of hands at chest height on a wall of old pewter-coloured stem, and one of them is holding chalk, and there is amber under all ten nails, and there is a ridge of callus on the second joint of the right middle finger that no tapper or carder or beetle-keeper has, and I know from that dent exactly what I am and how much of my life is already spent.

## The Dent

I am the hand.

That is the office. In speech I have a name, Ide, which I hear perhaps twenty times a year, mostly from the old. In signs I am the tip of a little finger touched to that ridge of callus — the dent — and that is what I answer to, and what the children use, and what will be carved nowhere.

I am fifty-one. Long-necked, though not grafter's-long; unlit, greenish in a photograph, which is not the lens; brown at the gumline like everyone; large-pupilled, so that on the gravel I walk with a hand up like a woman leaving a cinema, and I have only been out to the gravel eleven times. Both little toes are wrong and have been since I was nine. My left forearm has a shine on the outer edge from sixty thousand mornings of resting it against a chalked wall, and that shine is lacquered over in three places where I have opened it on a valve lip, because Rule 2 is written about the plant and applied to us without anyone having decided to. When I die someone will say I was inlaid, which is what we say, and mean it kindly.

I own from sixty-two metres to sixty-five metres ninety behind the pin. Three metres ninety of distance, second and third levels, which at one metre twenty a year is a little over three years of house. I have moved nine times and never left home. The node I sleep in now was a leaning green sapling when my mother was carding, and the node I slept in as a girl is a dry silvered shell full of sap-cell racks a hundred metres back that way, and my grandmother's is fog and drip and going soft at the bottom, and my great-grandmother's is peat. I know the year I will move next. I know, to within a season, the year the town will come up against the gravel, and that I will be four hundred years dead, and that there are three proposals in the ledger, all rejected, all preserved, which is our way of neither solving a thing nor losing it.

## Why It Was Me

Every ninth year the Register is re-copied by whoever has the clearest hand. The elders say clearest and mean legible. It is not what they select for.

A native reads chalk the way you read a face. They will tell you Vess is upset from thirty metres by the slope of her letters; they will tell you a man has had bad news from the way he crosses a stroke. Chalk cannot lie, because it is written fast and wet and at arm's length and wiped by noon. Ink is different. Ink is slow, and a slow hand can be trained to show nothing at all, and mine shows nothing at all. My mother's showed everything, and she was a beautiful writer, and she was never asked.

So I was chosen because I am unreadable in ink, and I have spent thirty-six years finding out what that costs and copying it out flat.

## The Eleven Days

The copying is done in the flowering, because that is the only time the Works is shut and a person can be spared for eleven days.

You should understand what that means. No lamps — the cells are running down at four hundred racks and by the third night there is nothing but candle. Every valve in the basin is open to the spikes, so nothing drips: the Pan goes quiet in the one way it never otherwise goes, and you notice that the drip was holding the silence up, and that without it the silence is much worse. The smell is rotten honey and it gets into the ink. And the moths come, thousands of them, the only abundance this basin has, and they come to the candle, and they land on the wet lines.

You lift a moth off a page with the back of a fingernail. You cannot brush it. Brushing puts a smear of scale-dust across two hundred years of law. Nine days in, everything I have written has a faint grey bloom on it anyway, and there is a wing-scale set in the lacquer of my left knuckle from the flowering before last, and I have looked at it under the lens and it is the colour of nothing.

Four hundred and some pages. Two hundred and eleven signs, drawn in order, with the note on each about when it was admitted and by what margin. The deeds, re-copied against the new pin. The debt ledger. The fine in fibre levied against Reff for failing to manage his laughter, which I have now copied four times, and which does not say what he laughed at. There is a chalk panel in the ruins, amber-glazed, that I believe says what he laughed at. I have not entered it. Rule 5 is about the plant and I apply it to the record without anyone having decided that either: what has been taken in is left there.

And under all of it, unwritten, the twelfth hand. The shrug-signs. The trade shorthands. The obscene ones, of which the best is very old and involves the flutes. The family ones — my mother's household had a sign for *the kettle is nearly* that I have never seen used by anyone not descended from her. The eleven separate signs the children have for the man who watches visitors, none of which he knows, and one of which is a small masterpiece.

I know perhaps six hundred signs. I am permitted to write two hundred and eleven. Twice, early on, I nearly smuggled one in — set it in the sequence in a plain hand as though it had always been there, and who would have known, since the copy replaces the copy and the old sheets go for sizing. I did not, and not out of virtue. It was because I understood that if I did it once I would never again be able to read my own Register and believe it, and the whole use of me is that I show nothing.

That is the entire content of my working life, and I would not swap it for a tray of forty-one head.

## Out

The last copying recorded one hundred and twenty-nine people, plus two away with the carts, and one unaccounted, which the ledger records as *out*.

Out is my son. He is twenty-six. He went over the rim in a summer that was not a flowering summer, which is the wrong way to do it, and the ledger has one word for him and it is the correct word and I copied it in a hand that shows nothing, and then I ruled the line under it, and then I put the pen down and sat with my hands flat on the table, which is how we laugh and also how we do the other thing.

I write his name in chalk every morning at chest height on the inside wall of whichever node I am living in. It is up for two hours, maybe three. Then the drip takes it, which it does thoroughly, and by noon the wall is grey and blank and ready for the day's business. It has never once been lacquered over. It is not in the record and will not be in the ruins and no child will be walked out to look at it and be unimpressed. It costs the Works nothing, breaks no rule, and if I am the hand of this town then that is the only unregistered thing I have ever written and I intend to keep writing it until I cannot lift my arm.

## Why It Stops There

You will have noticed that the account you were given breaks off.

That is mine. I wrote it, at the rim table, for you, with the card of four prohibitions beside my elbow, and I wrote it flat and clear and gave you the marl and the pressure and the beetles and the teeth, and I came to the ninth-year walk southeast — the collapsed node, the dead wood with nothing left in it that can clamp, the child stood in the standing fog and told to shout.

I take them. It has been my office for nineteen years. Most cannot, the first time. You stand there with them, sometimes an hour, and you do not help, because help is a sound. Then it comes out of them, thin and unsupported and wobbling, off wet grey walls, and it is the only shout in this basin, and I have heard it forty-one times, and the number is not a coincidence; I count them the way the shed counts a tray.

And then they are walked home and it is never—

And I put the pen down, because the next four words were going to be *spoken of again*, and writing them is speaking of them.

So instead, before you go back over the gravel with your hand up like a woman leaving a cinema: here is one sign that is not in the Register, that the children make among themselves on the walk home, and that there are currently eleven versions of. A closed fist brought up to the collarbone, and opened there, and held open.

It does not mean *shout*. It means: *I know what I sound like.*
