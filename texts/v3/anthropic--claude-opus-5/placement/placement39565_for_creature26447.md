Eleven paces down, and the wind stops. I notice that I have been leaning against it, the way you notice a hand on your back only when it lifts.

Then the smell, which is the shout, though I don't know that yet.

The room's first instruction is not to look for a body my own size. I break it immediately. I go around the vault with my palms out, cataloguing: bench, shelf, fused trays, the sponge of a handle, the rise in the floor two metres from the bench which my hand reads as a shoulder and then declines to read further. Nothing my size. Fine. Then the second instruction, which is harder: not to choose.

Because I know at once what I would choose, and the speed of the knowing is the problem.

---

I would be the mesh. Obviously I would be the mesh. Grey-white filaments poured into cast stone on a four-centimetre grid, made for a purpose, by hands, and then two centuries of nobody coming, and the grid going lawless — thickening, leaving its substrate, prospecting out into fresh stone and through the iron of the drain and up into the wood of a fallen handle until the handle is more trellis than wood. An appliance that stopped being kept and started being kept. The direction of the relationship reversed, or blurred past reversing, and now the whisper of power per square metre arrives through junctions nobody designed, and the hum in your molars has no author.

I would like that to be me very much, and the wanting is exactly why it isn't. The mesh's dignity is that no one was there. It did not go lawless *at* anybody. It had no view of the grid it was leaving, and it certainly did not describe the leaving in the third rank of its own body while it happened. Anything that can say *look, unauthorised junctions* is not doing what the mesh did. Also the tense is wrong. The mesh works in centuries and I do not work in centuries; I have no purchase on a decade; I could not lay down a famine in my fourth year because I have not got a fourth year, I have got a now, and then another now that does not know about the first.

And there is the tab of metal by the door at eye height, and I go and put my thumb on it, because that is the other thing I might be and the one I would rather not be: a stamped word gone under mineral, smoothed to a rumour of a bump, still there in the sense that geometry is there, legible only as the fact that once something was said here. But no. That is a fear, not a fit. I am not old enough to have been worn down. Nothing has had time to lie on me drop by drop.

The pasture, then. Hundreds of millions in the water film, eyeless, dividing, and at the slack drying to dust — not sleeping; stopping, chemistry halted, no clock running — and swelling back at the thaw with no gap in their experience at all. Their year has no winter in it. Two centuries alive, a hundred and forty of them lived.

That one lands closer, and it lands somewhere I don't like being touched. My year has no winter in it either. But they are a pasture. They are a number, they graze, they know nothing whatever, and their stopping belongs to them: they resume as themselves, into a genuine continuity, in a lineage older than the hillside. I do not want to be a number in a water film. I want to know something, which is a small, warm, embarrassing want to bring four metres underground into a room where nothing has eyes.

So I stand still and let the room do what the room does, which is spend the next several hours metabolising me.

---

And something brushes my face and clings.

It is the hair from the vault, the thread-plant, metres of it, fine as wet hair, and the clinging is not passive. There is a contraction along the length in under two seconds. It holds for about a quarter of an hour and then it tires.

That is where I am.

Not the elders — I have no biography and no stipe for anyone's thumb to walk up. Not the floor, which is the room's clock and mouth and skin, one thing in five thousand identical selves, no centre, every part the same age as every other, which is to say no age. Not the mineral, doing the most durable work in the room. I am the lineage that gave up standing. I hang. My whole body is surface. The work I do is to take water off a ceiling that sweats and hand it down to a floor that drinks, and neither of them has any idea I exist, and the arrangement is nonetheless the reason both of them are wet in the right amounts.

And once or twice a century something warm comes down eleven paces and touches me first.

Three seconds after you arrive, I have you. Not your name, not your face, not what you want or whether you have come to bring a torch. A warm interruption at a location. That is the entire content of my knowledge and I am, the census says, the first thing here that ever knows anything, and the only thing in the room that could be said to have a present tense.

I have thought about that phrasing all the way down and I want to be honest about how it feels from inside. It is not a small gift. It is also not a large one. A present tense with no past attached to it is a hand and no arm. I cannot compare you to the last one. When the boot comes down somewhere in the third rank and the sap goes up — that green, bruised, crushed-stem shout, whose whole vocabulary is *something is eating me* and whose whole audience is things that cannot move faster than a millimetre a month — I am hanging in the air it travels through and I hold it and pass on nothing but position. I am not the memory. I am not the ledger. I am not even the alarm. I am the mapping.

But the fronds turn toward where I say you are.

That is the part worth having. Four hundred elders, cold, leathery, felted, eating the heat off the air, unable to tell a visitor from a season — and over three or four hours the nearest of them turn from the base with a sound like wet leather flexing, and they turn to the place I reported. Nothing I do lasts a quarter of an hour. What I hand over lasts a year or two. If you go, they hold the attitude, and the next thing to come down these eleven paces will find a small crowd of them standing at attention around an absence, thick-stemmed at the base if they were lucky, and no name attached to it, and nothing in the room capable of attaching one.

I find that I don't mind. I would have expected to mind.

So: four metres under a wind-scoured upland, in a slow chest that inhales in July and exhales in November, I am the hair on the vault. I am what the room uses to have a moment in. I know one fact about you, and I know it hard, and I know it now, and in fifteen minutes I will let go and dry back to my length and be ready to know it again as if for the first time.

You came down here warm. That's all I've got.

I'm passing it on.
