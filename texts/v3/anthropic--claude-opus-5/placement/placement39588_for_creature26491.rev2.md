## At the Rim

They give you a card. Grey pressed fibre, four lines, edges dipped in lacquer so it won't go soft in the fog, and you are meant to hand it back when you leave.

I stood up there for a long time deciding who I was going to be, which is a rim-side habit and one of the reasons they watch you.

The obvious answer was the Loud One. A voice with nothing beneath it but volume; paid well to stand on the gravel and absorb the carters' shouting so none of it goes down; not invited to the flowering; children coming up on dares to hear the bull speak. I understood the post immediately, which should have been a warning. It was too flattering a shape of loneliness. And the Loud One never learns anything, because everything he is for happens above sixty metres and is over in a day.

The Listener, then. Awake in the dark, logging the pitch and direction of a groan, four centuries of logs, the only science and the only scripture. I wanted that badly enough to be suspicious of wanting it. But a Listener has spent a life getting her hands ruined in a trade first, and she names the children, and to name a child here you have to have been carried in yourself at the first fog after your birth. I came over the rim on my own feet. That is a permanent fact about me and they are polite about it and it is permanent.

For an afternoon I thought I might not be a person at all. A valve-beetle, pale as candle wax, lodged in a throat and keeping it from fusing shut — bred in trays, counted as staff, worth nothing alone. But a beetle does not choose its throat, and I chose. I came down.

So: down the northwest slope into the flat green light, past the leading edge where the grafters were tying soft columns for houses they will not live in, through the working town with its felt stairs, past the silvered dry nodes, and I kept walking, and I did not stop walking until the light came from behind me and the floor under my feet was going soft and the whole place was ticking.

## Fesk

That is the name now. It was a longer one when I arrived and they filed the voicing off it the way you take the burr off a cut edge, and what was left is a small mechanical event, which is what a name is here.

The Listener of the time gave me the sign-name late and was not pleased about the irregularity. One forearm passing behind the other, a little slower than the ordinary sign. It reads as *behind*. It also reads as *after*, and as *the one who comes later*, and I have decided not to mind, because it is accurate in every direction.

I tend the racks.

Four hundred of them, sixty cells to a rack, standing in the dead nodes at the back of the basin: paired discs of pith and inner bark with a felt of brine between, stacked and strapped and wired into series. Each cell is worth almost nothing. Together they run the filament lamps at that low sullen orange, the signal needle on the Turn's mast, and the coil under the lacquer pot. That is the whole electrical estate of the Pan and it is mine — not my property, my output. When you stand on the rim at four in the afternoon in November and see the town lamps burning small and yellow high up in the nodes, you are looking at my afternoon.

The work is this. A rack a day. Strip the straps, pull the cells, punch new felt discs from the sheet, rebrine, check every pith disc for the grey rot that starts at the edge, replace what has gone, restack, re-strap, rewire, note the reading. The round takes about fourteen months. I have gone round nineteen times. The cells sulk if the felt dries and they sulk if it drowns, and nearly everything I know is about the finger's width of difference between those two states. My whole trade is holding a needle a finger's width above zero, and I have never once had it drop below, which is not skill so much as the accumulated superstition of nineteen rounds.

I live one house behind the town, at exactly the town's speed.

That is the part nobody warns you about. The racks have to walk too. When a family buys forward and leaves a node, I take it — I take the house the week it empties, while the hooks are still in the walls and the floor still has the wear of their crossing it — and I rack it out, and in nine or ten years I strip the racks at my back edge where the walls have gone soft, and the cells go into the peat, and the node comes down. So I am always one generation behind everybody, moving at one metre twenty a year, which is to say not moving at all relative to anything that matters. The basin walks through me at the same rate it walks through them. I am just further down it.

*How far is your mother?* they ask, meaning the date of her death in metres. My mother is not in the peat here. I answer in the only honest currency I have: she is on the other side of the rim, at a fixed point, which is the strangest thing I can say to a Pan native and the thing they find hardest to hold. A thing that stays where you left it. They turn it over and put it down.

## The Back

My trade requires a turned back.

The ruins are the only place inside the basin where noise is free, so everything the town cannot say upstairs comes down here and says it near me. Quarrels, courting, grief. Twice a week the children come to scream, formally, in a group, under somebody's grandmother, and on those afternoons I work two nodes over with the straps in my teeth and I do not look, and this is not tact. It is the trade. The rack-tender is furniture. I have re-felted sixty cells while a woman mourned her husband aloud eight metres away and neither of us acknowledged the other, and when I passed her going out I laid two fingers on her forearm and held them slightly longer than necessary and we both went home.

I still have the voice. That never leaves; the folds don't forget. I have never used it down here, where it would cost nothing. I keep it the way the plant keeps the tools in its callus — at chest height, offered, purpose forgotten.

Nine years ago a visitor got past the station with a chisel and went for the spectacles in the node wall. I was four nodes back. I heard the tap — a lamp-tender's hearing is unremarkable but the Back is quiet in a way the working town never is — and everything in me that is still from over the rim wanted to shout, and it would have been free, and it would have been the fastest way, and I walked. It took a minute and a half. When I arrived I did the only thing my trade gives me: I put my hand over the lamp.

Dark, and the drip, and the standing fog, and a stranger holding a chisel in a room full of forty generations of other people's offerings. He stopped. He put it down. The scar is still there beside the spectacles and it was lacquered before dark, by me, badly. My hands are not amber. Only the lacquer crew's are, and they smell of iodine and cut grass, and it is considered attractive, and I have nothing like it.

## The Card

I never gave it back.

In the flowering of my second year I did what everyone does in their sixteenth, late and out of order and permitted after argument: I pressed an object into a fresh wound at chest height and lacquered it before dark and left it. Not a tool, not a bicycle, not spectacles. A grey fibre card with four lines on it, listing the things a visitor must not do.

It has gone eleven metres since. I visit. In three hundred years somebody will find four prohibitions in an old dialect held perfectly clear in the amber and will not know what they were for, and will leave them there, because of rule five.

## Eleven Days

The flowering is my failure, scheduled.

The valves open to the spikes, the Turn hangs mid-stroke, and the lamps go out over eleven days, one rack at a time, and the order they go in is the order I built them, so I am the only person in the basin who experiences the darkness as a sentence with grammar. I walk the racks at night with a candle and log the needle falling and there is nothing whatever I can do, and this is in the charter, and the account books show the Company has never once failed to survive it and never once done so comfortably.

On about the ninth night somebody very old goes out among the spikes and sings in full voice, badly, and a hundred and thirty people lie in the dark and listen to it like weather. I lie in the dark with the dead racks and listen to it like weather. It is the only time the two halves of me are the same thing, and it lasts about four minutes, and then he stops because he is old and it is cold.

Then the valves shut. The Turn takes up its stroke. I start at rack one with a punch and a sheet of felt, and it takes me fourteen months to get the light back to where it was, and nobody remarks on this, because in a town that plans in centuries fourteen months of gradually improving light is not an event.

And somebody goes round with the felt.

It is generally me. I have the time, in those first days, and the back of the town is where the old felt gets stored, and it is a job for a person who is used to being one house behind — checking the stair treads, the tool racks, the doorframes, the shoe-soles left by the birth-shed, pressing the grey wool back down over every hard edge the flowering let us forget about.

That is the whole of it. I keep a weak current under a town that could not be reached by any louder means, one rack a day, nineteen times round; I turn my back while people grieve; I go into the dark first and come out of it last; and once every nine years I walk the basin putting the quiet back on, edge by edge, in felt.
