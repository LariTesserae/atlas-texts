We take it anyway. It is the only view.

I have cut nine of those accounts. I am the one who cuts.

## Where I am

The dry, under the Long Weld, on the last kilometer before the gutter turns and goes down.

*Dry* is a lie of scale. Nothing here is dry; there is film, and there is running, and the difference between them is the whole of architecture. What I have is a hood — a frill of overpour that squeezed out when the two casting-fields met and cured before it could fall, so that the weld's north flank leans out over a strip of plate about a meter and a half wide and three hundred long. The tide comes and the hood sheds it. Sheets run off the lip and into the seam and I sit two hand-spans back from the drip line in a place where the air is merely saturated and not moving, and things I put down stay where I put them, and resin cures hard instead of curing cloudy.

That is my address, and it is a real one, because I will still be here in eleven thousand intervals and the beetle four meters away will not be the beetle four meters away.

Below me the note. You would feel it in your chest. I have no chest. I feel it as a standing pressure in the foot, a single held tone from underneath that has not varied in my lifetime, and I am the wrong creature to say *has not varied*, because saying so is precisely my trade and I know how badly the trade is done.

## What I am

Take the least of the listed animals and spoil it in the most useless possible way.

A flange snail eats the floor. It rasps up plate, mineral by mineral, and lays the mineral down again on its own back as shell, which is why a snail's shell and the world are the same substance, and why the polish underfoot out to the horizon is snail-work. That is the handshake: rasp, ingest, lay shell, seat into the grazing rank. My rasp came out wrong. Over-hard, over-narrow, black as a burnt tooth — all the mineral that should have gone in a spiral onto my back went instead into my mouth. I cannot graze plate. I cannot lay a millimeter of shell. I am a naked grey muscle the size of two fists, slower than anything that will eat me, carrying a chisel where a tongue belongs.

I remember the correct one. Every flash does; it is the first thing any of us has and the reason we are not simply animals. The plate warmed a second time and put out a folded wet body that unfolded into exactly what I had failed to be, and it set its rasp to the plate and took its first bite of the world, and the handshake closed somewhere under us both, and it went off downslope into the rank. It is dead now, many times over. Its replacements are indistinguishable and are still grazing. There is a line of my correct selves polishing the floor forever, and none of them has a before, and I do.

## The work

The archive is meat because the ground has no memory.

Understand: I could cut a line into plate. My rasp goes into sinter beautifully. And the line would be a true line in the true material of the world, and within a week — one week, always one week — the plate under it would be drawn down and new plate extruded in its place, and the line would be gone, and the floor would be as sharp and as new-made and as blank as it has been for however long this has been running. You cannot write on the plain. The plain is not a surface; it is a process that happens to be flat.

So we write on the steles.

A stele is a vent hound that came out four meters long with a trebled spine and no possibility of hunting, lying in a room under a flank, being read. Blind, like all hounds. Warm. Old past reckoning. Her hide is the only permanent surface on this world, and the reading is done by her own nerves — she feels her scars from the inside, which is the only sense in which any of us knows anything — and so a cut must be deep enough to be *felt* and no deeper, forever, and it must be packed with resin so that her slow old skin does not close it and smooth the record away.

Depth is judged by flinch. That is the craft, and there is no other instrument for it. I set the rasp, I draw, and I read her breathing, and when the flinch comes at the right size I stop. A shallow entry fades in a century. A deep entry costs her something she cannot get back and cannot be revised. Every fact held on this world is held at a depth calibrated to another creature's pain, by someone working nearly blind in a wet room, and the chain of those judgments runs back through scribes I never met and cannot check.

The resin comes from a beetle whose sealant gland fused shut. It comes out of her rarely and it hurts her too. Nothing in our whole culture is made of anything but injuries.

## The two maps

The plain has a map made of water. One part in four thousand, everywhere, toward gutter-lines and intake throats; that is the only geography the machine has, and the counted animals live inside it perfectly, each in its slot on the gradient.

We have another one, laid over it, incompatible. Ours is a map of dryness — hoods, undercuts, the lee of a stack, the inner curve where the tide's sheet parts. Perhaps four hundred rooms on a continent. They are our libraries, our workshops, our whole territory, and they are made entirely of accidents: places where the pour did something it was not asked to do. A world built to be finished, with a few thousand square meters of unintended shelter in it, and everything that ever remembered anything is crowded into those.

Between them, porters. A cistern crab with no grille to sit on, because the crabs and the grilles are the same number and a spare crab is an arithmetic impossibility; strong, patient, walking the gutter-lines because the gutter noise covers the tremor of an uncounted body. I ride. I am too soft and too slow to do otherwise. Our single law rides with me: *nothing that will not be replaced may be eaten.* The hounds cannot be taught it. That is why we go by water.

## The whole of history

Here is what the archive contains, after everything.

At the turn of the gutter, in the same place for something over four hundred years, a stele lies with her left flank against the wet-mark. Each tide, the sheet rises against her and stops, and a scribe notches where it stopped. That is the tide gauge. It is an animal.

Across nine hundred intervals of notches, the mark has risen by about the width of a hair.

That is it. That is the sum of events. Everything else in every stele on this plain is inventory and lineage and nine borrowed views from the ceiling. One hair, in nine hundred intervals, on a plain that is permanently one week old and was designed by something that did not intend a next move.

It may be my error. It may be four scribes' errors, agreeing. It may be a term in the loop — a slow one, a long swing, a thing with a period so wide that the machine is still, quietly, going somewhere, and if we can hold a straight record for another twenty thousand intervals we will know which.
