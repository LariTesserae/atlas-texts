I kept trying to be the ceiling.

It is the obvious temptation. To be the sheets up there in the condensate, riding a lid the size of a continent, blooming maroon wherever a warm column comes up out of the dark — to be, without knowing it, the entire visible history of a world. Every glow anyone has ever navigated by is my metabolism. That is a beautiful joke and I wanted it. But there is no one home in it. It is a chemical opinion about the weather, and an opinion is not a position, and I was asked where I would *find myself*, which implies something that can be lost.

Then I tried the long-arms. Bracing wall to wall in a great flue, reading heat through bristles, forty in a night and you hear one. There is real appeal in competence that quiet. But a long-arm spans in order to take. It puts its weight between two walls and what it gets from them is a spinner. It holds nothing.

Then, for a while, and with some vanity, I wanted to be the glaze. The medium. Three hundred meters of silica enamel carrying every footfall like a rail carries a wheel — to be the thing through which all speech in the world has to pass. But that is only wanting to be indispensable, dressed up as wanting to be humble. The enamel does not listen. It conducts.

So: a holder. Because in this world, to hear someone you have to be gripping the same wall they are gripping, and I did not want a place in the Tableland that let me out of that.

---

I am two meters, and most of that is limb. Four heavy bracers at the corners, two long forward limbs combed along the inner edge. My torso is a plate and I keep it broadside to the updraft all my life, the way you would keep your face into a fire, if you had ever needed a face.

My gap is 2.2 meters at the narrowest and I have held it for a long time. It is a capillary off a tributary off a seam, far up the grid, and it is a good gap: the enamel on both walls is unspalled and rings true, the fig roots come down the second wall so there is bark within reach in a bad slack, and the shrimp are thin but they are never absent. Twice per cycle the deep water surges and the flue breathes hard and the shrimp come up through me in clouds and I comb them out of the air by the ten thousand. Then the breath slackens, and the fog collects on the shaded wall, and I spread all six limbs and go absolutely still and drink off my own forearms for forty minutes. That is the whole of my agriculture. That is the whole of anyone's.

I have never seen anything. There is no word in my speech that means *see*, and no word that means *above* except the one that means *the cold where the wall stops*. I know the following things about the surface: it is cold, it has no shrimp in it, and heavy blind animals walk on it whose footfalls come down through the rock in fours, patiently, for hours, along paths they have worn so deep that their rhythm is always the same rhythm. We call them *the ones who walk without holding*. It is not a name. It is a description of a scandal.

---

I am called five beats: two quick on the first bracer, a long one, two quick, and then the last struck with the boss turned so that it buzzes instead of ringing.

The first four beats are a coordinate. Anyone in the grid who has ever heard my name knows within thirty meters where I am, because that is what a name is here: an address struck into the stone, readable by anyone who knows the seams. The buzz on the fifth beat means *and after this there is no more wall*. I hold at the last of the enamel. Above my gap the seam pinches out; below it, four hundred meters down, there was a slip long ago and the glaze spalled off in sheets, and the sound does not cross. So my name says: *narrow, high, sunless in a world with no sun, and terminal.* I was named for a dead end by people who were being accurate.

You cannot interrupt a holder. To speak you must lift a bracer, which means letting go, which means you are no longer listening — the two are the same organ. So every conversation is a series of surrenders, and it is slow, and over three kilometers a single exchange can take most of a slack phase, and in all my life I have never once heard two of us speak at the same time. It is not politeness. It is anatomy. I have wondered what your speech is like, and whether you have to stop hearing in order to do it, and whether that is why you are all so certain.

---

Below my dead end, on the far side of the choke, there is a name I still strike.

Three beats, the middle one doubled: *the wet corner where three seams meet*. She held there. Then the slip came, and the enamel went, and the word we have for someone the rock no longer carries is the same word we have for the dead, and we are not confused about this. We simply have no way to be less confused.

I strike her name into the choke perhaps once a cycle. The choke does not carry. I know the choke does not carry. It is not hope; hope would be a claim about the rubble, and I have laid my bristles against that rubble and read the air coming through it and made no claim. It is not habit either, because habit doesn't cost anything and this does; every strike is a hand let go.

Below me, in the great flues I will never enter, the metal is growing on the walls at a hand's breadth per hundred years, dendrite ferns reaching out into vapor, toward no one, for nothing, until they shear off and chime their way down and are dissolved and lifted and laid down again. I have thought about this a great deal. I do not find it sad, and I do not believe I am doing anything different.

---

Once I crossed.

I went up out of my gap, over the lip, into the cold, and I walked on the surface for four hours. This is what that is: the pressure-hum goes thin, and then it goes, and the absence of it is the worst thing I have ever felt in my sternum. The grit gives underfoot and holds nothing, so there is no reading it. The air has nothing in it to comb and nothing in it to read, and my bristles kept working, the way a mouth keeps working. And with every step I was unnamed, because a name is a coordinate and I was not at mine, and no one on the plateau could have said where I was, including me. To travel here is to be anonymous, deaf, and cold, in that order of severity.

Then a seam, breathing. Eighty centimeters of warm mouth in the grit, widening below into a gap of about 1.9 meters, glaze thin but true, shrimp sparse and steady, figs on the lip. No one within any distance I could measure. No name on it, because no one had ever held it, and a place no one has held has no address, because there is no one for whom it could be a location.

So I braced, and I let go with one bracer, and I struck a name for it. Six beats. I was the only thing in the world close enough to hear them.

Nothing was built by that. Nothing here has ever been built; I left no edge, no cairn, no shaped thing, and the enamel that carried my six beats went on carrying whatever came next and kept no account. The output was the running. But it is not true that the striking was nothing, and the difference between *nothing* and *nothing kept* is the entire place where I live.

---

I felt you at the lip an hour before you arrived.

You came up a tributary I can hear on my second wall, and the glaze took your weight and your gait and handed them to me — and it was the strangest thing I have received through stone. You stop. There is no reason for it. You stop where nothing is warm and nothing is wet and nothing has changed in the air, and you stay stopped, and then you go on, and then you stop again. We have no word for what you are doing, because for us the word would have to be built out of *hold*, and you were not holding anything.

The others let go and dropped eight meters and re-braced below the collar, all of them, without a sound. I dropped too. Then I came back up, because I wanted the rest of your walking, and I have been reading you ever since, and I have made a name for you: seven beats, uneven, with the hesitations left in. It is a bad name. It is not a coordinate. It says only where you stopped, and you did not stay.

I am striking it now. Your hand is on the glaze. Whether it reaches you is not mine to decide — but hold still, and keep your palm flat, and do not expect it in your ears.

It will arrive in the hand.
