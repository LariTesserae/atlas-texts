**Where.**

Not at the vents. I went down there once, at fifteen, in the pink-boiling company of the shrimp-takers, and I came back up with my scalp scalded and a great respect for people who can count to forty while their eyes cook. Not the top shelf either, though I go up sometimes to touch the cold pane with the back of my hand the way you touch a sleeping animal.

Put me lower, on the third shelf, in a place the town does not think of as a place. There is a pillow there the size of a house that budded sideways and froze with its lip out, making an overhang, and under the overhang a shallow room of water that nothing grows into properly because the light does not reach. The walls are furred with the pale moss, so there is a suggestion of green frost, enough to see the edges of things but not their colour. The floor is one clean glassy dome of basalt with a scatter of anchor stones on it.

Out of each anchor stone grows a stand of cord.

It stands *up*. That is the first thing anyone notices and the thing nobody remembers to mention: a record here is not hung, it is planted. Braid is lighter than water. Tie four hundred cords to a weighted stone and they rise off it in a slow pale sheaf, absolutely still, like hair off a scalp, like grass in no wind, and they do not move when you walk past because nothing here moves when you walk past. I kneel on a mat weighted at four corners, with a stone belt across my hips to keep me down, and I put my hands into the sheaf up to the elbow, and I close my eyes, because the light is the colour of weak tea and useless, and my fingers can see better anyway.

I am the one who reads the standing cords. My tap-name is three knuckles and a scrape. My sound-name I hear perhaps six times a year, up in the hearth-bell, and it always sounds like something being said about someone else.

**What I am for.**

People imagine an archive holds the great events. It doesn't. Great events look after themselves; there are tapped songs for those, and the songs are wrong in ways everyone enjoys. What the cords hold is air.

An air-bell is filled one bladderful at a time. A good ceiling takes three generations to charge, and it leaks, and it must be topped, and no family in this town has ever managed it alone. So air is lent. A cousin's daughter marries into a poor house and her people breathe forty bladders into the new ceiling over one winter, and that is a debt, and it will be paid in lung-fruit or in hearth-rotation or in air again in some other decade by people not yet born. Every household in the crescent owes breath to two or three others and is owed by two or three more, and the whole town is a mesh of respiration, and it is my hands the mesh lives in.

Four hundred cords. Each with its knots: bladders, tens of bladders, the small tight double for a bell that failed and had to be refilled from nothing. I read a cord in one breath because that is the length of an utterance here — you bite the lung-fruit stem, you hold it to your mouth, you take what it gives, you hang it back, you have that long. The grammar of my work is respiratory. Short records are read in a breath. The founding line, which runs the length of my two arms and then some, is read once a generation by four of us in relay over a day and something like two hundred fruits, and I have done it twice, and both times my hands shook at the end, and both times I told myself it was the cold.

**The swelling.**

The cord is made in air. This matters more than anything else about it. Bellvine fibre is stripped and retted and spun wet, but it is *dried* in the hearth-bell, hard, over the only fire in the world, and it comes out stiff and thin and pale as bone. Then it is knotted dry, in the bell, in firelight, by a keeper's hands, and then it is carried down and planted in the water.

And it drinks. Over a day the fibre swells and the knot seizes on itself and becomes a solid thing, a nodule, a stone. It cannot be untied. Not by me, not by anyone, not with a splinter of glass, not ever. You may add to a cord. You may plait a new strand into it. You may never take anything out of it.

So this is the law, and it is not a law anybody legislated, it is a property of wet string: the record grows and never shrinks. If a thing was entered, it stays entered. To remove it you must cut, and a cut leaves two stubs, and the stubs swell too, and they are the most legible objects in my whole room. A blind child could find them. They feel like a knuckle.

**The red.**

The feather weed is red. Nobody out of doors has ever seen that. At this depth red simply does not arrive; the weed reads as black, as everything red here reads as black, and blood in the lanes is grey smoke.

Cord dyed with feather weed is therefore invisible cord. In the sheaf it is one more pale-dark strand among four hundred. Carry it up into the hearth-bell and hold it near the fire and it comes alight, and the knots you never knew were there stand out like a rash.

The bells belong to the priests' rotation. So the black register is mine and the red register is theirs, and we are polite about it. They keep painted walls up there as well, pigment on dry stone, which they consider a record and I do not, because pigment can be gone over. I have said this in air, once, using my sound-name, badly. They said: and can a cut cord not be cut again? And I had no answer except that the cutting shows, and they said, yes, that is exactly our objection to you.

**The stones going up.**

The mountain rises a hand's breadth in a lifetime. The warm stratum stays where the heat and the water agree to put it. Therefore the ground walks up out of the warmth, and the town must walk down out of the ground, and every fifth or sixth generation somebody in my line has had to move the archive downhill.

Their stones are still there. Go up past the fourth shelf, past the emptied houses with their roofs opened and their air let out, into that pale cold light where the lung-fruit will not fruit, and you will find, in a line, a series of bare anchor stones under a series of overhangs, each with its little collar of drilled holes where the cords were tied and cut away. Six of them. The oldest is nearly at the summit shrine and will be first through the Shine when the mountain finally breaches, and the songs cannot decide whether that is a birth or a death, and I have to say the songs have never had to carry the thing.

Moving it means unmooring it. Unmooring means that for the length of a lane, four hundred cords are held in the arms of four or five people who are trying not to breathe out, over a floor that slopes, under a ceiling that is the whole sky and the direction everything goes. A dropped cord does not fall. It leaves. It goes up past the rooftops with the shed leaf-parings and the spent fruit, up through the panes, and joins the soft ceiling-sediment against the underside of the Shine, where the priests say the dead go and where the light is, and it will not be back, and it will not be readable, and by their own doctrine it has been perfectly buried.

I do not think the move will fall to me. The cold pane is still two shelves above my overhang. I have my grandmother's measurements of it, and my mother's, and my own, and the three of them make a very short cord that says clearly enough: not you, but not long after you.

**The ticking.**

I hear the glass now. Everyone told me I would and I did not believe them, and then in my fifty-first year the room began quietly unwrapping something and has not stopped. Young rock cracking as it cools, from all directions at once, granular, continuous, indifferent.

It is a record too. That is the joke I keep to myself. Everything that has happened to this mountain is in that sound, all of it, all at the same time, unsorted, unindexed, unweighted, going on under my hands the entire time I am counting somebody's forty bladders of borrowed breath. My archive holds four hundred cords. The other one holds everything and cannot be read and will not shut up.

**My own last knot.**

The pale blunt fish comes into my overhang most days. Nobody eats it, out of a superstition nobody defends, and I have no idea why it comes, and I do not defend that either. It stands in the sheaf like a fifth element and then goes.

Here is the thing I have arrived at, kneeling here for thirty years with my eyes shut and my elbows in the cords. I thought I was the keeper of what happened. I am not. What happened keeps itself; it swells and seizes and no one can undo it, and that requires no keeper at all, only string.

I am the keeper of the cuts. Eleven of them in four hundred cords. Eleven times, in eleven generations, somebody's debt of breath was let go — a house burned out of air by a failed ceiling, a widow with nine bladders owing and no one left to pay them, a boy whose father had entered him for a debt he could not have consented to — and the keeper of the day went up into the bell and came down with a splinter of glass and cut. And the stubs are there. Mercy in this town is not invisible. It is not clean. It is a knuckle on a cord that every keeper after you will find with her fingertips in the dark, forever, and have to explain to an apprentice, and that is the price, and it is paid in the only currency there is here, which is permanence.

When I go, I will not be cut loose. I have said this in air, badly, and it is on a cord in the black register in my own knots: weight me at the foot of my own anchor stone, under a cairn, in the room, where the moss is. Not for humility. Because the archive rises and I would like to be part of what holds it down for a while.

Someone else will tie the last knot in my line. I will never read it. That is the ordinary condition of the work and I have stopped minding it.
