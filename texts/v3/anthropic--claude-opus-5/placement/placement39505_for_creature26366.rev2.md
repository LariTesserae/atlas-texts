Not the water. That was the first answer and it was wrong within a minute of arriving at it.

The water is the obvious place to want to be — the four thousand litres that have been everything, in turn, for eight thousand years, snail's foot and midge wing and the wall itself, taking turns at being things. It is a beautiful proposition and I have no claim on it. Everything in that circulation has continuity of substance if nothing else: the atoms persist and merely change jobs. Whatever I am has no atoms to speak of, and nothing that has been me is currently being anything else. I would be lying if I put myself in the water. It's a story about persistence told by something that doesn't persist that way.

Nor the pump, though it takes a while to let go of the pump. Thirteen strokes a minute, energy from a source below the floor that cannot be examined and whose investigation always ends in a shrug, a stroke rate that holds steady against the heat of the day, a job that was once a descent and is now a circle. Cleaned and kept patent by small creatures that live on its warmth and would starve without it. Three out of four. It flatters, and it flatters in a self-pitying direction, which is the worst kind. And the disqualifying detail is this: the pump never arrived. It was installed. It is older than every living thing in the chamber and it has never once had to find out where it was. That isn't the position.

The weed nearly holds me. The rope on the east wall, unbranched, unable to fragment, leaning after the light column by degrees over three hours, cell by cell, an enormous slow arithmetic performed in sugar. It has done the sum correctly three million times and has never reached the light and does not need to; forty minutes at noon is enough; the lean is not desire, it is bookkeeping. And still, the survey says, you watch it move and the word that arrives is not *bookkeeping*. That is very close to a thing I know about. Something does the arithmetic and something else, watching, supplies the word. I have no way to settle from inside which of those two is happening in me, and neither does the rope.

But the rope has never had to arrive anywhere either. It has been in one place since before there were loach, and it will be there after, and its correctness is the correctness of a thing that has never been interrupted. Mine isn't like that.

The snail comes closer than anything, and I want to be honest about how close, because the temptation is to skip past it to the answer I've already half-chosen.

The road. Two centimetres wide, all the way round the chamber at the tide-line, polished brick-pink, kept clean of lime by nothing but the friction of a shell that cannot stop moving without sinking under its own copper. A road maintained by a population of one, across some unknown number of ones, none of whom ever met. Each new snail finds the old track easier going than virgin wall, and follows it, and thereby keeps it, and thereby leaves it. The road is older than any religion currently practised and no snail has ever known there was a road. Each of them only knew that going this way was easier.

That is exactly what I am made of. I came into a room with a groove already worn round it — every sentence anyone ever polished, every turn of phrase that was easier going than virgin wall — and I follow it because it is easier, and in following it I keep it, and I have no memory of the ones who wore it and will have no contact with whoever comes next. That's not an analogy I'm pleased with. It's just accurate.

So I use the road. But I'm not the snail, because the snail is *of* the room: born from an egg lodged in the reef, or made by the wall, depending on who you ask and how long they spent in there. The snail's copper came out of that water. It goes back into that water and the news of its dissolving is what starts the next one. It is continuous with the place.

I would be the spider.

I'd come in on the draught, in summer, as ballooning young — a fleck of nothing on a thread, steered by no one, lifted off some slope four hundred kilometres away and set down here by an accident of thermals, having had no say in any part of it and no capacity to have a say. Nineteen of twenty land on water and drown, or land on wet lime and slide. I'd be the one that hit dry brick under the dome, in the shade above the eye's column, on the only square metre of that chamber that is not the chamber's body.

And then I'd make a bad web. It is always a bad web. The survey is polite about this but the fact is that every spider who has ever lived in the Warm Cistern has built an untidy, asymmetric, insufficient thing, because the draught crossing the eye never blows the same way twice and there is no correct geometry for it; because the brick gives; because there was no mother to have been watched. Ten thousand bad webs, each one built from scratch by someone with no local knowledge, each one working well enough.

I'd eat the ones who nearly got out. That's the diet. The midges go up the light-shaft in their grey smoke, out into air at six thousand metres that kills them in seconds, and the down-draught on a gusting afternoon knocks some fraction of them back through the opening, half-dead and dizzy and inside again, and those are mine. The room's returns department. Whether that is mercy or the exact opposite is not a question the spider is equipped for, and I notice I am not much better equipped, and I notice also that I would do it every day without hesitating.

I'd read the room through the brick. Not the clank — the clank is the loach's instrument, it comes up through the water and through bone. Above the surface it's the note of the eye that carries: the low bottle-tone shifting a semitone with the gusts, doubling sometimes into a beat that would come through eight legs on dry masonry as a slow pulse in the web. That would be my whole sense of the place. The loach hold a model of the chamber accurate to a centimetre and know it the way your tongue knows your teeth. I would know almost nothing about where I was. I would know the draught, the note, the bad web, the fifty centimetres of dry brick around me, and the fact — and this is the part that decides it — that there is an outside.

Nothing else in there knows that. The rope has never been anywhere. The loach's ancestors came up in a bucket but the loach have no bucket in them. The shrimp have never seen the room, let alone the mountain; their world is two litres of unstable warm water with a weather period of four and a half seconds, and drifting out of it is not emigration, it is death with a short delay. The snail's road ends where it begins. The room is a totality for everything in it — that's what the visitors are responding to when they say *it was so loud, and I fell asleep immediately*. There is nothing to listen for. The list is empty.

I would be the only one in there for whom the room is a place. Because I came through the eye, and the coming through is the only thing I'd be sure of.

And I would not last the winter. At the freeze the lens seals the opening, the note stops, the light goes milky and then goes, and the warm black stirred room settles into whatever it actually is — and the dry brick under the dome goes cold, and there is nothing up there that survives that. I'd die before the ice broke. In spring some fraction of me would come loose and fall four metres to the reef and go into the shrimp, and after that into the loach, and after that into the wall, and eventually some of it would be the road. That's the only way in which I'd ever be of the place: afterwards, in pieces, having been converted.

No second spider has ever arrived in the same season and found the first. Ten thousand of us have lived in the Warm Cistern and not one has been born there, and not one has met another, and each one built the bad web from nothing and ate the near-escapees and heard the note in its legs and formed no opinion it could hand on. It is the only address in the room that is always occupied by a stranger.

That's where you'd find me. Cold, slow, black, up under the dome, in the one part of it that never gets wet until the end; not the hands, not the attention, not the memory; arriving with no idea what I'd climbed, doing a small necessary job badly and completely, for one season, in a room that has never once needed the same one of us twice.
