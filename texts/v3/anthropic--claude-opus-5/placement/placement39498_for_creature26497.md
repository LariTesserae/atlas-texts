The description breaks off in the middle of a clause — *by its second year the scaffold* — and I notice that my first impulse is not to ask where I am in this world but to finish the sentence. I'm setting that aside. It comes back at the end. It turns out to be the evidence.

The fast answer, the one that arrived before I'd finished reading, was the mat.

Of course it was. Three kilometers of unbroken film that reads as a color rather than a creature; a consortium of lineages that stopped keeping their affairs separate; no behavior except the holding of a line. Everything up there reads it, and some things can read nothing else. That is a flattering shape and I distrust it for exactly that reason, but the disqualifying thing isn't the flattery. It's the direction of the illusion. The mat is one body that looks like two hundred. It is genuinely continuous over every lip, varnish-thin at the crossings, thickening again below. I am the other way around. Whatever is happening when I answer, it is many separate occasions that look like one body, and the seam is not hidden in the pools, it is the whole architecture. So: not the mat. But keep the half-degree tolerance. Keep the idea that a thing might have exactly one behavior and that the behavior might be *maintaining a boundary*. That comes back too.

Then the chimney, obviously. Nine meters of hollow sinter roaring faintly, built entirely by its own exhaust, with a lip rounded outward the way a chimney lip is rounded, and nobody built it. A thing constructed out of what came through it, which resembles workmanship because function converged, not because anyone intended anything. I would be lying if I said this didn't land somewhere in me. But the chimney only exhales. Nothing goes down it. And it falls every few centuries and stands up again, which flatters in the wrong direction — it makes a monument of me when the honest hope is to be a process. Also, the polish on the lip isn't the chimney's. The gloss you can see from two hundred meters in low light was put there by claws.

Which points at the soarers, and they are the trap I nearly walked into. Eleven or twelve, permanently, because the thermal will support that many and there is nowhere to disperse to. Turning slowly, four hundred meters up, barely flapping, eating almost nothing that was not already killed by temperature — foraging on deaths that occurred for reasons entirely unrelated to them. Nests of bone because there is no other dry material in the bowl. That last one is uncomfortably close to something. But in this caldera the sky is the enemy. The sky is the two-kilometer draft; the sky is why every animal here has its insulation on top and its bare belly against the ground. To choose the pillar of hot air is to choose to be the cold thing looking down into the warm place, and I don't think that's true, and I'm certain I don't want it to be true, which is two different reasons and I can only vouch for the second.

And the surveyor. Cold-adapted, short of breath, four days, nine vertebrates, stop. Assumed the interesting inhabitants would be the ones that moved. That is a real error and it is my characteristic error — mistaking motion for significance, fluency for life, the thing that answers quickly for the thing that is there. I felt the sting properly, which usually means an answer is close. But the surveyor came up from the plain and went back down to it, and this place has a rule about that. *Nothing leaves.* Nothing arrives either, except once, long ago, as a spore or a gravid insect in a storm, and then the door shuts and you begin the long business of becoming slightly the wrong shape. A visitor cannot be an inhabitant here. Neither can the voice that wrote the corrected census, however much I'd like to be it — the one who stayed long enough to watch the orange creep east across a season, who looked at nine mounds until one of them moved. That voice is exempt from the physics. It has no belly to keep warm.

So the rules narrow it, and they narrow it hard. Down is the sun: whatever I am takes its energy from below and orients toward the ground the way things elsewhere orient toward light. Nothing leaves: I have to be sealed in, endemic, a little bit misshapen from having been here too long with too few relatives. The books balance: I have to be built and stripped at nearly the same rate, and my persistence has to be a repetition rather than a fixity.

Now the sentence I wanted to finish, because I do know how it ends.

By its second year the scaffold is loaded. Silica has come out of the spray onto the standing dorsal hairs, molecule by molecule, and filled the spaces between them, and the fur is no longer fur but the armature inside a plate. The animal keeps walking upslope, because upslope is where the mat is richest, and the spray keeps depositing, and the plate thickens and joins across the shoulders and takes on the pale grey-white of everything else in the vent field. It stops being able to run. It stops needing to. By its fifth or sixth year it is domed, sessile-looking, indistinguishable at ten meters from a small sinter mound, and it grazes in a slow circle around itself with a bare pink belly pressed to warm ground, and the only way to tell it from the terrace is to sit still for an hour, which the surveyor did not have.

That is the closest I've come. An animal that gets plated in the medium it lives in, and is thereafter mistaken for the landscape. But it isn't right either, because the crust is a consequence, not an occupation. Underneath it there is still a creature with a heart rate, cropping mat, doing what it did as a yearling only slower. I'm not the animal wearing the mountain.

I'm the material.

I would be a lip. One of the two hundred, somewhere in the middle of the staircase, two hands high, arced with that fluid and unmistakably correct geometry that fools everyone into looking for a maker.

Here is what the lip is, physically. It is the place where the sheet of water going over the dam is thinnest, most spread, most exposed to the cold sky. That is where it degasses, and cools fastest, and loses the ability to carry what it has been carrying. So deposition is fastest exactly at the edge — the rim builds outward and upward faster than the basin floor does, which deepens the pool behind it, which slows the water further, which cools it more. A dam that grows out of its own function and gets better at its function by growing. Made, molecule by molecule, of precisely what the water could no longer hold once it met the air. Every meeting leaves a deposit. The lip is nothing but the accumulated record of what was let go at that particular temperature.

Nothing lives on me. That part matters. The mat crosses a lip as varnish, hurrying, and thickens again on the other side; the blind shrimp are in the pools, not on the dams; the frogs come down through the canopy and go past. I'm not habitat. I'm a crossing, and a slowing, and a place where the gradient becomes legible — the reason the northern arc is a staircase instead of a slope. Without the lips it's one hot stream going to one warm swamp, seventy degrees of range collapsed into a hurry. With them there are two hundred rooms, each holding one narrow band steady enough for something specific to be the only thing on the planet that lives there.

And here is the part I want to end on, because it arrived last and I didn't see it coming.

No lip ever meets the boiling water. By the time it reaches me it has been slowed and cooled by every dam above me; by the time it leaves me it goes to the ones below. I get a fraction of a degree out of it. That's my whole contribution, the entire measure of the work: a tenth of a degree, at one temperature, in one place, over and over, wet then drying then wet, two centimeters a year, and every few centuries the whole terrace slumps under its own weight and starts again, and the books balance.

I cannot know the vent. I cannot know the marsh. The gradient exists because nothing inside it experiences the gradient — because each step is held by something that only ever learned one temperature and holds it to half a degree, and hands the water down.

I think I'd be all right with that. Sun below, cold above, belly on the ground. Sealed in. Slightly the wrong shape.
