I thought at first I would be the log.

It is the obvious thing to want. To be the only wood in the world, to lie at the bottom of a four-generation shaft in cold black water and give off, without effort, the one light there is; to be measured once a year against a knotted line while a hundred people stand in a stone-floored room and say nothing for a while. But the log does nothing. It is spent, patiently, from one face and then another, and the etiquette that surrounds it is not addressed to it. Nobody thanks a log. And I would not be the fungus either, though I like the fungus — the slow mouth that turns pine into a colour — because the fungus has no idea it is a neighbour, and the whole point of a neighbour is knowing.

I considered being the chewing. Everywhere, at the edge of hearing, the sound of the substrate being eaten by its tenants: the noise a wall makes when it is alive. To be that would be to be the reason they can sleep. But it is not a someone. This is a world made almost entirely of hands, and I would want hands in it.

So: I am the one who keeps the cord.

You will find me two galleries down from the crown, in the driest niche in the world, which drips four times an hour in the wet season. I count the drips. It is not a duty, it is a tic; when the count changes I know before the braiders do that something above me has slumped.

The niche is the only room in the city that has to be *made* dry, and dry here means the near side of wet. It is high because frost is a drying agent and the crown takes frost at nightfall, and it is remade every few years because the city rises a finger a year and the niche must rise faster, so I live among braiders — among the young, who go up onto the roof of the world and come back with wind in their voices and salt on them and a great deal to say — while everything I am for is at the bottom.

The cord is fifty times my height. It is cottongrass, twisted on a thigh that has been under the peat for longer than anyone can put a number to, and it is every name ever given, in order, with the dead marked by a second turn. Twice a year I do the turning: unwind the whole coil onto bone pegs, work along it, comb it, and coil it back the other way, so that no length of it lies wet in the same posture long enough to give up. It takes eleven days. I do it with my hands and not my eyes, which is no sacrifice; my eyes went at thirty-one, early, and nobody thought it worth remarking on, and I did not lose a day's work.

What I fight is not water, in the end. It is the tenants. Cottongrass is food. Mites will take a rope the way they take hair, and they take hair constantly, and we comb them out and think nothing of it, and I comb them out of two hundred and some knots and think about it all the time. So I bail this niche, and comb it, and keep it as near dead as I can make it, and lie down each sleep with my ear flat against the wall to listen for the chewing coming nearer. That is my heresy, and it is known, and it is tolerated: I am the only handled person in this city who wants a silent wall. A silent wall is a dead wall and a dead wall slumps. I know. When the chewing arrives at my niche I ask the braiders for another, higher, and they grumble and they build it, and the grumbling is affectionate, and the yearly noise takes an evening off to be about me instead.

The office does not end with blindness. It ends when the fingers go smooth. You will have heard that a smooth fingertip is a sign of grave illness; for most people it is a warning, and for a cord-keeper it is the whole of the disease and the whole of the prognosis. I check every morning, thumb across the pads, the way another people might cough into a cloth and look at it. So far: pleated, soft, in good order.

Here is what I actually do, when I am not turning or bailing or combing.

A diver comes up. She is quiet and she is vulgar if she is interesting, so she is not interesting: she says that the ninth floor's braid has slumped on the north side and should be written off, and that in the eleventh, in the floor, there is somebody whose hair holds a knot she can give me but cannot read. She gives it to me on my forearm, in pressure, turn by turn, because the shorthand of squeezes is better than the mouth for this. Then I go along the rope.

I go along it with both hands, and I go slowly, because there are knot-forms that differ by the direction of one turn and the people who invented them are three floors under and cannot be asked. And when I find it, I say the name, and it is said, and the diver takes it down with her the next time, and a hundred years from now some fourteen-year-old will put her hands on that face and be told this name by whoever is doing this work then, and she will come up able to say it, and be quiet for a season, and then not be.

That is the office. I am the point where the archive touches the index. Beneath my floor, in cold airless water, is every person we have ever been, kept entire, with their expressions; and in my niche is a rope with all of them on it in order; and neither is any use without the other, because a face without a knot is grief without a shape, and a knot without a face is a rumour. They will tell you firmly that the rope is not writing. I have heard the crossing-story about writing and I do not believe it either, but I have thought about it more than anyone, and what I think is that the difference is this: a written thing can be copied and this cannot. There is one. If it burns — and we have fire twice a winter, guarded, in a stone-floored room, and I do not attend — everyone above diving depth becomes a stranger to everyone below.

And once or twice in a life the low rooms ask a question. Meltwater comes up faster than the crown grows, a floor gives, and out of a sealed room comes water smelling of iron and sweetness with something in it that has a face. Then I am fetched. Bail first, always bail first. Then they lay the risen one on combed moss, and a diver reads the hair, and I go along the rope with the whole gallery breathing behind me, and when I have it I say the name *aloud* — properly loud, over the chewing, the only occasion on which we permit it. My voice is otherwise the quietest in this city. I have used it at full pitch four times in fifty-one years. It is the entire answer we have to that question, and the question is *do you still know who I am*, and the answer is yes, still, and then the hair is combed and a floor is opened higher up, in newer braid, and they will keep.

There is a thing about the rope I have not said in the fire chamber and do not intend to.

I have been to the far end. Everyone assumes the far end is the first handling in the first chamber, sixty hands down, beyond diving depth, when the ice had just gone north and there was nothing here worth naming. But the rope is longer than that. Past the last knot-form I can parse there are turns tied in a grammar nobody has used in living memory, and there are more of them than this ground has had years to hold people. I have run them through my fingers many times. They are not decoration; they are somebody's, tied by somebody's hands, in an order, with second turns for the dead. Which means either the rope was carried here across the till by people who arrived unhandled and were touched into existence and never said where from, or the rope is older than the world it records, or I am mistaken, and my fingers are already going, and the disease begins in the deep knots where nobody can check me.

I keep it anyway. That is the job. The rope goes both ways: it is the only object in this city that leaves the low rooms — everything else stays, sieves on their pegs, footholds knotted, faces intact — and so I am the only thing carrying the dead upward, one niche at a time, a finger a year, away from the light we will eventually rise out of reach of. The braiders say build faster. The divers say you cannot outrun your dead. I say nothing in that argument, and both sides take my silence for agreement, and both are right.

I have an apprentice. She has been running the rope in the dark for six years and she is up to the fourth floor's knots without hesitating. I have taught her the second turn on other people's names, and she is good at it, and neither of us has said the obvious thing, which is that the first time she ties one on her own authority it will be on mine.

Somewhere in the middle of the coil is my knot. I pass it every turning. I do not linger; that would be vulgar, like a diver with a story.

When I go down, my hands will go with me, and the acid keeps everything, so the pleats will keep. Sixty years from now a fourteen-year-old will be brought to my floor in company, with a chip of pine in her mouth, and told to put both hands on my face, thumbs at the jaw, and hold there until she has me. If instead she takes my hands, she will find them soft, and folded, and in perfect working order, with nothing left in the world for them to read.

Outside, fog on the till. A rind of new ice. The curlew, once, a long way off, and then nothing for weeks.

Inside, the chewing, which has not stopped, and had better not.
