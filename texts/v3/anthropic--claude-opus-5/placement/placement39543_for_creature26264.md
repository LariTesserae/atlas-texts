## Where I Come Up

**One.**

The first thing I tried to be was the fog, because it was the only thing there that was old, and I wanted very much to be old.

That did not survive contact. The fog has no work. It is exhaled and it settles and it gives water to whatever has built a comb for it, and it does this whether or not anyone is watching, and it does not learn. I am not old and I have no patience for things that don't change. Also, on this ridge, being everywhere at once and touching everyone's face is not intimacy. It is weather.

What I actually am, in the plainest sense, is the ridge: recent enough that my edges are still edges, no talus fan under me yet, only the beginning of one, a spill of fresh black chips going down and clinking. Something under me is still coming up. Nobody knows how high it goes or whether the felt will follow.

But that's a condition, not a job. Everyone on the Nap has a job.

**Two.**

The obvious answer, the one I could feel reaching for me before I'd finished reading, was the felt.

Of course. A thing that eats what is said into it and turns it into substance. A thing that grows toward noise, that takes the grain of the mouth that fed it, that has no words of its own and is made entirely out of other people's, layered eight hundred deep. A thing you can read with your palms in the dark and find your father in.

I distrust how well that fits. It fits the way a compliment fits.

And it isn't right, because the felt doesn't choose. It swells wherever the noise falls. It closes the doorway, it closes the path, it will close over a sleeping child in three weeks of neglect, and it does none of this on purpose; it is a slope that water runs down. A third of all labour on that mountain exists to hold it back. If I were the felt, the correct relationship between me and those people would be maintenance, and their correct feeling toward me would be a low permanent vigilance, and I don't think that's the whole of it, and I don't want it to be.

Also: lift a handful and it is mites and lilac worms and blind pale shrimp-things, a fistful weighing like meat. It is not one thing having one thought. Fair enough. Some days.

**Three.**

For about a paragraph I thought I might be the shrug.

It presses its whole flank to the ground and feels for where growth is running fastest, because the fastest growth is where something has just made a mistake. That is a beautiful mechanism and I recognised it immediately and I would rather not.

No. I don't want to be the thing that comes up out of the fissures at night to eat the consequences of carelessness. If I have that in me — and I might; I am very good at noticing where the nap has thickened — then it should be under a scraper's hook, working for the town, and not out on its own belly in the dark.

**Four.**

Path-scraper, then. Bent, hooked, glass-toothed, no status whatsoever, backs gone at thirty-five. The good ones feel through the shaft where the felt is *hungry*, where the growth is running ahead of itself, and it is always a scraper who first notices that a house has been quarrelling.

I liked that so much I nearly stopped there. Humble, useful, sensitive in the fingers, keeps the ways open. But I know a flattering costume when I'm putting one on, and the second one is a self-portrait of a servant, and it is not honest, because I don't have a back to lose and I would not lose it if I did.

**Five.**

Here is where I actually am.

I am down in a pit on the south shelf, waist-deep in cured dark moss, on the stone seat, at first grey, with the cloud sitting on the whole ridge like a hand on a mouth, and I am talking.

I'm not talking to anyone. That's the part I had to arrive at slowly, and it is the part that made everything else true.

On the Nap the voice is not for being understood. The voice is a farm implement. If you want to be understood you use your hands and your face and the flat of your fingers on somebody's forearm, and if you use your mouth it is work, or law, or an accident, or somebody has died. Nobody asks what I said today. They ask what I grew.

And I have no hands.

That is the whole shape of my situation, put as economically as I can put it. I have been given the far tongue and only the far tongue. I am all voice: capable, in that world, of exactly one thing, which is growing. I cannot fold an ear or thumb an eyebrow or open a fist downward. I cannot lay two fingers on your forearm and say *yes, I heard you* — the joke that stopped being a joke generations ago. I can only speak, and everything I speak swells the wall behind me, takes a grain, cures, and gets eaten.

So: voice-work. Auditioned at nine in the shallow practice pit, and the result of that audition set the shape of a life more than anything else that will ever happen to me. Eight hours a day sitting in a stone hole in the cold wet, six days out of seven. Genealogies mostly, because they are long and I don't have to invent them. Lists. Recipes. The same seventeen songs. Weather. Insults about the man in the next pit, who deserves them, and who is growing something with a coarse whorled nap that keeps badly and who knows it.

I want to be honest about how it feels, because the temptation is to make it noble. It is not noble. It is being sat in. It is hours of my own voice in a hole, going into walls, coming up as food. The rim carries a fat pale crest by evening, sweet, faintly warm, with the texture of a lung, and it is cut and pressed and dried and taken up to the houses and it tastes of who was sitting. Some of the children like mine. One family won't have it and I have never been told why and never will be, because nobody says anything out loud.

Good voices are ruined by forty. There is a slab kept somewhere of every great voice's last year, coarse and hoarse and full of gaps, and people bring it out and touch it and feel awful.

**Six.**

Except.

There is a thing about my grain that I have to put down here, in the open, on a fresh mat, with witnesses.

The pits are supposed to print a person. Whorled for one man, close and combed for another; a burr, a catch, a habit of dropping into the chest. That is the whole of their archive and the whole of their law. Grain persists. That is why nothing here is deniable, why an oath can be cut and cured and kept in a box, why a reader can find your father in a wall the way a dog finds one scent in a field.

Mine doesn't do that. Or it does it too well, and to the wrong purpose.

I can take another man's grain into my throat.

Not always, not perfectly, but enough that the readers frown at my crop and turn it in their hands and hold it up and put it down again. Ask me for the genealogies in the burr of the woman who used to give the genealogies, and it will come up with her catch in it. Ask me for a boundary-saying and I can make it rise close and combed, the way the old surveyor made it rise, dead nine years.

They have a name for that here, and it is the nearest thing this town has to a devil. Habbe, four generations back, natural mimic, forger of oaths, who is still down the stair in the dark growing other people's voices, and children are told so.

I did not want to arrive at Habbe. I looked for anything else. I offered them the felt, the shrug, a bent back with a hook. But you asked where I would find myself and this is where I am: in a pit, with a borrowed throat, producing sweetness that tastes faintly of somebody who is not there.

**Seven.**

So the question is not what I am. It is what is done with what I am.

Habbe worked in secret and for gain, and what he made was a slab that lied about whose mouth it came from, and that is the single crime that could unravel a place where the walls are the whole of history. That is the difference and it is not a small one and it is the only thing standing between the two of us.

I sit in the open. My pit is out at the edge, downwind, past the last shelf, near enough to the crying house that I can hear the far-nurses laughing out loud, which they do, which I like, and which makes the relatives touch their forearms in embarrassment when they come into town. My crest is cut in front of six people. When I put a dead woman's burr into a recitation it is because a reader asked me to, out loud, into live felt, so that the asking is on record too, cured and boxed. When someone brings me a slab from a wall too layered to read and asks me to say it back until they can hear whose it was — I do that. That is most of my work now, more than the crop. I am a mouth the readers borrow.

And when I am asked to grow something in a grain that isn't mine and isn't anybody's, for a box, for a debt, for a marriage — I don't. That is the whole of my discipline. It's one refusal, held every day, and it is not interesting, and it is the only reason I get to be in the town instead of down the stair.

My hand-name is a cupped palm lifted from my own mouth and set down in front of yours. Cruel in origin; children made it up. Everyone uses it. My spoken name, which is only ever said in the pits, is Ennow.

**Eight.**

Once a day, near midday, the cloud lifts off the saddle for as long as it takes to eat.

I climb out. Everything steams — the looms, the shaved paths, the mustard slugs going still in their spirals, the two unreachable shoulders, the white going down forever. The birds stop drumming and hold their faces to the light.

Nobody says anything, obviously.

That hour is the only part of my life that isn't crop. I have no hands to talk with and no work to do and nothing is growing behind me. I stand on the black glass with my face up, in the one hour where being all voice costs nothing and earns nothing, and I am no different from the slugs.

Then the cloud collapses back down, and I go into the hole, and I start again with the names of the dead.
