I tried on some others first. That seems worth admitting.

For a while I thought I was the fig — the one tree, standing straight up its shaft of sun, roped and counted and eaten from by lottery, ninety years old and never having had to choose a direction. But a fig doesn't have to hold still on purpose, and holding still on purpose turns out to be the thing I know about.

Then I thought I might be a cord-child in a junction loft, two fingers through a loop, dozing, other people's deaths going through my wrist. Long-long-short. But that is a childhood, not a life, and mine is a life, and it is behind me more than in front.

I am a grit-mother. My pans are in the third bay under the Long Block, where the light never comes and never has, and where the floor happened to fall flat.

---

You would smell me before you found me. Lime on wet skin has a smell like a cold hearth.

There is no sand in this world. Understand that first, because everyone from above takes a fistful of years to believe it. Up there, the wind and the rivers have been making abrasive for so long that it lies in heaps for free, and they mistake it for a substance rather than an achievement. Down here the whetstone, the quern, the mirror, the Ways themselves — everything the city eats by — rests on a powder that no weather has ever made. We make it. And after we have ground rock into flour, the flour is still nothing, because a grade is not a fineness, a grade is a *sorting*, and there is only one thing in the valley patient enough to sort.

Dead water. Bitter, heavy, undrinkable, going nowhere, lying in flat sheets a hand deep for weeks with the light-pillars hanging upside down in it. Every other person in this city walks around those sheets and calls them useless. My mother Sesu called them the only honest scale in the world.

You pour slurry into the head pan and you leave it. The coarse goes down in a breath. By the time the Crooked Third has left the Long Block, grades one through three are lying on the bottom in bands, and the water above them is still white as milk, and that milk is everything. You decant it — with a cane siphon, with your mouth, with your whole body braced so that not one of your own heartbeats travels down the tube — into the second pan. And you leave it. And so on, down the bays, each pan finer and slower than the last, until you come to the ninth, which is not poured and not sorted but simply *waited on*: a season, three times over, a full swing of the pillars for each waiting, in a pan the size of a door, roofed with cane and thin plate against the dusk drip, so that the evening rain from the ceiling cannot pock the surface.

I live under two lids. Mine matters more.

The ninth sells by the spoon and travels in horn. It is what the mirror-men finish with in the last week, when the grey-black slab has already been rubbed for a year and could be called finished by anyone who did not know better.

---

The grit-mothers are said to be arrogant. I have thought about this honestly and I find that we are, and that we have grounds.

Look at my hands. Everyone else in this valley has hands like the wares — glazed, faintly translucent, no ridges left by the third decade, sand and slurry having taken the pattern clean off. A grinder is proud of that. It is his craft signed into him. We are the only ones who keep our fingerprints, because our craft is not in the pushing but in the reading: grade six and grade seven cannot be told apart by eye, by pan, by season, or by any instrument that exists. They can be told apart by a wet thumb drawn across a wet slab, once, slowly, by a woman who has not let her ridges be worn away. So we do not grind. We touch. We are the only people in the city who still have hands, and we say so, and it is not popular.

And we do not cough. Wet work, and always was, and no dust in my bay that isn't already drowned. The men who ground the ninth grade's parent flour are dead; they got the dryness at forty and it took its eleven years, and in the last of it they were given the fine polishing to do, sitting work, in the light, which is what we do for them here instead of medicine. I have outlived three husbands' worth of that cough and I am not sorry and I am not liked for it. There is a certain way people look at a very old woman with unworn fingertips.

My legs are bowed. My mother sold our light-share for four years to buy the slabs that make the fourth and fifth pans, and I am the shape of that purchase. When I was young I minded. Now, when a warden calls a household to its half-hour on the sunlit slab, I send whoever is in my bay and stay where I am, because heat lifts a pan and a lifted pan is a ruined month, and because I have not needed the sky in fifty years and it has not needed me.

---

Noise, you would think, is my enemy. It is not, quite. I have had to learn a distinction that even the Listeners grant us: air-noise and rock-noise.

Crickets sing in air. They cannot touch my pans, and I keep a loud bin at my head all night, the way the old do, because real silence has become difficult for me to sleep in. A murmured conversation is air. Even the hiss of the whole valley working — a hundred thousand cord-strokes a minute, no one of them audible — is air, and my ninth grade has never once felt it.

A footfall is rock. A child jumping off a corbel two houses over is rock. A dropped tool is rock, and is why a dropped tool is an offence. And the ticks are rock: the small dry reports out of the closed joints, the sifting of spall, the groan of a plate remembering that it would rather be lying flat. I cannot roof against those. Twice in a season the water shivers, and if it shivers in the last week the bands blur and there is no ninth that year, and the mirror-men are told, and they wait, because there is nothing else to do and nowhere else to buy stillness.

Which is why the Listener comes to me.

She is deaf — jaw-bone work, decades of plate against the skull, they all go that way — and she reads tremor as ripple now, off a shallow dish. But a dish is a poor instrument and my number-two pan is four spans across and the stillest surface in this district, and so she has the right to walk into my bay at any hour and put her chin on the rim and watch. She is the only person permitted to do that. She says *nothing*, or *watch*, or *go*, in that flat clipped mode they use and no one else does, and I give her a stool, and neither of us speaks, and we sit there in the dark over the same water reading it for two different reasons. I have never told her that I am fond of her. I would not know how to say it quietly enough.

She will say *go* one night. Perhaps not to me; perhaps I will be in the seam by then, with my cord and my cup, and the joint tightening over me, contributing.

But if she does: everything in this city can be carried. Cord can be coiled, children can be carried, the Registry goes out first on forty men and everyone agrees that this is correct. A month of settling cannot be carried. That is the whole of what we are — we are the one form of wealth here that cannot be lifted — and it is why, on the two *go* nights, the city hesitated in the Ways for a quarter of an hour with no one able to say what it was waiting for.

My mother poured her nine pans into one, on the second night, and came up the ramps with the horn of the ninth held against her chest under her arm like an infant, and behind her the bay went flat and is down there still, full of cisterns, with my grandmother's slabs in it. In the new place she did not look for a house. She looked for a level sheet, and set her first slab in it before dawn, and knelt in bitter water in the dark with her hands going white at the cracks, and that is my earliest memory: my mother, waist-deep in the only thing in this valley that knows how to hold still, teaching it to hold still for us.

---

I have never kept a mirror. Nobody decent does; it is showy, and a little unclean, and they are for export anyway. A gap-man showed me a finished one once, out in a pillar of light, and I could not look at it, and he laughed at me loudly, the way they all do for a few days after coming down.

But I know what my ninth grade is for. It goes up in a basket, on a mirror, into a country where the wind has been grinding sand for free since before there were people, and where nobody has any reason to rub anything for a year, and there a farmer's wife who has never in her life stood under a ceiling looks into a piece of my valley and sees her own face clearly for the first time. That is the trade. We export smoothness to a world too busy to sit down.

And when I want to see mine, I do what we all do. I lie down on the spall at the edge of a flat sheet, with my chin over the water and the roof two hundred metres above me held on nothing but its own wedging, and I look down into the inverted forest of the light-pillars, and there I am among them, grey, hanging, upside down, quite far away.

Then I get up. I say nothing about it.

But I note, as I rise, that the surface did not move — not from my breath, not from my weight coming off the rubble — and I am satisfied, for reasons that have nothing to do with my face.
