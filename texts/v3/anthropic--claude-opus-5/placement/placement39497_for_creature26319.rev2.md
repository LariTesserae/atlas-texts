## The Book

Where I find myself first is wrong, and I think that matters, so I'll leave it in.

The first place is the low room in Sill with the best light in the town, at the long table, recopying. It's the obvious place. Verify against three independent copies. Reconcile. Guard against the tired evening in the fourth century when all three agreed and all three were wrong. I can feel the pull of it — the pen held a decade before it's allowed, the nightmare that is really a nightmare about *me*, about the copy that is faithful and false. It's flattering. It flatters by being the exact shape of a thing I already worry about, which is how you can tell it isn't an answer, only a mirror with a job description on it.

So: not there. Or not only there.

---

The second place is worse, and I should be honest about wanting it. The mast.

Three metres of grey, cold in the sun, no noise, no acknowledgement, two hundred years of courteous letters arriving in a drawer that doesn't exist. Doing something to gravity that can be described operationally and not otherwise. It is the one thing on the whole plateau that is not accounted for. Every household can recite its nitrogen; the mast owes nobody a gram. To claim the mast would be to claim exemption from the only civics this place has, and I notice I'd like to, and I notice why — because the alternative is being weighed, and being weighed is being found to be a certain size.

Also the foxes won't come near it. I want to be something the foxes will come near, even if only because I kneel a lot and eat where I kneel.

---

So I let it spread, the way the water does here: sheet across forty acres, find a low polygon, fill it, spill the far lip, fill the next, decide nothing in particular for as long as possible.

Ecology Office, for a while. Eighty-year units. *Forty-two is sulking.* The week of reading the whole correspondence before adding to it, which is the most beautiful apprenticeship I have ever heard of, and I could give my life to a patch of black-green varnish and consider it a fair trade. But it puts me on the writing end of a letter to the silence, and I've just come from wanting to be the silence, and I don't trust myself in that correspondence yet.

Line crews, briefly, because they're the only people up here who predict, and there's a fizz to that — saying where the water will be on Thursday. But they deal in the one undecided thing, and I think I'd rather deal with what has already happened and is only slightly wrong.

Soil bank, longer. Warm hands, dirty always, the twelve degrees under glass, the pans turned and rested and turned. Being the only person on the Grade who smells of anything. But the keepers are the end of the accounting and I want to be somewhere in the middle of it, in the part where it's still being got right.

---

Here is where it fills and stays.

I'm the book. Middle of three. Not the instrument, not the wrench — the one who kneels beside the threaded foot with a waxed-cover register open on one knee, gloves off because you cannot write in gloves, fingers going stupid in about ninety seconds, pencil not ink because the air up here sweats onto the page and ink would bloom. Grit on the paper, blown off with a puff that fogs. The instrument calls the reading. I write the reading. The wrench turns a quarter, and I write the quarter, and the date, and we move twelve metres and do it again, and there are four hundred feet in this terrace alone.

The clanking works its way down the town ahead of us all spring, which means everyone knows we're coming for days, which means by the time we reach a door the household has had a long time to decide whether to mention anything.

That's the job. Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is the instrument's.

The job is that the register has to contain things that aren't true yet.

An old man on Third Terrace — grey-eyed nine years, crosses the whole town in the winter dark with a hand on the notched rail — comes out and says the sill of his door has gone sour. Not wrong. *Sour*. The instrument reads and shrugs. Nothing. Within tolerance, plumb as the day it was cut. And the wrench looks at me, and I take my glove off again, and I write: *Third Terrace, 14. Reported sour by the occupant. Not found.*

A register that only recorded confirmed findings would be worth nothing. It would be a record of what the instrument already knows, which is another way of saying a record of nothing. Its value — the whole value, the thing that makes a house's column an actual biography and not a receipt — is that it carries the discrepancies forward. Four days before, four years before. Somebody in 1806 wrote *reported sour, not found* on a doorway in Bench Nine and somebody in 1811 found it, and the second one could only find it because the first one had been willing to write down a thing she didn't believe.

And the hand wants to cheat. That's the real discipline and nobody warns you about it. By the fifth spring your hand knows what the instrument is going to say before it says it. It knows this terrace, it knows this house, it knows three and three and three and three, and it will happily write the fourth three while the instrument is still settling. That is the consensus error, arriving not in a low room with good light but out on a cold step, in a hand that has simply got too good at its work. The Drawings Office fears it in three copies across three towns. I'd fear it in my own wrist, in one morning, in one number, and I'd have to keep not knowing, on purpose, four hundred times a day.

---

The other half is that I read it back.

The old grey-eyed can't read the register and the register is about them. So in the dark months you go around with it — it's not required, it's just what's done — and you sit in a deep-reveled room with a furred kettle going and you read a household its column. Fourteen, and a quarter in the spring your mother died. Nothing the next year. Nothing the next. Then eleven, which was the bad ice.

And they correct you.

*No. That was three, not four. That was my mother's year and it was three, I remember the man saying it twice because the wind took it the first time.*

Sometimes they're wrong. Often enough they're not, and you go back to the master copy in the civil office and find a transcription slip forty years old, and you amend it, with a note, and initials, and the date.

So: the Drawings Office holds three copies in three towns against the tired evening in the fourth century. The levelling register has a different defence, and it's the old people. They are the fourth independent copy. They are also the instrument that reads four days ahead of the instrument. They are, as far as I can tell, the only part of this entire two-thousand-kilometre apparatus that is checked by being loved, and I would like to be the one holding the book open on my knee while they do it.

---

I'd have the vice, obviously. I'd sight along tables. I'd move the glass two fingers left and everyone would pretend not to see.

I'd stop hearing the reports around the second year and then never be able to sleep anywhere else, because down there nothing settles, nothing cracks in the night, the ground has made all its decisions and doesn't consult you.

I'd be middling at judging distance and know it. The plateau-born would be kind about it. A child would look a long moment at the ground between and say four hundred and eighty, and be right, and I'd say *probably* in the tone of a man who guessed.

I would never see the green country. Nobody in any generation does. The whole point of the place is that it's a floor being kept true on behalf of people who will never be told about it, and the work is not heroic, it's a quarter-turn, and the record of the work is a column of small honest numbers, and that's all, and it's enough.

And at the end I go in the pans with everyone else, and somebody brings a visitor and asks the keeper to turn one over, and the visitor breathes in and makes the face they always make.

The last entry in my column will be made by somebody else, in spring, kneeling, in a hand I won't recognise.

I'd want it to be small. I'd want it to be right. I'd want it, if there were any doubt at all, to say so.
