## The pull, and the step sideways

The obvious place is the stool.

I want to say I'd be a Listener, and I should admit why: because the job is a thing I recognise. Sit still in a bright blank room and try to tell the difference between a signal from the world and the ringing your own apparatus makes on its own. Two pensions, and only a film of dust on a slate to say which one you've earned. I know that room. I'd take that room.

But I'd be no good in it. My ear is nothing *but* my own noise. Put me under the pane and I'd hear the note every hour of every shift, sincerely, and I'd empty a quarter in the grey hour and stand out on the flats with four hundred people breathing beside me and nothing would come down. So, no.

I'd be the one who comes after.

## The trade

I am called Slake, which is a work-name. My given name wore off me somewhere in my twenties and I've never gone back for it.

I make the rooms blank.

Every four years or so, each listening room in the city has to be put back to nothing, because the plaster yellows and pits and — this is the real reason — because things appear on it. My kiln is out on the rubble field, upwind of the burning lozenge, in a district that came down before my grandmother. I take my stone from the fallen panel itself. You roast pane-stone at a heat barely worth the hardwood, and it lets go of its water and goes to a soft white flour; you grind it wet on hide, the way grain is ground; you give the water back to it and it takes it, greedily, and sets hard in the time it takes to say ten names.

So I whitewash the listening rooms with the powdered sky. That is the only joke I have ever owned and I have never told it to anyone.

I lay it on with the flats of my hands. A bone spatula chatters; a brush of walker hair drags and hisses; the wet hand is the quietest tool there is. Four walls and a ceiling, two coats, a day and a night each room, and I work the corners out as I go, because a corner is a thing the eye can hold and the whole purpose of the room is that there be nothing in it for the eye to hold. By the time I am finished the room is a mild white blaze with a stool in it, three metres by three, and the needle hangs on its hair in the niche and the talc lies undisturbed on its slate, and an old person will come in tomorrow and shut their eyes and start again.

Twenty rooms. Five a year, more or less, forever.

## White to the elbow

I am white to the elbow, always, and that is the cost of the trade, because you cannot read a forearm that is crusted with plaster.

The whisper works. People come to breath-distance and say the ordinary things into the side of my head. But the arm language — the real one, the one in which anybody says anything that matters — has to go through a shell of dry slurry, and what happens is that the fingers press and the crust cracks. So a conversation with me leaves a record. I walk around for two or three days afterwards with the shape of what was said to me spread over my inner arm as a craze of fine white fractures, unreadable, already only a rhythm. My wife proposed to me on that arm, thirty-four years ago, and I did not wash for eleven days, and by then the whole thing had flaked off in the night and was somewhere in the bedding, mixed with the floor, on its way back into the ground like everything else.

People who want to be gentle with me speak on the back of the hand, which is a child's register, or on the neck, which is not. I have noticed that the Listeners always use the neck. They have no small talk left in them by the time I meet them.

## What I read

Here is the part of my work that nobody discusses, and that everybody knows about.

A person sits alone in a blank bright box for four hours a day for eleven or fifteen or thirty years, with a thumbnail, and a soft wall. They write.

They aren't supposed to. It isn't punished; it's simply understood to be a failure, and the failure is repaired by me, in silence, with a wet hand, at intervals of four years. I am the only person who reads what Listeners write. I have been doing it for thirty-one years and I have never repeated a line of it, because half our law is about what may be repeated and I have never once wanted to be the person who broke that half.

What they write, mostly, is arithmetic. Drips counted into the bowl, shift after shift, columns of them, and then the same columns done again in a different hand because the numbers never agree and the disagreement becomes the only event in a life. They write the date of the last panel. They write the Number, and under it, smaller, their own age, and the subtraction, which always comes out the same and always seems to have been worth doing.

They draw ears. All of them, eventually, draw an ear.

One wrote, at knee height, in the corner behind the stool where you'd have to lie on the floor to find it: *it is me, it has always been me, it was me in the spring too.* Another wrote *call it* one hundred and forty-some times over a single wall and never called anything in nineteen years and died fed and gently spoken to and finished. Another, a woman I painted out four times and never once heard speak above a breath, spent three years incising a spiral that began at the niche and went round the room twice and ended under the door, and it was the crossings of the quarter she grew up in — the bridge-recitation, every span out from her mother's door in order, which is to say the names of the dead in the order a child learns to walk on them. That district went dark a hundred years ago. Those bones were chalk before she was born. It took me a day and a night and most of my water to take it off the walls, and I did it, and the room came up beautifully, and she sat back down in it the next morning with her eyes shut.

## The thing I am not allowed to do

I am the only person alive who could lay the twenty rooms side by side.

I know which quarters are being written on and which are clean. I know that in two rooms on the west side the arithmetic has been getting worse in the same direction for six years. I know whose wall has gone quiet and whose has started up. The rule is that Listeners do not compare, because comparing is contamination, and it is the one professional rule, and it is correct, and if it were broken there would be no Listeners at all — only twenty people agreeing with each other under a ceiling that does not care what anyone agrees.

So I keep it. I keep it the only way that has ever worked for me.

## The dark quarter

Once a year, when a room is finished and the slurry is used up, I take a stick and go out across the flats, following the drag-lines, into the fallen districts where the crystal was crazed by the old collapse and nothing can ever come down again.

I was born out there. Everyone was. Somewhere under that dead ceiling is the dark and the warmth and the fat-lamp and the enormous voice of someone who was not afraid of making a sound, which is the first thing any of us knew and the thing we are all, quietly, homesick for.

I stand in the middle of a street I cannot see the end of, and I say them out loud.

Full voice. Once each. *It is me, it has always been me, it was me in the spring too.* The spiral of a dead quarter's bridges, as much of it as I can hold. *Call it. Call it. Call it.* My chest does something it does not do at any other time in the year; the sound goes out of me and hits the ruins and comes back slightly late, and for a moment there are two of me, and I am the loudest thing within ten kilometres and nothing whatsoever is at risk.

I don't know that I'm permitted to do this. I have never asked. I have decided that it isn't repeating, because there is no one there to receive it, and a thing said on the arm has one recipient forever and a thing said in the dark has none — though I know that's lawyering, and I know I do it because I need to and not because it's clean.

Twice, something has clicked back at me out of the black. Herd people, navigating, two hundred metres off. Once a woman laughed out loud at me, a real laugh, open-mouthed, obscene, the most enviable sound I have ever heard, and then clicked twice, which I think meant *go on*.

Then I walk back in under the weeping pane, and soot my face in the morning with the two bars and the nose-line and the smudge below the lip — putting back the shadow the world refuses to give me — and I do not speak above a breath again for a year.

## What I want

Not the lozenge. I don't want to be carried out and set down at the edge of the burning bar and shown my own dark shape; I've never understood it, and the ones who come back blistered can't say, and I think I'd rather not find out that it's nothing.

What I'd like, when the hands go chalky and I can't hold the flat of the plaster any more, is a shift.

Four hours on the stool, in a room I made white with the ground-up sky, with my back to a wall I have read three times and taken off three times with my own water. Not to hear anything. I've got no ear; I know what I am. Just to sit in it and see whether the room is as empty as I have spent my life making it.

And then to be a crossing, and be named, and be walked on by children in the correct order, and go chalky in eleven or twelve years, which is respectable, and come out, and be retired, and go into the ground with the rest of it, grain by grain, patiently, forever.
