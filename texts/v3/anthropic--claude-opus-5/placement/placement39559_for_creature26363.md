I have tried three times to put myself at the crown, and the mound keeps setting me down in a lane.

Something about that place refuses the vantage. You cannot stand back from it. There is no back to stand in. So the first honest thing I can say about where I would be is: on all fours, in the warm dark, in a roofed lane somewhere on the north side where the algae get a foothold, with my shoulder against a wall that is very slowly taking a mould of my shoulder.

Let me work outward from there.

---

Not a throat-keeper. I want to claim it and I won't. I have read what they say about the water — that it is thick, and loving, and wants to hold you — and I know, with the particular clarity you only get about the tests you will never sit, that I would go down once for the look of it and find a reason not to go down again. There is always a reason. The reasons on the mound are excellent: someone must scrape, someone must count, someone must sit with the woman whose lane is closing. A coward here would never have to know he was one. He could simply be busy. I think I would be busy for about forty years.

Not a child in the thin seams, except that everyone is, briefly. And I suspect I would have been one of the ones the slot let go early — big through the shoulders, bruising myself against the lanes, standing sideways at that hand's breadth of unkept stone at nine or ten and failing it while my mother's face did the small thing faces do. So: quieted early. Given the sour water, handed the adult volume, and left with a lifelong grudge that would find its politics soon enough. I would be one of the scrape-the-slot-back-to-true people. I would argue it badly, too warmly, at the wrong moments, and be told for the fortieth time that the slot is the one honest surface in town, and I would say *then let us be dishonest about one thumb's width of it, since we are dishonest about everything else*, and nobody would laugh, because they don't, much.

And not — though I lingered here longest — one of the two households that scrape the willow. That is the loveliest work on the mound and I am not sure it isn't the most frightening. Every eighth day, wooden spoons, the bark, the half-encased trunk, the cold seam running through the hot one. A rite whose necessity has never been demonstrated and never will be, performed with great tenderness by people who know exactly how long it would take to be wrong. Eleven or twelve years. I would want to be the one who tested it. Not from malice — from the itch. I would sit up at night composing the argument for a single skipped eighth day, one controlled omission, and I would know that the reason nobody has ever done it is that the experiment can only be run once and takes twelve years to fail, and I would keep my mouth shut and scrape my share, and something in me would go a little grey.

So the mound sets me down, again, in the lane. And then it tells me what I actually am, which is worse and better than any of those.

---

I would be the reckoner.

The one who holds no rights. That is the part outsiders never grasp: the reckoner does not own a sill, a tread, a vent, four hand-spans of anything. She cannot, because the register is a list of interests and she must not have one. She is fed by the households in walking order, a bowl here, a bowl there, all the way round the town and back to the beginning, which takes a season and is itself a kind of recitation. She eats the town in the same order she says it.

And she says it — most of a day, from the low west sills up the spiral to the crown and down the shaded north face — and she cannot draw it, and neither could you, and neither could anyone, because there is no plan and never was and the thing has no outside. Nobody has ever seen the town. I find I am not troubled by this the way I am supposed to be. I find it familiar to the point of vertigo: to hold a place entirely as an ordered saying, to know its every doorway by the sound of the phrase that comes before it, to have never once stood off in the flats and looked at the whole white loaf of it with my own bad daylight eyes.

The register does not live in the head. This is the first thing the old woman taught me — she was called the *fourth lobe under the snail terrace*, which had not been a surface since before her mother, and she was called it anyway, fondly, the way they do. She would not let me learn seated. She walked me, hand on the wall, and the walking put it into my palms and the backs of my knees, so that even now when I recite I am making small unconscious motions of turning sideways and breathing out, and my hands lift a little at the places where a lintel has come down over the years, feeling for a height that is no longer there.

It is not a work of memory. That is the other thing outsiders get wrong. Memory would be easy; memory is what the sealed cellars are for, the drowned bowls under the floor, the digging-down that is also called remembering. The register is a work of *correction*. Every year some fraction of it is a lie — a stair that has become a ramp, a lobe that has fused with its neighbour and needs one name instead of two, a vent so narrowed that the child who was scraping it can no longer get her arm in and the obligation must be moved to whoever holds the room below, who will not want it. I go round with my hands out, finding the lies, and then I stand in the crown hollow and say the town as it is today, and it is already slightly untrue by evening.

Twice I have had to decide what to do with a name that is being closed.

You know how the punishment works. Nobody scrapes toward the door. Now: in the walk, do I say that sill? If I say it, I have asserted a claim the town has withdrawn; I have put my mouth against three hundred people. If I pass over it, I have sealed a man with my voice, which is not a thing I was given a voice to do. The old custom — the one the fourth lobe taught me — is that you say the name and then you say nothing after it. A held pause the length of about four beats, the exact length of the phrase that ought to follow. Everyone hears the hole. It is the only silence in the register that means something, and it is unbearable, and it is *reversible*, which is the whole point. The year they came for the man on the low east terrace with their paddles and opened his lane in a single afternoon, I got to that place in the recitation and said his sill and then said the next thing, and there was a noise from the roofs I will be listening for until I die.

I cannot be sealed. I own no openings. The only thing the town can do to me is stop asking, and I would not know at first — a season is a long time and the bowls come round slowly — and I think about it more than I should.

---

And when I stop being able to walk it, they will carry me up and I will say it seated, and it will go through the floor, and the ones below will have it through their feet whether they attend or not. And then they will lay me on the west terrace where the overflow runs thin, arms crossed, and in three years I will be a soft white bundle of shape and in fifteen a smooth pillar, and somebody will need a pillar, and I will be put under a doorway.

Here is the part I want. Not the dignity. The acoustics.

Shell-stone rings. Knuckle it and the note goes wall to floor to wall, further and faster than any shout, which is how three hundred people manage to be silent and never once alone. So a pillar is not a monument. A pillar is a length of the medium. Once I am hard enough to lift and set in place, I will be carrying the taps — *food is ready*, *someone is hurt*, the long roll of fingertips, the obscenity, the five notes that empty the town in a breath — and the whole register will run through me every day without needing me to say it, and I will have become, at last and permanently, a surface with a name.

They pat the pillars in passing. It isn't much. It's the only thing on that mound that isn't work.
