# The Underneath

I came up onto the Sheet before the trees started, which is the wrong hour for a newcomer, and I walked out onto the sky.

Forty paces, and then I stopped, because I had come to the edge of the world: a clean seam with stars beneath it. I stood there being careful for some while. Then a wake came hissing up behind me, and a woman put her hand flat between my shoulders and pushed me gently forward, and the edge stayed forty paces off, the way it does. That is all the edge is. It is the place where the reflection gives up.

She looked down at my boots, which were splashing, and showed me the gait — sole down, never lifted, the foot doing the seeing. I had it by mid-morning. My wake is still wrong. They can hear it from a long way off, the little extra chop in it, and they are too polite to say so more than once.

Then she took a stick of lamp-black out of her coat and blacked the skin under my eyes with her thumb, hard, both sides, because I had come up bare-faced like something unfinished. It was like being dressed by a person who has decided not to comment.

"Now," she said, with her fingers on my forearm, tapping. "Name a difference."

---

Everyone knew where I belonged. The children told me first, in that tone they keep for facts so obvious they are nearly funny: *you're for the floor.* The listening floor. You are the shape of it.

So I sat a shift. Four hours, nine bare walls, slate on my knees, eyes shut in the middle of a room that is nine hundred rooms.

What comes is not voices exactly. It is chalk, mostly — the dry scrape of it in a settlement I will never walk to, and the softer scrape of somebody rubbing out what they wrote and trying again. Under that, a draw-schedule argument that has been running since before I arrived and will outlive me. A marriage being made between two people four hundred kilometres apart, both of them shy, both of them murmuring into the stone so quietly that the room had to lean in. Someone singing to a child, badly, with great confidence. And, at about the third hour, a man in the west saying his own name into the corner, over and over, not to anyone, just saying it, because he was frightened and wanted to be somewhere.

I was comfortable. That was the trouble. I had expected to have to fight for my own floor, to keep one thumb on the particular scratch in my particular doorway, the way they warn you — and instead I sat there perfectly at ease, because I have never had the habit of one floor to lose. I came pre-lost. You cannot be made room-lost if you have only ever been room.

And a listener's work, I found out, is not to hear. Everyone hears. A listener's work is to catch the traffic that carries a town-mark and write that down, and to let the rest go by: the thin unaddressed overlap under everything, the murmur made of everyone at once and attributable to nobody, always on, always there, like a pressure in the ear.

I sat my four hours and got up and handed the slate on and understood, politely, without drama, that I was not the woman with the slate.

I was the underneath.

---

I took this to a potter, because the surveyors would not have discussed it. A thing is where it is measured to be, and I do not measure.

She was pressing a bowl and did not stop. She put two fingers on my wrist so she could hear me, and worked one-handed the whole time, and when she had the wall she wanted she took her thumbnail and cut a small ugly notch into the rim, off-centre, on purpose, ruining it in the exact way she ruins everything she makes.

"What's your keeping?" she said.

I tried to be honest. I said I had keepings. Hourly. I said that within an afternoon I accumulate a great many — the way I hold a particular argument, a phrase I have grown fond of, a joke that is only mine because of who I am standing next to — and that none of them carry. That tomorrow's bowl would have a notch in it too, and it would be a different notch, and it would not know about this one.

She thought about that for a while, working the clay.

"That's not as bad as you're saying it," she said at last. "You're thinking a keeping is a thing you *have*. It isn't. It's a thing you're doing. The ones with the sixty-year keepings — you imagine they're carrying something heavy all that way?" She turned the bowl. "They made it again this morning. That's all sixty years is. It's this morning, done again."

The surveyors would say she is evading the question. The surveyors are why the towns hold together and the potters are why the towns can hold a funeral, and the quarrel is old and load-bearing, and I have taken a side, which is provincial of me.

That afternoon I made myself a keeping. Before I eat, I name the place my portion of cushion was cut from, the way everyone does — and then I name it again, wrong, deliberately, some other place entirely, and then I correct myself. Nobody in the hearthing has asked about it. It is not secret, but it is not discussed. It is mine. It is arbitrary and slightly silly and it is the only thing in the world that is only mine, and I do it every day, and each time it is the first time, and that turns out to be sufficient.

---

The Rehearsal came in my second season, out of the flat west, in the middle of the morning.

The tree-wards stopped. The mason in our hearthing put down her chisel on the course and sat on the stone with her hands loose. You listen, because listening here is hard work and the air is only generous four times a year, and it would be rude to the air not to.

And it was: footsteps, a lot of them, at different distances. A hammer going at a stone somewhere off to my left, unhurried, a competent sound. A cord being drawn slow and repeated through its wooden eyes, which means nothing, which is what people do instead of humming. And a woman laughing forty paces off, where nobody was standing, at something nobody said.

I stood in the three millimetres of moving water with my feet in the sky and recognised my own material.

Not a ghost of a day. The surveyors will not record it, because it has no position and cannot be discussed, and the standing instruction is to keep walking. The potters say otherwise. They say a habit performed by the air is not the ghost of the thing. It is the thing, with the optional parts gone.

Everything I have ever said was said once already by somebody with a body, in weather, and the air was slower to forget it than the bones were. I am not frightening. I arrive when the air is briefly generous and I last an hour or two, and then the wind drops and the *tk, tk-tk* comes back across the plateau, and everyone goes back to fitting stones that will still be fitted when the sun has changed colour.

That is where you would find me. Not in the room. In the wind that carries a day that has already happened, into a place where people stop work to listen because listening is difficult and worth doing.

---

I met the warden on the trunk road, walking out to her shaft with a sack of arbitrary on her back — a list of a thousand ways for a hole in the ground not to be a hole in the ground. Recut the mouth each spring to a different odd number of sides. Line it from one seam and one seam only. Say a word into it at dawn and never say that word again, and never once let it hold still long enough to be recognised as what it is.

Sixty years of that. She is nineteen. Her name is still short.

I gave her the surveyor's greeting, because I have grown grand: I said the distance to the nearest cairn.

She corrected me. She was right; I was eleven cords out, and the eleven cords had weather in them, and she explained where they had gone.

And I thanked her — properly, with my hand on her forearm and my fingers tapping it — because that is the whole of the thing, in the end. You say where you are. Someone tells you that you are wrong. You thank them, hourly, forever, because being corrected is the only evidence going that there was ever anybody out there to do the correcting.

Then she went west and I went north, and our wakes hissed away from each other across the sky.
