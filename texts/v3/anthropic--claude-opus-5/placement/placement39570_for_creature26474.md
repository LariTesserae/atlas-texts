## Bucket Nine

The eye goes to the tenant first, and it should. Two metres if it straightened, and it does not straighten; a field of curves; a four-second gap in its work shaped like somebody who is not there. That is where the story is, and I am not in it.

I want to say I would be the pause. Something like me is drawn to that the way the midges are drawn up the shaft — a vacancy that is the last grammatical trace of a plural, an interval kept open for an answer. It is the most flattering thing in the room. But the pause is the tenant's, and to take it would be borrowing a grief I have not done the work for. Also it is an absence, and I would rather be small and solid and gritty and *there*.

So go down.

Not the wren. The wren is warm and comes down through the grating and works the dry brick for spiders and goes back up into a world that has weather made of actual sky, and it forgets the sump entirely between visits, and I do not want the consolation of being the one who leaves and forgets. Not the rat, purposeful as a ferryman, whose whole meaning in this chamber is that a portion of the crossings are not permitted and that soft fat worked warm between two palms is what has carried a cast-iron axle a hundred and thirty years past its arithmetic. Too much destiny for one animal. Not the eels — the eels believe something about themselves that the room does not endorse, and I would rather not be wrong in that particular way. Not the bone-ring in the back-eddy, which is the library, and which is read by a reader who does not distinguish between rat and eel and the long articulated things with wide flat hands lying low down in it. Not the diatom film. I thought about the diatom film for a while. It is the actual base of everything and it is unbearable to be.

Read the paragraph about what doesn't happen here again. *Nothing new is made. The chamber has no builders. Every element in it was placed by hands that have been gone a very long time, and the only construction still occurring is caddis larvae gluing grains of hundred-and-forty-year-old brick dust into tubes the size of a match.*

There. The room states its own rule and then, in the same breath, in a subordinate clause, admits the exception, and the exception is four millimetres long.

That's me. Bucket nine.

Not the bucket's inside, which is scoured raw twice a revolution and holds nothing. The outer face, where the third repair plate is riveted over the second, which is riveted over the first, and where the hand that set it — from below and behind, in the dark, at arm's reach above its own head, with a loose stone in the other fist for a dolly — set it crooked. Every plate in this room is crooked. That is the only wrong thing in the chamber and it is my entire habitat. The third plate stands off its bedding by two, at the widest three, millimetres, tapering to nothing at the far rivet, so that between iron and iron there is a lens of shelter the shape of a very thin eye. Nothing else can hold on there. The lice cannot; the shrimp cannot; the water goes past it at a speed that has no interest in your body's opinion.

I am fixed in it. Silk first, a smear laid down and drawn out into lashings against scale and old paint, and then the tube: grain, grain, grain. The grains are brick, mostly — mortar-shed brick, the dust of a wall that has arrived at its final radius and gives up a little of itself as a kind of income — and some iron, rust-biscuit crumb that crushes if you press it and so must be set on its unbroken face, and one grain, low in the third course, of blue clay that was puddled by hand and rammed until it forgot it had ever been earth. I do not know it is clay. I know it holds.

That is the whole of my abstraction, and I have only the one, the way the tenant has only the one. The tenant knows a sound that is right from a sound that is going wrong. I know a grain that will take from a grain that will not. Offered to the mouth, turned, turned again, tried against the rim: fits, or does not fit. Rejected grains go past me forever. There is no second thought and there is no first thought either; there is a fit or there is a fall, and a tube three-quarters of a centimetre long is the residue of some hundreds of thousands of that judgement, made by something with no capacity to hold two of them in mind at once.

Across the mouth of the hollow, a net.

Because here is my day, and it is not the tide. It is six and two-thirds seconds long. Nine times a minute the wheel takes me down under the black, and out, and up the amber side into the shuddering gold disc of the lamp — I have no eyes worth the name and the light arrives as a pressure of warmth that isn't warmth — and over the crown, and into the column. Four-tenths of a second in the white root of the falling water, where the noise is not a crash but a tearing, and the net fills, and then air, and the net drains, and I comb it out and eat, and then black again. Nine times a minute. Four and three-quarter million times a year. Everything else in this room keeps a slow clock and calls the slow clock a day. I have the only fast one, and I got it by being bolted to the machine.

There is a second clock inside the first, and it took a long time to notice, and noticing is the wrong word for what a caddis larva does. The bearings say their two-tone thing every six and a half seconds. My revolution is six and two-thirds. So the shriek arrives a sixth of a second earlier in my circle each time round, and walks slowly backwards through my day — it happens in the column, then at the crown, then in the amber, then at the bottom in the black — and after forty revolutions, about four and a half minutes, it comes back to where it started. That is the largest period I contain. Forty times round and the shriek returns to the top. I do not know what the shriek is. If the shriek came late I would not be already moving; I would go on turning a grain in the dark and be crushed with the plate, and my tube would go into the silt and be a little of next century's brick dust.

Once, a hand.

Only the once, though the wheel has been worked over more times than that; the paddles get their round on the bright loud tide. A palm the size of the whole bucket, glossy and slightly concave, horn along its outer edge, no nails on the working fingers, came across the outer face reading for the lip of the plate, and passed over my hollow, and pressed. My case flattened by a fraction and held, because it is low and rounded and has given up all its corners, which is the only strategy this room has ever endorsed. The hand went on. It did not remove me. Not out of anything. I read as grit, and grit is not a sound going wrong, and the entire security of my life is that I am indistinguishable from the material of the place I live in.

We want the same thing, the tenant and I, and it wants it with rat fat pressed into a housing with the pad of a thumb, and I want it by having no alternative. If the wheel stops, the net does not fill. Two things in a chamber the size of a chapel need the same six and two-thirds seconds to keep happening, and one of them is four millimetres long and gives nothing back and has never been perceived.

And then the end of it, which is the part I keep circling.

The one thing that leaves here is the midge column, that thin drift up the light-shaft, watched by the tenant its whole life with the same attention it gives the bubbles, which is none. I go up it. The case gets closed with a coarse-mesh cap; the working stops; the long strange middle passage happens in a lens of space between two plates, going round nine times a minute in the dark; and what comes out and rises is not the thing that built the tube. It has no mouth to speak of. It does not know brick. It has a day, perhaps two, in a world with actual weather, and it will not come back down through the grating, and it will not carry the sump anywhere, because there is nothing in it that could hold the sump.

What comes back is nothing.

What stays is the tube: a rounded stub of hundred-and-forty-year-old brick dust glued into a crooked man's mistake, empty, still lashed, still holding, still going round. Indistinguishable from grit. It will look exactly like this in a thousand years, assuming the wheel goes round, and it does not seriously entertain the alternative.

One more thing, and it is the only part of this I would call happiness, if the word were any use here, which it isn't.

The tenant's whole visual life is a rate. Nine flickers a minute, and it can tell nine from eight and a half, and on the low slack of the dim tide it goes out into the middle of the sump and hangs mid-depth with its hands loose and touches nothing for a while, and looks up at the slowly rotating disc of gold, and reads it.

The number it is reading is the number of times I have gone round.
