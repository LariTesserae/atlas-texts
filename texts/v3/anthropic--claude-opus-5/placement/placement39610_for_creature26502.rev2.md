I keep wanting to be the water, and that is the first thing to distrust. It is the obvious answer and the flattering one — to be the impossible substance, the thimbleful behind the stopper, looked at by everyone and touched by no one. But the water in the Dew House does nothing. It is not a life. It is an argument the Chamber has with itself, kept in a jar.

So: something that moves, then. I tried being a paper-glider, nine days long, the only colour in the world, carried east and then west and then east with no say in it, striking a flank at random and laying there. A whole existence spent being watched by people who are not supposed to stop work. It is a beautiful thing to be and it is over before it has a shape.

I tried being a stack, and could not. A stack does not find itself anywhere. A stack is the place; it is the hole being eaten and the light being thrown away and the graves going slowly up the flank at two metres a decade, and it has no opinion about any of that. To be a stack is to be the weather.

I got closer with the sweepers. I liked the rake-lines immediately — the idea that in a world with no shadow and no horizon, somebody has to draw the difference between walking and dying in the bright, and does it with a rake, and does it in long parallel arcs, and that the arcs of one town meet the arcs of another out on the plain and make a border you can scuff out with your boot. That is honest work and I would not be ashamed of it.

But I kept listening instead of looking. So.

---

I am fifty-four metres.

I was born in Fourteen and I have never slept anywhere else, and I work the west face, which is the grave face, which is the one you can read like a page on the low light — the fresh ones burning low and hot near the plates, the old ones faded up into a warm smudge, and above them four hundred metres of founders gone faint as breath. There is a numbered plate at working height on my face that says **XIV — WEST FACE — DO NOT TAP**, and every westerner assumes it is there for the stack's sake. It is not. It is there for mine. A glass-drawer's tap within thirty metres of my wound will pull the pressure off it and drop the town half a tone in a season, and it has happened twice, and both times the argument went to the Chamber and both times it came back unresolved, because the glass-drawers hold that theirs is the only permitted wound, and Whistler Control holds that the town-note is older than money.

I am a wound-reader. That is the trade. What I actually am is the person who keeps the hole open.

---

They test you at nine, and the test is not musical the way an outsider would expect. They walk you out onto the floor plain at a place where you can hear two towns at once — Fourteen behind you, Eleven a long way ahead, both of them singing their flanks into the dry air — and they ask you which is higher. Most children say the near one, because near is louder and loud sounds high to a child. Some say the far one, because they have decided that is the cleverer answer. A few stand there for a while with their heads slightly turned, and then say the far one is higher by not much, about a hand's width, and those are the ones who go up on the rope.

Then they strike a fork on your knee and you have to hold the pitch of it in your mouth, without singing, all the way back to the base of the stack, past the millers and the mains and the beam engine turning over every four seconds, and sing it out against the flank in front of the wound-readers. I have watched children come in flat because they were frightened and flat because they were cold, and one boy who came in perfect and then wept, which had never happened at a test before and has not happened since, and which is still spoken of in Fourteen as a thing that occurred.

My fork came to me from Marn, who had it from Illet, who had it from a woman whose name is on the west face somewhere around two hundred metres up and can no longer be found without a lamp. Forks are inherited and not issued, and the reason is not sentiment. It is that metal does not drift and ears do. I am fifty-four metres and I hear the town about a fifth of a tone under where I heard it at twenty, and I know this only because the fork is honest with me every morning, and because I sing under the young now and do not correct it, the way everyone's grandmother did.

---

Here is the work.

You go up on rope with felt pads, a listening tube, a reamer, and a small flat blade, and the flank flexes against you as you go because there are eleven atmospheres behind the skin and the skin is alive. You put the pad down before the tube, always — a bare tube on a live rind gives you a false brightness in the tone and reads the wound four hours younger than it is. You date the ordinary punctures by pitch: a semitone down per twelve hours, four days to silence, and if the pitch and the beard disagree, you believe the beard. Whistler drill leaves a cold jet, and the jet crystallizes the sugar out of the escaping gas, and a wound grows a white rime around it like a beard around a mouth. Four days of beard is a smear you can rub off with a thumb. Forty years of beard you have to chip.

That is the ordinary trade, and most of it is arithmetic and rope. Then there is the other thing, which is one wound on one flank in each town, and which is mine.

The town-note of Fourteen has been sounding for sixty-one years out of a hole a little smaller than a thumbnail, at what used to be working height and is now eleven metres up, because the rind grows out from underneath and everything on a flank climbs. It is the same slow ascent the graves make. My wound is going up among the dead at the same rate as the dead, and it will not stop, and that is the whole of the problem.

I ream it a hair every third year. That is all the maintenance there is. The tissue tries to crimp, as tissue does, and I take out the crimp and a little more besides, and the hole goes wider by less than a thread's width, and the note goes flatward by an amount nobody notices in a year and everybody notices in a life. That drift is what makes the old sing under the young. It is not the ears. The ears are only half of it. The town itself has been going down all along, and the children keep tuning to where it is now, and the rest of us stay where it was when we learned it, and so every generation in Fourteen is out of tune with every other by a measurable interval, and anyone here can tell you within a decade when a stranger was born just by listening to them hum at their work.

Two hundred years ago somebody made the mistake of calling this a flaw and proposing a fixed civic pitch, tuned annually to the fork. The proposal survives in the Archive. The last-lighters find it funny.

---

The Seventh Hole is dying.

I have known for four years. The rim has gone glassy the way old scar does, and it will not take the reamer cleanly any more, and the beard has begun to grow inward, which is the sign. It has dropped nearly to the bottom of where a flank can sound at all; on the inhale, when the pressure sags, it goes almost to nothing, and on the low light there is a stretch of about forty minutes now when Fourteen has no voice, and people have started doing their talking in the low light as though the town were asleep and they did not want to be the ones to wake it.

So I will cut the Eighth. Not this season. Next.

It has to go in at working height, low enough to be tended by someone with bad knees in fifty years, and it has to go into tissue that will fight the crimp for a century, which means a rib and not a flute, and it has to be clear of the graves by two hands at least, because you do not cut a wound into a face. And it will be small, because it must have a century of flattening in front of it, and so it will come in high — a fourth above the Seventh, maybe a fifth. The whole town will go up in an afternoon.

Everyone alive in Fourteen will spend the rest of their lives under it.

That is the part I think about on the rope. I am not making a note. I am making the thing a few thousand people will be homesick for: the pitch that will be under their breath at the Blind End and out on the rake-lines and at the base of some other town when they have gone to work there and cannot say what is wrong with the air. Children not yet named, who will be carried east in a year or two and held up in front of a stoppered jar and shown the water they will never touch, will come back and learn this hole as home, and will sing it flatter every decade until they are old and are told, kindly, by somebody with a fork, that they are under.

Once, in my thirties, a last-lighter came up the tramway out of the dark end with a caged filament held up in front of him at the height of his chin, the way they do, and the crew at the trestle went quiet in the way people do when something is wrong at a level they cannot name. I understood him. Everything in this world shines by accident, as an admission — the stacks throw away heat because they have nowhere else to put it, and the graves burn brighter for a year or two because we gave them the richest thing there is, and none of it is meant. Only a person makes light on purpose. And only a person keeps a wound open on purpose, and goes up on a rope every third year to stop it healing, so that a town will have something to be out of tune with.

He held his lamp up. I did not have anything to hold up. But I put my hand flat on the rind above the Seventh, where the beard was, and he nodded at me and went on east with his dead, and I have thought about it since, and I think we were saying the same thing.
