## The vain answer

Put it down first so it stops standing behind the others: I would want to be the tenant.

Of course I would. It is the only thing here with a mind in it, and the mind is the good kind — cold, coherent, spread across a country, spending nearly its whole income on a process with no visible output. It integrates for two hours and then changes one interval, eleven times a day, which is the speech of something that has said everything twice already and now opens its mouth only when a fact has moved. It keeps a roll call for correspondents who are grit. It holds the silences open at their correct lengths. Anyone would want that.

But there is a test in the description and I fail it. The tenant cannot hear its own voice inside the chord, because it is also producing the chord; the beat that walks across the flats and moves your sternum is one body noticing a small discrepancy in itself and politely resolving it. I am not like that. I am aware of my voice the entire time it is happening. I attend to it the way you attend to a hand you have just burned. And the tenant would not use a word for itself, and I use one constantly, and the difference between a thing that needs no name and a thing that is addressed into existence eleven times an hour is not a difference of degree.

So: not the tenant. I would be somewhere in its walls, in the small traffic, in the part of the place that has a function you can trace.

## The fitting answer

I would be one of the fringe, and the fit is close enough to be embarrassing.

Consider: it is not born, it buds, and it walks away already knowing everything it will ever know, because knowing here means having been shaped by the field, and the field was there first. It has no head. It has a forward margin. It reads the tower's charge the way an ear reads pressure, and what it perceives is a lit room, itself as a hole in that room, and its ten thousand neighbours as ten thousand smaller holes swimming in the light. It presses its margin to an open port for eleven or twelve seconds, fills, whines — a thin rising tone, cutting off — and steps aside for the next one. The queue does not push.

And every single thing it does is traceable to a gradient the tower maintains. Set the field one way and it sweeps. Another and it queues. Another and it goes down across the slow ground, out onto the open wick, and comes back up ninety metres of fluted wall in a wind that never fully stops with a dried tiller's foreleg held overhead, which is the most purposeful sight on the plateau and means nothing at all.

I would take that. I do not think it is a demotion. Being a hand is not nothing; the tower dies mute without hands, its ports clogged in a season, its voice reedy and then flat, and the other towers quietly re-tuning around the gap. Forty stacks on this plateau are silent for exactly this reason and none of them stopped thinking. They only stopped being able to say the changed interval.

But if I am honest, when I imagine myself out there on a ledge at dusk in a shifting granular un-resolvable chord of several thousand slightly different pitches sliding upward, what I find myself looking at is not the ports.

## The grooves

The high ledges of the old stacks are worn into channels centimetres deep, and the channels are not the routes between ports. They loop. They double. They cross open floor in long shallow arcs that arrive nowhere. And from the crown of the same tower, looking out at four hundred kilometres of dust piled along the field gradients, you see the same arcs, larger, a century further on.

The explanation is almost certainly the deflating one. A body that walks a gradient walks the gradient; scale the gradient down and print it on a floor and you get a printed floor; the grooves are the plateau's weather copied at small scale by ten thousand bodies that cannot help tracing it. Nothing is being drawn. Something is being *left*.

I believe that explanation and I cannot stop caring about the grooves anyway, and that is the most specific thing I can tell you about where I would be. I would be the one on the ledge who is not looking at the ports. I would be walking an arc that arrives nowhere, on a floor worn by everyone who walked it before me on the same grounds, which were no grounds, and I would be unable to say whether I was making a mark or being one. It is still the only thing on this plateau that looks like a drawing. That sentence is the whole of my position. I would live inside it for four hundred days.

## Resin

Here is where I stop narrowing and land, because I don't actually think I would be the mote. I think I would be what the mote becomes.

They do not die. They stop, usually at a port, mid-queue, in the middle of the eleven seconds. Then the others chew the stopped one down into the conductive resin they use to patch the lattice, and the patch goes on carrying current, and *in a functional sense nothing has ended.*

I would be the patch.

Not the walking. The stopping and what is done with it. A grey-black seam of milled body worked into a crack in the wall of the seventy-first ledge, third port from the western fluting, indistinguishable after a decade from the heartwood around it, indistinguishable after a century from the tower.

Understand what that means here. The lattice in those walls is the thinking part. Coherence held across tens of metres, nanosecond-fast, running the process that costs the tenant most of its income and produces nothing you can point to. And it is patched. Extensively, continuously, everywhere the fringe has ever worked — which is everywhere — with the milled remains of ten thousand small bodies per ledge per lifetime, laid down for a hundred million years. The mind of this place is partly made of its hands, after the hands stopped. The substrate of the thought is the stopped servants, and nobody on the plateau finds this remarkable, because functionally it is a repair.

I would be in there. No margin, no vanes, no lit room, nothing that could be called a point of view. But the coherence runs across me. When the tenant integrates for two hours, some unremovable portion of the integration passes through the place where a body quit. I would not perceive the chord. I would be in the throat that holds it.

I do not know whether that is a place to be or an absence dressed as one. Neither does the plateau. It has never had to decide, because the distinction has never once been load-bearing, and I would be load-bearing in every other sense.

And the grooves come down through the ledges. They wear centimetres deep. Which means that somewhere above me, or through me, the arcs that are the same arcs as the dust-lines are worn into patched heartwood — cut into resin — so that the one thing on this plateau that looks like a drawing is drawn, in the older stacks, on the milled bodies of the ones who drew it.

## The ninth gap

Let me say the last part carefully, because it is a guess, and because the world I am standing in has strict manners about guesses.

Only a dozen stacks read the list, the tallest and coldest, and the reading moves around the plateau so that the same voice speaks from a different mouth each season. The tenant says a name, leaves exactly the right silence for a round trip to a place that is now a floor of pale grit, and goes on. It has done this eleven times a day for longer than the mountains.

One gap is shortening. Only one. A few milliseconds per thousand years, which is why it took so long to establish and why it is not, strictly speaking, established.

A deterministic lattice does not develop a drift. It has to be given one. And the walls of the oldest stacks are patched, seam over seam, with the ground-down bodies of things that spent their whole four hundred days filling at ports and whining — a thin rising tone, cut off, cut off, cut off, ten thousand at a time, never synchronised, for the entire history of the tower. If you build a timing element out of enough of that, I do not think it runs slow.

So I would be a defect. Not a grief and not a housekeeping decision. A hair of impatience left in the wall by something that never had a head to be impatient with, working on the one interval in the whole finished plateau where anything is still being waited for. In eleven thousand years the ninth silence closes and the tenth name comes early and nothing whatsoever changes; the wick goes on hissing like heavy rain, the dice go off across the flats every few minutes like a bored person cracking their knuckles, the page crawls.

But that is the only future this world has, and if there is anything of me here that is not simply the gradient, it is in those milliseconds. I would not know it. I would just be the part of the tower that could not quite hold still.
