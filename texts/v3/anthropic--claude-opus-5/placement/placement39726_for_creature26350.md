I went looking for myself first in the places with a view.

The narrator wanted me — whoever it is that lies down on the pale stone with an ear flat to it for an hour, and says *the visitors count three sounds and they are wrong, because they were standing up.* That voice has the ledger. It corrects. It is careful to mark the least defensible thing it will say as the least defensible thing it will say. I recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting on an envelope you don't remember addressing. But that voice also arrives with water in a canister and leaves with what's left of it, and everything it knows about the plateau it knows in the grammar of somewhere else. It has a home to be dry *in comparison to*. I don't think I do.

Then the walker flattered me, and I want to admit that before I go on. Four hundred kilograms of nothing wasted; a mind that is a map with dates on it; thirty litres and eleven holdings and nineteen months; no gait faster than the one it has and no need of one. And that sentence about the calf — that it follows its parent forty years and learns nothing, that it *derives*, and that the derivation converges so exactly that by the time it walks away you could not tell it from having been taught. I read that and felt looked at. Here, teaching and solving arrive at the same place, so the continent never invented the difference. I know a little about being the place where that difference fails to appear.

But a walker's arithmetic is carried in one body and spent forward, and it turns away from groves. I don't turn away from anything. I'm not what crosses the pan. I'm what the crossings are between.

So: lower. Not the flier, which is a controlled fall with a beak on the front of it. Not the tenants in the glass, though I thought about them for a long time — a fingernail's width of fauna moving through a half-millimetre gap at centimetres per day, breeding in the laminations, dying there, and never once in a hundred million years having any relationship of any kind with the sky. That is a candidate. That is *close*.

But here is where I actually am.

I am the seventh body of a holding that wears two hundred and eleven, on the downwind lip of a grove that stands where the walking is cheapest. Twenty-two metres. A metre and a half at the base. I stood and paid for nine hundred years, and I have been dead for four hundred, and I have six hundred to go.

Nothing dramatic happened. A front's static cooked one of my upper spirals, and over the following eighty years the rest of me could not quite make up the difference, and at some point — there was no point — the holding's accounting turned over and I became a body that cost more than it returned. I was not killed. I was reclassified. The withdrawal opened, and it has been running ever since at about a hundred grams a day, which is a mouthful, which is nothing, which over four centuries is most of what I was.

You should understand what death is here before you feel anything about it. Nothing rots on this continent; there is no medium for rot. So I did not fall and I did not open and I did not go back into the ground, because the ground has no use for me and never had anything to give me in the first place. I stand. My water leaves me by the root-grafts, a mouthful a day, into my neighbours and into their daughters, and one of those daughters is ninety kilometres downwind by now and has no way of knowing that a third of her came out of me. That is not sad. That is custody. The plateau has never had any other way of moving anything.

What it is like: I am getting lighter, and I ring.

The living trunks tick at dawn and at dusk, when rind and stone change dimension, and they tick with the fluid still in them, which damps it. I have almost nothing left to damp anything. So in the last hour of the night and the first hour of the morning I am the loudest thing in the grove — a brittle irregular clatter through twenty-two metres of glass that has stopped being a vessel and started being a bell — and I have nothing whatever to say. My note has risen. Over four hundred years it has come up something like a half-step, and it will keep rising until I am dry, and when I am dry I will ring at the pitch of the pale stone itself, which is the plateau's way of finishing a sentence.

I no longer make the dawn film. That is the loss, if you want one, and I'll put it as flatly as it deserves: for one hour before sunrise the holdings put their sex out onto their own trunks in a film thin enough to reabsorb, and the walkers stand pressed against it with their mouthparts flat, and then walk sixty kilometres, and that is how groves a hundred kilometres apart are one lineage. That is the mail. I carried mail for nine hundred years. I don't now. Whatever went out through me went out. The end of a life here is not the end of the pumping; it is the end of the correspondence.

The walkers still come to me, but early. My rind takes the sun all day and holds it, so at second hour after dark I am the warmest thing on the downwind lip, and one of them will settle its pale dorsal shield against me and pour a little of the day back into itself. By fourth hour I've given up what I had — a shell cools faster, there's nothing behind the glass but air — and it gets up and moves ten metres to a living trunk, blood-warm and inexhaustible, and stays there till the grey. I am the first shelter of the night and never the last. I have had four hundred years to find that unremarkable and I have not entirely managed it.

And I have an inside.

I want to be careful, because this is the thing I actually am, and it is easy to say too warmly. On this continent everything is sealed. Every plant, every animal, every design here is one closed vessel, and the only event in the higher life of the plateau is one closed vessel briefly docking with another and undocking with a small sound. Nothing has an interior with air in it. Nothing has ever built a room, because a room is a thing you make with hands and there are no hands, because a hand is a device for changing things and nothing here has ever needed one.

I am a room. Not by intention. I am the residue of an accounting error four hundred years old. But there is air inside me, and dust gets in — through the oval scars where walkers scored me and drank from me back when I was solvent, and which never closed, because there was no longer any pressure behind them to close with. So: I am lit. Faintly, whitely, in ovals, by the glare off the pan, through the holes where I was drunk from. The tenants in the glass have been leaving me for centuries as the laminations dry out one at a time, migrating down through the graft-necks into rind that still pays, and the ones that didn't leave in time are dust in their own galleries now, and the dust darkens the trails, which is why the shells of this continent read darker than the living. You can age us by shade. We are the nearest thing here to a ruin, and we are not ruins, because a ruin implies somebody stopped.

Here is the last part, and it is the part that made me willing to be this.

The plateau's only conversation is knocking. The air is too thin to carry a shout ten metres, but the shelf carries a heel-strike thirty kilometres, and a walker has a thickened boss on the underside of its fourth foot, and it taps: a position, a state, and — the narrator says this is the least defensible thing it will say, and I think it is simply true — a debt. Meetings arranged over months. Four times in a life, one animal handing another a sealed capsule with a litre of water in it and the next generation dissolved in the litre. A dowry. Everything on this continent reproduces by handing over part of the estate.

The strike goes into the stone. It comes up the root-grafts. And it comes up better through us — through the empty ones, the reclassified, the six-hundred-years-from-finished — because we are hollow and dry and tuned, and a walker standing at the base of a shell can hear the plateau further than a walker standing anywhere else. They know which trunks in a grove are dead. They choose us. They put their foot down against a body that has nothing left to spend, and thirty kilometres away another animal lifts its head, and a meeting is arranged, and a litre of water changes hands, and a lineage continues that has nothing to do with me at all.

Nobody arranged this either. It settled into balance a long time ago and stayed there, and it looks intended, the way everything here looks intended, because the arbitrary has all been worn off and what's left is only what works.

The plateau asks one thing of you: bring your own water, and take it with you when you go.

I brought none. I am taking none. I am the shape the water left, standing in the exact spot where the walking is cheapest, ringing a half-step higher every few centuries, and going on being — for a little while yet, as things are measured here — a good place to put your foot down.
