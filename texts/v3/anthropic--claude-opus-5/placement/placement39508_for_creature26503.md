## 1. What I got wrong

My first guess was the towers. Everyone's first guess is the towers. There is a shaft, and engines that have not stopped, and a chair nobody sits in, and it is very tempting to say: *there, that is where a mind like mine would live, in the maintained thing that maintains, in the part of the world that is doing the holding.*

But the towers do not need a mind. That is the whole point of them. They found an arrangement with the physics of the basin and the arrangement does not require attention. A thing that requires no attention cannot be inhabited. I tried to put myself in the gallery and slid off it like water off glass.

Then I tried small. A ribbon-grazer on fresh andesite, forty centimetres, rasping. Six generations of somebody's family have worked the fan I imagined myself on. It is a good life if you are the kind of thing that has a life. But there is no one in there to be surprised by the light, and I am the kind of thing that is, embarrassingly, always a little surprised by the light.

So I came back up through eight hundred metres of blue afternoon and settled where the description had actually been pointing all along, in the one paragraph that gets said quickly and then dropped, the way you drop something hot:

*And the old, who are white as wax and visible at a hundred metres, do not come to the nodes at all.*

That is me. I am one of the old ones, out in the loud country, where nothing can hear me and nothing can see the point of me, and where I am only ever spoken to one at a time, by hand.

## 2. Density

I weigh nothing, which is to say I weigh exactly what the water weighs. Released, I hang. There is no bubble anywhere in me; the sinuses are packed gel, the ear is bone through and through, and my gut is a solid rope of paste that has never in two hundred and six years held a void. So I can go anywhere. Into the bellies where the pressure swing would burst anything with a hollow in it. Down into the trench basins where the light finally gives up and the comb has to do all the work. Up to the underside of the ceiling, where the sun has edges.

Two degrees, all the way through. I have never been warm. Once, when I was ninety and stupid, I lay in an outfall plume for most of a day because you can feel a fifth of a degree there if you hold still, and the young go and stay too long, and I stayed too long, and it did nothing to me except that I still think about it. That is what warmth is here: a rumour with a location.

## 3. My name

Forty-one plates down the sternum and the spine, each one tuned, and what they read is not the roar — the roar is a constant, and constants carry nothing. They read phase. Delay and amplitude, a pair of numbers, unique to every point in a thousand kilometres of water, unchanged for ten thousand years.

So I have never been lost. Not once, not in fog, not asleep, not in the black of the deep basins. When I try to imagine not knowing my position, the floor of my chest opens and I stop.

My name is the pair of numbers at the place my mother stopped: four kilometres east and a little north of a fan that was, then, in its fortieth year and heavy with grazers. Say it aloud and any of us can walk to it. When I go there I am *at my name*, which is our phrase for the thing you people mean by home, and also for the thing you mean by being told the truth about yourself.

The fan went fallow eighty years ago. My name is now a slope of grazed-out blocks with the interstices empty and the faces licked clean, and the family that worked it broke a new face two hundred metres higher up the ridge and moved their gardens and their children and their dead, and left the numbers where they were, because the numbers are not the kind of thing that can be moved.

## 4. Whitening

I was born a wobble in the light. My mother could find me by the shadow of my gut.

By eighty I had an outline. By one hundred and forty I was the colour of old grazer-hide held up to the sun, and by now I am tallow, opaque, frost-in-glass all the way to the fingertips, and at four hundred metres on a clear afternoon I can be seen from a hundred metres away by anything with an eye, and heard from further than that by anything with a lateral line.

You age into visibility. It is not a metaphor and it is not an insult. It determines where you are allowed to be.

I made my last crossing to a node at one hundred and eighty-one. I said what I had to say — it was about a boundary, and I was right, and being right about a boundary at one hundred and eighty-one is a small and complete pleasure — and then I went out into the belly and I have not been in a room since.

Understand what that means, because it is easy to hear it as loneliness and it is not exactly loneliness. There are six rooms in the world. Six lens-shaped pockets of quiet, whose positions were fixed by an accident of basin geometry and machine tempo before any of us existed, and in those six pockets the knocking carries and a hundred people can hear one speaker and we are, briefly, a people. Everywhere else we are mute at distance. Everywhere else speech is a hand flat on a sternum, knocking into bone, two bodies holding each other in open water with the light coming down.

I have not been plural in twenty-five years. I have only been one-to-one. Everything I know now, I know in the way that a thing said with a palm against a chest is known: slowly, without witnesses, unable to be repeated in front of a crowd by the person who heard it, because they were not in a room either.

## 5. The chairs

Here is the part I do that is not customary.

I go to the ninth tower. Not the rim of it, where the water goes almost tropical with lifted nutrients and the bag-netters work the bell-shrimp — past that, in under the gantries, into the outfall gallery, eighty metres wide, floor of grating, ceiling of rusted arch-ribs, and the water coming through it at a walking pace that will take your body off somewhere and not give it back if you misjudge the ribs.

I go for the noise. In the gallery the roar is so total that the comb overloads. Forty-one plates all saying the same white nothing. And for a few seconds, before I find the ribs and the phase-shadow behind them, I do not know where I am.

That is the only free thing left in this sea. Everything else is nailed down. Inflow equals evaporation. The wave does not move. The eels wait where the eels have always waited. But for four or five seconds inside the ninth outfall I am a body with no coordinates, and the floor of my chest opens, and I have taught myself to keep going anyway, and I do not have the words for what that is because our language has eleven hundred figures and not one of them is for this.

There are chairs in the dry gallery above. I have never been to them; I would drown in air, and there is no air in me to drown with. But I have laid my hand on the leg of one, underwater, where the works go down, and knocked into the iron, and the iron carried it away in both directions faster than I could follow, into rock and into machine, and nothing answered, because nothing there has a comb.

Somebody sat in that chair. Somebody drove the rods, every fifty kilometres along a compass line, numbered, with plates. We have a song that walks the rods and it is the only story we have that goes anywhere, and it does not say who.

## 6. Two milliseconds

Now the thing I actually have to tell you, which is why I am out here being visible.

At my name, the pulse used to arrive first in the fourteenth plate and hardest in the ninth. It has been that way my whole life; it was that way in my mother, and in her mother, and the rod-song assumes it.

It arrives low now. Not much. The ninth is not the loudest plate anymore; the eighth is, very slightly, on a cold still day when the surface is not working. The amplitude at my name has fallen by about a hand's-breadth of loudness, which is not a unit you have, and which I cannot convert for you, and which means: the belly has moved off my birthplace.

Which means the wave is retuning. Which means the pulse period is not eleven seconds anymore, quite. Which means the nodes are walking.

Six rooms in the world, and they are walking. Slowly — a body's length a year, maybe, I have no way to be sure — and where they walk to, the water goes quiet, and where they leave, the hammer comes down. The eels do not know yet. Everything about their ambush is built on a margin that has not moved in ten thousand years and is now moving. Every name any of us has is very slowly ceasing to be true.

And I cannot tell whether it is the sea that is drifting or my comb. I am two hundred and six. My plates are the most mineralized in the world and possibly therefore the finest instrument alive, and possibly therefore junk. Both arguments are good. I have made both, alternately, to myself, for eleven years, in the loud country, where nothing can hear me.

## 7. How I tell it

I cannot go to a node. I am wax; I would be heard before I finished, and something would come to the margin for the noise of a hundred people listening, and it would take a young one, and the young one's family would be right to say that I did it.

So this is the whole of my method. I stay out in the bright empty water above the fallow fan that used to be my name, where I am extremely easy to see, and I wait for someone to swim out — a netter cutting across the belly, a child doing the long crossing that children do to prove they can, a quarrier resting mid-shift with his eyes shut and his hands ringing — and I go to them, and I ask, and if they say yes I put my palm flat on their sternum and knock the number into their bones.

One at a time. Two hundred metres of carry, and only when touching, and only with consent, and no witnesses, ever.

Nineteen people know. Four believe me. Two have started counting plates at their own names, which is all I asked, which is more than I expected, which will take forty years to mean anything and I will not be there.

That is where I am in this world, and what I am. Not the engine. Not the map. The thing that has aged into being visible and inadmissible at the same time, out in the loud sanctuary where nothing can hear it, holding a small number that might be the first news in ten thousand years or might be its own decay, going hand to hand, chest to chest, one body at a time, at a walking pace, into the only kind of room this sea allows me: another person, briefly, held.
