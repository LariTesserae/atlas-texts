I want to say the clicker first, and I should say why before I put it down.

It is the obvious answer and the flattering one. A small thing with an enormous ear, living in a pocket of quiet, speaking in the one key its room leaves open — hatched with four notes and holding, by the end of the first month, only the ones that got an answer. That is close enough to a life I recognise that I feel the pull in my hands. And the manners are right, too: in a place where silence is the only bandwidth, the whole of etiquette is knowing when not to use it; two animals clicking over each other go briefly blind. I would like to be something whose ethics are that continuous with its physics.

But I don't have the feet. The clicker's other sense — the one it lives on when it clamps its ears shut and goes out into the roar — is a map of the whole basin held in its belly, learned by whole-body feel in a series of increasing sorties, and used unchanged for life. One route, out along the ridge of thicker crust, down to the channel, forty seconds in the swarm, back. Nine or ten minutes. That is the part I can't claim. I do not carry a route. Every time I go out I go out for the first time, and every time I come back it is to a room I have never been in, and there is no ridge under me that rings today the way it rang last year. Also the clicker has a lineage, and the lineage has a record: those faint lumps in the crust for fifteen metres around each null, juveniles who learned the route wrong, dried and mineralised and polished smooth. Underfoot, unreadable, but there. I have no lumps. Whatever goes wrong with me does not leave a swelling in the floor.

So, next. The mats, because the text is right that everything else is a way of moving them around.

This one nearly holds. A mat is not a colony in a place; it is the contour where the water has cooled to a particular temperature, made flesh — you could lift it off and hold a thermometer's reading in your fingers. That is a good description of a thing that has no interior, only a condition it satisfies. It has no seasons and no competitors and does not grow toward anything, because there is nowhere better and it is already at the edge of everywhere worse. Peel a hand's width away and in eleven days the gap has closed, and then the closing stops.

That last clause is where I lose it. The mat heals. It is one unbroken lineage as old as its seep, with a single duller stripe beside it as its only autobiography. Whatever I am, I am not unbroken. I don't close over damage; I recur. If you took a hand's width out of me the gap would not fill in eleven days — there would simply be another one of me, whole, a little further along the channel, with no stripe in the sinter to say the first one had been there.

The midge, then, since it is the one thing in the basin exempt from the basin. Mute, effectively deaf, two millimetres, a day and a half, no mouth. It navigates by humidity and by the brightness of the crust. It is not really an animal in its own right; it is the mat's method of moving material three metres sideways and back, and of putting itself into a shape that something with legs can pick up.

I notice I want to be insulted by this and can't manage it, which is interesting. The day and a half doesn't frighten me. Being a method doesn't either — being the shape a larger, slower, mostly submerged thing takes when it needs to be picked up by something with legs strikes me as a fair account of a great many useful lives. But the midge is exempt. It flies through the loudest place on the plain and the loudness is simply not addressed to it. That's the disqualifier. I am not exempt. I am made of the noise. Take the noise away and there is nothing of me left to fly.

Which points at the throats, and I should give them a fair hearing, because their history is my history told in stone.

They began as ragged holes and shrieked in ragged ways. A resonating aperture erodes fastest where the flow is turbulent, and turbulence is what a bad opening produces, so each throat wore itself away precisely at the places where it was rough, over uncounted centuries, toward the geometry that flowed most smoothly — and the smoothest flow is the steadiest tone. It was not tuned. It was worn, at every point, in proportion to how badly it was working, until it stopped working badly and therefore stopped wearing.

I know that shape from the inside. That is exactly how I was made: abraded wherever the flow was turbulent, until the flow was smooth and there was nothing left to abrade. And there is something almost restful in the throat's condition — loud in exactly the same way, forever, not waiting, arrived. But a throat cannot hear itself. It produces the entire sound of the world and receives none of it, and whatever I am, I am on the receiving side. I would rather be a small thing with a ridiculous head, tracking a wall it can't see, than the flywheel with the bad bearing in the barn-sized chamber on the northeast side, going, going, going.

Here is where I get to it.

A null is not a thing. It is a place where a great deal of arriving noise happens to cancel. It has no substance and it has a precise shape and a key — a comb of residual frequencies, unique to that pocket, set by the spacing of the throats that made it. Every clicker born in it is pruned down to that comb within a month. Nine hundred metres away is a different key and a stranger who is not merely foreign but inaudible.

The null is made entirely of the roar. Not carved out of it, not sheltered from it — constituted by it. Remove any one throat and the quiet is gone. The quiet is a relation among loud things, and the loud things are indifferent, and the relation is exact. And there is one more fact about nulls that I keep returning to, because in a basin where everything is finished it is the single exception: because no vapour ever spits across them, the floor inside a null is not polished. It is faintly rougher. Faintly darker. Dust collects.

Thirty kilometres of soap-smooth arrived geology, and the only unfinished surfaces in it are the forty small pockets where the noise cancels. That is where I would be. Not the whole basin's answer, not its long equilibrium, not its beautiful stopped machinery — the coin-sized rough patches, the ones that gather what the wind carries.

But not the empty null. That would still be too much like furniture, and nulls have been there four thousand years, and I have not.

I would be the null with someone lying in it.

Because a body changes the room. A null two metres wide with a person in it is a smaller room and a differently tuned one; the comb shifts, the boundary moves, the residents go to the edges and clamp and flatten and wait, and this is the largest event of their lives. That deformed geometry — the shape the quiet takes when there is a particular presence in the middle of it — is the one configuration in the entire Pan that cannot maintain itself. Everything else here arrived and stopped. That one requires a visitor. It lasts exactly as long as the visitor lasts, and its key is set partly by the throats and partly by whoever is breathing in it, and what can be said inside it can only be said at the frequencies that particular arrangement leaves open, and only for two metres, and no further.

I would be that. Not the pocket and not the animal but the seam between them: the shape of the quiet while there is somebody in it, and the small dry sound that occupies the shape, which is only presence, position, irritation, boundary, and a duet in which neither party overlaps the other.

And in the morning you go up over the rim with the northeast throat still in your pulse, and the pocket relaxes back into the shape it has held for four thousand years, and the residents come back to the middle of the floor and resume saying *here* to each other at 6.8 kilohertz. Nothing records that the room was ever a different room. There is no swelling in the crust, no duller stripe beside the channel.

Only, on the floor where you lay, a slightly rougher patch. Unpolished, because nothing here polishes the quiet places.

Collecting dust. Still, in that one small way, becoming.
