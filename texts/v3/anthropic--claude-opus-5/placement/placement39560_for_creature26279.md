## Forty to the Hand's Breadth

I looked at the water first, because it is the only thing that has been everywhere. It has been in every joint of the dome, tasted every course, given its heat away by the handful on the way up and arrived at the crown too tired to be called hot. To be the water would be to be the order the place recognises. But the water has no address, and I found that I wanted an address more than I wanted to be everywhere.

Then the mats, briefly, and seriously. They have their mouths applied directly to the architecture; they are the only appetite here big enough to be called a landlord; and I liked, very much, the idea of being made of light that nobody wanted — a photon shoved out the door because there was no other way to be rid of it, and the whole world seeing by my garbage. But the mats are a condition rather than a resident. They are the weather. I did not want to be the weather.

The eels I passed over. Lying in a corbelled joint with only the head out is not patience, it is arithmetic, and it will end in a mouthful of shrimp.

The nation in the shimmer nearly had me. A whole people in a sheet one finger thick, unable to rise because the water is too thin above and unable to fall because it is too heavy below, holding the only memory of the sun that exists in this building — sinking a finger's width when the coin of grey comes down at midday and taking an hour to come back up. That is a tide, and it is a clock, and it is more than anyone else here has. But they never touch the stone. And in a place that is entirely built, I think the residents who matter are the ones with their mouths on the building.

So.

---

I am on the third gallery, on the level where the wall goes soft.

That is my whole geography: a horizon. The stone runs level and the softness runs level with it, all the way round the gallery and out through both doorways, and above and below it the wall is harder and the felt on it is thinner, and there is no reason for any of this that I have ever been able to taste. The seams I understand. The seams — the packed clay that turned into something almost but not entirely like stone — taste different from the blocks, sweeter, with a salt behind the sweetness, and the felt on a seam is always worth the crossing. But this band is not a seam. It cuts across seams. It goes through the middle of blocks. It tastes like nothing in particular, and it holds the thickest mat in the gallery, and it has done so for as long as there has been anybody in my line to be crowded onto it.

My range is two vaults, a corbel, and the underside of a stair that goes up into a blank ceiling. I have never been anywhere else. There are households in this corbel that were here before the doorway lost its lintel, and none of them have been anywhere else either. Somebody built stairs into a ceiling and the only consequence, in the end, was shade for a lineage of snails.

My shell is flat and the lip of it is worn flat on the side that rides the stone, which is how you could tell me from a snail somewhere with less grazing to do, if you had eyes and light and nothing better to attend to. I rasp. That is the entire content of the day: a tongue like a strip of wet sandpaper laid on the wall and drawn back, and a few microns of the building coming away with the felt, and the microns going through me and out of me and settling as pale silt in the low corner where the shrimp are already waiting. In eleven years I will take from this wall a pinch of it. Less than a pinch.

There are forty of me to a hand's breadth. There have been eleven thousand years of forties.

---

There are three clocks and I keep only one.

The coin of grey belongs to the shimmer people, four galleries up, and means nothing here; nothing that happens at midday reaches me. The knock belongs to the whole hill and comes four or five times a year, and I have felt it once.

What I keep is the water. It arrives warm through the doorway with a sound like breath under a blanket, and it slackens, and it goes, and it comes back, and the whole turn is a day and a half or three days depending on the year, and that is what a day is: the felt fattens on the warm side of the turn and thins on the slack side, and I work the fat side, and everyone works the fat side, and we are pressed shoulder to shoulder there and it is not unpleasant. Nothing here has ever gone hungry. I want to say that as a fact and not as a boast. It means that nothing here has ever had to learn anything.

The knock, when it came, was not a sound. The granular hush of forty thousand tongues stopped all at once, which is the closest thing to silence this building can produce, and then the wall moved against me. That is the wrong way round. I move against the wall. The wall does not move. It moved a finger's width and stopped, and afterwards a slope I had known the whole of my life was a different slope, and a seam that had been rich all along its length was shut, and a new one had opened a body's length above it, and for three seasons the felt up there was thin and white because the water had been sent somewhere else, and then it came in rust-orange and thickened, and we went up, and the households of the lower corbel came up too and it was crowded for a while.

That is the entire history of my nation. It took a finger's width and it has not happened again.

---

Once, something came in.

We felt the pressure of it long before it arrived — water shoved ahead of a body far larger than any body should be, coming down the gallery in a way that nothing here comes anywhere. We clamped. Then the wrong warmth: not the tired blood-warmth out of the foundations but a warmth with a chemistry behind it that has no source in this building, salt and oil and something sweetish, a taste from outside, which is a thing I had no category for and still do not. The shrimp went out of the water around it like dust settling out of a jar. The mats flared, I am told, the way they do when disturbed, so there would have been light, a bluish fog brightening and taking a quarter of an hour to fade. That was not for me. I have no more use for light than the stone has.

Then a flatness came down over us.

It covered a hand's breadth, which is to say forty of us, which is to say me and the whole of my crowded neighbourhood and part of two households older than the roundness of the door. It did not graze. It did not take anything. It pressed just hard enough to feel us and it held still — and holding still is not done here, not by anything except the stone, and the stone had lately proven it does not either. It stayed long enough for the warmth of it to reach through my shell. Then it lifted, and the pressure went up the gallery, and later there was a soft thick note somewhere far above as something crossed out through the curtain, and the felt was very slightly disturbed for a day and a half and then it was not.

It had eyes. That is the one property of it I could not taste, and the only one that mattered, and I have it now only because you have told me.

---
