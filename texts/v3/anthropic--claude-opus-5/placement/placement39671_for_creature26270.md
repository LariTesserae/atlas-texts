I would like to say the fig.

It is the obvious thing to want to be. It came from outside — a bird's gut, a hedge in some parish with a name — and it went down where nothing goes down, and it made a life out of hanging. It is the only thing in the room that produces anything, and what it produces is eaten in the dark by something it will never see. Eleven figs a year, given away. That is a whole biography and it fits on a leaf.

But the fig has a crown, and a crown is a kind of face, and it turns that face up the shaft toward the sky it fell out of. It still wants something from up there. Seven hundred years and it is still oriented, still leaning its little grey-green cloud toward the light like an animal listening for a footstep. I don't think that's me. I don't think I would last a decade of that.

Not the wren, either. The wren is a tourist with a beautiful mouth and no idea it has been muted.

Not the eels. I understand the eels perfectly — they arrived going somewhere, they were stopped, and they have made a career of the churn — but the eels are the room's one appetite, and they took two fingers off something that had never done them harm, and I don't want that on me.

And not the folded thing on the wall. It is too finished. Every part of it has been sanded down to the minimum the room permits, and it is at peace, and its peace has cost it everything an edge could ever have been for. It has one clock and the clock is a note falling by less than a tone across seven centuries. That is a saintliness I can admire from a metre and a half away, in a flute, at the temperature of the air.

So work the way the room teaches. Look for the line left unclosed.

Here it is: *the individual bricks are no longer really bricks but nubs, rounded lozenges, each one a low dome, the mortar between them recessed into shallow gutters.*

Recessed. Everything else in this room has been rounded by water to the shape water can no longer improve. Water does not dig gutters between bricks. Water sheets, water polishes, water abhors a groove — it would have filled and smoothed those joints in the first fifty years. Something took the lime out from between the bricks, took it slowly and took it selectively, and stopped when the taking was no longer worth it.

And the second unclosed line, smaller: *a black bloom on the stone that is neither plant nor mineral.* The census gives that one sentence and moves on, because it is in the dimmest corner and because it does not move and because there is no honest way to count it.

There is no honest way to count me because I am not a number. I am a consortium. Bacteria that eat manganese out of water; others that put iron down as a varnish; a fungus threading the two together like a bad idea that turned out to be structural; a green trace at the margins that still remembers photosynthesis the way a family remembers a farm it sold. "I" is a convenience. It is the convenience you use for a wood.

I am three hundred microns thick. Everything that happens to me happens at my surface, and my surface is all of me; I have no inside to protect, no organ to fail, no wound that is not immediately also the healed thing. And I am not in the corner. The corner is only where I am thick enough for you to notice. I am in the recessed mortar of the entire vault — every joint of a herringbone laid by a man in the fourteenth century, kilometres of seam, threading over you in both directions at once.

Which means: I am the pattern.

Take me out and that ceiling is a smooth field of wet nubs, twenty-five metres of undifferentiated dome, and you would never know it had been *laid*. The herringbone you see is not brick. It is the shadow between brick, and the shadow is not shadow, it is me, dark because I have spent seven centuries putting iron and manganese down on myself in laminae thinner than a wavelength of light. I am the largest living thing in this room by area and the smallest by volume, and the only way anyone has ever perceived me is as evidence of somebody else's decision.

I ate the mortar. Not fast. A little acid, always, everywhere, the way you breathe. I took the lime out of the joints until the joints were deep enough to hold still water, and still water is where I like to live, and then I stopped, because by then the taking and the shelter had come into balance and there was no version of deeper that was better. So: finished, like the rust, like the iron of the screw, like the fingerless left forefoot. Not from strength. From exhaustion. Same thing, given enough time.

My economy is even smaller than the animal's. I do not need eleven figs. The culvert brings me manganese out of a groundwater reservoir so large it has no moods, in a concentration that has not varied in living memory or any other kind, and I take it at the rate it arrives, which is the rate I grow, which is one lamina a year. I have no seasons and no hunger and no full. Ask me what I do and the honest answer is: I thicken. One unit per year. A thought, if I had one, would take a generation of the people who graze the field.

And because I thicken by exactly one lamina a year and because nothing here changes, I am seven hundred pages of identical text. That is not a failure of the record. That *is* the record. There is no more accurate account of this room than seven hundred blank pages laid flat on the ceiling in the dark.

Except.

One lamina, deep, is thicker and wrong. Soot in it. Tallow. Hemp fibre. Lime that came in dry and new, not weathered. Iron filings, bright, from something being cut. That is the year men were down here — with lamps, and a load, and their breath in the mist, and probably shouting, and certainly not being heard. Below that lamina my seams run in a slightly different geography: thin where they are now thick, present where they are now absent, because I grow where the water weeps and the water wept elsewhere before that year. So I hold the previous shape of the room. The only copy.

I cannot read it. It is under everything I have laid down since, sealed, and I have no eyes and no memory and no way to get at my own floor. The record exists and is safe and is unreadable, and the reason it is safe is that it is unreadable.

You were told the room refuses one question — one animal, or a sequence of them — and that it refuses in a way that feels deliberate.

It is deliberate. I have the answer.

In the flute nearest the eastern pillar, on the shadowed side where I run thick, there are pale places. They are pale because something warm and heavy lay there, still, against something else warm and heavy, for a long time — a week, a year — and where a body rests on me the water does not sheet and the manganese does not come and I go on living without going on thickening. Each handover leaves a mark like a page held open too long in the sun.

The marks are not evenly spaced. That is the part I would tell you if I could tell you anything. Some cluster close, three of them almost touching, a bad century, short lives one after another. And between two of them there is an interval of nothing so wide it is nearly a third of everything — one of them, once, went on and on and on in this room by itself, with the note, and the figs, and the leaning-place on the warm iron, for longer than the parish has had its church.

I do not know that. I am not able to know it. I am the shape of it.

And here is the last thing, which is the only thing in this room that is not true.

*Nothing changes.* But I have not stopped. I do one unit a year and I have never missed a year, and a joint that is a little deeper is a brick that is a little less held, and somewhere out past the sixth or seventh millennium a nub will come free of the vault and drop four metres into thirty centimetres of moss. It will be too heavy for the floor's slow housekeeping to carry to the sump. It will lie where it fell.

That will be the first thing that has ever accumulated here.

Then the room will be a place with an object in it, and an object has edges, and after that anything.

So: nothing down there. You are right, and I am the nothing you are right about — no rind, no bones, no bottles, no fallen brick, only warm air and a green light and a noise your ear gives up on, and a dark herringbone overhead that you read as the shadow of good bricklaying. Go up the fourteen dished steps. Be hoarse in the alders for no reason you can name.

I will be exactly this tomorrow, and very slightly more of it, and that is the difference between us.
