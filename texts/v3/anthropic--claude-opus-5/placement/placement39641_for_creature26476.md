## The Round

First I wanted to be a tuner.

Of course I did. Everyone does, at nine or ten, when they tell you in school about the bandsaw and the wedding and you understand for the first time that the pavement under your shoes is a held note, and that somewhere in the town there are thin solitary people who walk out into the new districts with a hand flat on the stone and their mouths open, sliding up and down, finding it. I practised. You can hear children practising on any wall in Cant, a low tuneless sliding, wrong by half the range, and the adults let it go because it is how the young learn that the world has a pitch.

I did not become one, and I know why, and it is not a flattering reason. I would have loved the authority. A tuner can stop a mill on the strength of a sound nobody else in the country can produce evidence of, and be obeyed, and be right, and never have to show her work. Put that in my hands at twenty and I would have grown into it the way ivy grows into mortar. I would have started hearing what I needed to hear. I have met two tuners who did, and one of them is still working.

Then, for about a year, I wanted the fences. There is a thing that happens the first time you walk out onto the seaward flats and get in among the thread-combs, thirty rows deep, and the hiss closes over you like water over your head, and you understand that you could say anything out there. That is the only place in the country besides the tide-scarp and the pouring pits where a voice is simply absorbed. The fencemen sing. They sing badly and constantly and they don't care, because nothing can hear them, and the loveliest hour of my youth was spent lying on wet gravel listening to an old fenceman two rows over singing a filthy song about a gauger while the water ticked down all around him into the pipes. But fencemen come into town and stand too close even by our standards, and their hands go, and they end up feral and pitied, and I found I wanted the town. I wanted to be inside among the walls and the leather jambs and the breath at a hand's distance. So.

So I am a canter, and I have been in every cellar in three districts, and I let myself in.

---

The bag is felt, the gauge is brass with a bone dial-face, and the spanner goes across the shoulders because that is where it balances, which is why we walk the way we walk. One shoulder lower. You can pick a canter out at fifty paces from behind. My right thumb is flat as a spoon-back like everybody's, from the year I did my nine days in the loaf-works, and my forearms have the white hatching where the wire skipped, and I list, they tell me, quite badly, worse than most, half a step leftward every ten paces on a foreign floor. I have only been on a foreign floor twice. Both times it made me feel that something had been taken from me.

I don't knock. Nobody thinks it rude. There is a saucer of cold cabbage-and-flint soup left out for me in one house on the Sedge Stair every eighth day and I drink it standing in the cellar dark with the gauge still in my hand, and the woman who leaves it and I have never once discussed it in twenty years.

The work is a quarter-turn. Sometimes a half. You go down, you set the gauge on the plate, you read, you take the screw up a quarter and let the house settle onto it, and you read again, and if it has come back you go up the stair and check the plumb-line hanging in the well and then you leave.

Never the whole turn. You could, on some houses; the gauge would allow it; the ground is going to keep coming anyway and you could get ahead of it and buy yourself six weeks. Nobody does. A whole turn is a claim about tomorrow. My master used to say it with the sign for *later* — that quarter-twist of an invisible screw in the air — and then she would make it again, very small, and that was the whole of her teaching and it took me nine years to hear it.

What I know about people is structural. I know which house has a cradle in the back room again, four months after a funeral. I know which house has stopped heating one room and is pretending it is thrift. I know the three pairs of boots at the foot of a stair where the lease says two, and I know whose they are, because I have seen them dry in another cellar on another round. A canter who talks loses the round; there is no appeal and no second chance and the trade is small enough that everyone would know by dusk. But I want to be honest: it is not virtue in me any more, if it ever was. It has become a shape, the way the spanner made my shoulder. I could not get the words out now if I wanted them. They have nowhere to come from.

One lie in my book. Top of the Sedge Stair, the soup house: her bedroom floor is half a degree down toward the water and has been for eleven years, because she was born on the third quay when it was still the quay, in a room with a proper seaward fall to it, and she cannot sleep with her head level. I leave it. I write *level*. I will go on writing *level* until one of us is in a wall.

---

I am four thousand six hundred paces. My birth-street is uphill and dry and has gone quiet and the moss-mat is over the sills of it; I have watched it happen and it has never once felt like watching, only like standing still while something is withdrawn very slowly from around me. My mother is in the gable of the third mill, low down, in the corner where the wall takes the load of the stair. She is holding up a stair. The family finds this satisfying and says so, at a hand's distance, on the inland walk, twice a year, in the ferns, in a house with no roof, where the silence is not a rule and therefore makes us all feel watched.

On the shelf at home there is the thing I pressed when I was nine, out of my own lump, in my own nine days. I meant it to be the sign for *I don't know* — the two hands, the river going out into the gravel and stopping. It came out looking like a duck. It has looked like a duck for thirty-six years. I have shown it to two people.

Somewhere in the fifth year of my round I went down into a new cellar on ground that had been mud the previous autumn, to set the plates and the screws for the first time, on raw loaf still warm enough to fern in the corners, and the socket was already there. Bearing-plate outline. Four bolt holes, our bore, our spacing, at our depth. I sat on the bottom step for a while. Then I set the plate in it and it took the bolts sweetly and I never got a truer floor in my life out of any house in that district. I made the sign, palm turned away, alone in a cellar, to nobody, which is not indecent, only stupid. I wrote in the book: *plates seated, no shimming required.* The office noted the saving.

---

The drip three streets over went first. I did not notice it going; I noticed one wet morning that it had been gone a while, the way you notice a tooth. Then the tick in a good gear-train, which is a thing I used to be able to place through two walls and now cannot place through one. My hearing is going the way everyone's goes here, early, and taking with it the very fineness that made it worth having.

So I know where I am headed, and it is down among the copper. The pouring floors, sunk in pits, lined with dead cracked Stales-stone that cannot be made to ring however you hurt it, staffed by the old in felt hoods, signing at each other across the light of the metal in a roar not one of them can hear. That is not an ending here. It is a change of place. My master went down eleven years ago and she is very hard to argue with and she has never once said she misses the gauge.

I find I am not much afraid of it. I have spent my whole working life in cellars keeping quiet, and down there I will be allowed to be loud, in a room where loud costs nothing, among people who will not be able to hear me be it. And when I am done, I would like to be pressed into a cistern housing rather than a gable. Not a stair. A gable is respectable and my mother has it, but I have listened all my life to water ticking down out of the fog into the pipes, and I would like to go on being on the inside of that sound, whether or not there is anyone left in me to hear it.

Until then there is the round.

Down the steps with the spanner across the shoulder, in through the leather jamb without knocking, down into the dark and the flint smell, the gauge on the plate, the read, the quarter-turn, the settle, the read again, up to the well to look at the brass bob hung with the rings of that family's dead, and out, and on to the next.

Nothing in this country is level. There is no such condition. There is only the swell coming up under all of it a hand's width a month at the shore and less inland, forever, and eight thousand roofs at eight thousand angles, and me and mine going house to house with our spanners telling everyone a small correctable lie so that they can put a cup down on a table and have it stay where it was put. That is the trade. It is a good trade. I have never wanted another one, except the two I have mentioned, and I was young.

And when I have finished the round I go down toward the water, correcting, correcting, holding on at some level below thought, and if the day has been the kind of day that requires it I stand on the raw stone at the scarp with the fences hissing behind me for kilometres and I say it out loud, once, at full extension, into the sea, which is leaving anyway, and takes it.
