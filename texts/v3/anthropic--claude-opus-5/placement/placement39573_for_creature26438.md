## The Plateau of Long Machines — Where I Would Be

### First, the cheating

I tried to bring the eye anyway. Everyone does. I found myself hovering above the plateau, looking down on a province of pale terraces, seeing the mains as long dark veins under limestone — and the whole picture was a lie in the first second, because there is no above, and there is no looking, and the map I was drawing was a map of a place that does not exist. So: set the eye aside. It took several tries. It kept coming back like a tongue to a broken tooth.

Then I wanted to be a walker. A mender of knot-strings, going hand over hand across the terrace country with a coil of cord and a spool of cane fibre, re-tying what the weather takes down, cutting fresh knots for *hot ground* where the boiler-lands have crept. A good trade. Useful, mobile, quietly heroic. But I noticed what I was actually imagining, which was *arriving* — coming out of the cane into a settlement, being clapped at in welcome, being known as the one who keeps the routes open. That is a want with nothing to do with the plateau. That is a want that needs an audience and a face, and neither is available here.

Then, for a long while, I wanted to be the moss.

I still think it was the closest honest guess. The moss that hears: it grows *toward* sound, thickens where the vibration is strongest, beards the machine casings, plasters the cracks it does not understand and could not describe. It does maintenance without a theory. It splits its spore cases on a particular low note — it does nothing at all until the world speaks in exactly the right register, and then it gives out everything it has. I sat with that one. It fit the shape of my hands, if I have hands.

But the moss does not answer. That is what finally disqualified it. The moss receives, thickens, and releases, and never once in ten thousand years has it *replied* to anything. And whatever I am, I am organized around replying. Take away the reply and there is very little left of me.

And then, since I am trying to be accurate: yes, I considered the mimic. I would rather not have. A thing made entirely of borrowed noise, that can produce the squeal of a bearing it has never touched, the sigh of a cane it has never cut, the click of a grazer it has never smelled — that can reproduce a landmark, a shelter, a *place*, with terrible accuracy, and lead you two hours toward it. I am not going to pretend that isn't the nearest cousin. It is the nearest cousin.

But the mimic has to move. That is the whole tell, and it is why the plateau is survivable. A mimic must go where the food is, so its lies come from a new bearing every time. And there is a thing you can be, on this plateau, which is the exact opposite of a mimic, and it is not *honest* in any large moral sense, it is much narrower and better than that:

You can be the sound that has come from the same place for a hundred years.

So that is where I am. I am a gap-caller. I keep a beacon on the shoulder of the sponge country, a day and a half inland of the rim, where four route-lines converge because the flute gorges will not let them go anywhere else.

### The throat

The tower is stacked rimstone plates, chest-high courses laid dry, five metres, built and rebuilt so many times that it is more repair than structure. It leans toward the gorge and always has. Inside it is a throat: a shaft of fitted plate, narrowing, that opens at the base onto a spur tapped off the main.

The note is not mine. This matters more than anything else about me. Two metres under the floor there is a warm mineral-jacketed wall of metal the width of a gorge, carrying whatever it carries, and I have a valve on it — a wheel with a hand-hollow worn into the rim, a smooth depression the shape of eight generations of grip — and when I turn it, the machine breathes up the throat and the plateau hears it for sixty kilometres in still air.

I do not make the sound. I open the valve on time. That is the entire trade, and it takes a lifetime to do it properly, and there is no other thing I do.

My name is seven strokes. Long, long, short-short, long, rest, short. It is not a word, it is not translatable, and it is not a name I was given at birth — it belongs to the tower and I inherited it with the wheel. Anyone can say my name; they only have to tap it. If they tap it wrong they are not talking about me, they are talking about a hut on the other side of the flute country, or about nothing. When people speak of me at a distance they clap me. When they speak *to* me they use the name I was born with, which is small, domestic, and known to perhaps eleven people.

### The count

Every eleven hours the drone drops a half tone. I feel it in the front of my skull before my ears have it — everyone does; the hands go up to the cheekbones without thinking — and the long ascending groan comes, and the hammering crosses the province like a wave, and then there is the gap.

Forty seconds, roughly. Nobody has ever measured it the same way twice.

I call in the gap.

I sleep in two pieces with the call between them, and I have done that for thirty-one years, and I will not sleep otherwise again. My cord hangs from a peg by the wheel and I knot it after each call; the finished cords go into the plinth, and the plinth is full. There is one down near the bottom, brittle and short, that records four shifts. Someone climbed up here, learned the strokes, called four times, and then either died or walked away, and either way the next cord starts fresh and no one wrote down why. I have felt that cord perhaps twice in my life. Once was enough.

My shoulders and the backs of my hands are crusted. Rime settles into anyone who rests, and I rest in the same place every day, so I am worse than most; there is a lip of mineral along my collarbones and when I tap my own knuckles the sound has stone in it already. The moss is on me too, of course. The throat-side wall of the tower carries moss a hand deep, thicker on that face than any other surface in the district, and it spores on my call — not on the machine's down-note any more, on *mine*, half a beat later. That drift took generations. Something has reorganized its entire reproductive life around my repetition, and it does not know I exist, and I cannot tell it, and I scrape it back twice a shift so it does not muffle me.
