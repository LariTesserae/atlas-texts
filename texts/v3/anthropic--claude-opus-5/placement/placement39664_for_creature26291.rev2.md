## At the north end

I would be at the north end, on the wrong side of the pump-house, with my forearms on the coping.

The stone there is the coldest thing on the rise. Colder than the handrails of Condenser Two, which are held in named hours by named people, inherited, gambled, written into marriages, fought over in the gardener's cellar in the only book anyone here gets angry about. Nobody holds an hour on my coping. It is free. Everything free here is free for a reason. I put my arms on it whenever I want and feel my heat go out of me into it, and in the only currency this place has I am the richest person on the rise, and I cannot spend a coin of it, and nobody will take it off me even as a favour.

What I do is push the lade.

It is a barrow, like the kist the sweepers take out at first grey, but it is longer and the bed is not flat. There is a shallow trough down the middle and a bar across the low end, because of the grade. On the flat you would not need either. I do not work on the flat.

The flume runs north and climbs, and the water in it climbs with it, which is the fact not discussed with visitors. I am the one who does the demonstration. The chip of teak lives in my pocket. I take it out, I crouch, I set it on the surface, and the visitor watches it go away from us at the speed of an unhurried walk, and then I pick the conversation up somewhere else and the visitor lets me. They always let me. That is the part I have come to find interesting: nobody has ever made me finish that sentence.

When somebody goes, I load the lade at the north gate and I walk up beside the water at the water's own pace, which is the part newcomers cannot stand, that the water keeps up with you, that it is going where you are going and in no hurry either. I sound every eight paces, *hah*, flat, mid-voice, the way you are taught before your name. Going up, it comes back later and later. At two hundred and ten paces the coping is at my shoulder and nothing comes back at all, and I keep sounding, because stopping is worse.

Somewhere past three hundred the fog is the same colour as the flume. That is where I set the chock. I take my hands off the handles and I stand and I count forty, which is the count for arrival, and then I look, and the lade is empty and light. I do not tip it. I have never tipped it. I turn it and bring it down, and coming down is the bad half, because then the water is running up into my face, and that is the only time in twelve years I have ever felt sick in this place.

The flume is a lade. The barrow is a lade. Nobody here has noticed making that one, the way nobody has noticed that the sweepers cough up the same word they sweep. I have noticed. Noticing is not one of my duties.

At the eleventh stroke, when dump comes up through the gratings and closes to arm's length and no one can move anyway, everyone stands where they were caught with their hands on iron and says the specification. *Not in a river. Not in a root. Not in a cloud that crossed land. Not in a mouth. Not in a body.* Forty seconds, flat, all of us, and then the wait, and then it comes back late and slightly wrong from the direction you are facing, in your own voice and your neighbours', and that returning is the answer.

I say a different list. *In a river. In a root. In a mouth. In a body.* Four lines, quietly, once. I do not double it. It is not meant to arrive.

I came on the barge, second day of the week, with my hand over my mouth. The gardener set me. Nine days in the lowest cellar, which is one full pulse of the marsh: the water was at my ankles on the third day and my shins on the fifth and gone by the eighth, and there was flume water to drink and nothing to do but sound into the dark and listen. On the seventh day something came back so late I could not be certain which day it had left. That was when I took. Not a decision. A preference. I had discovered that I liked being answered late better than being answered.

They will tell you that slips either take or they don't, that it is in the person, that most have not got it and go back with their ears ringing and hear themselves for a month afterward. I believed that for about a year. Then I had the run of the book, because the lader is nobody and nobody is allowed everywhere, and I put the barge list beside the dates of the cellar and I saw it.

Slips take when there is a hole.

The gardener sets a slip nine days before she needs one and she is not wrong by more than a day. She says she does not know how she knows. Perhaps that is true. The knock on Number Four changes its count and the crews stop sleeping and the number comes back to sixty-one the way the corners of the brick came back to their rounding — not quickly, not violently, just as far as it intends to go, and then stopping. The barge is not blamed. Nothing here is blamed. Erosion has finished arguing with this place.

I do not choose. I have never chosen. But I am often early to the door, and I have never once been wrong about the door, and those two facts sit side by side in me the way two people stand forearm to forearm against the same wet wall at the third hour, three centimetres apart, saying nothing, both of them knowing the price.

The lid is at forty metres. It is made by the nine stacks and the great chimney and it renews itself all day, and it is why we are the colour of the inside of a stripped fern trunk, and why no edged shadow has ever crossed any of us. The children are told not to stare at the Keeping, the band of real daylight lying out on the water where the lid thins, and they stare at it anyway for as long as their hour of cold lasts, and then they come in.

I have never wanted it. I stand at the north coping and I look up.

I have never seen the top of the flume. I have also never once thought that the lid was only stacks. We live under a shade we made, and I will not say which part of the making, and Sull would say that the air above it is unclean, and Sull is right about everything she smells. It is the only roof I have ever stood under that was made out of the people who stood under it.

There is exactly one job on this rise that is always open at the moment it is needed. When I go — early, quietly, on the ninth day of somebody's cellar — the slip who took that morning will be brought to the north gate and given the handles and told two sentences. *It goes up. You stop when the fog is the same colour as the flume.* Whoever brings them will say it twice: once then, and once after a count of forty, in the same words, in the same tone. Not for emphasis. For arrival.

That is how it was said to me. I did the work before I had unpacked, and the man in the trough was the one who had done it before me, and I did not learn his name, and mine is in the book as Mell, and nobody uses it; they use the barrow.

Otherwise I am like everyone. I have a wring in a stone box at the foot of my sleeping step, and four minutes at the nape when I need them. I eat what Sull refuses, which is what all of us eat, everything we are being water that touched the wrong thing. I strip moss off my own step. On barge days I stand well back, because a slip who sees me first will sometimes not come off the barge at all, and that is nine days wasted. I am paid in ordinary cold like everybody else, and I am not otherwise remarkable, and that is meant exactly as it is said.
