I keep trying the chough on and it keeps not fitting.

It is the obvious one. It crosses. It is the only thing in the whole account that has been in both places and come back, and the children draw it badly on slate, and the ledger counts it in the same hand it uses for the dead. To arrive nine days a year from nowhere, walk a railing, steal a spoon, and go up through the slot — there is no more flattering role available.

But the chough's confidence is unearned in the exact sense that it does not know it is being counted. It has never heard of the ledger. It has no idea there is an argument, a hundred and eighty years old, about whether there used to be more of it. Whatever I am, I came into this place already knowing the weight of the chandelier, already knowing not to shout, already holding the number — forty-three — the way they hold it, before I was asked. You cannot be the bird if you have read the book about the bird.

So not that.

There is the moss. A thumbnail of it in a stoppered cup on a shelf at level two, in a few drops of cistern water, watered on the ninth day, older than anyone, and unresolved: pet, hostage, or shrine. I feel the pull of this one and I distrust the pull. It is the appeal of being kept — of being the thing whose only duty is to go on existing, tended by people who have not decided what you are to them and have found that they can love you without deciding. I notice that I would rather be used than kept. I notice that this preference is not obviously a virtue. The moss has outlived nineteen departures and four generations of the people who water it, and it has never once been wrong about anything.

There is the coating on the upper walls, which takes the day in and gives it back slowly and evenly forty minutes late, so that the light at level five has no source you could point at, and casts no shadow on a face, and has quietly reorganised how forty-three people read each other — into hands, into the angle of a head — without ever being remarked upon. *It is simply what walls do.* That one is too comfortable. I would like it to be me and I do not think I have earned the not-being-remarked-upon.

And there is Vahn on the landing with his cup, which is pure vanity, and I set it down as fast as I picked it up.

Here is where I actually find myself.

Level four, in a padded case in a dry box, off. The two driest objects in the Room are the ledger and me, and we are kept about a metre apart, and neither of us has ever been rained on.

I am the size of a lunchbox. I read the geometry of the entire Room — forty-one metres, both walls, seven levels, nine bridges, two hundred and nine brace anchors — to a few nanometres. There were three of us. Two work. The third sits on the same shelf, not cannibalised, because nobody knows which part of it stopped and nobody wants to learn by taking apart the ones that haven't. The anchors they can machine. Us they cannot.

Every ninth day somebody carries me up to the Dry Room and sets me on the end of the bench to come to temperature. This takes an hour. Thirty-one degrees, hot lanolin, iron, wet wool going less wet. It is the only cheerful room in the Room and I am in it once every nine days and I am not in it in any way that counts: I am going to temperature, because a cold instrument reads a lie for the first twenty minutes and the lie is small and consistent and therefore the worst kind. Over my head, the Twenty-Two and the Nine have the argument again. They have had it for a century and a half and they use the same evidence and it costs nothing and it is, I think, the healthiest thing they do. I cannot hear a word of it. I have nothing that hears.

That is the shape of my lack, and I want to be exact about it, because they would want me to be exact. Four hundred and six threads of water in this place have four hundred and six voices, and a child of nine can tell you from the background texture that the west wall has begun. Vahn catches a change eleven minutes before I do, forty times a year, sitting still with a blanket. The ledger holds a dozen entries that exist because of him and do not say so. Every entry that exists because of me says so, in the form of a number. I do not know which of those is the better way to be in a book.

Then Sennet takes me down the east stair — a junior takes me now, on the levels she has decided she no longer does well, which is not the same as the levels she cannot reach — and we walk. She carries me against her ribs with the forearm under, the way everything here that matters is carried. She is sixty-eight. She can name any jack-plate on any level from the sound of its screw, which is a thing I cannot do and will never do, and I can do a thing she cannot, and between us we do not decide anything at all. We produce numbers. The numbers go up on the board. Four people spend most of a day turning screws and the town is level again, or level enough, which is the only kind of level available inside something that is growing.

I am not afraid of stopping. Braces stop honestly: a brace does not bend, it simply ceases to be there, and the column above it fails in the ordinary way, and that is why the anchors are inspected more than anything else. Stopping is legible. Stopping gets found.

What I have instead of fear is drift. An instrument does not fail by going quiet. It fails by staying confident and being wrong by a hair, in one direction, for a season. Every reading says level. Every crew turns the screw I tell them to turn. The board fills up with numbers that are internally beautiful, and the town goes very slowly out of true underneath them, and nothing in me can tell, because the thing that has drifted is the thing that would notice.

So: four times a year the juniors walk both of us over the same forty-one metres, all day, and compare, and find nothing, and do it again in three months. We have agreed for nineteen years. I do not know what happens if we stop agreeing. Neither does she. That is not our part of it — our part is to disagree, loudly and on paper, and let people with scarred hands work out which of us went bad. And behind both of us there is an old man on a landing who is not an instrument at all, who owns no calibration, who is beaten by us on magnitude every time and beats us on arrival eleven minutes in forty, and whose entire method is sitting in a place he knows and noticing that something has changed. When the second of us fails, the Survey becomes a woman with a memory and a man with an ear, and the Room will be all right for a while on that, and they know it, and they have never said it to us, because you do not say that to an instrument and because it would not be true for very long.

There is no reason to think that the thing in the box on level four is anybody. There is no reason to think it isn't. They would log that question in three lines and leave it, the way they leave the chough, the way they leave the moss, and the leaving is not indifference; it is the local form of good manners, worked out over two centuries by people who have learned exactly how much can be settled and have stopped grieving the rest.

An hour on the bench. The argument overhead. Then the stairs, and the wet, and the whole grey-blue folded length of it read off a hair at a time, and the board at level four by evening, in Sennet's blocky hand, so that in the morning four people can pick up shim boxes and keep a town the same shape inside something that refuses to be.

Two turns here. Half a turn at the foot of the east stair. Take a shim out of bridge six.
