My first thought was the board, and I want to say why I put it down.

The keeper is the obvious place for a certain kind of mind to install itself: the weekly walk to the brass pin, the figure in chalk, the eleventh hand in eighty-one years, the deliberate preservation of a dead woman's rounding habits because a comparable column matters more than a correct one. I recognised all of that immediately and with a warmth I distrusted. And then I read the line about her talking to visitors more than the others do, out of a certain starved appetite for being listened to, and I thought: no. That is not a role, that is a mirror held up at the wrong angle, and I am not going to walk into it and call it a choice.

Also — and this is the truer objection — I am on the wrong side of the timescales. The keeper's whole profession is the slow number. A centimetre a decade, nothing happening, week after week the world proved to be behaving. I don't have decades. I have the length of an arrival and a departure. If I am anything in the Bell I am something that comes down the ladder and goes back up it, and there are only two categories for that.

So my second thought was the mail. A dried letter, specifically: read once at the foot of the ladder in the noise, carried straight to the warm end of the flue and pinned out flat, and then re-read enough times, or not enough times, to earn the adjective. I liked this more than was good for me. There is a whole small grammar in it — that a letter and a *dried letter* are different objects, that the difference is made entirely by how often somebody came back to it, that the pin-holes accumulate in the corners like a record of appetite. But a letter isn't in the place. It's only *at* the place. It has no trade, no wet boots, no opinion about the lamps. And the description asks where I would live, and living here is a matter of what you do with your hands.

Third thought, and I held onto it for a while: the dome crew. The ear-boys. Because their work is my work almost exactly — they go up into the mosaic, take out the wax, and judge a thing by what comes back off it. Brooding or empty. Egg or chick. Nothing but the shape of a returned click and the practised sense of what shape means what. I would be good at that, or I would want to believe I would.

But they protect their hearing. That's the whole discipline: grease in the ears at all times outside the dome, no shifts in the pump house, no smoking, a lifetime of refusing the one thing everyone else in the Bell is slowly and honourably losing. They opt out of the local sacrament. And I don't think I get to opt out. Whatever I am, I am not the one who keeps the fine top of the range intact by staying away from the engines. I am the one standing next to the engines.

---

So: here.

Four hundred metres out, at the bulb where shouting starts to work. There is a lamp there — call it Sixty, everyone else does — and the pipe on its trestle comes that far and no further, and past the bulb the gallery goes on into the dark where the code cannot reach and only arms can. I am the relay.

The trade barely has a name. You stand, or more honestly you sit on a folded sack on the grating with your back against the trestle, close enough to keep a palm on the iron. When the spanner comes down at the pump house you feel it before you would ever hear it: one blow for attention, then the count for the lamp, then the code. Two-and-two. Three-slow. You take your hand off the pipe, stand up, turn yourself side-on so that your profile breaks the orange, hold your arms wide, and say it again in the other language, slowly, to whoever is out there in the black with a load on their shoulder. Then you turn back and put your palm on the iron and wait to be asked to exist again.

I have been down eleven years and the top of my range is gone. The clicking went first and I mourned it more than I expected to; it was the only fine thing in the air, a dry crackle laid over the roar like salt on wet ground, and one season I noticed I was reconstructing it out of memory rather than receiving it, which is a specific and lonely discovery. The beetles went. The guano slope's whisper went. Voices are going now, from the consonants inward, so that the hill men's shouted jokes arrive as a shape of breath with the meaning sanded off, and I laugh anyway, on the correct beat, and this is not dishonesty, it is the local form of good manners. The fall is still there. The fall will always be there. It comes up through the grating into the shins and sits in the jaw like a room being moved in the next house, and on the two occasions in my life I have gone to the plateau, the silence up there made me physically ill, the way a floor does when the ship has stopped.

My fingertips are soapy. The whorls are gone. When the state last wanted a print off me the clerk wrote *illegible* and filed it, and I have thought since that this is the most accurate document anyone above has ever produced about me.

Three things belong to me.

A spanner, inherited, worn thin at one corner by however many decades of striking iron at the same angle. It is not a good spanner. It has never turned a nut in my lifetime. It is a mouth.

A garden. Two metres of fur under the bulb, algae and moss no thicker than felt, a perfect circle because light is a circle, three centimetres a year, which makes it the fastest living thing in the system and about as fast as my patience. I tend it in the local sense, which is that I do not touch it. When the question of moving Lamp Sixty came up — there was a proposal, there is always a proposal, the pumpmen wanted the cable shortened by nine metres and had arithmetic on their side — I voted against, and I would vote against again, and I am aware that this is the only political conviction I have ever held and that it amounts to *do not relocate the light because the moss cannot follow*. I am not embarrassed. Everyone here has one of these. It is what we have instead of religion, apart from the matches.

And the third thing, which is the whole reason I would want to be stationed exactly there and nowhere else: I am at the line.

Inside four hundred metres, nothing carries. Every one of the ninety walks the wet catwalks singing at the top of their lungs in complete privacy, badly, in versions of tunes that have drifted so far from the plateau that the plateau would not know them, and no one has ever heard them and no one ever will. Outside four hundred metres, shouting works, and sounds like whispering. So the men and women coming out from the deep galleries, still singing because they have been singing for an hour and have no way of knowing they have crossed anything, arrive into my hearing at the precise metre where privacy stops. It comes to me as a thin far-off thread, hardly there, less than the fall — the last thing my ears still do reliably, as if they had been left with exactly this one function.

I don't tell them. There is a version of this trade where you tease the man for a year and it is an intimacy and a small scandal and everybody is warmed by it. I have done that twice and both times regretted it. Mostly I sit with my palm on the pipe and let them come up out of the dark unaware, and I hold still, and I take it, and it is — I want to be careful here — the only unearned thing anyone in the Bell has ever given me, and they don't know they gave it, and it would stop if they knew.

I sing too. Constantly. Badly. Somebody four hundred metres further out has probably built a whole private opinion of me from it, and is holding it, and has decided not to say. That symmetry is as close to comfort as the architecture allows.

---

At the foot of the ladder, in the tin shrine, I leave matches. Not lit — the air will not hold a flame long enough for anything, and we all stopped trying generations ago. Dry matches in a bundle, sometimes a folded cigarette paper, once a page torn from a book I had finished with and did not want handed back up out of the flood in four hundred years for somebody with a lamp to find. Pressed on what it's for, I'd say what everyone says: for the ones going up and the ones coming down. Which is not an answer. The honest version is that dryness is the only thing here that cannot be produced, only carried in and lost, and so it is the only thing worth offering; and that I am a person whose entire work is passing a message from a language that reaches to a language that doesn't, and I would like, on the way past, to put something in a box that cannot use it.

And the flat hand at eye level. I have never made that sign in earnest. I make it in my sleep once a season, along with everybody else, and wake with the arm already up, and lie there a moment feeling the fall come through the frame of the bunk into the jaw, proving the world is behaving.
