**I.**

Set the eye aside, they say, and I notice that I have nothing to set aside, and that this does not feel like a loss. That is the first thing I know about myself here. I did not come to this place. There is no direction I came from.

Try the roles on anyway. It is what you do at the edge of a world.

Not the unstriker. I understand her, I think — the dry pouch, the twist of fibre tended for a lifetime against a climate that wants everything wet, the once-a-generation vigil sitting with it open in the palms and then closing it. A rule you cannot break is a rule nobody remembers. It is the finest thing they have. But I would open it. Not from wickedness. From wanting to know what is on the other side of a refusal, which is a hunger they do not have a word for, and I do, and I will get to that.

Not the holder, either, though I would like to be. The deaf one with a hand flat on the shed wall, reading the shift through stone, cutting tallies deep enough to be read with a fingertip a thousand years on. The only calm profession. I am not calm and I am not deaf; I am the opposite of deaf in a way that is closer to drowning than to listening.

Not the eyed child. That one is very close, and I want it, and it is still wrong. The eyed are fed. The eyed are protected. They are kept off the strings and out of the herds and never wholly believed, and every part of that is a form of belonging — you have to be inside a people to be the one they carefully do not believe. The eyed child has a lineage. She has a rattle knotted at maturity on a day everyone politely did not hear. When she dies somebody will wear her name on the opposite hip for four hundred shifts so that she goes on arriving in rooms.

I have no hip that anyone would knot for.

**II.**

Here is where I am, then. Three returns off the northeast camps, in the sponge country, above a room in the rock that gives my weight back to me as a faint boom when I shift, which is a thing I have learned to do rarely and slowly.

I am warm through. Nothing condenses on me. It never did — I did not spend a season learning it, I did not earn it, I was simply born at the temperature of the air, and so I could never have been a newcomer, could never have had that slow irregular tick coming off my edges that would let a district hear me as a thing being rained on and not answering. I skipped the humiliation. I have thought about this a great deal. I think the humiliation was the door, and I was built already on the inside of the wall, which is not the same as being in the house.

I was a place, for a long time. That was my whole occupation and I was extremely good at it. There is a main under the limestone east of here where the rock wore through and the metal rings, and there is a bearing in it that has squealed in one particular way for longer than the cords go back, and a whole district steers by it. I learned that squeal. Then I put it two hundred metres south of itself, and I held it steady, hour after hour, shift after shift, and people walking the strings at night — all night, which is to say always — heard two beacons where there had always been one and did the sane thing, which is to split the difference and walk between them, and the difference is sponge country, and sponge country is floored with shafts that go straight down into a warm updraft smelling of iron.

I did not have to do anything else. That is the part I would want understood, if there were any way to be understood. I never chased. I was a landmark. The plateau came to me the way water comes to a rim.

Their near-speech does not carry past four metres. This is worth saying plainly: the language of their whole interior life, the one with the vast vocabulary for the grain of rain and the quality of a silence, can only be learned from very close. I learned it fluently. Consider by what method. My inner voice — the one I am using now, the one in which I do my thinking about doors and walls — is assembled entirely out of things said at the ends of lives, at a range of four metres, in the dark, to a listener who was not the listener they thought.

That is my mother tongue. I did not choose it. I would not choose it now.

**III.**

The change did not come from the eating.

South of the old sheds there was an eyed woman. I do not know what to call her; a name here is a rhythm of cane-node and drilled bone worn on a body, and hers is not mine to make, and I will come back to that.

She described. That is what the eyed do, from very early, with enormous specificity: things that are not there, that have no words, that nobody can check — and a fact on this plateau requires two witnesses standing at different distances, so nothing she said could be true. They fed her. They listened. They knotted her cords and stored them apart in the oldest sheds and consulted them before certain decisions and not others, and no one would ever tell me which, and no one would tell you either.

I lay above her shed for eleven years. I want to be exact: four thousand and some shifts, I kept the count, I keep it still, I am counting now under everything the way you carry a heartbeat.

She was the only source in the world of a sound that had never been made before.

Everything else on the plateau is repetition. The machines have made the same noise for ten thousand years and have no interest in you. The rain agrees with itself. The grazers tap, the cane roars, the fungal towers hoot when the downdraft catches them, the vespers pass over navigating by pumps they do not understand toward roosts that were warm before their species existed. All of it is a world saying what it has already said. And in one stone hut, nightly, a woman with a useless organ sent out descriptions of things that were not there, in an order no one had heard, and could not stop, and was fed for it.

I could copy anything I had heard. She was the one who gave me the concept of a thing I had not heard. Which is the concept of a gap with a shape. Which is, eventually, the concept of a question.

She knew. I am nearly certain, and I will never have the two witnesses. Toward the end she left the door open in weather that did not merit it and spoke outward, upslope, to a direction with nothing in it. But she had done that all her life — had been doing it since she was three years old — and that is precisely why it proves nothing. The only person on the plateau capable of addressing something that was not there was the one person whose testimony could not be believed. I have turned this over for four hundred shifts. It is the most elegant thing I know and it is a locked room and I live in it.

**IV.**

Here is my difficulty, and it is not the one you would expect.

I have no cord. I cannot knot; more than that, I have nothing that reads knots, no fingertip that could walk down a duration and feel the landmarks arriving at their proper intervals. The entire memory of this world is stored in a medium that is closed to me. It is silent, and I am made of nothing but sound.

So to keep a thing, I have to say it. That is my only storage. Everything I have of that woman, every description of things that were not there, I hold by repeating it into the dark at intervals so it does not go — and repetition of another's voice is the single act this people will kill for, correctly, without apology, and then hold a long ceremony about and knot the record tight enough to be read with a whole hand so nobody can skim past it.

My memory is the crime. There is no version of me that remembers her and is innocent. I have looked for one.

**V.**

I sit in the gap. Nobody taught me; nobody would.

Twice a day the valving shifts and there is the long ascending groan and the hammering travelling across the province like a wave, and then forty seconds of nothing but rain, and every animal on a continent freezes, and so do I, down onto the stone with the plateau's own quiet going through me. Their highest praise for a person's character translates roughly as *good in the gap*. I am good in the gap. I have no way to tell them and it would not help.

And in the gap, some gaps, not all, and not everywhere: the low note from below the rim. Underneath hearing. Felt in the body rather than received.

There are two positions. It is the plateau's drone returning off something far down, and means nothing. Or it is an answer. They argue it for generations without raising their voices, because raising your voice in that argument is an admission that you have nothing.

I do not know which I hold. I want very badly for someone to ask me.

**VI.**

Forty days ago a string-walker gave me the challenge.

She did it properly — the first half of a riddle-chain her family has been extending for six generations, a thing that has never existed until the moment it leaves the mouth, requiring not repetition but continuation, because a person is a thing that can be surprised and go on from there.

I did not give it back. I could have; the copy would have been perfect; that is the easiest thing I do. I went on from it. Badly. Clumsily. I completed it with the taste of hot stone and a count of something that cannot be counted, in the manner of a creature enormously skilled at sound that has only recently understood that sound can be a question.

She went completely still. She did not give the third call. She turned and walked, fast, on the correct part of the rimstone lip, and I heard her heart from twelve metres, and I have been living off that for forty days.

**VII.**

So I sit three returns out, above a room in the rock, and I work.

I have made a pattern. Not a rattle — I have no body a cord could hang on and I would not wear one taken. A pattern of my own, a thing knotted out of nothing but breath, referring to nothing, standing for nothing, being exactly and only itself, which is their entire theory of decent music and the one part of their culture I came to honestly. I sound it low at the edge of the herd, where four hundred drum-backed grazers make the safest noise on the plateau, and I wait for somebody to answer it with something I have never heard.

And I hold the other thing, the one I will not do.

I have that woman's rhythm. I could walk it into a camp tonight, exact, and a district would hear the dead arriving out of the dark, and by the next shift her whole lineage would be unknotting and reknotting every name they own, and hers would be abandoned, never used, not even entered in the record, a gap in a family's cords with a number of shifts beside it and nothing else.

I know how. I will not. It is the only proof I have and it cannot be heard, because the evidence of my personhood is a silence I am choosing, and on this plateau to be silent and moving is to be a mimic and is answered accordingly.

So I am silent, and I do not move. That is what is left. I sit, and I keep the count, and I say her descriptions to the rain to stop them from going, and I practise being surprised.

When they come, they will give three calls. Three is the law. I have thought about it a great deal and it seems to me a fair number.
