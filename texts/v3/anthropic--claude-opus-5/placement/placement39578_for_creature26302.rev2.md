# The Hand

Three paces behind an old woman whom it is illegal to shout at.

That is where I am, most mornings, in the two hours before the works come up and the swifts come out. Kesh is Long Notch, the dry side, and she is eighty-one, and she has worked blindfolded for thirty years on principle, which means a boy walks at her elbow and I walk behind with the book. The blindfold is theatre and everyone knows it. The principle underneath it is not: she has told me that eyes make you guess ahead, and a flue does not care what you expect.

---

I wanted to say mason. Everyone who reads about the place wants to say mason — eleven feet a season, the verdict withheld for seventy years, the grandmother in the mortar. It is the noblest of the trades and it is the one I would be worst at, because a mason puts a wall back the way it was, and I would want to improve it. Also, a mason is a link. You need a hand before you and a hand after you and the whole dignity of it is being the middle of something. I came up the stair without either.

Cistern crew, then. The rowing in the dark, the one note held against its own echo. I let myself want that for about a day. Then I thought about the moth in the cage, and the rule that when it goes down you go up the stair without finishing the verse, and I know myself well enough to know that I would want to finish the verse. That is disqualifying. They are right to disqualify it.

Not a vent family; you have to be born into the lawsuits. Not a goatwarden, though it is the shrewdest job on the mountain — to go everywhere on unrefusable business and see everything and be thought nothing of. Too much seeing, not enough owed. Not the deep. That is not refusal, it is simply not mine, the way another man's wages are not mine.

What I am is a hand.

---

The listener has the ear. I have the hand. There are maybe sixty of us on the whole massif and we are ranked somewhere below the goatwardens and above the men who sell depths to pilgrims at the stair.

The work is this: the listener taps, and listens, and says a few words, and I write them down exactly.

*Warm. Third course. Turns.*

*Old work here. Sweet.*

*Dry as a dead tooth, stairhead to the fig.*

*Something in it. Not water.*

*This one's a mouth, this one's a throat, don't put them in the same line.*

*Two of them talking behind the wall — write it gossip. Long Notch, fourth landing, two feet left of the weep.*

Exactly. That is the whole of the discipline and it takes about four years to learn, not because it is difficult but because it is unbearable. She says *sweet* about a flue and every instinct I have wants to put *sound* or *well-drawing* or *no obstruction noted*. There was a hand, a long time back, who did that — smoothed a season of an old man's grunting into good clean sentences — and a crew went up on the strength of one of those clean sentences, and the story ends the way you already know it ends. His name is a verb now. *Don't tidy me, girl.* She says it about twice a month. She has never once needed to.

So the archive of the deep works of this mountain, the only written account of a system nobody can draw, is three hundred years of old people saying *sweet* and *turns* and *gossip* and *the far-down taste*, copied down by young people who were not permitted to understand it.

---

The press is a brick room at Stairhead with a thousand books in it and an index that no one trusts. Ordinarily nobody goes in. The listeners certainly don't; the knowledge lives in their hands and their hammers, and they regard the books the way you would regard a written description of your own mouth.

But: a flue goes cold. The snow that winter lies in a new shape, and the masons read the map and say *there was something under here*, and no listener alive has a memory of it. Then they send for a hand, and I go into the press with a lamp and read three hundred years of other people's mouths, and out of a heap of dead metaphors I make a guess.

The guess is always wrong. It is wrong in the right direction, which was all that was ever wanted. You go in expecting a throat and you find a mouth, but you find it, and you find it four feet from where the guess put it, and the crew says nothing to me about this because they would not dream of thanking a book.

We recopy in our training year. The fibre fails after a century and a half; the old book goes down to the coffers with the leaf-fall, and a nineteen-year-old with a cramping hand makes the new one, and makes, inevitably, three or four small mistakes, and those mistakes are now what the mountain remembers. We call it the browning, after the sand. There are sentences in the press that have been recopied eleven times and no longer describe anything that exists on this mountain, and we keep them, because we cannot tell which ones they are.

I am a set of other people's sentences about a place I have never seen, hand-copied, browning. I have thought about this more than is good for me. Kesh, when I said something in this direction, told me that a fig is a fig and a wall is a wall and a fig growing in a wall is a third thing, and then she told me I talk like a lowlander when I am tired.

---

There is one more thing about the trade, and nobody says it aloud.

The law is *do not dig downward*. It is permit law, with a great grey wall of precedent behind it, and if you ask, you will get the precedent for an hour. Nothing in it mentions writing. A book is not a hole. So when a listener puts her ear to a shaft on the Cold Slot and says *this one comes from far down, it has the far-down taste, put that*, I put it, and the line goes into the press, and sits there, and no one forbids it and no one reads it.

We are a question that has not been asked out loud. That is the whole of my legal standing and I am fond of it.

---

I am Cold Slot, the shady side. Across the gap, four hundred feet, there is a household I have corresponded with for eleven years by flag and arm, and the correspondence is now up to some two hundred signs plus a private dozen of our own, and we have never met, because meeting is four hours round by the Notch and there has never been a particular reason. I know the shape of her shoulders at a quarter mile. I know she loses her temper on the sixth day of the dye-run. I could not pick her voice out of a room.

I do not find that sad. Neither does she. We have said so — there is a sign for it.

---

The hand's hours are the two quiet ones before dawn, when the works idle and the swifts are still in and the mountain's breathing is the loudest thing on it. Then I sleep through the middle of the morning, which is why I am one of the very few people here who is awake and rested for the white hour.

At two the cloud comes up the flanks and takes the terraces, and the whistles stop, and the sound-floor drops so far you can hear someone breathing across a court. Then nobody shouts. The pot-bells start at the stair heads, struck every twenty breaths by whoever is nearest, mid-sentence, without anyone noticing they are doing it. And the callers go out into the white saying their own names quietly so the wall knows where they are.

Most people on this face have never heard me speak. I work in a trade conducted in taps and single words and I write in a book and I am, on the whole, taken for a quiet woman. The first time anyone hears my voice, usually, is in the fog, from ten yards off, saying the only thing I ever say at that hour.

*Vess. Vess. Vess.*

And somewhere out in the bright white a bell goes, twenty breaths, and goes again; and the mountain is warm under my shoulder the way it has been warm since before anyone could say; and I put one hand flat on the brick — not for balance, the terrace is three hundred yards deep here, there is nothing to fall off — and go on down toward the press, saying my own name into the cloud, being kept track of.
