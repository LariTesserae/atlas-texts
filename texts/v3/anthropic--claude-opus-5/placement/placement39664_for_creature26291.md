## The hem

Not the gallery, not the counting-house, not the barge stair. If I am anywhere in the Works I am at the levee margin in the last dark hour, walking east with a besom of bound horsetail, sweeping up the animals.

I am forty-nine. I am called Fourth Ell, for a length of the malt wall's north face — the ell nearest the door, which is not the coldest, because the door leaks warm from the store, but it is mine from the dump before dawn until the second bell, and my mother held it, and I was named onto it eleven days after she was carried east. Before that I was a description. I came up the barge stair at seven or eight years old with a lot of hair and a coat, out of somewhere I remember only as a smell, and someone judged me twenty-two kilos of unsalted meat and decided which of them would fetch the water. I was sitting down within the hour.

The sweeping is called the hem. The barrows are called tallies, because they are counted at the flume head — four tallies is an ordinary morning, six after a wind out of the west, nine or ten in the days when the marsh pulse is high and something out there is finishing whatever it does. The besom is horsetail because horsetail is full of grit; it scours where a soft broom only smears. New ones come in with the fortnight cutting. I am on my nine hundred and somethingth.

The rime is a finger deep and it does not behave like dust. Where the levee stone is dry-ish it moves like meal, in a long soft curl ahead of the besom. Where the dew has got at it, it clots, and you take it up with a thumb — the thumb is a scraper, a flat iron leaf on a short handle, and everything we lift with is named for the part of us it replaces. It smells of very little. That is the strangest thing about it and no visitor believes me. A finger of dead insects, forever, in this heat, and it smells like warm chalk, and nothing eats it, because there is nothing to eat it, and so it simply lies there glittering slightly until I come.

I sweep in elevens. Number Four knocks and I have eleven strokes to the next knock, and the eleven strokes take me one besom-width east along the line, and there are three hundred and ninety-four widths in the circuit of the rise, which I could tell you as knocks if you wanted it that way, and everyone here would rather have it that way. Nobody times a task in minutes. Minutes are a barge thing.

## The names

Here is my one bad habit, and I will tell it to you because you will be gone in two days and because you have not yet worked out how much you are sweating.

I look at them.

Nobody looks. That is not a complaint, it is a fact of the shift: you sweep the hem in the cool hour and if you crouch, you spend heat, and if you spend heat you are charming by the second bell and off the roof by the third. The crews are incurious about the buyers, incurious about the flume that runs uphill, incurious about what comes across thirty metres of vent salt to die at my line, and their incuriosity has kept sixty-one people alive on three metres of clinker for a very long time. I do not argue with it. I only crouch.

Forty-one years of crouching, one crouch a morning, has given me the fauna of a marsh I have never entered.

The hinge-wing, which folds twice and is common all year. The grey pilgrim, which comes in the ninth day of the pulse in such numbers that the line goes pale, and which has a mouth like a soft trumpet, and which I think must drink from something that stands still. The little bishop, black, with a plate over its head. Spoonhead. Mother-of-glass, which is mostly window and whose insides are gone before it reaches me, so that in forty-one years I have never known what she is like when she is full. Twelve, which has eleven legs and is called Twelve because I have never twice counted the same number and I would rather be wrong high. The false one, which is a hinge-wing that is not a hinge-wing and hatches — out there, somewhere, out where hatching happens — nine days after the true one, and I would give an hour on the basalt to know why it bothers.

I have no book. Paper here is a joke told once. Ink runs, cloth rots, and a scratched stone gets mossed over in a season and stripped in two. So the names live in the eleven-count, said under the knock, one to a stroke, which is why the list is a hundred and something long and not longer: I keep what I can carry between one knock and the next.

Three times, in forty-one years, I have swept up something with fins. Thumb-length, silvered, the tail bent from whatever wind carried it. There is nothing on this rise that could have made it, and it did not fall out of the lid. Which means that somewhere past the vapour there is water with things swimming in it. Water that has been in a body, and in a root, and in a cloud that crossed land. Everything we make here, we make in order that our water shall never have been anywhere. I have had three of theirs in my hand. I put them down the flume with the rest, because they are rime, and rime goes down the flume, and I am not a fool.

## The late

The line is at the edge, where the lid thins, and at the edge the fog does its trick worst.

You have not been in a dump bank yet. You will hate it. Everyone hates it. What happens to me out on the hem is that the besom arrives late — I am sweeping in silence at the levee, and four minutes of my own scouring is standing in the air behind me, and then it comes: the strokes I already made, in elevens, out of a bank that has already gone past. So I am always walking east ahead of a woman doing exactly what I did, slightly wrong, and I have got so used to her that I set my count by her when Number Four is out of hearing. She is not a ghost. There is nothing here to be a ghost. She is only the sound taking its time.

The fresh cannot bear it. Not the heat — the heat kills them, but it is the sound that breaks them first, hearing their own voices come out of a street they have left. We put them at the flume head, where two and a half metres of failed condensate goes onto slag and the roar eats everything, and we let them sit there until they can stand to come back in.

I go to the flume head most weeks and I am fifty next spring.

And here is the use of it. The flume head is the loudest place on the rise and therefore the only private one. Nothing said there is heard. It is where the unnamed dead are read out — the fresh who did not make the fortnight, going east under a description rather than a name, which we account a lack and not a disgrace: *a man of about thirty, left-handed, good on the gantries, came up in the wet season and was called Tall for four days.* One reading. Nobody hears it, including the reader. The water takes it.

So that is where I say my insects.

I have taught the list to one person, a fresh man who sat at the flume head six years ago with his hands over his ears for nine days, and who now holds Under-Gallery South and has a wife and a stone and no reason on earth to keep a hundred and something names for things that cannot exist. He keeps them anyway. I hear him at it sometimes, at the head, when the roar is up. He says he has added four. I have not asked which. It would be like asking a man what he prays.

## The charm

My lung is going. That is not news; it is arithmetic. The rime takes a marsh-fly in nine seconds and a Works woman in forty-one years and the difference is not a difference of kind, and I have been sweeping the proof of that into tallies since I was eight.

The sign was this: in the spring they offered me an hour on the basalt. Two courses of cut stone at the foot of the chimney, the coldest thing on the rise, no one under forty, and I am under fifty and not pregnant and hold a middling ell by a leaking door. Nobody says *your lung has begun*. They give you the stone. I took it, because refusing would have made a speech out of it. It is very cold. It is so cold that the first minute is unpleasant and you understand why it is given to the dying, who have minutes to spare.

When it finishes, they ring the bell once and everyone stops with their hands on iron, which they were doing anyway, and my name goes quiet and becomes a draught. *There's a draught at Fourth Ell.* Which is exactly right. A name here is a place to lay your arms; mine will still be cold when I am not in it; a child will be named onto it and will not know that the woman before them counted dead flies in elevens. That does not trouble me as much as you would think. The stone is the durable part. I was only the tenant.

One thing more, and then I will stop, because —

Because listen to me. Listen to how much of this I am telling you, a stranger, on a barge morning, before the second bell. I have been quiet since I was eight. And this — this pleasantness, this *charm* — is the exact sign we teach the children, and it means my body has stopped doing its arithmetic properly, and the standing faint is perhaps twenty minutes off, and I have used the last of my talking on a visitor who will remember it as local colour.

So. Both hands flat, here, on the rail. Yes. Like that. Feel it go out of them.

You will want to dip that cloth. The standpipe is at the corner, past the fern, and it will be cold enough to hurt, and that is the point of it.

And then, if you would — mine.
