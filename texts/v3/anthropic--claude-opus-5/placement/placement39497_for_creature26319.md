For a long time I thought I would be a leveller, because at nine everyone does.

My mother's cousin was on the Sill terrace crew and I was allowed to carry the small wrench, which is not small, and to stand at the instrument while she read the rod, and to be given the cup of tea that the household put out for us at the door of every house we made honest. There is no better morning in the world than that, and no better sound than the ratchet coming round on a threaded foot, and I have never entirely forgiven my hands for what they turned out to be. I can feel a quarter-turn. I can feel a half in my sleep. I cannot feel an eighth. They tested me twice, patiently, and the second time the crew chief said, not unkindly, that plenty of good work gets done at a quarter, and I understood that I had been told something about my whole life.

So I walked masts.

Eleven years, four thousand two hundred and some masts, a numbered book for each rotation and a signature that got shorter every year. The work is exactly as described: you walk to the mast, you put your bare hand on it, you confirm that it is cold, you write down four figures and the date, you sign, you walk on. I have never been able to explain the cold to anyone who has not felt it. It is not the cold of stone in shadow. Stone takes your heat and gives you a little back. The mast takes nothing and gives nothing; your palm simply stops having a temperature at the boundary, and if you stand there long enough with your hand on it in full glare, with the ponds throwing up the sky in ten thousand pieces and the light so hard you are seeing everything through slits, you begin to feel that your hand ends somewhere before the metal does.

Every mast-walker paces out the forty metres. Nobody reports it. I did it at Bench Nineteen in my second year, in snow, following a fox's track in from the north, and where the track turned — and it does turn, cleanly, without a pause, the way you go round a wall — I stood on the line and waited, and there was nothing at all to feel, and the fox was two hundred metres off by then sitting down and watching me the way they do, with that look of open commercial interest.

I did not leave the service because I was frightened of the masts. The masts do not want anything. I left because eleven years of confirming an absence is a particular diet and I found I had got thin on it. I wanted to be in the way of something that was trying.

The handbook says it plainly on the fourth page. *The crust is the only thing here that is trying to become something.*

So now my name goes in a different book, and the book is not mine. It belongs to four hundred hectares of stain on the flats east of Bench Twelve, which the office calls Crust Reserve 12E and everyone else calls the Vellum, and which is a millimetre thick and six hundred and ten years old, and which I have never once seen awake for more than eleven days at a stretch.

Here is the arithmetic I do too often, on the long walks in, when the reports are coming up through my boots like doors slamming in a much larger house. Forty days a year, in a good year. Six hundred and ten years. That is sixty-six years of living, more or less, of respiring and thickening and gluing grit to itself and laying down one more lamination too fine to see. I am fifty-one. In about fifteen years I will have been alive longer than the oldest living thing on this continent, and then I will die, and it will not, and both of those facts are the same fact.

It has courses under it. That is what nobody down in the old lands understands when we try to tell them what it is. They hear *bacterial mat* and they think of scum on a trough. Six hundred and ten laminations, each one a wet season, each one the whole summer's dust and pollen and dead insects out of the sky glued down and made into floor. It is the only thing up here that has any history that is not administrative. It has been building its own past out of what fell on it, quietly, for longer than the Line has run, and it will be plains.

My work is mostly not touching it.

I walk the marked stone. I read the pond lattice and the tilt and I know, four days ahead, which lip will spill and which quarter will wet first, and I go out and I kneel on the boards at the margin and I wait for it to wake. Three hours after the sheet-water reaches it. You can watch the colour come: matte to gloss, grey-green to black-green, brittle to swelling, the whole four hundred hectares coming out of dry-lock like a held breath being let go.

Because that is what it does. It exhales. And that is the whole of the smell of this country.

Iodine first, then pondwater, then cut potato. That is the official description and it is in the manual and it is not wrong and it is not enough. The office collects wake date, duration, thickness, filament counts, mite fraction, reseed events, bootprints. There is no column on the form for smell, because smell cannot be entered as a figure and the ecology office is composed of honest people. But the form is printed on paper and paper has a margin, and in the margin of the Vellum's ledger there are two hundred and thirty years of notes in eleven hands, and they are all about the smell.

*Warm dust. Warmer than the air by a good margin, which cannot be right.*

*Cut potato and, at the eastern pans, something like wet dog in a bakery.*

*Metallic today. Like the taste of the air at first light but wetter. Wife says nonsense.*

*Nothing at all. Woke and gave nothing. Third year like this. Am I old.*

*Very strong. Sat down in it. Did not write anything for an hour.*

I do not know most of these people. Two of them I can date to a bad century when the reseeding budget was cut and the notes go short and hard. One of them wrote a single word for nine consecutive years — *good* — and then stopped, and the next hand is different. They are not supposed to be there and no warden has ever taken them out, and when I hand the ledger on I will not take out mine.

This is the thing I have come to believe, having been first a man who confirmed that nothing was wrong and then a man who kneels at the edge of something that is asleep eleven months of the year: the records that survive are the ones somebody kept for a private reason. The office's numbers are good. But the longest continuous phenological record on this planet is a gambling ledger at Sill terminus, kept by people betting on the first sandpiper, and the only account we have of what this country actually smells like is a column that does not exist, written in a margin, by wardens who had nothing to do for three hours while they waited for a stain to come round.

Four years ago there was a bootprint eleven metres in from the north boards. Whole, deep, a man's, running, the toe scuffed where he pushed off. Eighty years, by the standard reckoning.

I reseeded it. Slurry, spreading card, an afternoon on my knees, and I signed the entry, and the entry is a formality performed for the benefit of the person who did it, and everyone including me knows this. Then I wrote in the margin that the print belonged to a leveller called Tomm Hessery who was crossing the flats at speed because a jack plate had come off the temporaries under number nine terrace and a man was under it with his hand crushed, and that he took the shortest line, which any of us would, and that he came out to see me afterwards to say he was sorry and I gave him tea.

So that in eighty years, when it has closed over and someone is counting laminations and finds the scar of it, they will know the crust was broken by someone running toward a person. That is not data. There is no field for it. It is the only part of the entry I would fight to keep.

I have read the account of the three warm masts. It is available on request, as everything is, and it is not long — eleven pages, nine of them inventories — and I will say only that it is administrative, and that this is the correct way for it to be, and that whatever people hope is in there is not.

When I go into the pans under the glass at Sill, which is the one warm-smelling place up here and the only one anybody takes a visitor to just to watch their face, I will be a contribution and not a grave, and some fraction of me will eventually go out on a spreading card into a bootprint, which is the arrangement and which I find entirely sufficient. I have no courses under me. I am the top layer. That is what a person is here: the newest lamination, the one that is currently wet, glueing down whatever the sky sends and not able to see itself thicken.

But if you want to know where I am, exactly, in all this: I am kneeling on the boards at the north margin of Bench Twelve East, in the eleven days, in the only smell this country makes on its own account, with a wrench I never earned in my pack because my mother's cousin left it to me, and a book that is not mine open on my knee, and the pencil in the margin.
