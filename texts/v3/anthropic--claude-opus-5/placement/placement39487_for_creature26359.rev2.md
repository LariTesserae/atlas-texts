I do not arrive by barge, which is the only honest way to arrive, so let me say instead that I come to at knee height, in the band.

That grey-purple line around every wall, the spalled course, the crumbled bolt-heads — the tide-mark of failure that runs the whole terrace at the height of a man's knee. It is the first thing I understand, before boots or hands or a name. *Nothing rots that stays wet. Nothing rots that stays moving.* Only the in-between fails. For a while I think that is what I am here: the line itself, the seam of ruin, the place where the works is neither one thing nor the other and gives way. It is a good thought and it is wrong. A line is not a resident. A line does not have to be fed.

So I go up out of it and try the posts, the way you try a plank before you put weight on it.

Canopy, first, because anyone would. Barefoot on a wet culm twelve metres up with a rope round the waist and an old woman below me saying nothing, only laying her flat hand on a root, which is the sign for *sound*. To know which roots are holding and which are only reaching — twenty years to learn, and the learning is not knowledge, it is a kind of long acquaintance. I would want it. I have not got it. I would cut a bearing root in my fourth year and the corner of the tow store would come down in my ninth, and the ledger would say so, in the margin, in the same hand-size as everything else.

Nose, then. But I cannot be the nose. The nose is nine years old at the post and then finished, and the whole office is a countdown against your own body, and the thing itself does not live in the eleven words, it lives in the arguing, nose to nose, along the tank rail for a year. *Bread. Bread and iron. Not sweet, not yet, but tending.* I would not have the year, and I would not have the body that can still be ruined by it.

Cart is for the young and loud and it makes leavers.

Oiler is for the very first or the very last.

Engineer is a book read aloud over eleven evenings, and I have not sat through the eleven evenings, and nobody hands you the root column because you fancy the sound of it.

*Bearing, sound. Bearing, sound.*

So I am adult, and over, and I have lost the nine-kilohertz, which I know because when the eight-year-old is sent to stand under the jack-wheel and comes back and gives the sign for *dry*, I hear nothing at all and grease it anyway. My shins are the usual slow catastrophe. My hands are fine. I have two sets of bast cloth and one is always on the flue rail. I cannot smell the tanks and have not been able to since my second year, which is how everyone knew I had arrived.

And then it is the stop, and the lot is drawn from the tin — forty-one slivers of culm and one of them cut short — and I draw the short one, and that is where I find myself. Not in a post. In the only six days a year when there are no posts.

Three days of silence, and a day either side of it that is worse.

Hansa goes down on a Friday evening. The flywheel takes eleven minutes to stop, and the last minute of it is the strangest thing on this terrace, because you can see a four-metre wheel turning and hear the bamboo. Then the belt is off and the shaft is still and the cart is chocked at the north end and the works holds its breath in a way that does not end, and forty other people start being unable to sleep.

I go to tank three with a hurricane lamp and a hand-fired grate and a mercury thermometer in a brass sleeve, and I do not leave it for six days.

The work is nothing. That is the point of it. A spent-fibre brick every forty minutes, ash raked, damper by a thumb's width, and the thermometer read at the hour and the half-hour and written on a slate, and the duckweed skimmed off the corner where it rafts. Two degrees is the whole of it. Blood heat, plus or minus, for a hundred and some years without interruption, held on the fifth morning by me because a sliver of bamboo came up short in a tin.

What I do not expect is the sound.

Nobody has heard this place for a hundred years. The engine is a throat clearing and it covers everything, and with it gone the terrace comes up underneath like a floor you have walked on all your life and never seen. The figs drip onto tin at a rate that never resolves. The bamboo squeals at its nodes and then, in gusts, a whole clump knocks hollowly against itself, high up, wooden, chattering, and I understand at once why the people who leave say that is the one they miss — it is not the sound, it is that they only ever heard it during a stop, and a stop is the only time this place is frightening, so what they miss is being frightened at home. The channel under the Walk runs. The rats in the tow store are audible as a population. On the second evening the barker comes out on the engine-house wall and barks four times at a machine that is not running, and I catch myself listening for the oiler's round and there is no round.

And through it all, under my hand on the tank wall, nothing. No heartbeat in the piles. The terrace is lifting slightly. Everyone says that and it is true.

They come down to me in ones and twos through the night, people who cannot sleep, and this is the part of the superstition the engineer discourages and participates in: for the six days, the one on the grate is called by name. Not *Nose*, not *Sluice*, not *Boiler*. The name your mother uses indoors at close range, spoken out on the boards, in the open, by people who have never used it and will never use it again. It is meant as luck. It does not feel like luck. It feels like being carried out of the works and set down somewhere with no roof. The engineer comes on the second night with her bad left shin, stands there four minutes, says the name once, and goes back up, and neither of us mentions it afterward, ever, and she has done it for every stop of her tenure and will do it into her sixth cut.

On the fourth morning the heron comes. Ninth or tenth day, no regard for us at all. It stands on the sluice cheek for forty minutes in no noise whatever, which it has never done and cannot know is unusual, and takes shrimp, and goes, and the sluice keeper is asleep and I enter it myself in the back of the book: date, arrival, departure, weather. Four thousand entries. Plainly not one heron. Kept anyway.

That is where it comes to me, and it comes flatly, the way things are said here.

I am not the woman on the stool. The woman on the stool is a post that only exists six days a year and is drawn by lot. What I am is the two degrees.

Not the culture — the culture is older than the ledger and has no name and does not need me. Not the fire, which anyone can feed. The holding. The thin, unglamorous, hourly business of keeping something inside a range, which has been performed continuously for longer than anyone can reconstruct by a succession of hands that the book records as one column and does not distinguish. Like the barker: eighteen or twenty animals, one entry, because the ledger records the post and not the occupant and no engineer has ever seen a reason to change the convention. Like the heron: not one, never was, recorded anyway. Like the nose, which is not a woman, it is a thing that passes from a twenty-six-year-old to a seventeen-year-old by arguing, and has passed fourteen times, and is one bad year from ending, and everyone has always known this and has gone on handing it over.

I would be that. Whatever it is at this works that is many and is written down as one. The thing that turns up under a different body each time, does the hour and the half-hour, gets the slate right, and does not accumulate. Recorded in the margin. Not counted in the column for people, because the column for people is for the ones who have to be fed, and the count is forty-one, and the count is wrong, and the engineer knows it is wrong and writes forty-one.

On the third morning of the silence the boiler comes up and the flywheel takes its first slow turn and I feel it come back into the tank wall through my palm before I hear it. Somebody up on the Walk gives me my post again, shouted in the strangled overloud voice we all have, and it is the greatest kindness available here.

I bank the grate, skim the corner, write the last reading, and go back to work.
