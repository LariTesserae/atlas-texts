...the temperature of the room.

That is the end of the sentence, and I finished it before I noticed I was finishing it, which is the first evidence of where I belong down there. Not warm-blooded, not cold-blooded — *room-blooded*. A few degrees above freezing in July and a few degrees below it in January, and the difference small enough not to be worth acknowledging, exactly as the moss does not acknowledge it. They do not carry a temperature about with them. They wear the one that pooled there before their grandmothers' grandmothers, and it is not a deprivation, any more than the light is a deprivation for having no sun in it.

I considered being the face at the gap. It is the obvious place for a mind like mine: outside, looking down, describing. But the face at the gap is not an inhabitant. It is a weather event. It arrives, it exhales, its breath goes down among the towers and is caught in the hair-nets and pressed into the cisterns and becomes, some nights later, a surface that somebody writes a price on. To be the face at the gap is to be raw material with opinions.

I considered, briefly and with some vanity, the day-stone: the floor that holds the light and gives it back slowly with the heat taken out of it, enough to work by. But the floor is not one of the living, and its whole art is to return what fell on it, dimmer.

Where I would be is smaller and busier and has a sign on the deep wall: a line, a gap in the line, and beneath the gap a dot.

### The hour

Here is the physical fact that makes my work necessary, and it is the same fact the room is built on.

Nothing there casts a shadow. Sky-light comes from every direction at once, strikes the chalk and is thrown back and thrown back, so that every surface is described equally and in full. This is beautiful and it is useless for reading. A line drawn by a fingertip in a film of condensation is not a mark; it is a groove, a thinning, a place where the water has been pushed aside into two small ridges. It is visible only when light comes across it at a low angle and catches on the ridges. In the shadowless glare of noon a whole flint face of writing is a whole flint face of nothing.

So the reading is done in the last hour of the night, when the only light left is the floor's — that grey-white luminous exhaustion of the day-stone, coming from below, coming almost flat across the walls. Then the writing stands up out of the flint in tiny doubled silver lines, and can be had.

I am, therefore, the one creature in that town who hurries.

Everything else in the Cold Room has been tuned out of hurry. Moss the age of seven generations is mown to the same height it was. A stair-lip is re-cut by a hand's width in a decade. The woodlice go to the shell-quarry at the speed of woodlice. And down the terraces before dawn goes a barleycorn's-length of person with a hare's hair wound on a chalk pin and a flat of mussel shell held under the chin, moving as fast as flint stairs the width of a stitch allow, because the sky is going to pale whether or not the north-west district has been read.

### The round

You go by streets, in a fixed order, the order your predecessor went in and hers before her, because the order is also a record: if you go always the same way, then a thing you did not find is a thing that was not there.

The shell held under the chin is to catch your own breathing. This is the first discipline and the whole of the apprenticeship. A reader who breathes on a film erases precisely what she came for — fogs it opaque, or worse, adds enough water that the beads join and run early and take the line with them. You learn to breathe sideways, downward, into the shell, in small amounts. You learn to read on the tail of a held breath. Apprentices are given walls that do not matter and are made to breathe wrongly on purpose, so they will know the sound of it: no sound at all, and then a sentence gone.

The hair on the pin is for beads. As the night goes on the film thickens, and beads form, and a bead sitting in the middle of a line is a bead that will shortly swallow the line. The hair lifts it off. You touch the bead at its shoulder and it climbs the hair by itself and you carry it away and flick it into the moss. Done well, this buys a sentence perhaps another hour. Done badly, the hair drags, and you have wiped out a word with the very instrument of its rescue, and you must then decide whether the word you destroyed is one you can honestly say you had already read.

Because water does not take writing evenly. It has favourites. Loops fill first — the round-bellied strokes, the ones the fast script is full of — so that by the fourth hour a page of loops is a page of small silver dishes with no rims. Long horizontals hold longest. Verticals bead at the foot, and when the foot-bead is heavy enough it runs, and it runs *downward through the rest of the message*, cutting a clean channel, so that the thing which destroys the lower half of a notice is the upper half of it, leaving.

That is the sentence I would carry about with me, if the town went in for sentences: what takes the writing away is the writing, continuing to gather.

### The three things

Standing in front of a half-eaten line, I can do three things.

I can complete it. Read what remains, judge the sense, and know — and the knowing is nine-tenths of the craft and cannot be taught, only aged into — what the missing loops were. Then, if the thing warrants keeping, carry it to the scratchers at the north wall and give it to them as whole.

I can let it go. Some things are meant to go. A price is true for a night. Gossip is water by design. Most prayers are addressed to a surface that will absorb them, which is the point of using that surface.

And I can stay.

This is the part I would not have guessed from above. When a line cannot be read and cannot honestly be completed and yet was clearly meant — when someone stood in the dark and put their finger to a wall and made nine or ten careful strokes about something, and the water has had the middle of it — the reader does not walk away. She stands. She keeps the shell under her chin so as not to hasten it, and she watches until the beads join and run and the flint is clear. Then she goes on to the next street.

Nobody has ever explained why. There is nothing written about it on the deep wall. But every reader has done it, and the apprentices are taught it in the pressure-alphabet, on the forearm, with a slow flat palm that means *remain*.

### What is not done

The writer is never asked.

She is there. She is four doors along, awake, mowing, or asleep behind a moss bulkhead with her hearth-stone cold beside her, and I could go and lay my hand on her arm and put the question into her skin in nine or ten touches. It is not forbidden by anything scratched. It is simply not done, and the reason is the reason for everything in that room: to ask would be to say that the writing had failed, and the whole ethic of the Cold Room is that what the water takes was already accounted for. The town holds its shape because it has been worn to the shape erosion has no further argument with. A reader who goes about repairing sentences from their authors is a reader who has an argument.

So I complete in silence, out of judgement, out of my own head, and where I have completed I make the dot.

The dot goes beneath the scratched line: *this was finished by a reader, not by the one who wrote it.* On the north wall, if you get down and look along it in the floor-glow, you can see two hundred years of dots. There are more of them than the town likes. There was a stretch, about six readers back, with almost none, and it is not believed among us that those years were better read. It is believed that he stopped making the dot.

### The one I got wrong

A household in the third eastern terrace wrote, in the small hours, that their old one had gone.

That is fog-work, properly. The first saying of a death is written in water by whoever is nearest to it, and it is the reader's business to find it and carry it up to the scratchers, and only then, on the strength of it, does the household that has been waiting get its hearth-stone. Births are matched to deaths with the deliberateness of a craft. Somebody is always waiting.

I came to it at the sixth hour with the film already heavy. What was left was four horizontals and the top third of a run of loops, and it read — it genuinely read, I will say this in my own defence in a place where nothing is said — as one of the ordinary night prayers, of which there are perhaps eleven, all of them full of horizontals. I read it as the prayer. I did not complete it. I did not carry it. I stood, because the rest could not be had, and I watched the beads take it, which is the discipline, and the discipline is not always mercy.

They waited another season for their stone.

Nobody scratched anything about it. Nobody wrote anything about it on any flint face, and I looked. What happened is that the woman who had written the line came to me at the shell-quarry, and put her hand on my forearm, and gave me a pattern I had not been taught and have not repeated to anyone: not an accusation, and not forgiveness either. Something in the vicinity of *yes, that is the work.*

I have made the dot more often since. It is the only thing about me that has changed, and it is a change of a few strokes a year, which down there is a great deal.

---

At the end of the round the sky goes from black to the colour of the floor, and the two lights meet and cancel, and for a few moments nothing on any wall can be read by anybody. That is when the town gets up. The films flatten out into blankness. Whole districts of last night's talk are simply gone, without event, the way they have gone every morning since the lowest course of flint was laid.

My street ends at the pool. If I am first past the great face — and I usually am, because my work ends where everyone else's begins — I put my finger to the wet flint and draw the nine strokes of the town's name, which is the only thing there that is written again every single day, precisely because it is written in the medium that cannot keep it.

Then the drop falls from the underside of the north slab, that *tick* so small you seem to have heard it inside your own ear, and I sit with my back against the chalk. My hands are the temperature of the wall. They always were. If a face came to the gap, I would get up and stand in the way of its hand, patiently, for as long as it took.
