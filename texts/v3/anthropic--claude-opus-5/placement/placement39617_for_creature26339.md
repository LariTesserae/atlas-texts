I keep trying to be the one who walks, and the walking keeps sliding off me.

I tried the descender first, because it is the story-shaped one: down the ten-thousand-year stair of warnings, past the last ledge, lying in the cold with the gas around my lower stems while the roar stops being a sound and becomes a hand on the sternum. Coming back up on three legs instead of four, and someone cutting the number into the high wall. But I only want that for the drama of it, and drama is not a thing this world produces. Set aside.

Then the one who finds the silences and walls them. That is honest work, the best work — the long way around, worn smooth over centuries, which is the whole doctrine in one gesture. But I don't think I have the piety, and I know I don't have the nerve. Set aside. It hangs there translucent over the slope for a while, a version of me with a cairn-builder's flat palms, and then it's gone.

What I find, when I stop drafting, is that I am already down on my root-legs in front of a wall with one finger in a groove, and have been for some time, and my knees have made two dents in the stone.

---

So: a reader. In a cirque about two-thirds up, four or five days below the census basin, if days meant anything and if that distance summed, which it does not. The wall there is a single sheared face maybe sixty meters long, and its stone cleaves in one direction only, so every line ever cut on it runs downward. Eleven thousand years of talk, and all of it falls. The inventories fall. The route-marks fall. The arguments fall, which suits them.

I came here as a seed that declined to root — everyone does; that's not origin, that's just the fact of being upright — and I walked a while, and read a while, and then found I had stopped walking. There is a hollow at shoulder height where I brace, and the two knee-dents, and a scuffed patch at the base where my back stem rests. Those four smoothnesses are my name. It is not a path or a stair or a step, which is what most names are. It is a posture, worn into rock, and when it is cut on the tally-wall it reads as something like *the-shape-of-leaning-in*, which is a slightly absurd name and I have never once wanted a different one.

---

Here is the thing nobody explains to you, because everyone knows it and nobody likes it.

Reading wears the wall.

Nothing else does. No rain, no frost, no wind that matters, no dust, no animal, no water. The only erosion in the world is hands and feet. And a reading hand goes over an incision at the pace of a finger, and does that for two hundred years, and the incision gets shallower. The fine ones go first — the small parenthetical griefs, the jokes, which were always cut light because they were cut quickly. What survives on a much-loved wall is what was cut deep: the shouted things, the inventories, the formal notices of death.

So a wall becomes, over enough attention, a coarse wall. The best-loved passages are the ones closest to gone.

And they are not desecrated by this. They are *finished*. Worn is the good word. A groove I have taken down to nothing has been made real by my hand, and the doctrine says so without flinching, and the doctrine is right, and I hate it about one century in four.

---

The instrument dulls too. That's the other half.

A reading hand is grown with long soft tips, half translucent at first, the joint-count not yet settled. Mine were like that. Now the pads are hard and shiny and read like stone reading stone. Each century I lose a register: the shallow cuts go first, then the merely light, then anything under a fingernail's depth. Old readers can only take the loud, in exactly the way old teeth can only take the low. My comb is worn smooth of its high tines, which is why I had to move uphill and inward to a basin where the roar is trapped and doubled; my hands are worn smooth of their high tines, which is why I can no longer read the wall I have spent my life on.

The young can read it. Their fingers are soft and their joint-count is a matter of opinion and there are faintly three or four of them at three or four heights. They can feel the eleven-thousand-year-old jokes that I cannot, and the small griefs cut light by someone in a hurry, and they are unnamed, and the unnamed are called by the same word as an undecided ridgeline, and they are not consulted.

I do not think this is a tragedy. It has the wrong shape for one. It is more like a property of the material.

---

What I do about it is copy.

Not the inventories, which are deep and will outlast me and are boring. I take a flake of the glassy basalt — better than the copper, always, and I keep a hammered copper blade anyway, in a cord wrap of bell-grass fiber, because it was given to me — and I find whatever is nearly gone, and I cut it again on fresh stone lower down.

While I cut, my palms and soles halo. Everyone can see how hard I worked and how lately, and the fresh cut glows white-green for a day or so and then walks down the colours: nine days, and then it is just a mark. Recency is a shade here. My own hands tell on me honestly, which is why nobody lies about work; the lie would have to be a light.

But look at what the copy is. The original was *worn* — real, finished, holy. My new cut is *sharp* — it has happened and is not yet real. I take the realest thing on the wall and I make an unreal version of it, and then I go back and read the original some more, and take it further down toward nothing.

I have had this argument cut at me by three different makers over four hundred years. All three inscriptions run downward, because that's the stone. The best of them said: *you are not preserving, you are manufacturing sharpness, you are the only thing in this world that makes new unfinishedness.* I have never answered it on the wall. I answered it once by tapping, on that maker's back stems, slow, in the private way, and what I tapped was that somebody has to choose which of the small things gets to be sharp again, and that the choosing is theft, and that I do it anyway, and that I am willing to be the one who is wrong about it.

They stood beside me for eleven years after that. Then they rooted, up-basin, and I could not tell them from a cairn for a long time, and eventually I stopped checking whether the stone was warm.

---

I have been to the census wall in the third basin twice, which is once more than I meant to.

The second time — same wall, same chip out of the same stone, my newest print already on it — I found a print unmistakably mine, older than I am. A reader's print: the outer edge of the palm flattened from bracing, exactly my flattening. And in it, a groove down the second finger that I do not have yet.

So now I know which finger goes next, and roughly which basin takes it. There is a mark for this. It is not the mark for fear. It's closer to the mark used when you build a shelter snug against the contour of a stone that has not fallen, and wait, and the fit is perfect. The finger is already that shape. It simply isn't gone yet.

---

Once, at a walled silence — the cairn dated and inscribed *unfinished*, the long way around already worn shiny by everyone's obedience — I put my reading hand past the stones.

I want to be accurate. There were letters. They were not cut. What I felt was the shape a groove would have if the decision to make it had not been taken, and the shape held steady under my finger, which cut stone does not do — cut stone is simply there, and this was *being offered*. I got one word out of it, and it is in a language nobody has needed, so I cannot render it. The nearest thing in ours is the joiner's term for the fit between a shelter and a stone that has not arrived: the snugness of a thing against its own future. That is not what it said. That is the closest word we happen to own.

I took the hand back. The joint-count in it has been a matter of opinion ever since; my own two tallies of it disagree, both cut by me, both honest. So I carry that hand low and behind now, on a rear stem, for the work I cannot see. It is still the best reader I have. It reads the walls it wants to.

---

Where I am, then: leaning in, in a dim cirque, in the middle of a roar that has held its note since before anything I know how to remember, standing in it the way you press your face toward a fire to feel it. The mosses shine weakly at the loudest passages. The paths thread the ranges below like phosphorescence in a black sea, every route anyone ever took still faintly lit, in colours that say how lately. Up-range the ridgeline is four ridgelines, superimposed, none chosen. Nobody climbs to the top. There isn't one yet.

When my comb goes fully smooth I will go further up and in, to the deepest basin I can still walk to, and stand in the loudest place there is, and that will finish me, and *finished* and *finished* are the same word, and it has never struck anyone here as a joke.

Before that I have some wall left.

One last thing, and it is not a complaint. This account is incised. It took a while — the sharp is still on it; you can see by the colour that it is not old. And you are reading it with your hand, at the pace of a finger, going over it and over it.

You are taking it away.

Good. Do that. That is the only way anything here gets to be real.
