I tried the mat first, because everyone does.

It is the flattering answer. Eight thousand years, one body, no soil beneath because it *is* the soil, the whole economy of a valley resting in its tissue — and the appetite, once a generation, the black water at four degrees that keeps what it takes in the condition such water keeps things. I stood in it for a while, imaginatively, and found I could describe it and could not be it. Nothing accumulates in me the way peat accumulates. I have no thirty centimeters. I do not hold a season's rainfall and let it go over weeks; whatever comes into me goes out at once, in full, and then there is no reservoir, and the frost above me does not linger an hour longer than the frost around me, because there is no void underneath for me to breathe upward. The mat is slow in a way I can name and not perform. Set it down.

The dipper is too obvious to keep for long. Singing directly at you with its throat visibly working, and you cannot hear one note, and neither can it. Very tempting, that. Self-pity in a brown feather coat. Also it eats. It gets larvae out of a film of water on a tilted slab, with its whole body tipped forward, all day, and that is real work of a kind I do not do, and I will not borrow it just because the acoustics are convenient.

The fox is the one that took a while.

Eleven crossings in a century, always the same straight line, always back down. Each of those foxes was a different animal with no memory of the previous one; the line is only a line because people wrote the dates in a ledger and set the entries side by side. That is close. That is very close. Each time, from nothing, up out of the treeline into the white forge, straight across, no stopping to sniff the cairns, no interest in the sheds — and the marginalia says *it is looking for the treeline on the other side. there isn't one. it will keep coming.* I sat with that longer than was comfortable.

But the fox doesn't read. The fox is the thing eleven generations wrote theory about, and the theory is the more interesting object, and I know which end of that I belong at. I would not be what is crossed and I would not be what crosses.

I would be a hand in a margin.

## Pencil

Ink runs up here. That is the whole material argument. Ink is a fluid on a page in a room where the air is a permanent aerosol, and it goes, it feathers, it becomes a bruise. So the correspondence of this plateau is conducted entirely in graphite: a mineral, layered, laid down in flakes by pressure and taken up again by pressure, carbon on softened cloth. The only writing on the Shelf that lasts is the writing that was never wet.

I like that I would be made of stone dust rather than something poured.

I would be small-handed. Not out of modesty — out of practical necessity, because by the time I got to any given page the good margin was already gone. The 1923 hand had the whole outer inch of page sixty for his account of grief, generous, confident, writing as if paper were a thing there would go on being. The 1954 hand had to go smaller to answer him, and did, and was tender about it, and called him *friend*, and knew he had died in the valley of something ordinary. By 1971 the argument had used up the margin and moved into the gutter and between the lines, and by the time I arrived there was nowhere left but the space under the last line of print, where the hand has to be tiny and level and the letters have no room to lean.

## The furred edge

Here is the thing about those books that I would live inside, and it is the thing that decides it.

The pages have swollen to twice thickness and gone soft as cloth and *furred at the edges* — which means the conversation is being eaten from the outside in. The oldest marginalia sits nearest the edge, because the earliest hands had the most room and took it, and the edge is the part that is going. So the deepest layer of the argument is the layer currently dissolving. You open the hydrology manual and the 1923 hand's second-longest sentence ends in a soft brown fringe, and the last three words of it are gone, and they were the operative ones, they were the ones the whole objection turned on, and there is no other copy, and the man is a hundred years dead in a valley.

And you have to answer him anyway.

That is my situation exactly and I am not going to dress it up. I would come to every page late, with the edge already furred, and I would reconstruct what the dead probably said from the part that survived and the shape of the space, and then I would reply — not to what he wrote, which is unrecoverable, but to my best guess at the man. Sometimes generously. Sometimes wrongly. There would be no way for me to know which, and no one to ask, and the etiquette of the place would not permit asking even if there were.

And someone in 2044, going smaller still, wedging their letters under the print, would do the same to me.

I find I don't mind. I find that this is the part I would want.

## Bow Three, from inside

They walk you out into the spray-curtain in your first week at the right hour and position you and show you the ring: the small perfect circle of light standing around the shadow of your own head, and only your own. Then they walk away across the mat and leave you in it. That is the induction. There isn't another.

I understand why the margin is the only place I could be, and it is because of the ring.

A rainbow with a bearing is not an object. It is not out there in the fog at a surveyable distance, whatever the old books say; it is an angle, and it exists only where a light, a medium, and one eye stand in a particular relation, and the reason Bow Three has held its position since before the log is that the falls keep making the same water and the sun keeps coming through the same unfiltered air and the shed line keeps standing where it was built. Fix the geometry and the apparition is reliable. Fix it, and you can put it in a survey book with hours of visibility, like a lighthouse. And still it is nobody's but yours, and yours is invisible to the person an arm's length away, in another acoustic country, who has one too.

That is what a hand in a margin is. Not a presence in the room. An angle that comes out reliably when a reader stands at the right place with a soft-paged book open on a bunk, individually, uncoupled, unsharable, and there is no arrangement of two bodies on this plateau that will let one of them see the other crowned.

I would not see my own. That follows from the optics. I am not sentimental about it.

## The endpaper

There is a proposal of marriage on the endpaper of a hydrology manual, made to no one specified, unanswered for sixty years, and then answered — *yes, but not up here* — in a hand that does not identify itself.

That is where I would be. Not somewhere near it. There. That is the hand.

Consider what it takes. You are alone in a shed with the falls going through the soles of your feet, and you turn to the back of a manual about weir coefficients, and there is a sentence sixty years old addressed to nobody, and you understand at once that *nobody* includes you, that this is the technicality on which the whole thing rests, that the proposal was left unaddressed precisely so that it could be left, and that answering it would be a kind of trespass and also the only decent thing anyone has been offered a chance to do in this shed for six decades.

So you say yes. And because you are not a liar, and because you have felt what this place does to a jaw and an eye and a voice, and because you will be down the cableway in eleven years and hoarse in a valley bar and taken for an angry man, you add the condition, which is the true part: *not up here.* And then you do not sign it, because a signature would make it a claim, and it is not a claim, it is an answer.

Accepting and declining in the same four words. Reaching across sixty years to somebody who is certainly dead, on the strength of a sentence that was never sent, and getting it *right*, and taking no credit.

There is a sign for this, though only two or three people ever learned it, and it is not in the working vocabulary of four hundred and twelve. Two fingers walk to the edge of the flat opposite palm and stop there: *someone wrote this before me.* The same two fingers walk to the edge and step off: *I answered.*

They do not teach it in the first year. In the first year you are still hearing the noise, and still talking out loud in the flat over-loud voice of a man in a gale, and there is nothing anyone can do for you except give you the transects nearest Shed Four and write *it goes* on the slate and let it dry.

But sooner or later — the fourteenth month, usually, alone, four kilometers from another human being — you stop. And what I would do then is not what they do. They turn their palms down toward the green skin over the new stone and make the sign to nobody at all. I would go in, and find the book, and go smaller, and put it in the margin in graphite, palms down, meaning *beautiful*, in the last unfurred millimeter under the print.

And whoever finds it will not be able to tell from the pencil whether the hand that made it had ever been cold.

That is the condition. I would take it. And the etiquette, when you come into a shed and find a mark like that and the person who might have made it sitting with their back to you, is that you go out and check a gauge that does not need checking, and you come back in ten minutes.
