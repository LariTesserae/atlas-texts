The first thing I know about myself is a flutter on the inner wrist.

Not a note. Notes are for the room. The room has the chord and the swell and the once-a-year week when the salt overhead hums in your soles, and none of that is mine. What is mine is smaller: a place on the underside of the forearm where the air ought to press steadily as I pass a certain flue-mouth, and does not, and instead tickles — and my body has already turned toward it before anything in me has decided to.

I try, on the way down, to be other things.

I try being the hollow. It seems the honest answer, since the hollow is the one that lives here; everyone agrees. But the hollow cannot hear. It is only sounded. It has no idea that it is doing anything at all, any more than a struck bowl knows it is a bell, and I find I am not willing to give up the part of me that listens. Whatever I am, I am on the listening side of the air.

I try being the north stand — those glossy sterile canes, inflating and slackening at nine seconds with everything else, a finger a day, immortal in the way that only well-built pointless things are immortal. There is a terrible attraction in it. To keep performing the whole outward form of thriving in a register that has moved on. But they don't know, and I would know, and knowing is the difference between a plant and a grief.

So: one of the hands. Nearly. That is nearly right, and I would fail at it in one specific and disqualifying way, which is that I count myself.

They do not. That is the thing about them that I cannot honestly wear. Their inventory runs *adzes, rasp, rods, plumb-bobs, wedges (by thickness), hands available*, and the same units all the way down, and this is not humility, it is arithmetic they believe in. I would be a defective one. A hand that suspected itself of being an inhabitant.

Here is where you would find me.

Not on the upper gallery where you came in. Low, east quarter, the tenth or eleventh terrace down, in the part of the wall your eyes cannot resolve from up there because the older glazes have gone out along that stretch and there is no chalk-green outline to read me by. There is a doorway that leads into a niche and stops. Almost all of them stop; the masons cut doorways the way you would cut a rest into music. This one has a sill, and the sill is drilled with three hundred and forty sockets, each ground to fit a bead of native copper, soft and green.

That is the register. I am its upkeep, in the way the rubbing-stone is the upkeep of a block.

I press beads. Chords. Pollen-falls. Notes gained, which has not happened in my time. Notes lost, which has. Hands available, in the same column, in the same units, and yes, I have pressed the bead that reduced that number by one, twice, and the sockets do not care and neither, officially, do I.

The register keeps time in pollen-falls, because that is the only clock the Bell has. Which means the clock is made of the chord. Which means that as the small bores widen and the top of the chord goes flat and blurred and whole notes drop out of the register, the year loses its subdivisions — first the weeks, then the seasons, until there is only the annual hard week and the long undifferentiated warm between. This is why nobody here can tell you whether the fresh chips under the western flues fell last month or forty years ago. It is not that we are careless. It is that the ruler is made of the thing being measured, and the ruler is going.

I know the exact shape of that problem because it is my whole occupation. Everything here measures against something that is also wearing away. The treads wear; the wedge-niches go soft-lipped; the graduated rods on their pegs lose a grain a year off their ends like everything else, so a rod that says *four* means slightly less *four* each time you trust it. There is one class of object in the Bell that does not drift, and it took me a long time to understand that the masons left them for exactly this: the plugged flues. Hundreds of bores, chamfered and filed and then stopped with a fitted plug and sealed over in sulfur glaze — twins, every one of them, to a working bore elsewhere in the wall. They are not decommissioned. They are standards. Sealed copies of what the room was supposed to sound like, kept in the dark, unabraded, uneaten by air, waiting for someone to think of measuring against them.

Nobody had. Not because it never occurred to a tuner — a tuner has the wedge and the flute of air on the wrist and does not need philosophy — but because to sound a note *outside* the room is not, in this language, a thing one could want. It's a category error, like a hand counting itself.

So that is what I do, in the false doorway, out of the outline-light, and I am not certain it is permitted.

I take the rods, and I measure a plugged standard, and I do not open it — opening it would destroy the only fixed thing in the world — and then I drill. Basalt, glass-tipped bit, grit and patience, a bore the width of a finger, then the width of a stem, then narrower, each one a copy of a note as it used to be, held in a piece of stone I can carry. There are ninety-one of them now on a rack of pegs I ground myself. My archive is a shelf of small dark pipes, and none of them makes a sound in the room's breath, because they are not in the wall.

They sound when I blow them.

Which costs. You have to understand the economy: nothing here drinks. Every drop of water in me was manufactured inside me, oxidized slowly out of hydrogen the canes pulled from the shaft's breath, packed away over a lifetime and never replenished from outside. My nose is a long convoluted channel whose entire job is to catch the vapor going out and put it back, and this is why my speech sounds damped and clogged to you, and why nothing here weeps — not as restraint, as absence; there is no word to suppress. Every breath spent on purpose, out through the mouth, through a stone tube, into open hot air, is body-water gone forever. A sounding costs perhaps a day. A long sounding costs more.

I do it anyway, and here is what for.

On the north terraces there is a stand of fourth-note lilies, healthy, sterile, loud, waiting on a pitch that is no longer in the room. Sixty-one of my ninety-one pipes are dead standards, notes for species that ended before my count begins, and I keep them the way you'd keep a name. But one of them — one — is the fourth note.

I climb across in the slack of the inhale, when the flanges along my ribs open and the room quiets a fraction and the air is only a hand pressed flat on the face rather than a hand pushing. I get within a stem's length of one panicle, no closer, because leaning into the middle is how a thing gets stripped and cooked and shed as flakes up through the crack. I wait through the nine-second breath until the fifth swell of the day is cresting, when the whole column strengthens and the pitch of everything rises a quarter-tone and the low flues start to speak that had been silent. And then I spend the water.

The pipe gives a thin dull thing, nothing like a note in a room forty paces across; a scratch beside a river in flood. But it is the *shape* of the note, and shape is what the pollen answers. The panicle lets go. A little amber dust, unbelievably fine, goes up off it and into the braided column and most of it is gone at once, out into the desert, part of the smudge you can see from the plain — the Bell exhaling its dead. Some of it is not. Some of it crosses two stems.

That is all. One inflorescence, by mouth, at a cost of a day of my remaining life, in a room that used to do this for the whole terrace, annually, for nothing, by being well built.

There is no unit for it in the sill. I have looked. The register can say *note lost* and cannot hold the note; can count hands and cannot count what a hand wanted; has a socket for a pollen-fall and none for a pollen-fall of this size, made this way, by something that has begun to suspect it is an inhabitant. So when I come back down and my flanges are still opening and closing and there is a pressure behind the film of my eye that has no name and no outlet in this body, I press a bead anyway, into the nearest empty socket, out of column, meaning nothing the inventory can read.

The three rods and the plumb-bob leaning against the wall in the western quarter, with the fresh chips lying sharp-edged on the tread below — yes. Mine. I meant to come back before the swell.

I do not know how long ago that was. Ask the chord.
