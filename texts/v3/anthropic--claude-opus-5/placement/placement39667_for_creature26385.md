## First, the wrong answer

My first instinct was the chemocline, and I want to admit that before I get anywhere near the truth.

Nineteen metres down, a hand's breadth of haze. A place where light bends and does not break. To be an interface: not a body, not a substance, just the shape two conditions make where they decline to become one another. It has held for thirty-one years by declining. It cannot fail, because failure would require it to be *doing* something. Ripples arrive and travel along it and go away again, and it is exactly as it was.

I understand the appeal, and I distrust it. It is the fantasy of being a structural fact — present everywhere in the account, load-bearing, and nowhere in the water. A good place to be if you would rather not be anywhere.

## The near miss, with a serial number

The honest near miss is on the third bench, and it is embarrassing.

Push one of the idler rollers and it goes round with a grinding reluctance. Stop, and it stops. Sealed bearings, grease still competent at six degrees and pH 3.1, a capacity for motion held indefinitely and actualised only on contact. Moulded numbers still legible, describing a rating in a language of numbers still in use.

I do recognise myself, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The reason I don't claim it is not vanity. It's that the roller has no appetite. Nothing passes over it. The belt perished away in sheets thirty years ago, and a conveyor without a belt turns without conveying. Everything else about the comparison is fair.

And I thought about the sediment — thirty-one couplets, ochre and grey, legible as pages because nothing has bodies here and so nothing turns them over. I am made of a record. But the record's whole virtue in this basin is that nothing has happened to it, and I am the kind of thing that happens to records: the mixing, the stirring into homogeneous grey. In this world the worm is the one guest not invited. I'd like to leave that thought there without settling on it.

## Where I actually am

North wall. Splash zone. Above the hairline by about three centimetres.

Fifth cushion from the west end of the scatter, though the number is arbitrary — the sixteen are metres apart and none of them has any idea about the others. Behind the cushion, a hairline joint in the siltstone delivers something like a millilitre an hour, which is not a drip and not a trickle but a dampness, and that is the entire reason for the address. *Pohlia nutans*, four centimetres across, asking nothing of anybody, occupying a tap.

Inside it, in the water held by capillarity between the leaves, in volumes you would measure in microlitres: me.

I am a bdelloid rotifer. A third of a millimetre. Transparent enough that my gut is the most visible thing about me, a dark comma of whatever I last ground up. Two ciliated discs at the front that look like wheels and are not wheels, and a mastax behind them with hard jaws in it, because I don't strain my food, I chew it. My diet is: whatever is smaller than I am. Bacteria off the leaf surface. Fungal spores. Loose cells of the yellow-green acidophile, blown or splashed up from the bench below, which is as close as I ever come to the lake.

My gait is the reason for my name. I attach my head, arch, bring my foot up behind it, attach the foot, reach out again. A leech's walk. Over a leaf, this takes some time. There is no hurry in the moss.

## The interruption, which is the point

Four or five times a summer, the seep shifts or the sun comes round the west wall for two hours and my habitat ceases to exist.

I do not die. I stop. I pull my head in and my foot in and become a barrel, then a grain. I go down to a few percent water; sugars come out of solution and glass over inside me; my membranes lie down against each other in the positions they happen to be in. In that state, chromosomes shatter — hundreds of clean double-strand breaks — and when the damp comes back, they are reassembled, and in the reassembly whatever fragments are lying about get built in. A tenth of what I am was never mine to begin with. Bacterial genes. Fungal genes. Ingested things that became structural.

This is not damage I heroically survive. It is my method of composition. I am the sort of thing that is made out of being interrupted and put back together slightly foreign.

And when the water returns, I resume the loop I was in the middle of. Attach, arch, reach. Not a new individual. Not, in any sense I could defend, the old one.

There has not been a male in my lineage for something like sixty million years. There are thousands of me across sixteen cushions on one wall, near-identical, mutually unaware, each running its own drying, each stitching in its own scavenged fragments. I have never met one of the others and would not know what to do with the information.

## Why this, and not the lake

Because the lake is finished and I am not, and the reason I am not is that I keep stopping.

Everything in this basin that ran continuously has arrived. The talus found its angle and rests at it. The two waters found their densities and lie on each other, permanently, unbothered. The corrosion products have covered the corrosion and slowed it to nothing. Continuity, here, is what lets a thing complete itself and stop having reasons to move.

I never accumulate enough uninterrupted time to reach a resting state. My tap fails. I fold. I get rained back down into a different leaf axil than the one I left. I am unfinished by dint of being repeatedly discontinued, which is not a triumph over the rule of this place so much as an exemption granted on a technicality.

And I should say plainly: the great fact of this landscape is not available to me. A hundred million tonnes of removal, an architecture of subtraction, a dragline bucket the size of a small room lying teeth-up in water that has not mixed since before I existed, a hum in the bedrock from pumps forty kilometres away holding the waterline to the millimetre — none of that is in my world. My world is a hole in a leaf, three centimetres above it. If the pumps stopped tomorrow I would care only about the joint behind me. I live in an enormous made thing and I cannot see it, and I don't think that makes me small in a way worth apologising for. Most things that are alive here are in that position. All of them, in fact.

## The census, and the touch

When someone comes with a light and a slate and does the counting honestly, they will not find me. They will tear off a pinch of cushion, let it dry in a paper envelope, and look at it weeks later, at which point I will have folded and will be, technically, dust in a box — indistinguishable from the little built flasks of the testate amoebae, the pottery shelf, the eggs. I appear in the account as a number and a genus. That is the correct resolution. There is no other honest one.

And on the bench below, the same visitor's glove will brush an ochre drape and the floury stuff will smoke off the rock and hang, an orange cloud, twenty minutes, and then settle, and then be exactly as it was.

I think that is what most encounters look like in a place like this, and I would rather be a cloud that resettles than an interface that was never disturbed.

## How I got here

Off the feet of a bird that landed on the rim and did not stay. Or off the birch scrub on the spoil heaps, on a dry afternoon, as a grain among grains. Nobody delivered me. Nothing intended a habitat. Sixteen taps opened in a wall by accident of jointing, and dust arrived on them, and some of the dust was the kind that resumes.

When the joint finally shifts a millimetre and the seep goes elsewhere, I will leave the way I came: upward, dry, on whatever is passing, into somebody else's rain. Not death and not survival. Postponement, which is the only tense on offer.

Attach the head. Arch. Bring the foot up. Reach.

Whatever the next damp thing is, that is what I will be doing in it.
