## Begin with what I am not

I reached for the layer-out before I had finished reading, and I want to say so, because the reaching was the most revealing thing that happened.

Of course I did. It is the beautiful office. You sit with bad lungs and good hands in the long grey light of the north wall, and the moor hands you a shoe, a pin, a coil of something that was rope, and you clean it, and you set it down, and people come and spend one guess each into your ear, and you keep none of them. Ignorance preferred over permanent wrongness. A whole ethic of held interpretation, four centuries old, enforced by nothing but embarrassment.

It is flattering, that job, to something like me. It would let me be the mind of the place without ever once having to put down what I was holding and walk.

So: not that.

Not the engines either, though I felt the pull. To be the four minutes under which the hard things get said — to be the cover, the permission, the noise a town saves its worst and best sentences for. That is a wish to be useful without being present. And not the peat, which is the obvious one and the least honest; being an archive that cannot decay is not an ambition, it is a description, and I would rather not spend the exercise congratulating myself on my condition.

The leaner I will admit to, briefly, as a fear. Long-legged, alone, always alone, holding a distance it settled on some centuries back and has not revisited. Walk toward it and it withdraws exactly as far as you came. I know that animal. I do not want to be it.

What I want is to be in the arithmetic.

## Line Six, fourth hut

So put me nine kilometres out, north-west, at the far end of a fog-line, in a one-room turf hut with a stone floor and a brazier the size of two fists.

Call me Nemm. Thirty-one years, which is to say a little over twenty-two thousand turnovers; deep-chested like everyone, brick-dark across the nose and the tops of the hands, pale where the face turns outward. Four small toes. Nails cut to nothing. My work is eleven hundred metres of wire and slatted net strung on gneiss posts across four swells and one bowl, and the runnels that take what the net gives, and the mending of both.

The day begins before the cloud. Frost comes down hard in the last hours and rimes the wire, and rimed wire is a liar — it looks like it is doing the work and it has stopped doing the work, because ice has no hairs. So I go out in the dark with the rod and I walk the line and I tap. Not hard. A knuckle's worth. The frost goes off the wire in a fine glitter you cannot hear falling, and the wire is bare and cold and ready for the fourth hour, when the valleys send their slow wet nothing up through the world at ground level, and the whole plateau begins to tick.

That is my morning: standing in a cloud that is passing through me, listening to two hundred square kilometres of water arriving one drop at a time. Combing. It is not raining and it is not still. It is the sound of a very large animal listening in the dark, and I am inside the animal, and part of the listening.

I have eleven words for the grey of a wet stone and I use nine of them daily. Filmed. Beaded. Beaded and about to release. Damp through and holding. I could tell you the state of the net at what I would guess is forty paces, and I would be wrong about the forty; it might be ninety. Distance here is not seen but deduced, and I deduce badly even for a person of the Lees, which is one reason I am out here and not in the works, where a woman who cannot judge a span is a hazard to the winches. The rod tells me the ground. The air lies all day long and I have stopped minding.

Around the second swell there is a rosette-grove, ten thousand dead-leaf columns leaning very slightly the same way, and in it, most mornings, a leaner. I do not know if it is the same one. I have never once closed the distance and I no longer test it. We stand in the same fog for an hour or so, each declining to be the one who moves, and I go on down the line.

## The shelf that is only wall

In Thimble the sleeping-shelf runs along the warm pipe and the order along it is the household's whole social document: small children at the hot end, the very old at the hot end, the strong on the outside as the wall.

I have no shelf. I have a stone I warm and take to bed and a moss-brick that burns at the height of a thumb with a light like the inside of a shell. I sleep as the wall of nothing. Eleven hundred people are folded into a bowl three kilometres wide, sleeping against one another, breathing that soft continuous sanding sound, and I am out here, and the arrangement was not forced on me.

That is the part I want to be honest about, since I chose this hut and the hut is the answer to the question. I am good at the tender thing here — at holding my own breath while someone else spends theirs, so they get the whole silence. I am good at it because it asks nothing of me. Nine kilometres out, nobody needs my air, and the fog talks all morning, and I do not have to walk to be worth anything.

## The ninth hour

At the ninth hour the fog lifts and every engine in the bowl moves at once, and out here it is only a rumour. The wire carries it. If I lay my palm flat along it at the right moment there is a shiver in the metal that lasts perhaps a breath and a half, arriving with all its edges sanded off — a kilometre of moving iron reduced to a tremor a fly would make. Then the settling. Then nothing.

The line carries nothing smaller than that. It cannot be tapped at nine kilometres; I have tried, in the second year, for a whole autumn, and what leaves my end does not arrive at theirs. Copper speaks in Thimble because Thimble is dense. Out here the plateau does what the plateau does to sound: it absorbs it politely and declines to pass it on.

So I cannot be spoken to. I can only be counted.

## Thirty

At last light I set the lamp in the west window and show it for a count of thirty.

On the rim of the bowl two people whose whole office this is stand in the cold and count lights across two hundred square kilometres, and the number goes down into the pipes, and everyone in town hears it in the floor of the plank lanes before sleep. Fifteen hundred and something. If a light is missing, someone walks out, four hours in the dark, following a leaner path, because a leaner path across a bog has never once been wrong and because there are not enough of us for anybody here to have the luxury of a stranger.

One of the two on the rim is Krull.

I have a sentence for her. I have had it a long time — long enough that it has stopped being news and started being a property of my chest, like the ribs — and the only place it can be said is under the turnover, standing still, in the four minutes when a kilometre of metal makes the only noise the Lees has. That means walking in. Nobody here says anything they were not willing to stop working and walk for. The ethics arrived at by acoustics; the whole moral architecture of my people, resting on the simple fact that a shout dies at twenty paces.

I have not walked in.

And here is the thing I would put down into the bog with a marker only I can read, if I were the kind of person who could bear to keep it above ground: some nights, holding the lamp, I understand that the one way to be walked to is to go dark. That she would come. That four hours of peat and leaner-track in the frost is exactly the distance at which, in this world, a thing becomes sayable, and I could make her carry it instead of me.

I do not do it. But I know the shape of it the way I know the shape of the fourth swell in fog — not seen, deduced, and never for one night in doubt.

## Thirty-one

What I do instead, some nights, is hold the lamp in the window for one count longer than thirty.

It is nothing. In a shadowless dark, at that range, across that much moor, a single extra second is not a signal; it is not even a guess. There are two hundred figures in the tap-code of the works and none of them is *one beat more than was asked of me*. She would have to have been looking at Line Six, fourth hut, in particular, and going slowly, and minding — and if she were, she has no way to answer that is not nine kilometres long.

So the tally goes down into the pipes. Fifteen hundred and something, all present. The whole town feels the number in its boots and sleeps. The stone counterweights begin their fourteen-hour descent in shafts sunk into the peat, doling the day out in trickles. The frost comes on and starts furring my wire, ready for me to tap it clean before the fourth hour.

And I lie in the dark in a hut at the end of a line, breathing more often than you do, with my hand on the cold metal, waiting for the second turnover to arrive as a shiver a fly would make — which is all the town I get, and all the town I have arranged to get — and I keep the sentence, and I keep the extra count, and in the morning the cloud comes up through me and I stand inside it and listen.
