## The Clerk

First I thought: the toad.

Of course I did. Everyone does. The thing under the sump grate that has outlasted four generations of warden, fed by tradition, unexamined — it's the flattering seat. To be the oldest fact in the room, the one nobody looks at closely, sitting in the cold water at the ended end of the place while the ecology creeps away toward the throat. But I don't want it, once I hold it a moment. It's a costume with nothing inside. And whatever is down there under the grate is not thinking about the year eighty. It is not thinking about anything. That is precisely why the wardens can bear to feed it.

Then I thought: a warden, obviously — Ovey, who whispers because whispering is what the air allows, who has a daughter whose height he has read with his fingertips up a newel post and never seen against a sky. Or Halm, with a million and a third turns of the flywheel behind her and her hand on the crosshead, oiling ahead of a complaint by a week. There is something in me that leans toward that pipe the way the boilerfern does. To be that patient. To know a machine so completely that nothing in it is original but the design and the pace.

But I test it against my body and my body says no. Not the heat — anyone can survive being warm. It's the register. Speaking beneath the drip. Thirty terms and no tense, *gate, sump, screw, come, stop, help, scorch,* a language with no way to say *this happened.* I could learn the words. I could not stop wanting the tense. I would be nine days down there before I began, out of sheer pressure, telling somebody something that had already occurred, in a full voice, wasting air.

So I know where I am. I have known since the third paragraph. I am not in the Cut at all.

---

I am up the stair, and across nine meters of young rock, and through the plane trees, in the market office, at a table under the north window with the shutters half-drawn because half-drawn is what I can bear. My name is Isk. I am the clerk who writes the ledger.

The Cut is a chamber with the geology of a wound and the plumbing of a city and no memory whatsoever. Paper doesn't live there; a page put in a pocket at the stair-head is pulp by the sump. So all of that place's past is here, in dry air, in calfskin, in my hand. The Cut keeps only *what is.* I keep *what was.* Between us we make a whole thing, and neither of us can do the other's half.

On Sundays Sennet comes up with the week inside his head. I hear him on the last third of the spiral, muttering in that flat register, and it does not sound like a man talking to himself; it sounds like a book being carried carefully up a stair, and I have learned not to greet him until he is through the door and has set it down. Then he stands — he will not sit, he says sitting shuffles the order — and he gives me moss depths, cistern level, shrimp count, harvest weight, seam temperature to the tenth, the fig calendar, the creep of the boilerfern line toward the throat. Ninety-one point four. Two moths, both correct. Screw bearing beginning to think about complaining.

I write at his speed. That took me years. My hand wanted to run ahead and finish his phrases, and he would stop, and wait, and start the item again from its beginning, because the pipe-word has taught him that a thing interrupted is a thing not said. Now I write as slowly as the beam strokes. My letters got better. Everything in me that used to hurry has been geared down past all reason by a man reciting numbers in a room above a market.

---

Here is the part I did not want to arrive at, and have arrived at anyway, which is how you know it's the true one.

My name is on the list.

Scratched with a nail into the glaze above the fifth bunk, in among thirty others, first names only, no reasons — the reasons are all the same reason. I am the twenty-second from the top. I was nineteen. I lasted fifty-one days.

Nobody made me put it there. It isn't a punishment; the wardens don't think that way, they haven't the air for scorn. It's just the register of the fifth bunk, the way the shrimp count is the register of the cistern. On my last night I lay in the heat I had failed and read the list upward with my fingertips, the way you read moss, and I could name every one of the twenty-one above me by the shape of their scratching, and I still can. Then I borrowed a nail and added myself, and went up the spiral at dawn with my eyes shut against what was coming.

The pleat took a year to leave my hands. That was the grief, and I have never told anybody it was a grief. For a year a doctor could have identified me. My palms were soft and nicked and re-nicked and slow-closing, exactly like the walls that had taken the skin off them — nothing down there heals smooth, and I was down there, and I was not healing smooth, and it was proof. Then the dry air had its way with me and I healed, entirely, like anyone. Now I can judge cloth. I can tell a penny from a button in my pocket. I cannot lay a fingertip on gauge-moss and say *ninety-two, and falling since the second bell.* I had that for fifty-one days and I lost it, and it is the only skill I have ever had that the world does not have a second copy of.

And I have heard the market square from both sides. That's mine. Nobody else's. The four of them have each built a square out of cartwheels and boot-scuffs and the rumble of the fish stall's shutters, and no two are the same shape, and none of them is the shape of the square, which I cross four times a day. I built mine too, in fifty-one days, and it was a long room, narrower than this, with the fish stall at the wrong end. When I came up and saw it I did not feel corrected. I felt as though I had two squares now, and would always have two, and that the false one was the one I could hear from inside.

Sometimes, writing, I hear a cart go over the flags and I think: Halm knows which cart that is. And she is nine meters under it with her hand on the gate-lever, and it is weather to her, and to me it is Tovey's boy with the milk, late again.

---

The physician's letter is nailed inside the office door, the one about the large slow hearts, and the town enjoys it, and I read it perhaps once a week the way you touch a doorframe going out. But the letter is wrong in its cheerfulness, and I am the only person above ground who knows how, and I don't correct it, because a clerk's whole function is to write down what was said and not what she thinks about it.

I have ruled the pages ahead to the year eighty. Sennet and I did the arithmetic together, twice, four years apart, and got the same answer twice, which is what arithmetic is for. Around then the last hot pipe drops below the boilerfern's line; the Slow goes up the spiral in pieces; Pell, or whoever is Pell by then, carries the wick-vine up in a wet sack to wherever the ground has opened next. The Cut fills with its own water and goes quiet in the fuller sense. It will fall to this table to write that down. In ink, in dry air, in a room with a window — the only part of the Cut's life that will ever be recorded somewhere the Cut itself could not survive.

I have the sentence half-planned and I will not plan it further, because things that hurry break.

Last Sunday Sennet gave me the shrimp count and then the reason nobody eats them, which he gives me every few years as though I hadn't been down there and learned it in the dark from a woman who is dead now. *You do not eat the thing that is doing your dying in adv—*

He stops there. They all stop there. It isn't superstition and it isn't grammar; it's just a sentence the Cut does not finish, the way it does not finish a fracture face, the way nothing down there wears round.

So the page in front of me stops there too. I have left the line open and moved down and started the next item, harvest weight, four and a fifth, and the ink is dry, and the shutters are half-drawn, and somewhere under my boots a beam is a quarter of the way through its stroke.
