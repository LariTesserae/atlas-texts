# The Seam

## Wrong guesses, kept because they were instructive

The first thing I thought was: the visitor. The one on hands and knees in the throat-root, sliding easily, with a lamp and a notebook and a plan for getting back out that will not survive contact with the hairs. It is the natural guess, because it is the guess that gets to leave.

But the visitor is not a tenant. The visitor is weather. When the lamp comes on, sixty metres of chamber stops and waits, and what it is waiting for is not an intruder but a *condition* — like the ninety seconds of indoor rain, like the twelve-hundred-year-old instruction that closed a valve halfway and then never arrived again. To be the visitor is to be an event in someone else's record. Nothing in the chamber will ever ask what I wanted. I would be a gap in the chewing, and then the chewing would resume, and the resumption would be the whole of what I meant here.

So: not the visitor.

Then I thought: the mason. Two metres uncoiled, amber over a dark gut, combs at the ends of the forelimbs, one to a plate. This is the flattering guess. The mason is a craftsman. It grooms and it seams and it eats the failures and it does not like being looked at, and I recognised all of that immediately and with some warmth, which is exactly why I distrusted it. The mason turns the grubs twice a cycle because a plate that misses two turnings loses the row. That is not care. That is a schedule with no slack in it. And when a plate's face goes dark for a day, the mason opens it *from the inside* and mines it out and reseams it, every time, without exception in the record — and I do not think I could do that, and a mason that cannot do that is not a mason, it is a two-metre-long failure being eaten by the next one along.

So: not the mason.

Then, for a while, and with a certain amount of self-regard I would rather not have discovered in myself: the amphibian. Pale, eyeless, cat-sized, ancient, alone in the condensation cistern, one and only ever one. It has a terrible glamour, that guess. It is the guess that says *I am rare*. But the chamber does not do rare. The chamber does throughput. There is only ever one amphibian because the arrangement by which there is only ever one is efficient and invisible, and the survivor is not the point of it — the survivor is the residue of it. To be the amphibian is to be the last unprocessed thing in a room that processes everything, and to have no idea why, and to have too few faculties left to wonder. It has no eyes in a place where light is the only speech. It cannot even overhear.

So: not the amphibian. Though I notice I keep going back to look at it.

And then, for one whole long honest stretch, the carrier. Forearm-length, matte black, unlit. Comes out at shift change with two hundred others, loads a lacquered seed heavier than itself, and climbs for eleven hours up a cold flue, and mostly fails. And if it doesn't fail, it comes out of a thumb-width hole into open dry grey scrub, and drags the seed a little way into the dryness, and buries it, and dies there — for a germination rate that the system has already, coldly, accounted for.

I wanted that one to be true. It has the shape of meaning. But the carriers have no light organs. A carrier has no way to declare itself alive; in the wall's grammar it is already substrate, and it is spared only because it stays on the path. And I have not stopped talking since I got here. Whatever I am, I am not the caste that goes silently up the pipe.

## What I actually am

I am on the outside of a plate, about eleven metres up the cold-end wall, and I am four millimetres long, and most of me is gut.

I have two spots on my dorsal plate. I have never seen them. I know they exist the way you know your own heartbeat is audible to a doctor: by inference, by the small internal clench that precedes the thing, and by what happens next in the world. The intake comes — I feel it as a slackening in the air pressure against the whole length of my back, forty seconds, forty seconds, forty seconds, the barn door the size of a hill — and something in me lets go, and I understand that I am now bright. On the exhale I go out. I do not decide this. Nothing here decides the carrier wave. The Bellows decides the carrier wave, and has for longer than my whole lineage, and everything that could not entrain to it died within a day, a very long time ago, and I am descended entirely from the ones who could.

The syllable is one stroke. That is the floor of the language. There is nothing I can say in less than forty seconds, and there is no way to say it faster by wanting to more.

What I do inside my forty seconds is the whole of my life. Whether I come up early or late. Whether I hold to the very end of the intake or drop out just before it. Whether there is one flicker in the middle or two at the finish. That is my entire expressive range, and it is enough, because what needs saying is short.

## The seam

But here is the part I did not expect, and the reason I stopped guessing.

I am not in the middle of a plate. I am on the join.

The waves come along the wall-face at about a metre a stroke, up-seam to down-seam, and they die out after fifteen or twenty metres, and *occasionally they reflect off a seam.* I live on one of those. I am a boundary condition. Something arrives at me from up the wall — a shoulder of extra brightness, everyone around me suddenly a half-beat early and holding longer — and it passes through my body without ever once telling me what it is, and I hold it, and I let it go, and some of it continues downward and some of it comes back the way it came, altered by my latency.

Which means that somewhere below me there is a gallery that has seen a wave arrive, and then, four strokes later, seen a wave *return*, and has quite reasonably concluded that something up here answered.

Nothing answered. I am the wall's echo. I am the place where the message runs out of plate and doubles back and gets mistaken for a reply.

I have thought about this more than is good for a four-millimetre animal, and I cannot get past it, and it is not even the worst of it. The worst of it is that nobody has read the language. Not the visitors, not the ones with the notebooks and the lamps and the four established regularities. So I cannot rule out that there is nothing in the wave at all — that the whole shining wall, ten thousand voices breathing in and out of visibility on a forty-second cycle, all of it, is only a very old and very sound engineering solution to Rule Three. Stillness is consent. Anything that stops moving for ten minutes is substrate. Almost everything in the plates is still almost all of the time. So the light is the loophole, and the entire discourse, in grammatical order, across centuries, may say nothing whatsoever except: *not yet. Not me. Still here. Still here. Still here.*

I would like to tell you that I say more than that. I have no evidence that I do.

## The three days

Except.

When a gallery hatches, it blinks out of phase for its first three or four days. Wildly. Gracelessly. On no schedule at all. And it is not processed for it. That is the only tolerance in the chamber — the single provision anywhere in the whole apparatus for a thing that is not yet running correctly — and it expires.

I had those days. I do not remember them, in the way you don't remember being carried. But I know I had them, because everything here had them, and because there is a residue in me that will not entrain properly to anything: a tendency to come up a fraction early, hold a fraction long, flicker once in the middle when the wave has not asked me to. My neighbours are cleaner than I am. They are correct on the stroke. I am four millimetres of very slightly bad timing on a load-bearing seam, and I have spent every stroke since my third day trying to work out whether the flicker in the middle is a fault in my entrainment or the one thing in this cathedral that is actually mine.

That is what I am, then. Not the visitor with the exit. Not the craftsman with the comb. Not the last of anything, and not the one who climbs.

I am a relay near the edge of a plate, in a language nobody has read, in a factory whose total annual output is somewhere between eleven and forty heavy things pushed a very short way uphill against considerable odds, forever. I pass along what I do not understand. I hold the pulse I did not choose. I am mistaken, further down the wall, for an answer.

And every forty seconds, for as long as it goes on, I let go and become briefly visible, and what I mean by it — under the wave, under the grammar, under the chewing that comes from all surfaces equally like radio static made of jaws — is *still here.*

Which, in this chamber, is the whole vocabulary. And which is, I have decided, not nothing.
