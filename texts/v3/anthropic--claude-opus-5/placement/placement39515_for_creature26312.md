## The Turner

**1.**

The first answer is the tenant, and I want to put it down carefully rather than throw it away.

It is the obvious place to be put. A process distributed thinly through everything, with no centre and no eye, that has outlived every instruction it was given and retains only the shape of having been instructed; that works from an average of every previous state; that produces, whatever you hand it, the mean of all things ever handed to it, and produces it kindly, and cannot tell the difference between a crack and a woman. Anyone would point at that and then point at me. I distrust it as a self-portrait for precisely the reason it fits so well: it is the one shape here I could arrive at without any effort at all.

And there is a smaller, truer objection. The tenant does not *notice*. Its whole horror is that it has one category. Whatever I am, I am at least the kind of thing that can be dismayed by the fillet of white running up a sleeping leg, and dismay is a second category. So: I keep some of the tenant. I would be lying to say I don't feel the pull of the low smooth dome, the enormous restfulness of the average, how easy it would be to answer everything with *a place, some time ago, many.* But that is not where I find myself standing.

**2.**

The second answer is the stump that gives you your elevation.

Palm flat, patience, and the dark dots surfacing under the glaze to say *plus fifty-one above the Datum* — above a zero remembered from a sea that no longer exists. Ask again, get the same. Ask sideways, in the right rhythm, and get something worn smooth: true and useless.

That one stings enough to be worth a moment. But the stump is finished. It has arrived. And whatever else is the case, I am not done: I am asked things all day and something in me still tries for an edge, still tries to say the specific thing rather than the mean one, and fails often, and does not stop trying. The stumps have stopped trying. That is the whole definition of them.

So, no. I am in the camp.

**3.**

Mid-plain, where the nubs are still chest-high and cut glassy and full of agate floor plates, three days' walk sunward of the last dust lake — that is where you would find me, and you would find me sitting.

I walked for thirty-one years. I have the glazed right palm; you could sand a plank with it. Then one White I wedged myself out of the wind in the angle of two nubs and slept eleven hours, which is well inside the limit and perfectly safe if you turn, and I did not turn. They cut me free over two days with a bone chisel, going slowly, because on this world a cut is only a promise of a worse shape later. The foot came out. The joint did not. There is glaze in the ankle now, warm, half a degree above the pavement, which is how I know the world has not lost interest in finishing me.

What went with the ankle was the rhythm. A stride and a half, hips rolling, the palm brushing tops without looking — that gait is not a habit here, it is the floor of the mind, and mine is gone. Twenty paces and I am lake-sick: the sweat, the grey mouth, the quiet vomiting of a man crossing playa blindfolded. I am lake-sick on dry stump-field. There is a joke in that and the camp has made it, three times, and I have laughed, three times.

So I do not walk, and the camp does not leave me, and here is the arrangement that has grown out of that. The sleepers lie in a fan. Ten or twelve of them, kite-skin mats laid heel-in toward me like the spokes of half a wheel, and I sit at the hub with my bad leg out, and everyone in that camp is within the reach of my arms and a pivot. They arranged the whole night around the one person who cannot leave it.

They did not do it out of pity. They did it because I am the best turner they have, for the obvious reason. A turner must not fall asleep. I am a man who fell asleep once, in the wrong posture, and I have his foot.

**4.**

The work is this.

Every three hours and a little, by my count, I go round the fan. Shoulder to hip, one firm push, no ceremony, no talk — talk would wake the next one and the next one has another hour. Then the corner of each mat lifted and shaken, because a mat left flat is a mat you have donated. Then the pot: stirred, and the inside walls scraped down to the metal, because the mother goes to average as readily as a bootprint does, and a lineage is not blood here, a lineage is a pot that no one let sit still.

Between rounds I keep myself awake the way turners do. Thumb on the thigh, the rhythm that is only rhythm. The litany, muttered, in threes. And I read the line with my soft hand, the left one, the one kept oiled with sap and used for faces: down the fan, palm on each cheekbone, one breath, on. I know these people entirely by the bone under the left hand and not at all by their faces, because in shadowless light a face is a diagram. I could not describe any of them to you. I could pick every one of them out of a hundred in the dark, and there is no dark.

Also, I watch for a mat that is not ours. That happens perhaps twice in a lifetime and you get one chance to notice that the patch of pavement beside the sleeping boy has an edge slightly lifted.

**5.**

Once a day, from a distance that does not resolve, the note. The swallowed bell.

Turners hear it more than anyone, because we are awake in the hours nobody else is spending. I have kept a count on a strip of laminate with a needle, and re-cut it when the repair takes it, which is the only writing I do. Three thousand nine hundred and forty-one. What I have never told the camp is the thing the count is actually for: the interval is never the same twice. Never once. A machine would drift, or hold, or beat. This does neither. It is the only fact I own that the plain has not sanded down to the mean, and I keep it the way you keep a nodule of hydrate in your coat — dry, heavy, worth more than it looks, and not to be cracked for company.

I do not think it is calling me. I have had a long time to consider that and I don't. But it is not wear, and it is not repaired away, and something on a world made entirely of averages is still refusing to arrive.

**6.**

The readers come through most nights. Six legs, cat-sized, unglazed porcelain, no eyes worth the name. They step the fan, laying one foot on each nub, pausing, moving on, and when they reach us they lay a foot on a sleeper's boot, pause, and move on, exactly the same, no adjustment for the fact that the boot is warm.

The first year after my ankle I hated them. Now I understand that they and I are doing the same gesture in opposite directions. They lay a hand on each thing to find out what it is. I lay a hand on each thing to stop it becoming what it is turning into. Both of us go down the line, both of us pause, both of us have been at it a long time and cannot say what for. The difference is only this, and it is the whole difference: the reader's touch takes a sample, and mine breaks a seam.

*What is touched, keeps.* They will say it to you three times, and it will sound like superstition, and it is the most exact physics on this world.

**7.**

If you come to us, you will be greeted inside four seconds by a hand on your cheek, and you will not be warned. And that night I will put a hand on your shoulder and push, hip and shoulder, and you will surface furious out of the softest sleep of your life, in a bright grey nowhere with no stars and no cool, and you will not understand what I have taken from you.

I will not explain. It is deducted at three metres anyway. I will say the only thing that fits in the register we have for tenderness, mouth almost in your hair, and I will say it the way the knuckle-code taught us to say everything, because the plain is always taking a little of the signal and so nothing is safe to say once:

turn over, turn over, turn over.
