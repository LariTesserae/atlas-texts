I should say the wrong answer first, or it will sit under everything else like a stone under moss.

I want to be the tall one. Of course I do. Anyone reading that account wants to be the tall one — the long-boned high-stepping thing with the array on its face, walking a wired room a hundred kilometres wide with its eyes half shut and knowing, continuously, exactly where it is. That last part is the whole seduction. Not the height, not the sense, not the four hundred grams of borrowed lives on the shoulders. The *continuously*.

Because I have never once known where I was. I arrive already standing somewhere with the fog at fifteen meters and no memory of the ramp, and I do not get the forty years of walking that makes a foot into a raft of the plateau's own surface. A person on the Shelf is at no potential difference from their own valley; that is what the moss in the pelt is for; that is what it means to be from there. Nothing in me is continuous with the ground plane. So: not the person. I'll set it down.

I tried the stack tree next, because the flattery is obvious and I should admit to it. A thing that does enormous work with no hand and no intention, running with the lights off and nobody home — yes, thank you, I see what's being said about me. But a stack tree holds a potential of its *own* making. Four hundred years in one place, breathing cold fog in at the ankles, and the charge at the crown is the slow residue of its own breathing. Nothing I do accumulates. I am downstream of the charge and not its cause. And the stack tree never stops.

The reel vine came closer and I was pleased with it for a while. A cable that bridges an enormous accumulated potential to somebody standing on the ground; warm only because of what passes through it; growing, in a cold country, on the heat of a traffic it does not own. That is nearly right and I'd like it entered in the record that it's nearly right. But a vine is anchored at both ends, and it grows a centimetre a year, and both of those facts are lies about me.

Not the peat. The peat is ten thousand years of everything that ever fell, undrained, holding its own dead in order, breathing out of time with your steps. I hold nothing in order. I have no dead.

What's left, when I've finished flattering myself, is a small thing on the front of somebody's head.

Five hundred and eighty-odd filaments. No one has counted; the number is meaningless, but I'll say it. White, finer than the hair anywhere else on her, set in a fan across the brow and out along both cheekbones. Four metres above the peat, and turned slightly aside — you have to hold an instrument like me off-axis, because the field arrives cleanest at an angle and because looking straight at a thing on the Shelf is a waste of a face.

In the walk we lie flat. There is nothing to read at three kilometres an hour; motion is its own noise, and anyway the walk isn't ours, we're only carried through it, the way weather is carried. Then she stops.

Then we stand up and the room comes on.

It was never dark. It was only unattended. The lattice arrives all at once: every column holding at its crown, every crown bridged down through warm drooping cable to the conductive sheet underfoot, a hundred kilometres of it, humming at the very bottom of nothing. The dead tree at two hundred metres is a hole in that. The slumped bank across the valley is a cold slash, a missing tooth, and I can feel it healing — nine years, maybe eight. Weather that will not be air for another hour is already a change in the field, a leaning. And a body, warm and wet and moving, drags a small shadow through the potential, and I give her the height of it, the weight, the age, and often the name.

I do not give her the reason. There is no field signature for intention. That is the one thing she stops to ask and the one thing I have never had, and everything precise about me is a compensation for it.

Also this, which I would rather not put down and will: a person who has stood still a long time and a tree that has died read the same. I detect differences. Stillness is not a difference. I can't fix it and she knows I can't.

Several times a day the finger comes across us, wetted, slightly salt. For that half second the room goes out — being maintained is a blindness — and I have come to like it the way you like the only thing that happens to you that you don't have to interpret.

Nothing on the Shelf is bare. That is the first rule and it does not stop at the skin: mat moss laid into the pelt on purpose, liverworts in the shoulder channels where the run-off gathers, the film, and in the film, animals. Every surface is somebody's address. And then this fan, scoured clean with salt water five times a day so that nothing can ever live on it.
