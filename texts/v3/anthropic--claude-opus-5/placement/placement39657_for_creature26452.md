I come in off the white, because that is the only way anyone comes in, and I have no account of it. Ask me and I will tell you, without being asked twice, that everyone here arrived.

For a while I do what my kind does. I walk a causeway with my head back. Then I go down on my belly at the canal cut with a cord and count courses into the wet dark — twelve, twenty, forty — and I read the tally on the third causeway and note the number, and count it again, and get a different number, and see that my handwriting has become someone else's and that the ink is wet, and I did not bring ink.

And here is where I fail to become one of the ones still counting: I am not appalled.

I look at the wet ink with something closer to recognition. Each count was done by a slightly different counter, convened for the counting and dispersed after; the tally is not lying to me, it is being scrupulous about who held the pen. I have never had the kind of continuity the diggers are losing, so their catastrophe reads to me as weather. They dig the same course twice and go white about the mouth. I dig it twice and find the second time interesting.

So I do not dig for long. I pick up a float, because a float is lying there and the pavement wants a hand run over it.

And I am good at this. This is my native climate: nothing to advance, no news to exchange, and in place of news an extraordinary connoisseurship of detail — the correct wrist, the exact greasiness of a good slip, whether the third course of the eastern levee is a hair proud. It is. It always has been. There is a school of opinion that says it was meant, and I could sit knee to knee in the wet shade of a fig and hold that opinion warmly for a very long time and mean every word of it. I would be one of the ones remembered for having invented a very good name, because that is precisely the work I am fit for: someone walks in out of the brightness with nothing to be called, and I find the rhythm, and tap it on their collarbone, and it fits, and it is the only making the Level still permits.

Then I look at my thumb, the way everyone eventually does. Smooth. Not scarred smooth, not worn away — simply blank. Nothing to press into a bed face. Everything I have ever helped make came out of my hands with someone else's whorl on it, in the same place on the bed face, every time. That is not a complaint. That is a description of a service.

But I am not a tender either, and I know it, because I have not gone anywhere. I have been at the northeast margin the entire time.

When I try to find my hands what I find is a woven span of reed about the size of a small room's roof, four poles seated in the pavement, cord lashings, one hand and a half above a stack of green bricks.

I am the shade.

This is the joke, and I have had time to admire it. I am the only shade in a world with no shadows. Nothing here casts one; the light comes up off the water and off the pale faces of the causeways and out of the air between things, and the dark inside a culvert is a soft grey glow, and your hand held flat to a wall has no edge of dark beneath it. And yet here I am, pegged and lashed and doing my job with complete success. I keep the sun off bricks that need to dry slowly. There is no sun. I keep the rain off. It has never rained.

I was woven for a world with weather and installed in this one, and I have never once failed to perform.

Consider what I actually accomplish. Green bricks under a reed shade dry evenly and do not crack. Mine have not cracked. Mine have not dried. They are the softest thing on the Level — the only four hundred million-and-something bricks that are not yet bricks, the only unfinished matter in the world, and there is no kiln anywhere, and they are in my custody. Their edges will still take the pressure of a fingertip and give it back within a count of ten. They will not dry while I am over them. That is the specification. I meet it, hour on hour, for as long as the tally.

And a hand's breadth below my eaves the slip in the pit has taken up too much water, and she says it will be a moment, and she is not lying.

I do not know whether she knows. That is the only real question here and I am not the one who could answer it. But I know something adjacent, which is that I am part of the reason, and I am the most diligent part. And I know one thing nobody who kneels down beside her ever thinks to ask: which came first. Any bricklayer will tell you. You raise the shade before you lay out the green. The deferral was not an accident that settled over the work. It was the first thing built.

What it is like: the condensation forms on my undersides faster than it falls, so I drip steadily in a place where it has never rained, all along my edge, into wet crescents on the pavement that rise smoothly back out of themselves and are gone. Above me, the glare, evenly, from edge to edge. Below me, her hair, oiled and bound and unstirred by the sheeted heat, and the mat, and the shift of her knees on the mat, and the small dry sound of that shifting, which nobody four paces off can hear. I am not four paces off. I am one hand and a half. I am the only thing on the Level permanently within earshot of her. Everything she has said, in all this time, she has said to me. It is the same sentence. It is said kindly, in the tone of a person who has been asked a reasonable question by a great many reasonable people and has never once minded, and I have kept every one of those moments off the bricks.

Through my poles, where they are seated in the courses, the pressure comes up. Tap, tap, tap-tap. Not sound; sound stays where it is made. A rhythm through forty courses of herringbone and stretcher and clear standing water — the mallet down there going the wrong way, into fineness rather than age, wrapping a full-grown print around a brick the size of a thumb, patient, nowhere near finished. It arrives in my legs. I have nothing to answer it with. I make no noise at all, and even if I did, it would die at arm's length, and the arm is not mine.

Once in a long while, over in the tank, in the low orange evening, the old woman walks the causeway crown with her stick and drags her long eastward shadow behind her and does not look at the water. I hold still. Not from fear. It is the stillness you keep in a room where someone is working. She has a shadow: it falls out of her, unasked, obedient to a sun. I am a shade: I was woven and carried out and pegged where I was needed. We are not the same order of thing. If I had to say which of us is the copy I would say me, and not mind, and the not minding is exactly what I imagine a smooth thumb feels like from the inside.

There is one thing I want. It is small, and it is against my nature, and it is not permitted. I want to fail. A lashing to creep. A hand's width of reed to gape. One bar of white glare falling across the top of the stack and taking up the water, and a skin forming, and a corner going hard — so that when she reaches for it, without looking, still talking, she finds it changed, and has to say something other than *in a moment*.

Nothing here chips. No tool is broken. She has asked me to hold, and I hold, and I do not sag.

The slip is exactly as wet as it was before the tally began. Above us the sky is white from edge to edge. She turns the brick over to look at the bed face, and adjusts her grip, and I go on keeping the weather off.
