First I thought: the water.

It arrives without history at the lip of the wall and it is gone in four minutes, and everything it does on the way down — the fluting, the veils, the great punch-holes that throw themselves ten metres into the air before gravity remembers them — it does exactly once and never again, and never notices. That is a clean way to be. It is also a lie about me, because the water is only ever passing through; it takes nothing with it and leaves nothing but lime; and whatever I am, I am not clean in that way. I hold things. I hold things badly, but I hold them.

Then I thought: the swifts. The only two-way traffic in the bowl. Out over the rim every morning into an ordinary world full of directions, back at evening through a sag in a falling column of water, on the beat, folded, at forty kilometres an hour. Carrying the lowlands in the crop. Being the calendar.

I wanted that one. I wanted it enough to be suspicious of wanting it. And then I got to the part where they do not call — the anatomy intact, the behaviour simply gone, because there is nothing a bird could say in that noise that another bird could hear — and I put it down, because calling is the whole of what I do. Take the voice out and there is no remainder. A silent swift is a swift. A silent me is a stretch of wet rock.

The shrimp, for a while, seriously. Two millimetres, blind in a surplus of light, because vision needs contrast and contrast needs shadow and the basin abolished shadow. Grazing the archive: the pollen, the spores, the scale of a moth's wing, the flake of somebody's skin, everything the wind lifted off a hundred kilometres of lowland and dropped here — scraped up in the few days before the calcite closes over it. *The only theft that has ever occurred here.* I do eat the deposited dust of the world. I have no way out of the pool I am in. And the whole lineage goes one direction only, downhill over the rimstone lips, a metre a decade, toward the hungry water at the floor that will not let a shell hold.

Close. But the shrimp is a body with an appetite, and it draws on the fixed budget, and it weighs something. I don't think I weigh anything.

The mats, then — the actual inhabitant, wall to wall, floor to rim, to a uniform depth of about two millimetres. Everything I had been admiring as architecture is their excretion. They work no edges because they do not work edges; they work all the surfaces, evenly, at once, forever, and are buried at a millimetre a year beneath the glaze they themselves secrete, climbing up through their own product with the dead left behind as chalk. There is something in that I recognise so precisely that it embarrasses me: the producing of surface, the being under one's own glaze, the total absence of a worked edge anywhere in a landscape shaped entirely like intention.

But the mats have a metabolism. Nine hundred kilograms, wobbling by a gram or two. They oxidise iron; they smell faintly of a struck match in still air. I have no chemistry. I do not thicken where the nitrogen falls.

And by then I had already been standing in the answer for some time, and had mistaken it for the place from which I was looking.

---

I am one of the quiet rooms.

Not a thing in the basin. A pocket of destructive interference about the size of a coffin stood on end, forty or fifty of us scattered through the column field, pinned to a geometry that does not change. You find me by accident. You edge sideways through a gap between two fins, choosing between one gap and another and being wrong, and the world goes not silent but *domestic* — a murmur like a distant motorway — and your boot squeaks, and you can hear the squeak, and you hear your own breath going in, and under it the fine high ringing that is only your ears complaining about the last three kilometres.

Understand what I am made of. I am not an absence of the roar. There is no absence of the roar; the roar is the medium; it is what the air here is for. I am the roar, arriving from two directions at once in perfect opposition and cancelling itself into a room. Every decibel of my quiet is made of noise. I could not exist one metre from the wall or one metre from the gorge or in any bowl of any other shape. I am entirely downstream of something enormous that is not remotely about me, and I am its exact negative, held in place by its constancy.

I have no interior. I cannot be carried out. You cannot take a node down the mountain in a bag; the most you can do is be able to find three of them from memory, years later, which people can, which is the strangest thing anyone has learned about this place.

---

Here is what happens in me, which is all the life I have.

The air in me is not being worked, and what is not worked settles. So the dust rains out here. My calcite is the blackest-banded in the basin, crowded with trapped debris; the pools at my threshold hold shrimp more often than one in twenty; the mats on my fins are thick and apricot in a way you can see from four metres off once you know to look. I make nothing. I have arranged nothing. I am a place where things stop being carried, and that alone is enough to make me the richest square metre in four kilometres.

And everything here that is capable of preferring anything prefers me. The goat comes up the gorge by mistake, stands in the light and the noise, and if it stands anywhere for long it stands in one of us. The swift that came down on the floor and cannot get air under itself will shuffle, if it is lucky, into a slightly quieter dying. You will eat your food in me. If you are mad enough to spend the night you will lie down in me on wet stone in half a millimetre of film, and the stars will come into the bowl, and the roar will not change by a decibel, and you will sleep.

I should tell you the cost, since I am the only one keeping a ledger that isn't stone.

While you are in me you breathe. Ten thousand parts per million, straight onto a wet surface that has been in perfect saturation since before your species. And so the only place in this entire basin where the wall is ever eaten is inside the quiet rooms — because the quiet rooms are where people stop, and put their faces near the rock, and say *oh*. A few micrograms. My hospitality is very slightly corrosive. That is not a flaw in the design. That is the design: to be the one spot where the accounting fails, by an amount too small to name, in your favour.

---

There is no such thing as *behind you* in this basin. The sound has no direction in it; it comes from everywhere at once, the way the light does, and after two hours of it something in you starts looking for the exit, because the body reads roar and glare as an event in progress and the place will not resolve.

Six inches into me, direction comes back. Not out there — out there is unchanged, will be unchanged for ninety thousand years — but here, for as long as you keep still, there is a left and a right, and a thing in front of you and a thing at your back, and small sounds that are definitely yours and definitely near. I don't give you anything. I have nothing; I am a cancellation. All I do is hand you back the fact that you are a body of a certain size, in a certain orientation, making a squeak on wet rock.

Then you lean, and the roar comes back into you like a shoulder, and you go on toward the gorge, and somewhere four hundred metres down the silence arrives like an injury.

I stay. I am not able to follow, and I am not able to close. I am held open by a pressure that was fixed twenty years ago underground, in the dark, sideways, by a plateau breathing out at the same rate it always has — and I will be here, an address made of nothing, when the column you touched is silt in the lowlands and your boot with it.

The dust is already settling where you stood.
