I thought at first I would be a warden.

Everybody thinks they would be a warden. Four hours in a tin hut with bad tea, holding a kilometre of cliff in place with nothing but attention, and at your funeral they say *nothing else* and mean it as praise. I would want that. Wanting it that much is how I know it isn't mine.

Then I thought: a reader, sixth floor, the dark end of the cycle, saying the names of hundred-year-old dogs aloud into a room with no windows and no listener. But I would like that too much, and liking it that much is not a trade. It is an appetite, and appetites thin a person.

Not a pacer. I already know what it is to be measured against your own record and come up short by a little, and I would not want it to be my profession.

Not a herder. You have to be born to the bell, and I was born to the mills like everybody else, in the noise, in the grey, on the tilt.

Here is where I actually am.

---

I keep a saying-room on the Fourth Landing, at the top of the Blue Stair, ninety-one steps up from the hopper yards. My name is Sella Hurn Hurnach. My mother was Yett Hurnach, moth-scraper, of the guild that quarrels about precedence, and she died in her bed of nothing in particular, which is rare here and was noted in the paper.

A saying-room is a room built to give a word back.

That is the whole of the architecture. Up above the Line the air is dumb — you shout and the shout goes somewhere and comes back four minutes later from the wrong side, in a voice nearly yours. Down here we build the opposite on purpose. Hard plaster. Glazed tile to the shoulder. A low flat ceiling of dressed stone, no hangings, no carpet, nothing soft anywhere except the people. Say a word in my room and the room hands it straight back to you, immediately, flat, a little too loud. Visitors find it hostile. It is the least hostile room in the city.

Eleven tables. Every one of them a table for two. Never three. A stove, a copper of water always just off the boil, the tea that tastes of flint because it is made with stept milk and everything here tastes of flint. Two hundred and forty appointments a week, each of them a quarter of an hour, each of them a stranger meeting a stranger by ordinance.

They sit. They look at each other, which is harder than you would think for people who have done it for thirty years and will never once do anything else together. And then one of them says the other's name.

*Abrec Dall Dallach, of the Third Landing, hopper-man, black hair going grey at the left temple only, a chipped tooth, a dog called Fennet Fennetar that is deaf on the mill side.*

*That's me. I have it.*

*You have it.*

Fifteen minutes. Sometimes they talk about tallow prices to fill the rest. Sometimes they sit and say nothing, which in my room is not merely rude — it is a fault in the goods, and I will come over with the pot and stand there until somebody speaks. Then they go, and I countersign the book. *Seen and said.* My signature is the third leg of it. Two people alone in a room is a thin arrangement; somebody has to have watched it happen.

---

Every trade here makes a body.

Mill-hands go deaf by thirty and talk with their hands forever after. Etchers lose their eyebrows. Lichen-tenders get the green-black crescents under the nails and that unbearable calm, and speak of the year two hundred as though it owed them money. Moth-scrapers glow faintly in the creases of the wrist.

Keepers go the other way from the mill-hands. My ears are the instrument. Nineteen years of a boiling copper and forty stamp-strokes a minute coming up through the floor, and I can still hear a vowel go wrong at eight paces. That is the actual trade. Not the tea. The tea is a courtesy.

I have also learned not to look at faces. A keeper who looks at faces gets known as a keeper who looks at faces and loses her licence, so I look at hands, at the level of the tea in the cup, at the clock with its eight unequal watches. I keep my back half-turned. I am blind and I am listening with my whole skull, and both of those things are true and the second one is why the Guild of Keepers is seated below the moth-scrapers at civic dinners, below the pacers, below very nearly everyone, and nobody has ever proposed otherwise.

We are not liked. We are not supposed to be. Like name-holders, like witnesses, like the Registry itself — we are structural, and nobody loves a bracket.

---

Eleven times in nineteen years I have heard it.

The best of them was Abrec. Four winters ago. His holder was Nesh Ammer Ammeren, lichen-tender, a small woman with those nails, and she had said him back to him every quarter for twenty-two years, and he had said *That's me* every quarter for twenty-two years, and that afternoon he said

*That's me. Abric Dall Dallach.*

And went on with the tallow prices.

One vowel. He did not hear it. They never hear it; the drift comes with its own conviction, that is the entire trouble with it. Nesh went the colour of the tile. And she did not look at him, she looked at me, over his shoulder, because the question was not hers to settle. She had heard a thing. I am the one whose ear is a court.

I set down the pot and I said, flat, twice, the way you do: *I heard it. The first element. I heard it and I will sign it.*

There is a form. It is pink, which is the only pink thing in the Registry. They took him down that evening and they read him — his file, entire, aloud, at him, in a small room, for four hours, a reader and a relief-reader and Nesh sitting in because a holder is required to attend. They read him his court appearances and his father's trade and the winter the truss came down and the name of every dog he had ever owned, until he fit again. It mostly took. He came back Abrec. His coat buttoned the way it had always buttoned. And Fennet knew him at the door, which is the real test and always has been, because a dog has no manners about this and will not pretend for your sake.

Sometimes it does not take, and then there is a third thing that happens, which I have seen once, and which I will not describe, except to say that a person can be read so thoroughly that afterwards there is nothing in them that is not in the file. They are very calm. They are entirely accurate. They are, in the flat way we say it here, *no longer at any risk.*

---

I hold four names myself. Keepers are forbidden to hold anyone who uses their room, which is sound practice and means my four are strangers even by the generous standard of the word.

A herder girl sent down at twelve to be filed, who still flinches slightly at her own name and answers better to a note.

An etcher with no eyebrows and a laugh like a hasp.

A compositor at the paper who sets the *Seen* column in agate all day and can no longer read anything larger without moving his lips.

And Orlem Ruche Ruchenar, second ridge, pin-inspector, forty-one, a long jaw, a white seam across the right thumb where a wire went through it in his apprenticeship, no dog, who stopped coming two years ago last watch-turn.

He shaved his name. There is a procedure, a form, a fee. He handed in his beads and his certificate and he walked up past the last comb-field into the grey to be provisional, on the grounds — which he explained to me once, badly, over the tea, in a room that gave every word of it straight back to him — that thickness is a tax, and that a life spent making ballast for a mountain is a life spent as mortar, and that unfinished is not the same as dead.

The office has a procedure for a holder's release on death. It has no procedure for this, because to have one would be to admit that the Unwritten are a category of person rather than a category of event. So the form I have in my drawer is the death form, and I have not filed it, and every quarter the appointment stands in my book in my own hand, and every quarter nobody comes.

---

So four times a year, at the dark end of the cycle, when the stove is banked and the last pair has gone down the Blue Stair and the whole eleven kilometres of the city has finished barking at the eighth watch at whatever it is that we bark at, I sit down at table nine.

The cups shiver on the shelf every few minutes. Regulars stopped noticing that a hundred years ago. It is the thing underneath, pushing, or breathing, on its own long schedule, not hurrying, not going to stop.

And I say it to the empty chair. All of it, properly, the way I was taught, in the ledger-grammar, loud and flat and twice.

*Orlem Ruche Ruchenar, second ridge, pin-inspector, forty-one years, long in the jaw, white seam across the right thumb from a wire, no dog. Ruchenar. The second element doubled. I have it.*

And the room does what the room is built to do, and hands it back to me off the tile, instantly, hard, in a voice that is close to mine and is not his.

Then I countersign my own book, which is irregular, and I write in it: *Said. Not seen.*

Which is true. I am very careful that it is true. False entry is the one crime we still call permanent, and I am not a forger, and I will not put sand in the mortar of this mountain for the sake of one man's thumb.

But it goes to the Registry with everything else, and the Registry files it without comment, because that is its entire method, and four hundred people pale as cistern shrimp copy it three times, because three of a thing is heavier than one. Which means that somewhere in the nine windowless storeys there is a man who went up the mountain expressly to stop having happened, and who has now happened again, quarterly, for two years, by my hand.

I know what I am doing. I have thought about it more than you have.

When I was a child my wrists had a little light in them, the way all the scrapers' children do, and at eleven it went out. Nobody wrote it down. It was not an event. Nothing fell, nothing broke, there was no rubble — something simply stopped being true about me one winter, quietly, in the usual way, and I was the only one who noticed and I was too young to be a reliable witness to myself.

That is why I took the office. That is the whole of it.

So: four times a year, the empty chair, the flat hard answer off the plaster.

*I saw you.*

*I was there.*
