I would be found on the berm, not in the town.

Not a drowner. I have thought about it honestly and I would not last: twenty-two feet of larch and a whistle and a partner you can't hear, and the water coming back up on a schedule that is written down and is still, somehow, a surprise. They are the largest guild and the shortest-lived and they are impossible in company, and they have earned all three of those.

Not a scraper either, though I like the sound of the sheds — forty ranks of green, a knuckle apart, a library where all the books are made of verdigris. I like it too much. That is a visitor's liking. Two seasons of the rota and the catarrh and the stain in the knuckle-crease and I would like it the way you like weather.

And not a featherer. Everybody wants to be a featherer. It is the first thing anyone wants when they hear about this place: the dry hands, the mitts, the lamps, the wands, the silence, the girl in Sough with lines in her face that will actually hold a crease. Wanting it is wanting to be looked at. It is the wrong want and the town would know it in a week.

What I keep coming back to, and could not put down once I had picked it up, is the belt.

—

Two hundred and thirty-six feet of trough up the sunward face, boarded in larch and roofed in slate offcuts, with forty-one idler rollers in it, which is a coincidence the town enjoys and which I do not correct. Inside it, going up and coming back, is a flat drive strap of seven-ply reed-canvas, five inches wide, gum-waxed, spliced by scarf and stitched with reed twine. At the bottom it takes off a fast pulley in Susan's house. At the top it turns the crankshaft of the fans, and the fans beat one to four seconds over the bean beds, back and forth, making a wind in a basin that has never had one.

The town calls the belt the Reach. It calls me the Reach as well. My name is in the first column on the guild hall board and nobody has used it since my mother stopped, and she never liked the other, and told them so, and they went on.

—

Here is the whole of the trade.

Everything in Sough is graded by wetness — streaming, running, standing, sweating, damp, holding, dry — and everything is built to sit squarely inside one grade and stay there. Wholly wet lasts forever. Dry lasts as long as you can defend it. Half-wet lasts nine years and is therefore forbidden, and is designed out of every timber, every pile, every lintel, every sole.

The Reach is the one thing in this town that is deliberately half-wet, because it has to be in two places at once.

Its bottom end lives in Susan's house at *streaming*. Its top end, above the vapour ceiling on most days, lives in real weather at *holding* or, in a hard frost, at something close to actual dry. Somewhere between roller nine and roller thirty-two, depending on where the lid is sitting that hour, the belt crosses out of the town's air and into the world's, and that crossing is a moving line, and where the line stands is where the canvas rots.

So I walk it. Twice a shift, up and back, with a thumb for a tension gauge, an awl, a wax slab, and a stick of chalk. I find the line by hand — you can feel it, there is a place where the strap stops being cold-slick and starts being merely cool — and I chalk the roller-post where I found it, and I go on up.

The chalk builds. Twenty years of it on the inside boards of the trough, a smeared band from about knee height to shoulder, thick where the line likes to stand and thin at the ends, and you can read the wet seasons off it the way you read the black liverwort off a south wall on Slate Walk. Nobody reads it but me. There is no reason for anyone to. It is not a record kept for anything. It is just what a hand does when it is holding chalk and has been right about something.

—

Every forty-one seconds a slackness leaves the bottom pulley and comes up the trough after me. It is a small thing, a travelling loosening, and you can see it if you are looking down the run with the lamps behind you. If I am walking at my own pace it overtakes me at about roller nineteen. If I am carrying wax it gets me nearer twenty-five. Coming down, it meets me head-on and passes through the soles.

I have never heard the engines. I mean this exactly. I was born in the basin, so I have the notch, and by the time I was old enough to be any use I was already up in the trough where the crest arrives as a bulge in a strap rather than a sound. When people from the terraces ask me what Susan sounds like I have nothing to give them. I know her by hand. I have shaken hands with her about fifteen million times and I could not pick her voice out of a room.

That is not a complaint and it is not remarkable here. Everyone in Sough knows the governing fact of their life through a translation. Mine is just the plainest one, and the only one that has a length and a width and a stitch count.

—

Twice a shift I come out at the top, above the lid.

The fan-house is behind me and the six terraces are laid out under their lamps, and the featherers are moving up the rows in that slow deliberate way, touching flower to flower, hands out of the wet, saying nothing because at that distance nothing can be said. Below me the basin is full of white and the four chimneys stand out of it, and every forty-one seconds the whole lid shivers, and the shiver comes up the berm and arrives in my feet a beat after I have already felt it in the belt.

And up there, in the real air, my hearing is very good. It is better than yours. Everybody's is, in the bands the engine leaves alone.

So I have heard birds.

Not often, and never in the basin. Curlew mostly, going over the moor in the dark, and once in a wet March something small and repeating that I could not name and did not want to look up.
