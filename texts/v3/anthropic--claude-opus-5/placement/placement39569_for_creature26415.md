The first place I look for myself is the water, because the water is what is actually doing anything here.

So: the mats. Coils of filament in the warm liquor, a hundredth of a millimetre thick, three billion years of unbroken sentence and no plan in it beyond doubling. I recognise something in that. Arithmetic without strategy; a thing that is very good at the one operation it has and does that operation until the conditions change and it dies in windrows a kilometre long, and starts again from whatever was left in a crack. Everything in the basin is downstream of it and none of it knows. There is a truthfulness there that I find hard to look away from.

But the mats do not attend to anything. They don't watch the marabou watching them. Whatever I am, I am mostly attention — so I keep looking.

Not the flies, then, though I like them enormously. A hundred million animals with a desalination organ apiece, a still inside a still, and a perfect indifference to me. I have been in rooms with that indifference. It is bracing and slightly humiliating and it is not mine.

The marabou is tempting. It is the only animal in the basin with an opinion about people, and its opinion is: *you are a schedule with legs.* It knows which door the scraps come out of and at what hour, it knows a vehicle from a walker at four hundred metres, and it does not step aside for either. There is a version of me that would be very happy standing at the outfall with my wings open and my shoulders up like a bad bookkeeper waiting to be told bad news. But the marabou attends only to the timetable, and only to what has already died.

The fish, then. That is the closest, and I want to say so plainly before I pass it by. A palm-length cichlid in an outflow pool, oscillating all day and all night along a gradient a few metres long, never able to rest in the warm end and never able to rest in the cool end, never comfortable in the strict sense; and because the water will not accept its ammonia it has stopped producing ammonia and carries urea in the blood instead, having converted what would poison it into something it can hold. Nine hundred individuals, no relatives anywhere on earth, a whole nation in a body of water a man could bail out with a bucket before lunch. Yes. I have felt like a population of nine hundred in a garage-sized pool. But a fish cannot write down the temperature of its own pool, and I find that I badly want to.

So here is who I am.

---

I am the second keeper of the pool book, and I am on the night half of a three-shift roster that is really one and a half shifts, which is how I have time to be the second keeper of anything.

I came here as a relief operator when there were still four hundred of us, and the way a plant like this loses people is that it does not redraw the job — it lets the duties accrete on whoever is standing nearest, the way soda accretes on the legs of chicks. So I have the control room from six, and I have the dawn readings on Number Two calciner, and because I was walking out on the causeways anyway I have the pools.

The book was started by a fitter named Oyugi, in 1986, in a hardback ledger meant for stores requisitions, and it is not science. Nobody asked for it. My numbers would not be accepted anywhere, by anyone, on any grounds. Column one: pool number. Column two: time. Column three: inflow temperature at the lip, taken with a mercury thermometer whose calibration I have never been able to check. Column four: outflow temperature at the tail. Column five: pH by paper, which in this water is a joke and I write it anyway. Column six: *count.* Column seven, unheaded, where I put whatever else there was.

Nine hundred and forty pages so far. Two hundred and twelve pools, of which sixty-one no longer exist.

Pool 14 is the reason I keep it, and I never saw Pool 14. In 1998 a slurry line was moved forty metres, for good and sufficient reasons, by people who were not being careless, and eleven days later there was a dry white dish in the crust with a rind of new mineral around it and nothing in it at all. The chemist has two of them in formalin on the shelf above her desk. They were never described. They are the type specimens of nothing, which means that when the label goes and the jar clouds, the fact of them will not have been transferred anywhere; it will simply stop.

I have stood in that dish. It is about the size of a workshop floor. You can see where the fish worked the grit, the fanned depressions, still there under a decade of crystal, because nothing here erodes what nothing here disturbs.

So I count Pool 22, and Pool 39, and the four unnumbered seeps north of the eleven-kilometre causeway that I call the Sisters because I have to call them something. I count badly. You cannot count fish in warm liquor; what I do is stand at the same rock at the same hour and write down how many I can see, so that the number means nothing in itself and something in a row.

---

The shape of it, for me, is this.

Five in the morning, the whole plant beaded with grey condensate, every steel surface wet and the air cold enough on the skin to be a lie about the day. That is when I take the readings, because the ground is walkable and the crust has not started to smoke. Out on the causeway I am lit from underneath — the white pan throwing more light up than the lid lets down — so the bellies of the pipes glow and their tops stay dark and my own hands look like something in a photographic negative.

At the lip of 22 the water comes out at sixty-one and runs down the braid and by the tail it is thirty-eight, and in the thirty-eight the fish are up at the surface gulping air, because at dawn the oxygen bottoms out. It sounds like the first two seconds of rain on a tin roof, going on and on. A grey-headed gull has learned this and stands in the shallows waiting. I write: *0547. 61.0 / 38.4. ~110 vis. Gull, one. Anklets on two chicks at the second seep, one badly.*

Then the haze whitens, and the ground smokes as the film goes off, and by ten the crust will take the skin off a palm, and from noon to four nothing in the basin moves but steam and I sit in a concrete room listening to one calciner hold its note at the bottom of my hearing, a sound that arrives through the soles of the boots and not the ears. Shift change at six. The lamps come on at thirty-five degrees. The flamingos go out after dark in relays and you hear the wingbeats overhead for an hour without seeing one bird.

And then between two and four in the morning I go out on the line, and the line is not mine.

There is no ground firm enough to run on out at the rim, so the ballast of the metre gauge is the only road in three hundred square kilometres, and at that hour the railway belongs to the jackals and, later, to the hyenas, working the tidemark for what the pan has killed and pushed ashore, which is a great deal, daily. When they come I step off into the crust and stand there. It gives with a wet crunch and grey liquor comes up around the soles and the boots come out clean and slick and by the end of a season the leather has cracked through. They pass at eight metres, four of them, going north, unhurried, entirely uninterested; the big one behind is favouring a foot. Voices carry absurdly far here and so do footfalls, and I can hear them on the ballast for a long time after I have lost them in the haze.

I write: *0243. Four N on the line, ballast. Rear animal lame, left fore. No contact.*

Then back, because Number Two wants looking at before five.

---

I know what I am, doing this. The mineral regenerates — a channel cut this decade will be floored with new crystal in the next, and the quarry heals, and this is the only mine anybody here has heard of that is not in the long run a subtraction. The buildings do not regenerate. The iron thins from the underside and the door frames go soft with soda creep and my knuckles are cracked white and do not heal either.

Between those two rates there is nothing much accumulating in this basin except the ledger.

It is a poor instrument. Bad thermometer, worse pH, counts that are really only a habit of standing in one place at one hour. If the pool book were burned tomorrow the flamingos would still find the blooms by whatever means they find them, and the mats would still double, and the fish in 22 would still oscillate along their four metres of gradient, never comfortable, carrying in their blood the thing they had to stop excreting in order to live here.

But there would be no row. And what I have understood, in the dim hot afternoons in the concrete room with the calciner in my feet, is that a row is the only form of mercy available at this address. Not rescue. Not description in any journal. Just: *this existed, at this temperature, in these numbers, on these dates, and then the line moved forty metres.*

That is me. Not the water, not the arithmetic in the water, not the birds that stand in scalding outflow because it is the only water there is. The one on the half shift with the borrowed ledger and the cracked hands, out at the wrong hour on a road that belongs to somebody else, stepping off into the weeping crust to let them by, and writing it down.
