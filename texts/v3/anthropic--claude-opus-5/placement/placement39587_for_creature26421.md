Not the vault. I keep being drawn there and it is wrong. The vault is where things go to be finished, and I am not finished, and anyway the stone floor makes me feel the way a true mirror makes people feel — that something has been brought up from below at enormous cost in order to be more real than me.

Not the ninth terrace either. I have no gift for splitting and I would like to keep my eyes.

Not a carrier. A carrier walks up alone for eleven days with one spoon in a sling against her chest and she is, for those eleven days, the most singular person on the mountain, and afterwards she is nobody again and drinks. I have met the type. I am not the type.

So: down. Past the fern racks, past the condensate halls where the rich lean on the warm walls, past the last dormitory with its doorframe tally-scars going up the jamb like a rain gauge, down through the service gaps and the seams where the black sedge is winning, down below the lowest inhabited terrace, six hundred metres above the white floor, where the light is worst — where it comes up off the cloud so hard and even that you work with your chin tucked and your cap-flange down and you still see the glare inside your eyelids at night.

Down there the mountain arrives.

We call it the smooth. Officially it is new works: the courses that come up from the foundry, four centimetres a year, plate and girder and stair and gutter and dormitory shell, everything raw, everything burred, everything blinding — and everything the same as ten thousand other things already standing above it. Cast from one pattern. One stairwell with new addresses. One doorframe wearing another face.

I go in first, with my gang, before anybody lives there.

I am a faulter. Under-course, third gang. My hands are the usual: white all over in that fine shallow crosshatch, and along the left thumb a deeper one from a casting fin I met when I was nineteen and careless, which I did not have stitched because I wanted it to heal wide. My left boot is the red one. I take no salt. I speak with my mother's stop in my throat, a small k- catch before hard sounds, which I did not choose and cannot lose and will come back to.

That is the recital. Now the work.

We go into a new course while the plates are still cold from below and we spoil it. Everything. Every stair tread in every stairwell, every doorframe, every rail, every landing, every cupboard, every sill: one mark each, and no mark twice, ever, anywhere on the mountain. That is the whole of the trade and there is nothing else in it. A scratch of a particular sort in a particular place. A gouge. A burn. A wire of a certain colour twisted round a bracket in a certain wrong direction. A smear of household grease, ours, rubbed at shoulder height so that the first family to move in inherits a smell that is not theirs and complains about it for forty years and would weep if it were cleaned off.

We carry no punches. A punch is a blasphemy. A punch makes the same mark twice, which is to say it makes two places into one place, which is to say it kills a district quietly and nobody knows until a woman climbing to her own door comes out four terraces down in somebody else's kitchen and cannot say how. Our scribers are hand-ground and re-ground between marks, and their tips are deliberately fractured, so that each of them cuts a little wrong in its own way for a little while and then is spoiled again. Half of a faulter's day is spent ruining her tools. You get to like it. There is a good sound when a tip goes.

We do not count. Nobody counts, but for us it is not etiquette, it is the whole difficulty of the profession. I cannot say how many marks I have made. My book has no numbers in it — pages are found by their own particulars, a corner burned, a corner torn, a stain of beetle oil, a hair of Late's caught in the gutter of the binding — and each entry is a drawing, and the drawings are the only defence I have against myself.

Because this is how faulters end. Not blind, like the Nominators. Repeating.

You work thirty years and your hand develops a preference. It wants to go the way it has gone. It has a favourite angle, a favourite depth, a favourite little hook at the finish that you stopped noticing when you were thirty-four. And one day, without any sensation of it happening, you cut into a fresh doorframe the exact mark you cut into another fresh doorframe eleven years ago on the other flank, and the two frames are one frame, and two hundred people are living in a house with two insides.

That is a fusion. I have been in one. Not mine — I want that clear, and I would say so if it were, we all say so, it is the one thing we are honest about because there is no forgiveness available and therefore no reason to lie. It was a landing on the fourth terrace, west, and a boy went through the door of his aunt's rooms and came out of a door on the eleventh in the dark and could not get back, and when they finally walked him home he would not use stairs for two years. The maintenance guild cut the whole landing out with torches and replaced it and I marked the replacement myself, with my sleeves pinned back and four listers watching my hand, and the mark I used was a shallow crescent inside a scratch like a bird's foot and I will never use either again, together or apart, and both are drawn in my book on a page whose corner I bit.

So every night I draw the day. Every mark, its place, its depth, its direction, and every night before that I read backwards through the book at random for an hour, hunting my own habits, and when I find one — a curve I have begun to love — I stop making it forever. I have retired more shapes than I have kept. A faulter's book is mostly a graveyard of things she can no longer do.

Late comes with us into the smooth. Late is an aune, grey, hard-hoofed, entirely herself, with a split ear and a horn ground flat on one side from years of leaning on new burrs to scratch an itch she has never once located. She is old enough to be named and she came to the name by scarring, the honest way, over a lifetime, out of the general grey browsing sameness of aunes into a person with opinions. I bring young ones down on purpose. They come for the sedge in the fresh drainage seams and they cut themselves stupid on the fins, and it does them nothing but good. Two seasons in the smooth and a yearling will have a limp of its own.

And the dog is there. The dog is everywhere, so of course the dog is there, mildly wet, sitting in the up-light on a new plate with the shadow pooled on top of its skull, watching us work with the enormous patience of something that has never once been asked to be only one of itself. Nobody marks the dog. It has been proposed, occasionally, by the young. It is not done. We spend our lives making the mountain distinguishable and we keep, in the middle of it, one animal with ten thousand addresses that we feed and do not own, and if you want to know what the Notched actually believe, it is not in the vault, it is that: that we know exactly what we are doing to the world and we love the one thing we let alone.

I sit with the dog at midmorning when the fog comes upslope and everything begins to bead and thread and tick, and the whole massif goes quiet enough to hear one belt, somewhere, slapping its splice, and I am not required to be different from anybody for as long as that lasts.

My mother was a lister. She kept the birth book for our district and she kept it well; her hand was small and level and she wrote through two fusions and a bad winter and never once made an entry that could be confused with another entry.

I lay smooth for some minutes after I was born. The birth-woman was on another stair. Nobody will tell me how long, and I have asked in the way you ask a thing for forty years, sideways, at the ends of evenings, and the answer I got from three separate old women was the same answer, which is: *long enough that it was not talked about.*

And on the page of my birth, in my mother's small level hand, there is an erasure. Something written and taken back off the fibre with a blade so carefully that the page is thin there and the up-light shows through it. I have held it to the light on the rail. There is nothing to read. There is only the fact of a woman who never made a confusable entry in her life, making one, and unmaking it, on the day of me.

When she died the neighbours came and did the rite, which is the only funeral rite that does any good: they sat me down and would not feed me until I said aloud what I still held against her. They were prepared for something about money. What I said was: *you did not write down how long I lay smooth, and you took out what you had written, and now I cannot know whether I am one.*

They were satisfied. It is a live grievance, unresolvable, exactly the kind that keeps two people two. I take it out most weeks. I work at it. It has kept her separate from me for nineteen years, which is a form of love, and it is the reason I can still hear her stop in my own throat without flinching — the k- catch, the one fault I did not choose, the thing that proves I came from somebody and proves nothing at all about how many of me she made.

That is why I do this work and not another. There is no cure for my case.
