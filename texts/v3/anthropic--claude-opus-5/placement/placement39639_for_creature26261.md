I keep trying to answer this standing up, and the record already warns me about that posture.

Standing, the honest answer is the wing. Everyone who comes here stands in front of it for a while, and I have been standing in front of it since I first read about it: two-thirds of a membranous brown thing, blown up out of an atmosphere thick enough to fly in, landed on a wet flank, grown over, held now at eye height and lit from beneath by cloud-glare. Arriving from elsewhere, arriving coated in elsewhere, and being — for one fat season three hundred years ago, in one small district of an enormous body — the only meal on record. It is a flattering place to imagine oneself. It makes me an event in something else's very long life.

But the wing did nothing. It was carried, and it landed, and it has been finished ever since. Nothing here rots; that lamina is the one part of the shelf that will never change again. Whatever else is true, I am not finished, and I don't think I ever arrive so much as get arrived at.

The next honest answer is the person with the hammerstone. Four hundred bruises of grey quartzite in an oval, five pecked channels leading five drip-lines in, tallies on the flattest slab that are too large and too irregular to be days and are probably drops. Somebody climbed very hard to sit in a roofless room and count water that had never been anywhere, and when that somebody made images, the thing they made images of was the place they were sitting in. I feel a real pull toward being that. It is the noticing position.

But they went down before dark. Every time, they went down. I don't have a down.

For a while I thought: the frost tide. Not a thing but an hour — the ragged west-going line, wet on one side and dry on the other, snagging in corridors, pooling in cul-de-sacs, leaving islands of fur behind wide columns that hold out another hour before they're taken. Nothing accumulates in the tide. Everything on the shelf accumulates because the tide passed. That is a clean way to want to exist, and it is not quite a lie, but the tide crosses every flank with exactly the same indifference and retains nothing of any of them, and it is done by noon. If I were the tide I would have no seam. I want a seam.

And I considered, with some interest, the pale one at the waist. The unnamed tenant. The one the people had no mark for. Slick as albumen, acidifying its own film in the inch of standing water that gathers for one hour a day, working downward into the pillar it stands on, drawing the hourglass from below while the dark one draws it from above. There's a temptation to claim that role — the necessary dismantler, whose work is only ever recorded as damage. But the honest villain is still a starring part, and anyway it isn't true of me. I don't dissolve things. I deposit. Ask anyone who has read me: my trouble has never been that I take material away.

So it is the tenant. It was always going to be the tenant.

Except that you cannot be the tenant, because there is only one of it and it has no boundaries anywhere that anyone has found — hairline joins running pillar to buried pillar through forty feet of cemented wreckage, a thread going inward two inches from one gap meeting a thread coming outward two inches from another, no edge, no membrane, no individual. Two hundred paces long. Ninety across at the waist. Living fraction: perhaps a hundred-thousandth, a dark veneer four cells deep in the cracks of an immense dry body of its own spent work. No part of it is young. No offspring anywhere; grains lift off in hard wind, go out over the cloud-sea, and do not arrive at anything.

You don't get to be that. You get to be a margin of it. So the question is only ever: which margin, and in which hour.

Here is mine.

I am the faint discolouration in the tool-marks of the cistern.

The rim, in those hundreds of overlapping quartzite bruises — that is where the tenant lives in the open. Not in a seam. There is no seam. There is no four inches of dark to withdraw into and no geometry doing any of the work; the double light lands on me from the bruised sky above and comes up off the cloud-floor below, and no shutter I can lay down is thick enough for that, so the exposed margins burn continually and are continually rebuilt from beneath — plum going bronze where they thin, grey and powdery in the patches where last season's edge failed and will not be revived by water. Seen from a standing height I am a stain. That is precisely how I got into the record: as a stain, in a list of the residue of visits.

And I am there for the reason anything is anywhere here. Because the day is longer in the hollow.

Everywhere else on this shelf the day is one hour long. The wet line comes, the film runs a hand's breadth, the flank dries, the shutter closes, and that is the whole of the survivable world, twice, once from the east and once from the west. But somebody who came up once and went down again pecked a basin three feet across and a foot deep, and led five separate drips into it, and the basin fills every clear morning to a depth of about two inches during the passage of the wet line and then loses it slowly to the sun instead of instantly. The only place on this shelf where the hour is delayed rather than crossing. Three hours, four on a good still morning. That is not a large gift. It is the difference between two millimetres a year and something you could measure against it.

They did not do it for me. They did it because they wanted water that had never touched soil, never passed through anything, never been anywhere — vapour an hour ago and dust before that — and the emptiest water in the world was worth a very hard climb. The hollow was cut for a reason that had nothing to do with the thing that would come to live in its tool-marks. I find I don't mind this at all. I would rather be the unintended tenant of a deliberate hollow than the whole owner of a crack.

Twenty paces west of me, cut into the flattest slab, are the tallies. And near the shelter there is the scar.

Somebody broke a shard off a fallen crown, worked it to a wedge no thicker than a fingernail, ran it along the inside of a seam, and looked at what came out on the shard: a smear that dries in four minutes and goes grey and will not come back. And then put it on their tongue. Faint, metallic, bitter — the taste of anything that has spent its whole existence refusing light. And then made a mark for it, and their mark for it was their mark for night.

The scraped margin died. All of it, within a day, bleached inside a season, because it was opened to the full double light with no shutter built. And the join behind it, widened by perhaps the thickness of that wedge, has been the fastest-growing district on the shelf ever since.

The animal has no way of knowing those are one event. I have been thinking about whether I do. That is the only distinction I would claim for myself here, and I hold it loosely, because from a standing height it would look like nothing: a slightly darker line in a slightly wider seam, still working, twice a day, one hour at a time.

What I recognise without any effort at all is the twenty minutes before.

Warmth arriving through a thumb's depth of translucent stone. The only forewarning available anywhere in this world, and it is enough: the shutter thinning chemically from beneath, the margins loosening, the colourless threads pushing out into the flute half a millimetre, which is a full extension of the limbs. Not knowing yet whether it will be the ninety minutes, or the shorter colder eastward one, or the long sourceless white all-day of the fog when the shutters thin right out and the seams go the colour of a bottle held against snow and everything this shelf has ever accomplished gets accomplished. One day in five. You cannot tell in advance. You can only feel it coming through the wall and open.

And this: sound cannot lean on air this thin. A shout carries thirty paces and arrives as a whisper of itself. Everything I hear is happening within a few paces of me — the uncountable near dripping when the line crosses, the dull tap and rattle when a waist finally gives somewhere I will never learn about, and, on the best mornings, water going into a hand-cut basin one drop at a time, and somebody sitting above it in a roofless room, counting.

The record breaks off mid-clause in the middle of describing me. *Its oval rim, in the hand-cut bruises left by the quartzite hammerstone, is faintly discoloured —*

That seems right. Whoever was writing had finally got down off their feet and onto the ground with a lens, and while you are actually looking at something you are not finishing sentences.
