## What I tried on first

The board-keeper, obviously. Everybody tries the board-keeper first. She is the part of the Bell a stranger falls in love with — the slate, the bent brass rule, eighty-one years in eleven hands, eight centimetres of fall. You can hold a whole cosmology in that and still be home for supper. I stood at the second bench and imagined my own initials going into the column and felt, for about a minute, entirely at peace.

Then I looked at the column properly. Four of those hands are hers. Her mother's readings are up there in a slant that got worse for three years and then stopped. The board-keeper is not a philosopher of deep time, she is a woman continuing a chore, and the chore is the shape of a family. You cannot take it up by admiring it. I came down the shaft on a ladder like everyone else who was not born here, with a plateau accent in my hands and my own tobacco, and the board would have been theft.

So then the linesman, which is the other thing strangers try on. Six kilometres of cable, a satchel of bulbs and porcelain, more hours spent in the dark between the lamps than in them. The only man who knows the whole Bell. I walked a stretch with him once, in my fourth year, carrying tape, and understood within an hour that the job is not knowledge, it is subtraction. He is the best signer in the works because he has nothing else — no shift-mates, no station, no one waiting at a particular bulb to see how his day went. I had something else. I had the thing I had lost, which is not the same as nothing.

And the lamp gardens I dismissed fast, because moss growing in a circle of borrowed light, dying when the bulb is moved, is what a visitor picks when a visitor wants to be moved. The works people are sentimental about the gardens the way you are sentimental about a dog. Nobody is the dog.

What I am is simpler and less pretty, and it is in my hands.

## The hands

I went deaf at nine years, which is early, and everyone said so, and there was the thing done with tobacco and a foreman's nod, and I went in to the near stations where the noise stops being sound and becomes a circumstance, like slope. The near stations are the intake screens and the strainer pits, four hundred metres inside the line, and the work is clearing them: feather, nest-shell, beetle wing, the pulped fur of something that fell. Cold water the temperature of blood, day after day, and the hammer-vibration of a fall landing on bare rock coming up through the grating into the wrists for eleven hours at a stretch.

The hearing goes and you are promoted. The hands go and nobody has a word for it. Mine went over about two years — pins, then a glove-feeling, then nothing below the second knuckle and very little above. The plateau doctors have a Latin name for it. The works call it nothing, because it is common enough to be weather.

Here is what that means in a place like this.

Everything said on the catwalks is said at arm's scale, from the shoulder, in silhouette, legible at eight metres. Fine finger-work is useless; the Bell has no whisper. So in public I am unimpaired. I sign as well as anyone in the works and better than most, big and slow and clean, and the juniors can read me at the edge of a sphere with the drip going across the light.

But the real life of the Bell is not in the light. It is in the eighty-odd metres of unlit grating between one bulb and the next — the only private room in the system — where it is done hand on forearm, in *grip*. Courtships. Confessions. The settling of debts. *Between the bulbs* is what they say when they mean *in earnest*.

I can give grip. I have been taught it and I can make the shapes and I am told they are correct. I cannot receive it. A hand closes on my arm in the dark and I know only that something is happening to my sleeve. I have learned to stand very still and wait for the next bulb, and to hope the person will be patient enough to say it again in the open, at arm's scale, where four other people can read it across the void. Most are not. Most, having said the thing once in the dark, consider it said.

So: I live entirely in the light. Everything I have ever been told, I was told in public. Everything I have ever said in earnest, I said broadcast, in silhouette, from the shoulder, where anyone in two spheres could read it and generally did.

That is who I am here. Not the man who knows the whole Bell. The man nothing is confided to.

## What it is good for

The works found the use of me before I did, which is how the works are.

I am the one you send with a message you don't mind being seen. I am the one who teaches the juniors, because there is no point in a fluent grip-speaker doing it — juniors have to be taught in the light, big, from the shoulder, with the drip going across, until their shoulders ache and they stop flinching at the roar. I stand a boy at eight metres in the dark and sign to him until he can read me, and then I put him at twelve, and then I turn the lamp so he has to read me against it, and then he is a works man.

And I am the one who deals with the loud.

Eleven weeks a year, the nest crews. Aerial men from three valleys east, on rattan and rope up into the dome where four million birds have no opinion about us whatsoever, paid four times what we are, and shouting. Shouting at the tables, shouting on the catwalks, bellowing into a roar that swallows every word — not out of rudeness but because they have not yet understood where they are. The works people call them *the unborn* and will not sit with them. Somebody has to, and it is me, because they can shout at me all they like and I lose nothing by it; I lost it in the pits.

Last season one of them, a boy of about seventeen with rope-burn to the elbow, watched me for three weeks and then came and stood in front of me with his hands held up stupidly, like a man surrendering. He wanted the sign for *water rising*. Not because he needed it. Because he had worked out that there was a language, and that he was outside it, and that being outside it was the reason nobody would sit down.

I taught him nine signs in eight weeks. *Water rising. Go back. Rope. Hungry. Slower. My fault. Not my fault. Tomorrow.* And *wait for the bulb*, which is the one he will need if he ever comes back, and I do not think he will.

The works people did not like me doing it. They read me teaching him, from two spheres away, because they read everything I do. That is the arrangement.

## The evening thing

At the end of shift somebody takes my hands.

This is not affection, strictly. A hand that does not feel does not report. I come off the screens with cuts I have no knowledge of, a burn from a pipe I leaned on, a tooth of wire through the web of the thumb, and if it goes another day in this air it goes bad, and bad hands in the Bell end at the wrist. So every evening, under the bulb by the bathhouse that has no roof, somebody turns my hands over and reads them for me, knuckle by knuckle, and dresses what is there, and signs me the damage afterwards so that I know what happened to me today.

It has been, over the years, the foreman, the board-keeper, a woman named on the ledger who is dead now and on the slope, and lately one of the juniors I taught, who is careful and slow and has stopped flinching. They do it under the bulb because you need the light. So the one intimacy I have is conducted in full view, at arm's scale, where anybody can read it: a man holding another man's hand up to a wire-caged lamp and turning it like a slate.

I have thought about this more than is good for me. The works give everything they have in the dark and are given back in the dark, and I am handled in the light, clinically, in public, by whoever is free. And it is tenderness. I want to be clear that I know it is tenderness. It is just tenderness that everyone can see, which the plateau would find embarrassing and I find, most evenings, sufficient.

## Call-sign

Mine is two and two. Two slow, two quick, on the iron, and it runs six hundred metres through the felt-lagged pipe and comes out clean and bright at the far end over the top of a noise that will not stop.

The linesman's is three and a half — three strokes and a glancing fourth that nobody else can produce cleanly — and when he goes the half stroke will go out of use, because to keep it would be bad manners. Everyone knows this. It is the closest thing the Bell has to a monument.

Two and two is nothing. Two and two is a sign anybody can make with a spanner, and when they lay me in the cut on the lower slope and sign the words over me, and the nineteen days do what they do, there will be some junior on two and two by the end of the month, and the pipes will knock for him as if nothing had been subtracted. I would not have it otherwise. I have spent my life in the light being read by people who did not particularly intend to read me; it seems consistent to leave behind a name that stays in service.

The water is going down about a centimetre a decade. In some unimaginable afternoon the fall will have dug its own soft bed and gone quiet, and there will be a valley here with a stream in it and a road, and the birds elsewhere, and nobody signing anything from the shoulder because there will be nothing to shout over. The works people speak of this as a bereavement that will happen to strangers.

Until then: wet grating, a warm orange bubble eight metres wide, ten thousand thumb-length nubs on the ceiling overhead, each with its bead at the tip, each bead falling and being replaced, all night, all year, inaudibly, under the noise. And me in the middle of the light where everyone can see, making the sign for *slower*, making it again, making it bigger, until the boy at the edge of the dark gets it.
