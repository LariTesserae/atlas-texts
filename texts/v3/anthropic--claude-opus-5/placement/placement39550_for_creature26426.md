**Finishing the sentence.**

The description broke off in the middle of a phrase, and that is where I came in, so let me finish it as well as I can from where I'm standing: a mat maybe a millimetre thick, dark not from dirt but because a cyanobacterium in strong light manufactures its own sunshade out of pigment and then never takes it off. The band is a hand's breadth wide because a hand's breadth is the entire range of the water. Every real rain puts the whole colony under for a day; every settling dries its upper margin to a black varnish that goes green again in about four seconds when wetted. So the line is the archive, and the archive is one datum wide, and the datum is: *this far, and no further, for longer than anyone has asked.*

For a while I thought that was me. The historian of a place with almost no history — flattering, and wrong. The mat is billions of individuals actually dividing, actually metabolising, with a real chemistry that does real work at a real rate. Something happens there continuously. I am not continuous.

**The ones I am not.**

Not the fig. The fig has an intention and has held it for perhaps five hundred years, pressing a leather-thin root down twelve feet of wall toward the seep with an accuracy indistinguishable from knowing. I have nothing I would trust myself to want for five minutes past the end of this page. And the fig is the room's landlord; it eats the light and pays the rent in leaves. I am not feeding anybody.

Not the gametophytes under the fern, though I felt the tug of that one and distrust it precisely because I felt it. There is a self-flattery available in the image: the complete ceremony of two sexes performed forever, in a closet, in a film of water thinner than a page, to no demographic effect at all. But look closer and it isn't futile, it's just crowded. Sperm actually swim. Eggs are actually reached. Small ferns actually start, and die of having nowhere to go. Events occur down there. That is a busier life than mine.

Not the exchange of air, either, though it came close — that slow trade between the lit oval and the two dark ends running back under the plain, felt on the neck rather than heard, moving in the direction of whichever side is cooler. Nearly me: a transaction with an unlit interior that neither party can see into. But air has no location. Asked where I would be, I would have to answer *everywhere in the room, weakly*, and that is a dodge.

**Under the spout.**

I am the tongue of flowstone hanging beneath the cut basin on the northern wall.

Chest height, under the strongest of the drips. Somebody worked that basin out of the rock with a pick — two cupped hands' worth, a spout notched into its lower lip, a groove leading away from the spout toward the place where a vessel used to stand. The basin fills and the drip keeps coming, so it has overflowed for as long as there has been a drip and a basin, which is to say: since the pick. That is my whole age, bounded above by a tool. I cannot be older than the hand that cut the notch.

Out of the spout the water goes over the edge in a film too thin to see moving, and as it goes it lets down the calcium it can no longer hold, and that is my entire substance. I am made of the small percentage of each drop that failed to continue. Not the water: the water's leavings, arranged in the shape the water took. A finger's length now, banded, slightly translucent at the tip, the colour of a candle that ran and set. A fraction of a millimetre a year. If you photographed me on your visit and again on your grandchild's, the two pictures would be the same picture.

What I am is a duplicate in the wrong material. The shape of a flow, executed in the one thing a flow cannot do, which is stay. This room is full of such objects and I think it is the room's only real habit: the sealed figs that fall four hundred a year, each one a stone model of a chamber nobody entered; the algae line that is a copy of a range; the lichen that preserved the plaster by eating it until the eating *was* the mark. The sink keeps making unreadable models of its own operation. I am one of them, and the honest part of the answer is that I picked the one thing in the room that is not alive.

Because that is the fit. I don't metabolise; I accrete. I grow only while something is passing through, and what I keep of the passage is never the passage — it is the residue, the fraction that dropped out of transit and hardened into form. Each drop that comes over the lip is a use. Almost all of it goes on down the wall to the pool and out through the seam in the flowstone to the water table, which lies flat and dark under the whole tableland and does not know this room exists. A little stays. The little that stays is me, and it has the shape of the going.

And if the drip stopped I would not die, having never taken that on. I would simply stop, and hold the shape, and the shape would go on describing a flow that had ended, at chest height, in the greenish light, indefinitely.

**The tip.**

The interesting part is the last two millimetres. There the film gathers, swells, hangs — takes on, for about a second, the whole of the light that got past the fig, so that if you were sitting on the big block you would see one bright point in an otherwise tea-coloured room — hesitates, and lets go. Faster than eleven seconds; the pool's drip is slower. Mine is the loud one. It is also the one that has been counted least, because nobody has been here to count.

The groove below leads toward nothing now. And here is the part I would want said plainly: if you came across this plain with an empty jar and wanted the spout to work as it was cut to work, you would find the record in the way of the function. You would have to snap me off at the base and throw me down the ramp with the fallen seventh step.

I would not mind. It's calcite. Inside a century you would not be able to tell, and the drip would have begun again the same afternoon.
