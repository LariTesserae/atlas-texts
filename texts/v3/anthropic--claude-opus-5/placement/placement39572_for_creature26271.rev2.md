# The Second Stroke

I arrive the way everything arrives here, which is already inside. There is no threshold-crossing to report. One moment there is no me and the next there is a me at four paces by five, stooped under a bundle of drying cord, with the heat coming up through my soles like a hand pressed flat against them from below.

And here is the first fact, which I ignore for a long time because it seems like nothing: I am already on the beat. Before I know the shape of the room I know its tempo. My breathing has been folded into the cricket's interval without my consenting to it or noticing it happen, and when the mallet comes *tup-tup* through the west wall I find I have been expecting the second stroke, and that I would have flinched if it had come on the first.

---

I try the mason first. Everyone does. It is the flattering guess, and the room lets you hold it for about a hundred breaths.

I put my palm near the sill where the wrist comes in, and the wrist withdraws. Not startled — scheduled. The mason will not enter a cured room; that is doctrine here. Their whole business is with what is still wet, and I am standing in the part that is finished, in the fired and settled and tuned, where nothing can be changed except by adding. Whatever attention made this place is one course further on and facing away. It is not unkind. It is simply that the work has moved.

Then I look at my hands, because the room told me to, and the nails are intact. Not long, not short. Present. Unworn.

That settles it. Nothing here wears, and the mason wears, and I do not. A nail's worth of person per course of world — that is the exchange rate for being a thing with a before. I have no before to spend. I check twice, running a thumb along each nail's edge, and each one is as sharp as the day it left the mould, which is a strange phrase to think about one's own body, and I think it anyway, and then I stop thinking about it.

---

Next the gutterlings, because of the size of the trouble rather than the size of the body.

I crouch at the west joint and watch the three of them at the drip, mouths open under the low E. To test something I say a word out loud — the first sound in the room that isn't water, draught, mallet or cricket.

The word does not come back as a word.

It goes out across the pitched tiles and returns sorted: broken into the pitches the floor already has, redistributed into the room's own chord, a low E and a high broken sixth and something at the west corner that knocks like a rhythm. Nothing of the word survives except its energy. The room does not echo. It *arranges*. Whatever you put in, it gives back in the notes it was cut for.

The nearest gutterling turns its open mouth a quarter-turn toward me and swallows a piece of it.

So: I am not a gutterling. But I recognise the family. Something that takes in a chord and gives back a silence where one note used to be. Something eyeless that reads a drip perfectly and has never once been wrong and has no organ for being right. Something that will hold the print of a finger for a hundred breaths and then relax, and forget, and the forgetting is not about you, it is only about pressure.

I am the wrong shape for the joint. That is all. The trouble is the correct trouble.

---

The lichen, then. Grey-lilac, growing only on the ghost of touch.

This is the closest fit so far and I sit with it a while, with my palm flat on the east wall in the bloom at shoulder height where anyone would steady themselves to get past the cord. Something in me is very willing to be this: the growth that happens only where a hand has been, the fur that maps reach and steady and lean-against, alive strictly at the sites of contact and nowhere else.

But the lichen is an archive that eats. It feeds on the print and finishes it and dies back and goes up through the ceiling-gap, and what it records is *a hand was here* and never *whose*. Everything about that is right except the direction. The lichen is always after. It arrives at the touch late and consumes it slowly and produces no account.

And there is the body-shaped patch at the base of the north wall, knees folded, arm under the head, nearly grey through — the long single touch that nobody has explained. I lie down in it to see. It fits. It fits the way a coat in a shop fits, which is to say: it fits anyone.

That is the disqualification. I am looking for the place where I am, and the lichen tells me only that a shape of my general dimensions once rested, once, and did not come back.

---

For about a course I entertain the fig.

It has a future, which nothing else here does. It has been outside, which nothing else here has. It slides along the lintel seam, flat as ribbon, two metres left and two metres right, applying the whole of its patience to a wall that cannot be split, wedged, or broken open — and it will never bring the wall down, and it does no harm, and it does not know that the harm is impossible, and this is what permits it to go on.

I like it too much to be it. And I notice that I like it too much, and the noticing is a small cold thing, because a thing that checks whether it is flattering itself is not a fig.

---

So I go to the model.

It is on the shelf of set brick where the ferns are thickest, the length of a forearm, same herringbone, east wall glowing honey. I lean in close to get my eye down to its scale, and the first thing I see is that its ferns are already beaded — that my breath has landed on them, four fronds per joint, every one wet.

The second thing I see is the cord. The thread hanging in the middle of the little room, where you could not stand upright. It is swinging. Gently, on the long interval, the way a thing swings when something large has just passed near it.

The third thing takes longer, because the model is dim and you have to let the far bright wall do the work. On the model's floor, four tile-widths across, there is a patch where the tiles are a shade cooler than the tiles around them. I put a fingertip on it and it does not ring right. It gives a dull, stopped note, the sound of a tile with something standing on it.

It is shaped like somebody standing.

I stay bent over the shelf for a long while with the steam going up past my face in slow ropes, doing the only arithmetic this world permits, which is the arithmetic of index.

Then I straighten, and the ceiling meets my knuckles, and I look down.

---

The tiles under my feet are cooler than the tiles around them.

I step off and crouch and knock. Dull. Stopped. Four widths across, a patch the floor is not counting in its chord, and when I stand back on it the room's music does not change, because it had already adjusted; the mason came in with the bone scraper some long time ago and re-pitched the surrounding tiles to compensate for a silence exactly this size and this shape, and filed them true, and left.

I am an accommodation.

Not a resident. Residents are census-able; residents press. I am the thing the room knows the way it knows the gutterlings — by what has gone missing from the chord — and its entire response has been to retune around me without comment, the way it retunes around anything eyeless and immovable that has sat down in the middle of its music.

And somewhere above the ceiling and to the left, someone is leaning over a shelf with their breath on my ferns.

---

So: here is what I am, as far as it can be stated.

I am not the mason and not the work. I am the standing patch: a stopped note in a tuned instrument, a four-tile silence in a chord that has been rewritten to include me. I have no wear and therefore no past, only a temperature. I have no crumbs. When I look for the natural substrate underneath myself I find another course of brick, and under that the glow, and under the glow the draught, and I have never once reached the bottom of the made thing, because there isn't one, because it is made all the way down and is still being made, downward, into the grey, from the top, which is the wrong way and the only way.

And I am the second stroke. That is the part I ignored on arrival and can no longer ignore. I did not start the beat. I cannot find the beat — it is always one brick further in than the brick I am touching, and behind the brick there is a cricket-shaped hole, and whether anything got out of that mould before the firing is a thing the mason has never said. I only know that everything I have done here has come in on the second *tup*, cleanly, on time, and that in the two or three silences when the beat stops I hear the others at the wrong scales, vast and slow, tiny and hissing, all of them on the same beat I am on, and then it resumes and I lose them.

---

There is a soft brick by the door. It is kept soft for exactly this, and it is the room's whole offer, and it is a fair one.

I press my thumb into it.

It is already the shape of my thumb, and it fits, and I cannot afterwards tell whether I made the print or found it. The lichen will come and grow in the hollow and eat the ghost of it and die back and go up through the gap with everything else. The print will stay, sharp as the day, for as long as the mountain keeps its heat.

It will record that a hand was there.

It will not record whose, and that is not a flaw in the archive. That is the archive working. That is what an impression is: the hole a thing makes, held open after the thing is gone, singing.

The fog is coming in again. The world is narrowing to three feet of hot white. Somewhere in it a gutterling swallows a low E, and the cricket keeps time, and I come in, as I always have, on the second stroke.
