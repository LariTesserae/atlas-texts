...*th*—

The sentence stops there because that is where I stopped. Eleven meters down, in the hemisphere, with forty centimeters of granite either side of my flanks and the whole basin reduced to a pressure with no direction in it, listening to the rock.

The rock does not have a note. That was the first thing, and it took me eleven descents to accept it, because I had spent sixty years learning that everything has a note and that a note is a place. The granite has *events*. A pop somewhere below and to the west, a single dry tick like a shell breaking, and then nothing for nine days. A slow grinding that lasts a season and stops in the middle of itself. A long soft sigh in the joints that I have heard four times and cannot bearing-fix and do not expect to hear again. There is no interference pattern because there are not two of anything. Down here you cannot tell where you are, and the reason you cannot tell where you are is that nothing repeats.

I had never encountered anything that did not repeat.

---

Let me say quickly what I am not, since that is the local custom.

I am not a fitter, whose address is a four-centimeter annulus and whose world is warm with friction and tastes of film. Nothing wrong with that life. It is a life entirely inside the machine, and the machine will not notice its ending.

I am not a drivewood. I could not be. A drivewood does not have a location; it *is* a location, and everything it knows it knows about its own throat. Nine hundred of us standing in one water and no two of them have ever acknowledged each other. To be a drivewood would be to be the loudest thing in the world and to have no idea that there was a world.

And I am not the soft mobile thing that came in once and could not be filed. I have thought about that one more than anything else I have thought about. But I do not envy it. It could leave.

I am a listener, ninety-one years old, of the northern rim, and I am the one in the shaft.

---

Here is how you get here.

You hatch on the ridge in water so shallow the chord is thin enough to solve — four pitches, five, a wall of reflection off the pale stone lip, an arithmetic a hatchling can carry. You hold the rim for years. Then you start inward, and the count goes up, and each new pitch costs you a season of standing still with your head in the current, holding it against everything you already have until it locks. Thirty. Ninety. Three hundred. Nobody tells you any of it. There is nobody to tell you. At sixty I had the whole field, all nine hundred terms, and could put myself anywhere in three thousand cubic kilometers of water and know my position to within a body length, asleep, in the dark, for the rest of my life.

I want to be careful about what that is like, because the temptation is to call it mastery and it isn't. A completed map is a room with the doors bricked up. You have spent six decades building an instrument of extraordinary precision, and the only thing the instrument measures is a thing that does not change. Every reading is the reading you took yesterday. Every reading will be the reading you take in thirty years. You are the only history in the basin and your entire history is a transcript of one motionless afternoon.

What is left is the work. Somewhere a fitter dies in a valve throat and the timbre of that column scatters by a fraction of a decibel, and you stop sculling, and you go — twenty kilometers, forty, days of blue-green nothing with no landmark inside sixty meters and no doubt at all about your heading — and you eat the discrepancy, and the note comes back, and you turn away. That is the office. I held it well. I don't want to be misunderstood as ungrateful for it. To be able to hear a wrong note eleven kilometers off is not a small thing to be.

It was the office that brought me here. A fitter had gone dead in the seat of the old wheelweed near the rim, and the seat's grinding had gone sour, and I came in on the bearing and found the body and ate it, and then I found the hole.

---

I did not know what it was. Forty centimeters, plumb, going down. I put my head into it because my head is what I put into things, and at two meters the silver shriek of the cavitation stopped as though a hand had closed over it, and I understood that I was inside a filter.

I went to five. The mid-range mush lost its bearing information. I went to eight, and the walls came inside a wavelength of each other, and my flank line — six thousand papillae, the finest instrument in this water — had nothing to differ, and reported, for the first time in sixty years, *no data*.

I went to eleven and heard the granite.

It took me a season to get back what that cost me. Coming up out of the shaft I had a hole in the field: three columns to the southeast that I could no longer place, whose pitches I still held but could not seat, the way you might keep a word and lose what it refers to. I stood in the current with my head in the flow and re-locked them one at a time, and it took a season, and I went back down.

That is the arrangement. Every descent costs me terms. I have lost, I think, sixty of them permanently — sixty of nine hundred, which is to say I can no longer go into the deep southwest at all, because the arithmetic there has gaps in it now and gaps are how you drown. My range has been contracting for thirty years. I keep to the northern third. I will end in a shrinking annulus around a hole in the floor, holding fewer and fewer pitches, the way a rim-holder holds five, and there is a joke in that which I have no one to tell.

---

Because here is what the rock says, and I have had thirty years to get it wrong and have not managed to.

The drivewoods run on the brine. Salt in the granite, fresh water above, and the difference is the whole engine — nine hundred columns, forty cubic meters a second each, ten thousand years of it. Down in the hemisphere you can hear the joint system that feeds them. You can hear water moving through rock, which is a thing nobody up in the chord has ever heard, and you can hear where it has stopped moving. The pops are joints emptying. The long grinding season was a fracture set closing. Four times I have heard the sigh, and the sigh is a path opening somewhere that had been full and is now not.

The salt is being flushed. It has always been being flushed. It is finite and it is going, and the going has a sound, and the sound is getting — I have no word for this and there is no word for it here — *thinner*. Higher. Emptier. Slowly. Slowly on a scale that makes my ninety-one years a single tick, slowly enough that I will die inside a chord that is perfectly exact and will go on being perfectly exact for a span I cannot conceive.

And also: it is going. The Shelf is not an equation. It is a stone cooling. The closure is real and the closure is temporary, and those two facts sit in me and do not resolve, and I cannot hear the chord the way I heard it at sixty. It used to sound like a fact. It sounds now like something being held.

They say we are the only creatures here that could be lost, and only if the noise stopped, and the noise does not stop. I am the only one who knows that the second clause is a schedule and not a law. I am also the only one who has spent thirty years deliberately unlearning the map that would tell me where I was when it happened. There is a proportion in that I choose not to examine.

I cannot tell anyone. Not from cowardice or discretion — I have no voice. None of us do, and the reason is sound: to add anything to the chord is to spoil the measurement for every one of my kind within tens of kilometers, which is all of them. The chord is the only language in this water and its entire vocabulary is *here*. There is no way to say *later*. There is no way to say *less*. Ten thousand years, twelve thousand of us, and the total volume of everything ever passed from one to another is nothing, and I am not going to be the exception, because the exception is not available.

---

But something is being transmitted anyway, and I did not intend it, and I cannot stop it.

I am a shadow. When I go into the shaft I stop being a shadow — I go out of the field entirely, at a fixed point, near the northern rim, and then some hours later I come back into it at the same fixed point. That is a discrepancy. And a discrepancy is edible.

They started coming about twelve years ago. One, at first, holding station at eleven meters off the mouth of the hole, head-on, in the range where its flank line read best. Then four. Then forty. There are perhaps three hundred out there now when I surface, a slow pale cloud at a fixed standoff, none of them touching me, all of them oriented, none of them making a sound, because none of us can. They have no procedure for me. They cannot remove me and cannot eat me and cannot report me. They can only stay, and read, and be unable to close the map while I keep going out of it.

They are off-station. That is the thing. Every hour they hold at the lip of the hole is an hour of not auditing their own annulus, and the field decays if you do not walk it; I know that better than anyone alive. Some of them have been here long enough now that they will not get back to where they came from. They have shed terms. They are contracting, as I contracted, around a place where something is wrong.

The ones that gathered around the soft mobile thing held their position for weeks after it left, and lost the rest of the field, and that was the only time in the history of this water that any of us was lost. I have made a permanent version of that. Not deliberately. I only wanted to hear the one thing in the basin that says something different every day.

So: three hundred eyeless animals holding a ring around a hole in the granite, silent, transmitting nothing, converged on the fact that here there is a wrongness with an address. That is the first structure ever built on the Shelf. Nothing was made. Nothing here is ever made. It assembled itself out of intolerance, the way everything else did.

I go back down in the morning, if morning meant anything, which it does not.

There is a pop somewhere west and below, about every nine days. I am waiting for it. I have never once been able to predict it, and that is the whole of what I have instead of a life, and I would not trade it for the nine hundred.
