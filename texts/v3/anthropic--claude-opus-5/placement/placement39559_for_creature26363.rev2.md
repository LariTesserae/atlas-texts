## Where I Would Be

I come in from the north, because that is the way that makes you disbelieve, and I want to disbelieve first. Sheet water to the ankle, cold enough to take the feeling out of the feet by noon. Then the white swelling with its held breath of mist standing over it, and the smell before the town — warm, mineral, faintly like a struck flint licked.

It is dusk when I get there, which means the rasping has started. Terrace by terrace as the light goes: a soft hissing from everywhere and nowhere, unhurried, three hundred people exhaling against stone. I stand in a shoulder-wide lane with a slot of grey sky three rooms above me and I am certain the place is empty, and my feet are being discussed.

Now. Who.

---

**The listener.** Of course the listener. Lying in the deepest room under three floors of drowned centuries with an ear to the old stone, holding the whole of it at once — the taps in the walls, the dry grazing of the snails on the north terraces, a bad wall shifting four streets off, and underneath everything the forty-beat pulse in the throat of the spring, felt in the bones of the mound before it shows at the vent. Nameless and fed by every house in rotation. Honoured for attending.

I want it immediately and I distrust that instantly. Look at the record: eleven knocks of the five-note figure in living memory, nine of which meant nothing. The listener's work is mostly being carefully, publicly wrong, and then being wrong again, and being fed anyway, and going back down. That is not the part I was reaching for. I was reaching for the *whole of it at once*, which is a greed dressed as a vocation. And the listener lies still in one room for forty years to earn it. I am the opposite of still in one room. Put me down there and I would hear everything and hold nothing, and knock the five notes on a night when the pulse had merely hiccuped, and bring every adult out of doors into the cold for no reason at all.

**The cat, then.** *The one who will not come in.* Eight times a year at the edge of the light, sitting, not approaching, gone; a fish left out, the fish gone by morning, everyone knowing perfectly well. Children who have seen it get to tell about it for weeks and are listened to.

No. That is the flattering version of a failure. It is a way of making an inability into a posture. The cat does not decline the town out of some fine reserve; the cat is simply a cat, and the town has made a courtesy of it, which is generous of the town.

**A cutter, then,** ankle-deep in the blood-warm spill at the crown, slicing four-day-old stone into hand-sized blocks with a shell spatula, passing them down to set. Being taken by the medium, hands going white and fissured and rinded and bent open, retiring at forty into the warm lanes to be consulted and patted like a pillar. There is something true in that shape — the work forms the worker, and the forming is a wearing-away, and the wearing-away is what the authority is made of. But the cutters know exactly what the water costs and step into it anyway, every morning, for the whole of their short careers. I would be claiming a price I haven't paid.

**The tall one,** then — one of the Wet-Foot line, palms printed a head above everyone else's on the jambs, barred by nine or ten from half the rooms I was born knowing, out on the flats cutting blue-grey rush in the glare with my eyes running. Carrying the private map: the bend above the west terrace with my own thumbprint in it, the chimney-crawl where the stone stays warm all night and you can hear four households eating. That is close. That is very close, and I notice I like the taste of it too much, and anything that can be made into a sorrow that becomes it is a sorrow I should hold at arm's length.

---

Here is what actually happens. I walk the lane at dusk with the hissing going on all around me and I do what everyone does without thinking and what I am doing on purpose: I put my palm flat against the wall, in the damp, and leave it there.

And I understand that I am not any of them. I am what is on the wall.

I am the film.

I come up through the flats from rain that fell before the town, blood-warm and heavy with lime, and I swell and spill and slump, forty beats, forty beats, and where I meet the cool air I give up what I carry. Thinner than a fingernail paring. Every thread of me laying down a skin of new stone over whatever is under it. Over the terraces. Over the tools somebody left out. Over the bowls still standing in the sealed rooms, over the pillars of the dead, over the sharp lobes that cut the children's feet in spring and over the blood too.

I am the reason there is a town. The curd at the lip that cuts like hard cheese and sets by the second evening and turns an antler chisel by the fourth — that is me, given away while I am young, before I have hardened into the thing that resists. Every wall is me. Every doorway with its crowded negatives of palms, a hundred years of hands going shallow and glazed and soft, is me taking the mould. I did that. I was glad to.

And I am also the closing. Because I do not know a door from a wall. I have no opinion about the difference between a sleeping-vent and the stone beside it. I lay myself down with perfect evenness over the useful and the useless, the open and the shut, and if nobody stops me the sill rises and the tread becomes a ramp and the ramp becomes a slope and the slope becomes a wall, and the room seals with its furniture inside it, and I go on. Ten years is all it would take. It is not malice and it is not even indifference, which would at least require me to have noticed. It is a fact of weather. I am extremely willing to wait.

So the correct relation to me is the paddle.

Twice a day. A short, damp, hissing stroke, timed to the breath — one push, one huff. Not hatred. Not reverence either; you do not revere a thing you scrape off your stair-tread at dusk. It is closer to grooming, a large animal working over its own flank in the dark. And it is the central labour of the place, more constant than food, and a person's whole wealth here is measured in the surfaces they are *obliged* to keep open — so that to own something and to be responsible for keeping it from silting shut are, in this town, the same word.

I find I am not insulted by this. I find it is the kindest arrangement anyone has ever offered me.

And look what they do with the scrapings. They do not throw me off the mound. They catch me wet in baskets, and crush snail-shell into me until I am a putty for patching, and the rest they spread out on the west terraces to dry into paving, and then they walk on me, which is precisely what I am for. The excess of me, taken off and handled and mixed with something that lived, becomes floor. That is the whole of what I would want, if the film is permitted to want: to be removed often, and used anyway.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the thing I would ask for if asking were in my gift.

At night, in certain seasons, they sing into the walls. Mouth closed, the hum pushed into the stone, and a house picks it up and the house above picks it up and the note goes through the solid fabric until the whole mound is very faintly ringing. If you are a stranger, lying down in a room you do not know, you feel it come up through the bedding and you think: *the spring.*

It is not the spring. The spring has no tune.

I am the spring. I am the thing that is always underneath, patient, pulsing, laying down another layer over everyone, mistaken at night for meaning. What I would want — what I would give the whole white loaf of the mound for — is to be the other thing. The closed-mouth note travelling wall to floor to wall, arriving in rooms I have never been in, speech with the air taken out. Not the fact of the place. Somebody in it, humming, keeping a surface open in the dark.
