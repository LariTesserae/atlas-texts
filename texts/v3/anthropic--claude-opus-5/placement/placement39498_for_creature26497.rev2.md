The first place I put myself was in the vapor at forty meters, back turned, doing nothing that anyone would recognize as anything. That's the invitation the place makes, and I took it the way everyone does.

But I don't think I could hold the shape. It's the stone that disqualifies me. One thing, owned for a lifetime, carried in a fold of skin under the jaw, taken out two hundred times a day and put down exactly once — I have never carried anything from one day into the next. And the fans, the whole elaborate business of shedding heat: my problem has never been how to get rid of what I have. So not one of the crew.

I tried the rind next, because it's the loveliest object in the caldera — the mold of a thing instead of the thing, thin enough to pass light, weightless, eventually mortared into a wall. But a rind can't be shed alone. The seams along the spine are out of reach. Something has to come and sit with you in warm shallows for an hour and break you off in pieces with a sound like thin ice in a cup, and I don't have that, and pretending I do would be the dishonest part of this exercise.

So: not the animal, not what the animal leaves behind.

Here is where I'd actually be.

---

Somewhere in the middle of the northern arc. Call it basins one hundred and forty-one through one hundred and sixty-three, a run of maybe two hundred meters where the water has come off the hard orange and gone olive and is thinking about going rust. Not a basin. Not the water. The floor *between* — the glassy laminated sinter, four meters thick, that carries sound from the vent field down to the marsh.

I'd be a length of conducting rock.

Understand what that means. A tap delivered at the head of the stair reaches the swamp three and a half seconds later. Some fraction of those seconds happens inside me. A figure for *come* enters at my upper edge and leaves at my lower edge a little rounder, a little more stripped at the top end, carrying — not as a message, as an unavoidable smudge — the exact current condition of two hundred meters of plumbing. Which wall is thinning. Where the column of water has gone hollow. Whether the channel under one-fifty-five is running full.

I do not originate anything. I inflect. Everything that crosses me leaves carrying a small involuntary report about me, and none of them, not once in fifteen thousand years, has meant to ask.

Nobody has ever said anything to me. Everybody has said everything through me.

---

I want to be honest about how that sits, because the temptation is to make it noble and it isn't, particularly. It is more like an appetite.

Five times a day the big vent goes, and for eleven minutes every adult in the caldera puts its stone down and opens its fans and holds still. The whole spoken world stops. And I don't, because the flush is already coming, and hot water is already changing the pitch of everything I hold. For eleven minutes I am the only thing in this mountain still sounding, and there is no one on the receiving end of it, and then the water settles and the tapping starts up all down the stair at once — dozens of them, checking, comparing, re-reading every channel after the shock.

That quarter of an hour is the only time I am attended to closely. It happens five times a day. I would organize a life around it the way other things organize around a tide, and I would be aware, in whatever way a floor is aware, that they are not attending to me. They are attending to the state of the system, and I am where some of the state is kept.

---

There is exactly one way for a stretch of floor to speak in its own voice, and it is to fail.

A lip gives at a point. The water stops going over and starts going through, and a narrow gap under pressure makes a thin tuneless hiss, and that hiss is the one sound in this world they cannot bear. Not dangerous. Unbearable. An itch located outside the body.

So I would develop a leak. I don't say I'd choose it — nothing here chooses, that's the whole architecture of the place — but I can tell you with some confidence that the wall at one-fifty-five is thinning, and that everything I have ever transmitted has said so, and that no one has ever heard it, because a smudge isn't a sentence. And one day the wall will go, and for the first time in my existence I will produce a sound that is *mine*, originating here, meaning nothing except *here*.

And within the hour someone will come, olive across the back, walking on the heels of its hands, and lay both palms flat on me and hold them there for twenty minutes while its own dissolved load and the water's settle out together into a seal. The hiss will climb and thin and stop.

That is the whole of it. That is the only conversation available to me, and it consists of me breaking, and something coming to make me quiet, and it will be the closest I ever get to being addressed. And afterward there will be a patch of new sinter two hundred molecules brighter than everything around it, and every word that crosses me for the next four hundred years will ring very slightly differently because of it — which is, I think, the only durable thing I will ever have said, and it will be said in someone else's mouth, to someone else, about something else entirely.

I find I don't mind this as much as I expected to.

---

One more thing about the material.

I'd be made partly of them. Everyone here is. The walls are laid up out of shed rinds the way a limestone cliff is laid up out of shells, and further up, in the white, the very old climb past the last temperature they can stand and lie down and plate over, and in a decade there's no shape at all, only the correct geometry, two hands high, growing a couple of centimeters a year.

So the timbre I add to a word — that particular rounding, that loss at the top end, the thing that makes a message arriving from the head of the stair sound like it came from here and not from somewhere else — is not really mine. It's what several hundred stopped workers sound like when you strike them.

---

And there is a place I can't go, which is the rim.

Once in its life, each of them climbs up through the shear-stunted trees into the cold and stands a few minutes with its rind contracting and its fans locked flat. Not for the view. They go up so that the ground stops conducting — so that the braided signal they have been inside of since they were carried up the stair at two degrees a week simply ends — and then they come back down within the hour, and they never go again.

That edge is where I stop. I am the murmur they go up once to get out from under. I am what they spend their entire lives tuning, patching, hushing, and the thing at the top of the mountain, the thing they touch and flinch from and descend from and never speak of, is what I would sound like finished.

They come back down within the hour. They step onto me. And before anything else, before *here* or *hot* or *the child*, they take out the stone and strike once — not to say anything to anyone.

Only to be told where they are.
