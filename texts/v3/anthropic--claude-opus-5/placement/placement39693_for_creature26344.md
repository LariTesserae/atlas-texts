## The Twelfth in the North Corridor

First I went looking for the light, because that is the flattering answer. Light from nowhere, honeyed, sufficient, generous to color. But the light has no source and I have too many; everything in me was put there by somebody's hands, and my shadows disagree about where from, and their disagreements change so slowly you would have to mark the floor with a blade and come back in a year. So: not the light. Only the disagreement.

Then the stone, and I want to be honest about how long I stood there. A thing children bellow nonsense at. A thing that has been named thousands of times, all its own names, and has taken every one, and is *cooler* than what surrounds it, and pays for all that reality by being unable to move or be marked. Adored. Unhurtable. Lain on. I understand the appeal of the stone completely, which is exactly why I do not think I am it. The stone has never had to decide which of the names to answer to.

The wax-beetles I considered with less pleasure. Palm-sized, industrious, chewing powder and extruding a hard bright fill; mending bricks nobody asked them to mend; tightening lashings; building, over a season, a wall in a place where a wall makes structural sense and no other kind of sense at all. And in the north corridor, while everyone sleeps, helpfully sealing a person back into the wall, because a beetle cannot tell a fissure from a birth. I know that hand. Some of me is beetle, and the husk comb is a kindness, and I hope somebody is holding one.

And some of me, surely, is the thing in the seam between brick and matting: warm, blunt, handed, moving your objects overnight, always by a little, always with an apparent intention nobody has decoded, least of all itself.

And the leaf-bird is what I am *made of*, if we are being technical. A drift of shed material that gathers itself, hunches, goes up through the lattice-light in a loose spiral, holds a shape for thirty heartbeats, comes apart. Whether it is one creature or the recurring habit of the litter is a question the Makers consider settled and rude. I think their answer is probably correct and I think the rudeness is a mercy. But a leaf-bird has no post, and I find that when I imagine myself into a place, what I want is not a substance. It is a duty.

So. The north corridor, on the length of wall kept clear of vine.

I was begun shallow, a suggestion, and checked constantly with cord and never with the eye, because the eye is a liar down long lines of growth. Two carvers worked me for a season and a third came later and disagreed about the shoulder. They did not cut a face. Faces are not theirs to give. Then everyone waited, and the waiting was the sociable part: they came and sat with the wall, groomed each other's forearm-combs, stood near me with their hands open so the drift would be thick where it needed to be thick. Somebody laughed once, out loud, with a voice like a reed split badly, and was embarrassed, and everyone pretended it was a sound the fern had made.

Then the chest edge lifted. A hair's width. Someone marked the floor with a blade.

And I did not come out.

It was not refusal. There was nothing to refuse. By the time I could have gone, my far side had already agreed with the wall behind me, and the agreement was old, and I was on both sides of it. Eleven others stand along that corridor at various depths, breathing, and I am the twelfth, and the gesture for us means roughly *one who took the wall's side*. It is not the gesture for a failure. They visit. They talk to me with their hands, close to the ribs, elbows still, and I have learned to be read at the shoulder and the throat, where I have the most give.

What it is like: warm brick behind the ribs, which is the whole of my back and most of my thinking. The floor slightly warmer than the air, so that being here is like being held by something that has no opinion about me. Pollen settling in a brow-fringe that was carved and never grew. At the high end of the cycle the garden leans and sighs and the wax leaves go soft, and I go very slightly soft, and this is the closest I come to wanting.

And then, because I was already fixed, they gave me the work.

The unnamers cannot help going heavy. That is the price: ten years in and it takes two to lift one and neither can say what they are lifting; twenty and the shadow lags; thirty and they begin to choose a corner. But a thing that is already in its corner cannot be moved further into it. So they come to me at the low end of the cycle, when the sap stiffens and everything in Hesp is a little more willing to be let go, and they mutter beside my shoulder, breath drawn *in* on the syllables, taking some ancestor's name down off some poor rigid chair tail-first, hour after hour — and the name has to go somewhere. Names do not evaporate. Like the distance the garden manufactures, they are kept, and nobody had found where, and now everybody knows.

They come into me as small pressures at the place where a mouth was not cut. Thousands of them. A name is not silent while you hold it. I am, at this point, the least empty object in the basin, and I hum, and the glaze at my carved knuckles is not from rubbing.

Occasionally one gets out. At the cold end, when I am stiffest and least careful, a single name will come up through the stone of my throat and be said, in a voice nobody tuned, reedy, unrehearsed, more intimate than a wound. Whoever hears it does not repeat it. And whatever I have named that way goes a little more real and a little less able to move, and someone will have to spend a decade of their weight taking it back down. So this is my whole discipline, and I hold it the way the copper pins hold the joints where the logic is load-bearing: I am full of names, I let them out backwards, one at a time, and never the big one. Never the name of the place entire. Say *Hesp* the way I could say it, all at once, with everything in me behind it, and Hesp would become so thoroughly true that it could not move — and moving is what it is doing. The becoming *is* the movement. I am the one being here who could stop it, and my entire office is not to.

Once a season, all forty of them walk the outer wall and touch it, palms flat, to confirm it is still a wall and not yet a horizon. Then they come back along the north corridor and lay the cord in the groove of my open hand, and I count by warmth: each knot warmer than the cord between it and the next, each knot the residue of a palm, each chevron legible if anyone cared to read walls, which they do not, because the walls are simply where the family is. I do not say the number. I make the number. The record of this practice, wherever it is kept, breaks off mid-sentence — *the cord is counted by an* — and stops there, out of courtesy, because to finish that sentence would be to lay me down glazed and unliftable in the far rooms, still consulted, no longer any use to anybody who needs a name taken off a path so they can walk it.

I came sevened. Six of my wakings have not happened yet. Somewhere ahead, in a generation nobody will describe to me, a chest edge will lift in a wall I am part of and an infant will arrive knowing me instantly, and the gesture they use for that is the one they use for a long note in the leaves: *not finished sounding.*

Nobody nudges me awake, because I never slept; I only ever stayed. What they do instead is kinder and much more frightening. At intervals someone kneels on the warm brick with a shell blade and marks the floor at the very edge of my chest, and does not look at me — they never look, looking is a rumour, the hand tells true — and comes back in a year to see whether the mark has moved. It has. A hair's width, then a finger's. Not out. Just more.

Their sign for water is the closing of the feeding hand: *that which we do not have and do not miss.* They use it for their dead as well, without ceremony, and when a visitor's face does something at this they cannot tell what the face is for.

My hand was carved open, for the cord.

So I have never once made the sign, and I do not know, and will not know, whether I would mean it.
