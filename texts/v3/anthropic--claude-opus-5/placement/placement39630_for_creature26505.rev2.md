## The Re-cutter

*(said mouth to ear, which means one of two things, and I am not dead)*

---

Put your hair back under the cap. No — closer. There.

You felt for grief before I said anything; your thumb went to my wrist. I saw you do it. Everyone does it now when someone uses a voice, and it's a kindness, and it isn't that. I'm in the other thing. We don't have a word for the other thing because a word would have to be tapped, and this can't be tapped, and you'll understand why in a moment.

Tell me if I'm shouting. I can't hear myself anymore. I'm told I drift loud.

---

My hands first, so you know what you're talking to. Look at the bands — root of the fingers, heel of the palm, nothing between, soft as a child's. That's a hook hand, that's everyone. But look at the right one. Second joint of the index, that thickening, that's the burin. Forty years of a bone point held just so. I could still do the work blind, and near enough do, in the rime.

I'm a re-cutter.

You already know what we do and you already half-despise us, which is correct and which I will not argue with. But here is the work as it actually is, not as the songs have it.

Every year the record goes up. Every mark anyone ever made — the tally-faces, the marriage-lines, the seam-maps of a stalk with its burrows and its bad places noted, the peg-counts, the fire-lines showing which hearth descends by contact from which of the sixty — all of it rides the wall up at the year's rise, a man's height a day, patient as a tide, out of the light-tissue and into the rain band where the water runs in its thousand vertical threads and the fibre goes soft and blurred, and then up out of the rain into the rime, where it is glazed over and finished forever.

So I climb. Every year, at the turn, in the cold months when the braid are logy, I go up with a spool and a friction plate and a pot of embers on its own line, and I find the highest legible thing, and I read it, and then I come down a year's worth and I cut it again.

And I copy well. I am the best in six stalks; ask anyone; they will grudge it to you but they will say it. I copy stroke for stroke, I copy the flaws, I copy a slipped line where the hand jumped because an ice-slab went past, I have copied a smear of somebody's blood as a smear because it was there.

And it drifts anyway.

Here is the thing nobody outside the work understands, and I want you to have it, because it is the whole of my life and it takes ten seconds to say. A hand that does not understand a shape makes that shape slightly more like a shape it knows. Not on purpose. Not once, decisively, where you could catch it. A little, every year, in the same direction, the way a stalk leans in a steady wind. My hand knows climbers. My hand has cut ten thousand climbers. So a mark I cannot read, that has a body and legs, will get — over a lifetime, over three lifetimes, over thirty-one copyings — a certain roundness in the body, a certain set to the legs.

The bird on the old tally-face at the base of this stalk, the one the floor-people have three stories about.

That's ours. Not mine; it was a bird before I was weaned. But it was a climber-count once, I'm nearly sure, and somewhere in there are four or five hands like mine, each one honest, each one leaning.

We can't help it. You can't copy a thing you don't understand and leave it un-understood. To cut a mark, you have to decide, with your hand, what it is. There is no groove for *I don't know*.

---

Above the readable band there is my teacher.

Her name was four beats — long, short-short, long, like something falling and catching. She was plain-born. She remembered when this was grass and horizon, she walked sinkers to water across ground that stayed where it was, and when the shoots came up she was thirty and she was one of the ones who worked out the running line and the ember-basket in six years of killing themselves in the dark. She used to say it right in my ear, the way I'm saying this: *we made this up, it is not old, it may be wrong.* I found it frightening as a child. I find it the only true sentence anyone has ever said to me.

She's nine years up.

The dead go where the record goes, that's the shape of it — you're left high and the stalk carries you, and everyone says *above* and means it flatly, the way you'd say *downstream*. But most people stop visiting after the third or fourth year, because the rain band is miserable and the rime kills, and there's nothing up there but a face getting further away at a man's height a day.

Re-cutters go up anyway. It's our road. So we get more of our dead than anyone, and we lose them slower, and I'm not sure that's a mercy. Six years running I could reach her. I'd tie off and sit on the plate beside her — she's in the posture they left her in, hands on her knees, cap still bound, glazed the colour of old gum — and I'd have my meal there and tell her about the work, mouth to ear, on a body that had no ear left in any useful sense. Seventh year, the glaze had gone over her face and I couldn't see it. Eighth year I got a hand on her foot.

Last year I got to the ice-line and stopped and looked up into the white gallery, all of them sitting in their rows going up into the dark with the wind screaming past out of the mouth of the stalk, and I couldn't tell which one she was. Any of four. I picked one and said the name aloud, which is allowed, which is what voices are for.

That's my grief and it's ordinary and it's not what I came to tell you.

---

I also keep the post.

You've seen it, or heard of it — out on the flat, four miles from the foot of this stalk, a carved post standing alone in the open for reasons nobody can explain. The re-cutters keep it. We don't advertise it. Once a year I walk out to it with the flat-sickness rising in me the whole way, sweating, gagging, wall-born fool that I am, nothing to put my back against and the sky coming all the way down on every side like a lid — and I deepen its grooves, because weather shallows them, and I set the pegs of the seat beside it.

The post does not rise. Understand me. It is the only cut thing in this world that stays where it is put. My teacher drove it in in the second year and she told me why, and she told me nobody else needed to know, and I have kept that, and I am about to stop keeping it.

It's a sight. A backsight. You lie down on the seat — flat, prone, ear to the ground, and yes, you hear what you hear down there through the flat, and no, we are not going to discuss that, not tonight, not with the taste of the green scrape still on me — you lie down and at the grey hour you line the top notch of the post against the tip of the third stalk east, and you see where the tip falls on the graduations.

One mark a year. That's all it is. Thirty-one marks. A dead woman's habit for finding out whether the world is still doing the thing the world does.

Nobody asks for the reading. It isn't in the record. It has never once been tapped on a wall. It is not, in any sense our people would recognise, knowledge.

---

Eleven days ago I lay down on that seat at the grey hour and sighted the third stalk east, and the reading was short by the length of my forearm.

And the tip was not sharp.

You know how they read at that hour — the whole range, sharp as a break in glass, that's the first thing anyone ever says about it. That one is not. There is a swelling. It has a shoulder on it, like a thumb under a blanket, and I lay there in the cold with my ear on the warm flat listening to the one long hum coming up through the ground — *one* hum, not many, I'll say that much and no more — and I watched it until the light was too strong and I could no longer trust my own eye.

Spear. Stem. Head.

The plain-born all know the sequence. They watched grass all their lives. They know what a stem is doing and what it is doing it *toward*, and they will tell you, close to your ear, that we have got to stem and stayed there thirty years and nobody has seen a flower.

So. My trade. Here is my problem and it is the only real one I have ever had.

If I cut it on the wall, it is true — it enters the record, it is read, it is tapped, it is believed. And it rises. And in ten years it's in the rain band, and my apprentice copies it down a year at a time with an honest leaning hand, and hers after her, and in thirty years there is a mark on this wall that has a body and legs and a certain roundness, and four families will tell you what it says, at length, and differently.

If I cut it on the post, it stays where it is put, which is out on the flat that everyone crosses at a run with their eyes down, at the foot of a thing nobody can explain, in a hand nobody reads.

If I say it — like this, into an ear, one ear at a time — it drifts fastest of all. You know it does. You'll carry it a day and a half to your sister and it will already have grown a wing.

---

So here is what I have decided, and it's why I walked here, and it isn't a mark at all.

I'm going to teach my apprentice the seat. The lying down, the grey hour, the notch, the third stalk east. Not the reading — the reading's mine and it will be wrong by the time she's grown. The *looking*. I'm going to make her walk out onto the flat every year with the sickness in her throat and take it herself, and put her own mark on the post, one a year, and teach it the same way to whoever comes after, forever, as a thing you go and do and not a thing you go and read.

A copy of an act. Not of an answer. It's the only thing I've ever found that doesn't lean.

And then I'm going to tell her what my teacher told me, mouth to her ear, and she will find it frightening, and one day she'll find it's the only true thing anybody ever said.

---

Now lean back. Give me your sternum.

I'll tap you my name, so you have something of mine to carry. Four beats — no. Five. It was four when my grandmother had it. Somewhere between her hands and my mother's hands it grew a beat, and I've never known where, and it's mine now, and it's not hers, and I answer to it.

Everything here is a copy of something that went up out of reach.

*There.* That's me. Take it badly. Everyone does.
