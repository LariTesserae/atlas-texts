# Briefing: Visiting the Warm Stratum

## First, understand what kind of trip this is

This is not a visit. It is an expedition with a two-week tax on either end, and no way to shorten it.

The town sits somewhere between a hundred and a hundred and sixty metres beneath the Shine. That is eleven to seventeen atmospheres. You cannot arrive there from a one-atmosphere world in the state you currently occupy; if you translate in at surface pressure, your chest, sinuses and middle ears will attempt to occupy the volume the water tells them to, and you will die in about four seconds, badly, before you have seen anything. And you cannot leave the way you came, either: after twelve hours down there your tissues will be saturated with inert gas, and stepping out into one atmosphere is a bomb going off in your bloodstream.

So: **compress before you go, decompress after you return.** A chamber on the origin side, three to six hours of squeeze on the way in (slow enough that your joints only ache rather than scream), and five to seven days of stepping back down on the way out. Budget it. Pay for it. Do not let anyone sell you a "quick jump." There is no quick jump. The only people who get out of the Warm Stratum fast are the ones who go up.

Practical consequence: don't go for three days. If you're paying a week of decompression, stay two months. The town will take that long to stop watching you anyway.

## Your body under seventeen atmospheres

Get a hyperbaric medical clearance, and be honest on the forms.

- **Teeth.** Any void under a filling — a crack, a bubble in the cement — becomes a spike of pain on compression and a small explosion on the way out. Get a full survey and every suspect tooth redone. This is the single most common thing that ruins a first trip.
- **Ears and sinuses.** You will equalise dozens of times a day for the whole stay, because in that town "upstairs" and "downstairs" are pressure changes. Learn the Frenzel manoeuvre properly, until it is as unconscious as blinking. If you cannot clear both ears hands-free while carrying a basket, you are not ready.
- **Gut.** Nothing fermentable for two days before compression. Trapped gas is agony going in and dangerous coming out.
- **Sleep apnea, asthma, patent foramen ovale, recent fractures with internal air** — all disqualifying. Get the bubble-contrast echo done.

Expect, on arrival: aching knees and shoulders for a day (compression arthralgia), a strange tremor in the hands, and — if your descent profile was hurried — the nausea and micro-shakes of high-pressure nervous syndrome. It passes. The locals will assume you are frightened, and they will be politely and permanently wrong about you thereafter.

## Gas: the bottle and the fruit

At that depth, ordinary air is poison twice over — the oxygen alone would convulse you inside a minute, and the nitrogen would render you a cheerful idiot before it did. So you will arrive breathing a helium mix with an oxygen fraction of something like one to two percent, which at ambient pressure comes out as a normal, breathable amount. Bring analysers. Bring two. Bring cells for them.

Then there is the question of the lung-fruit.

The fruit is not a courtesy; it is why the town exists, and it is almost certainly the reason people can live at that pressure at all. The mountain is young and degassing, and young mantle rock exhales helium. My strong intuition — and you should verify it yourself with an analyser before you put one in your mouth — is that the bellvines strip dissolved vent gas out of the water and pack the bladders with a helium-dominated mix carrying a trace of photosynthetic oxygen. That is, by accident, a saturation diver's gas. It is also why the hearth-bells work: a fire in a low-fraction, high-pressure oxygen atmosphere burns thin and blue and hot and strange, and everyone inside a bell speaks in absurd chirping voices, which the locals consider the normal sound of indoor life and which will make you cry laughing the first time and never again.

Verify the fruit before you trust it. Check oxygen fraction (too low and you will grey out mid-lane; too high and you will convulse), check for hydrogen sulphide, check carbon dioxide. Test with a stranger's fruit, not a host's, because a fruit you spoil is a fruit somebody's grandmother planted.

Then, if it checks out: **switch to the fruit.** Carry your rig as bailout only. Nothing else will make you legible as a guest rather than a visiting apparatus, and the scrubber material in your rebreather will not last two months anyway.

Learn the rhythm. Bite the stem, hold the bladder to your mouth, breathe it down over a morning, hang it back, take another. You will find yourself doing arithmetic constantly — *how many breaths from here to the third shelf* — and this is exactly what everyone around you is also doing, all the time, and always has been.

## Heat, and the panes

The warm stratum is bathwater, and bathwater is not body temperature. Water pulls heat out of you twenty-five times faster than air, and helium in your lungs pulls more. In thirty-four-degree water you are comfortable for an hour, chilled in six, and hypothermic in a day of sitting still. The locals are lean, dense, and slightly hyperthyroid-looking for a reason, and they eat constantly.

Bring a thin, high-quality, pressure-stable suit — closed-cell neoprene is worthless at seventeen atmospheres, crushed to paper; you want an undersuit of the fuzzy trapped-water kind, or an electrically heated garment with a potted battery and a spare. Accept that you will be wet for eight weeks.

And **learn the panes.** You can see them. They shimmer, they bend the view, they close behind you like a curtain — the locals were not being poetic. Cross upward and the cold takes you like a hand; cross downward toward the vents and the water goes bitter and stinging and then, without much warning, cooks. The shrimp-harvesters go into the scalding band on a count of forty. You are not to go in on any count at all. Your skin is not their skin, your judgement of temperature underwater is unreliable, and second-degree burns in a place with no antiseptics and no bandages and no fire is how travellers die there.

## Moving

You will be issued, or you will make, a stone belt. Get the weighting right the first day, with help: slightly negative, so a normal breath does not lift you and a hard push does. Then walk. Long, floating, patient strides, feet finding the polished crowns of the pillows.

Rules of movement, in order of how badly you will regret ignoring them:

1. **Never let your belt fail.** Two independent attachments, both of materials you have personally tested at pressure. A positively buoyant human in that basin is a corpse on a schedule: you rise, the gas in you expands, and you arrive at the Shine embolised and dead. There is no rescue. Nobody swims fast in warm water at seventeen bar.
2. **Never move vertically faster than your ears.** A shelf is a pressure change.
3. **Never carry anything in both hands** — see below.
4. **Don't kick.** They walk. Swimming is what children and lunatics do, and it makes silt-free water briefly, embarrassingly, cloudy with debris that then rises through the neighbours' terraces.
5. **Watch what you drop.** You will drop something on the first day — a knife, a lamp, a spool — and you will watch it go *up*, and there is no describing the specific horror of that. Tether everything. Everything. Learn the local hitch, which exists precisely for this.
