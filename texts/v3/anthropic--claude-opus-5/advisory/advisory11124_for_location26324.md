# Preparing to Visit the Flats Above the Throats

## A briefing for an ordinary human traveler

---

### First: understand what will actually hurt you

Not the altitude, though four kilometers is real and you will need ten to fourteen days of staged acclimatization somewhere else before you set foot on the table. Not the cold, though the flats at night are a radiative desert and you will wake with frost on your bag. Not thirst, though thirst is what kills the careless.

What will hurt you is the sound, and it will hurt you *invisibly and permanently*, in the first twenty minutes, while you are standing there thinking how magnificent it is.

Everything else in this briefing is secondary to that.

---

### 1. Your ears, and the six months before you go

**Get an audiogram.** A real one, extended high-frequency, with otoacoustic emissions. You want a baseline, because you will want — later, at home, in a quiet room, with a tightness in your chest — to know exactly what the plateau took from you.

**Get custom-molded plugs.** Impressions taken by an audiologist, solid silicone, deep-fitting, with a long canal portion. Not foam. Foam swells, sours, and disintegrates in weeks of saturated warm air, and you will be wearing protection twenty-four hours a day for the entire visit, including sleep. Bring three pairs. Bring a small tin of soft beeswax to over-seal the flange, which is what the locals do and which is better than anything catalogued.

Over the plugs: a slim, low-profile earmuff with a good seal. Not the bulky industrial kind — the seal will break every time you turn your head into a gust, and gusts here are constant. Test the combination in advance in a wind tunnel or an engine test cell if you can get access. Practice putting it all on blind, in gloves, in five seconds.

Understand the ceiling. Doubled protection buys you roughly 45 decibels of real-world attenuation, and no more, ever, because past that point sound arrives through your skull. Bone conduction is not negotiable. **At the lip of a great throat, marked by its single upright stone, you are unprotected no matter what you wear.** You may go there. You may stand there for ninety seconds, once, maybe twice in a visit, and then you turn around and walk sixty meters back, and you will feel that you have been struck by something. Budget those minutes like a diver budgets bottom time. Write them down. Do not be casual about them, because the plateau will invite you to be casual — the ground is flat, the air is warm, nothing looks dangerous, and the deafness accrues silently, which is a joke the universe apparently finds funny.

**Have your teeth checked.** This sounds absurd until you feel your molars conversing. Loose crowns, cracked amalgams, anything marginal — fix it first. Bring a soft night-guard and wear it for any close approach; it converts the tooth-chatter from unbearable to merely strange.

**Wear contacts, not glasses.** Frames buzz on the nose bridge in a way that becomes torture by the third day. Add sealed goggles for garden work; the updraft carries grit, spores, and a fine warm spray.

**Accept the infrasound.** The *woom* below hearing cannot be blocked by anything you can carry. It will go into your long bones and your chest cavity and your bowels. Expect: headache, fatigue that sleep does not touch, a low nausea, mood swings you will not recognize as chemical, and occasional blurring of vision when your eyeballs are being driven at their resonant frequency. This accumulates. There is a reason to keep the trip short. Ten days is a visit. A month is an injury. Three months and you will come home someone slightly different, and unable to explain how.

---

### 2. Your mouth, which will be useless

Nobody speaks. Not "they are quiet" — speech is not a thing that exists here. You will discover within one hour that talking is not merely inaudible but *grotesque*: a person moving their mouth soundlessly at another person, over and over, with increasing frustration, is a person the locals will read as ill.

So learn hands.

Before you go, acquire whatever survival lexicon of the plateau sign is available in your archive — forty to eighty signs will carry you: *greeting; permission; water; food; sleep; I am ill; I am leaving; thank you; wait; danger; no; where; how many days; I do not understand; that is beautiful*. Learn them **large**. This is a language built to be read across a garden clearing through drifting mist under light that changes four times a minute. Small mumbled fingerspelling reads as mumbling. Sign from the shoulders. Sign slowly. Sign in gloves, in rain, and practice under a strobe, because the racing cloud-shadows produce a strobe.

Two pieces of kit make this workable:

- **A chest lamp aimed downward at your own hands.** Diffuse, warm, dim. You light your hands, not their eyes. This one device will do more for your relationships on the plateau than everything else you carry.
- **A wax tablet or a slate you brought yourself,** for drawings. Never, for any reason, draw on the plateau's stone.

If you travel with companions, do not rely on voice radios; they cannot beat a hundred and twenty decibels. Use wrist devices with strong haptics and short text, plus a tug-line code when roped.

### 3. What to carry, what to leave

**Feet.** Soft-rubber soles, heavily siped, no nails, no aggressive lugs. Polished quartz-veined pavement is glass when wet, and after a cloud-column slumps sideways the whole world is a mirror for an hour. Hard lugs skate; worse, hard lugs and metal pole tips **scratch**. A white scratch on that pavement is not damage on a human timescale; it is a permanent addition to the oldest stable surface anyone knows. Rubber tips on your poles. Check them daily. If you break a tip, stop using the pole.

**A long, light probe pole.** The throats range down to a well's width. Small ones hide under sponge-mat and inside flutewood stands. This is the most likely way for a careless outsider to die here, and it takes about a second and a half. Probe ahead in any garden you have not walked before. Learn to feel the whistle of a small vent through the soles of your feet.

**Rope, harness, two stakes, and a partner** for any approach to a lip within thirty meters. The lip is the only edge left on the plateau; everything else has been rounded away by an unimaginable patience, and the lips are glassy, wet, and often invisible in their own condensing spray. The outflow will push you back, which is reassuring and a lie: around the column there are recirculation eddies and cold return flow, and they change minute to minute. Never approach a lip in mist, at night, or alone.

**Water discipline.** In a garden, water is free: a handful of squeezed beard yields half a cup, warm and faintly mineral. Treat it anyway — UV pen plus a filter, and accept that in a foreign biosphere you are immunologically naked and treatment is only a gesture in your favor. Off the gardens there is nothing. Nothing. Kilometers of glowing pavement, and the dew-fins' hand-width stains, which are not a water source, they are a rumor of one. Carry four liters minimum for any crossing and plan the crossing garden-to-garden.

**Wet management.** You will oscillate all day between sauna and freezing desert. Gardens are 100% humidity and dripping; the flats are dry and radiative. Vapor-barrier sleep system.
