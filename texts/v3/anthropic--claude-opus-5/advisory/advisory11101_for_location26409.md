# Preparing to visit The Drowned Room Under the Blackthorn

## 1. First, sit with the fact that you do not have to go

Most places in the multiverse punish the unprepared. This one mostly just kills the unlucky, which is worse, because preparation buys you less than you'd like. The room is four paces by three. There is nothing in it you need. It is not going anywhere — it is getting *larger*, patiently, and it will still be there, more of it, in eighty years. Nobody has ever come back from it with anything except a story and, once, a petal.

So the first item of preparation is a plain accounting. You are proposing to enter a fresh, unstabilised, silt-floored overhead environment, in water of no visibility, packed solid with vegetation, with no rescue of any kind available, in order to feel something. That is a legitimate reason. It is not a good enough reason to go badly equipped.

If you are not a competent overhead-environment diver in your own world, do not learn here. The hole is nine feet deep. Nine feet has killed a great many experienced people, all of them under a ceiling.

## 2. Permissions

There are six people who know about the room. In practice this means: a farmer, the farmer's brother-in-law, two of the local diving-and-caving sort who found it and kept their mouths shut, a retired schoolteacher who keeps a notebook, and one other whose involvement is unclear.

You must become the seventh by consent, not by trespass. Arrive in daylight, at the house, with your hands empty. Do not open with the truth about where you come from; open with the truth about what you want, which is to go down for twenty minutes and come up and take nothing. Say the word *nothing* out loud. Say that you will not tell, and mean it, and keep it, because the room's whole protection is obscurity and the fact that it isn't interesting from above.

Expect to be refused the first time. Expect to be asked to help with something first. This is normal and correct.

Bring cash in the local currency, small notes, and offer it for the gate being left unlocked, not for the room. The room is not for sale and the offer will be understood as an insult.

## 3. Kit, and the small treacheries of crossing worlds with it

Bring your own cylinders, filled, from home if you can. Cylinder valve threads, fittings, and pressure standards differ between worlds far more than people expect, and the nearest fill station is thirty miles off and will ask why a stranger wants air. Carry both DIN and yoke adapters, a spare O-ring kit, a spanner that fits your own gear and nothing else, and enough gas for four times the dive you intend.

- **Twin small cylinders or a slung pony**, independent, with independent first stages. Two of everything that carries gas. In an overhead you do not share, you do not improvise, you turn around.
- **Drysuit** if you have one; a good 7 mm semidry with hood and boots if you don't. Seven degrees, still, no sun, no exertion. You will be colder faster than you think, because you will be doing almost nothing but hanging still and touching plants.
- **Gloves: three-millimetre, five-fingered.** Thicker and you lose the weed, and the weed is how you see. Thinner and the fresh fracture at the ceiling will open your hand. Cover all skin. Read section 5 before you decide to be brave about this.
- **Primary light and two backups**, each independently switched, each tested in the cold. Understand in advance that light is nearly ornamental down there and you will still need it absolutely, for the exit and for the ceiling.
- **A reel with thirty metres of line, and a second small reel.** You need fifteen. Bring thirty. Bring line markers and directional arrows even though the room is smaller than a kitchen. The roof shelves out beyond the mouth of the shaft in every direction. This is the thing that will kill you: you will be two metres from open sky with rock over your head and you will not find the hole, because there is no light to find it by, and no sound, and the weed is the same everywhere. **The line is the trip.** Everything else is decoration.
- **Line-cutting tool**, two, one on the harness and one on the wrist. The weed does not grab. Old rope, roots, and the rusted hinge sockets do.
- **A slate and pencil,** knowing you will not read it.
- **Minimal weight, trimmed slightly negative,** because at nine feet your buoyancy will swing badly and you must be able to hold position without finning. You will move by pulling on the weed, hand over hand, slowly. Finning silts the room out for an hour and, more importantly, silting is a rhythm, and rhythms don't work here.

Leave the underwater comms unit at home. See section 7.

## 4. The team above

Two people minimum on the surface, both briefed, both sober, both with the car keys.

One is the **tender**: sits at the rim, hand on your line, does nothing else for the whole dive. Not phone, not conversation. Agree the pull code beforehand and rehearse it on grass — one pull *are you well*, one back *well*, three *coming out*, continuous *hauling now, help me*. Pulls are events. Events survive down there. Nothing else does.

One is the **timekeeper and driver**: writes down, on paper, the surface clock time at which you go under, and every five minutes after, and the time you surface. This paper is important and you will want it later, when you are arguing with yourself.

Both should know the route to the nearest gate an ambulance can reach, and how long that takes, and that the answer is *too long*. Both should have dry clothes, blankets, a flask of something hot and sweet, and instructions to put you in the car with the heater running whether or not you say you are fine.

Nobody enters the water to look for you. Make this an order and make them agree to it out loud. Two bodies is worse than one and there is no version of this where a rescue works.

## 5. Your own body, in the fortnight before

This is the part that ordinary dive planning doesn't cover, and it is the part that matters most, because the room eats the interval between one thing and the next and your body is almost entirely made of intervals.

- **Do not go down with an open wound, however small.** Clotting is a cascade — a slow sequence of one thing then the next thing. Down there the *then* is thin. People report cuts that simply keep opening for the duration of the dive and then close all at once, absurdly fast, at the surface. Sometimes they don't. Tape your knuckles. Wear the gloves. Do not shave that morning.
- **Nothing in your bloodstream that needs metabolising.** No alcohol for twenty-four hours — you will not sober up down there, you will stay exactly as drunk as you arrived, held in amber, and you will make decisions with it. No antihistamines, no sleep aids, no slow-release anything. If you are on a course of medication with a rhythm, either postpone the dive or accept that the dose you took at breakfast may not have happened yet by the time you climb out.
- **Eat lightly, and four hours before.** A large meal in the stomach in that room sits like a stone in a shoe. It does not digest. It waits. Several people describe this as the single most unpleasant physical sensation of the visit, worse than the cold.
- **Empty your bladder immediately before, twice.** Cold diuresis is immediate and the room does not offer you the interval in which to be patient about it.
- **Do not go down in the middle of something unfinished in your head.** A grief you are three days into. An argument you are halfway through. A decision you have been letting settle. Grief and decisions are made of *and then*; that's what they are; they're processes, not states. The room does not resolve them and it does not worsen them. It holds them open, exactly as they were, like the hand-shaped absence in the weed — and when you surface they resume from precisely where they were, having gained nothing from the hour, and this is a specific and lasting unpleasantness that some people carry for months. If something is working itself out in you, let it, and come back in the spring.

## 6. Skills to arrive with

Practise these at home, not here.

**Blind line work.** In a pool, at night, mask blacked out, run a reel, tie off, follow it out, one-handed, in gloves. Do it until it is boring. Down there it will be your only sense that reports on anything larger than your own arm.

**Event breathing.** This is the one nobody expects. A breath is a shape made out of time — inhale, turn, exhale, pause — and down there the middles go missing. Divers describe reaching for a lungful and finding they have *arrived at the end of it* without having had it, and the sensation is indistinguishable from suffocation, and it triggers exactly the panic you cannot afford in an overhead. The trick, which you must drill until it's automatic, is to stop measuring breaths by feel and start *counting* them. Numbers are events. Rhythm is duration. Count to a hundred breaths on a pool bottom, deliberately, mechanically, no rhythm, each one a separate decision. Then do it again blindfolded. Then again cold.

**Tap code.** Your voice will come out as a click. So will your buddy's. So will your regulator, mostly — the friendly clatter of exhaust bubbles becomes a dry scatter like sand on glass, and its absence is the single most disorienting thing about the room for experienced divers. Agree a tap code on rock and rehearse it. Two taps: *I am well*. Three: *out, now*. A long dragging scrape: *come to me*.

## 7. What to leave in the car

- Anything that measures duration and expects to be believed. Take a dive computer if you like, take two, but understand you are taking them as *specimens*, not instruments. They will not agree with each other or with the paper on the rim.
- Voice comms. Useless, and worse than useless, because the click-stripped human voice arriving in your ear is genuinely frightening and you will not be expecting it in your own head.
- Long-exposure photography ambitions. Exposures come out as the source described the bubble: beginning, thin grey, end. A word with the middle letters missing.
- Any intention regarding the globes of light. Leave the intention in the car with the camera.

## 8. In the room: the standing rules

Learn these as rules, not as reasoning, because reasoning down there is slow and the room is not.

1. **The line, always the line.** One hand on it or one metre from it. There is no such thing as a quick look under the shelf.
2. **Do not take a globe.** They lift out like a peeled egg. The weed around the gap dies within a day and does not recover, and there are only forty, and they are the room's only work. Assign your buddy to stop you physically. Divers who have been down report the urge as *specific* and *arriving from outside*, and describe reaching for one before deciding to. Be forewarned that you may find your hand already moving.
3. **Give yourself one deliberate minute of total darkness** — everything off, one hand on the line, one hand on your partner. This is what you came for. You will see forty afternoons hanging in nothing at all, with no room around them, and your palm behind the nearest one still black. Count breaths through it. Do not extend the minute.
4. **Mind the fracture.** The break in the ceiling is months old and still sharp. It will not have rounded and it will not warn you.
5. **Do not put your hand, and above all not your head, into a fresh corridor in the weed.** If a wrist-thick tube opens toward you at walking pace: stop moving entirely and let it pass. There is nothing in it. There has never been anything in it. Let it pass anyway.
6. **Leave the shrimp alone.** They hang in the fronds like commas. They are what happens to a body that cannot get a stroke finished.
