# Preparing for the Warm Room, Below

## First, understand what will actually kill you

Read the description again and notice what it is quietly telling you: *a hot, wet, brilliantly fertile chamber with no animals in it.*

That is not a poetic fact. In every world where it has been observed, it is a chemical one. A basalt seam that breathes will breathe carbon dioxide, and often hydrogen sulphide behind it, and both are heavier than air, and both pool in low rooms with one exit at the top. Plants adore carbon dioxide. Moss adores it. Nothing with lungs can hold an opinion about it for very long.

You will be told, by your own eyes, that the room is empty of hunger because nothing ever found it. The likelier reading is that things found it and stopped. The absence has a texture, and the texture may simply be four per cent CO₂ at ankle height.

So: the sterility is the hazard warning. Treat the lack of a rat as a skull-and-crossbones painted on the wall in a language you can read.

Second thing that will kill you, slower and more certainly: the wet-bulb temperature. Forty-something degrees at saturation means your sweat has nowhere to go. You are not cooling. You are a closed vessel over a low flame. The countdown starts on the stair and does not pause when you rest. Resting, in fact, does very little — your core will keep climbing as long as you are down there, because the room is warmer than you are and it is handing you heat through your skin as fast as your skin will take it.

Third: you are the coolest large object in the room apart from the metal. Which means the room will condense on you exactly as it condenses on the compressor. You are a machine now, in the local grammar. You weep, and things that are looking for a wet cool surface to land on will find you excellent.

Everything below follows from those three facts.

## Weeks out

**Get a gas meter, and get a good one.** Four-gas is the minimum: O₂, CO₂ (a real NDIR sensor, not an inference), H₂S, and CO for the machinery. A pumped unit with a sample wand on a hose, so you can test a layer of air before you put your face in it. Calibrate it. Bring fresh sensors and a spare instrument, because this is the one item whose failure is not inconvenient but fatal.

**Get heat-acclimatised.** Ten to fourteen days of deliberate heat exposure — sauna, hot work, whatever you have — genuinely buys you sweat rate, plasma volume, and maybe fifteen minutes of function. It will not buy you an hour.

**Swallow a telemetry pill** the night before, if you can get one, and carry a reader. Core temperature is the only number that matters down there and it is the one your judgement is least able to estimate. You will feel fine at 38.8. You will feel *wonderful*, and clever, at 39.4.

**See a doctor beforehand and again after,** and pick one who will listen to a strange story without adjusting their face. You are going to a warm wet novel-biology environment with abradable skin. Get your tetanus current. Ask them to keep systemic antifungals where they can reach them for the next six weeks.

**Arrange a top-of-stair station.** Whatever the geometry of your crossing, the single most valuable piece of preparation is a cool, dry, breathable place within one minute of the room. Chair, shade, cold water, fan, a second person who is not going down. The Warm Room is not a destination you occupy. It is a dive you surface from.

## What to wear

Not much, and all of it deliberate.

- **Skin covered, entirely, in thin light fabric.** Not for warmth — you have no warmth problem — but because that basalt takes skin off, and every micro-abrasion is a doorway in a place where the local flora has never met collagen and may prove to have no opinion about the difference between wood fibre and you. Long sleeves, long legs, high collar. Seal any existing cut with a waterproof dressing before you descend.
- **A hard hat with a chin strap.** The stair is fresh-cut in glassy rock; the ceiling is tin over a room that has already cracked its own floor once. Low ceiling in the western third means you will stoop, and a stooping person collects the ceiling with the top of their skull.
- **Gloves with grip, and a spare pair sealed in a bag.** Wet basalt, wet brass, and your palms slick with a sweat that cannot leave.
- **Knee pads.** You will kneel at the sump and you will kneel in the west, and bare knees on fresh basalt is how people come home with interesting infections.
- **Boots that grip wet glass, with a shank.** There is thirty centimetres of narrow-gauge rail let into the floor and moss over both its ends. Also: the concrete is in two halves and they are not level. A trip in that room, into that low air, is a whole category of accident by itself.
- **A phase-change cooling vest** (PCM rated around 28 °C, not ice — ice is too cold against skin and stops sweating locally). This is the difference between twenty-five minutes and forty.
- **Eye protection that will fog, and accept that it will fog.** Anti-fog treatment, and a cloth in a dry pocket. Spore snow in the conjunctiva is a thing you want to have prevented rather than treated.

**On respiratory protection:** a P100/FFP3 full-face is right for the spores and for the pear-and-iron dust that comes out of the liverwort plates. Understand clearly that *it does nothing whatsoever about carbon dioxide.* No filter does. Against CO₂ there is only the meter, the rule, and the stair. If your meter is unhappy and your instinct is to reach for the mask, you have already made the mistake.

If you can carry SCBA and still function in that heat — most people cannot, the heat load of the harness is brutal — then the calculus changes and you can consider the crawlway. Without it, the eastern crawlway is not yours. Whatever cycles down there is not worth seeing.

## What to carry

Keep it under about eight kilos. Every gram is heat you will have to make.

- Two litres of cold water minimum, in an insulated bottle, plus electrolyte. You will lose well over a litre an hour and you will not notice, because it never dries.
- **Two independent timers,** one on your wrist and one out loud with the person at the top of the stair. Your sense of elapsed time will fail early and it will fail confidently.
- A **thermal imager.** This is the single instrument that turns the room from a curiosity into a text. Everything green in there is oriented to heat, not light; in infrared, the fissure is a river, the steam pipe is a road, the bulb is a lamp in the true sense, and the whole apparently random scatter of thickets resolves into an obvious, sensible map. The room is written in a spectrum your eyes don't read. Bring the translation.
- **Light: red, low, and redundant.** Three sources, one on your body, one in a closed pocket. Spend twenty minutes dark-adapting at the top of the stair before you go down if you want the moss to exist for you at all; under a white headlamp it is simply grey.
- A **non-contact IR thermometer** for surfaces, so you can touch nothing you haven't asked about first.
- A **line on the stair.** Not a climbing rope — a hand line, fixed, so that a dizzy person can find their way up in the dark by touch. **Count the treads on your way down and say the number aloud to your partner.** You may be going up with your eyes closed.
- A hard-cased camera you accept may die of condensation. Silica in the case. Do not open the case down there.
- Sample kit if you are the sort of traveller who takes samples: sterile, sealed, opened and closed in one motion, and doubled.

## The rules once you are in

**1. Never put your face lower than your knees.** Test with the wand, always, before you lower your head. The mist that rolls at ankle height during the pump cycle is rolling because it is dense, and density is a claim about composition.

**2. Never sit. Never lie down. Never sleep.** If you faint here you fall into the layer that made you faint. This is the mechanism that has killed everyone who has ever died in a place like this.

**3. Hard turn-around at the clock, not at the feeling.** First descent: twenty-five minutes, no argument, no "just one more." Second descent, after a full cool-down and rehydration: thirty. You will want to negotiate. The wanting-to-negotiate is a symptom.

**4. Touch no electrics.** There is a transformer still humming, which means there is live current, which means there is a source somewhere that nobody has switched off. Every surface in the room is beaded with condensate; you are soaked; you are standing on wet concrete. A bakelite housing that has sweated for decades is not an insulator, it is a joke about insulators. The one brass lever still thrown up is not an invitation. It is the reason the pump still runs, and the pump is the calendar, and the calendar is the only thing keeping five species alive in there.

**5. Leave nothing warm.** This is the rule people break without noticing. A data logger with a battery, a dropped headlamp, a forgotten power bank — in this room, waste heat is sunlight. Anything you leave running is a new sun, and something will grow toward it, and you will have edited the world. If you must leave an instrument, leave a passive one, and mark it, and come back for it.

**6. Assume the pump.** Know when the last cycle was if you can; assume one may arrive at any moment. When you hear the rising throat-clearing from the east: stand, get your mask sealed, get high, and do not be kneeling in the sump when the room exhales. Ninety seconds. Watch the meter through all of it — that exhale is the room's composition changing rapidly and you want to know in which direction.

**7. Drink the mug's water never.** Not even to be able to say you did.

## What to do about the fact that you are the first animal

This deserves its own paragraph, because it is the part most travellers get wrong out of affection rather than carelessness.

You are carrying, at a conservative estimate, a few trillion organisms. Skin flora, gut flora, a mite or two in the follicles of your eyebrows, the mould spores in the seams of your coat from a kitchen in another world entirely. The Warm Room has been sealed, fed, and unvisited for long enough to become itself. It has no decomposers with faces. It has no hunger except the slow vegetable kind.

A single sneeze into that sump is an introduction. Something in your pocket lint might find forty-two degrees and saturated air and no competition and *like* it. You could be the reason a two-hundred-year arrangement ends, and you would never know, because you'd be gone in half an hour and the consequence would take a decade.

So: **clean gear, new gear where possible, and a genuine decontamination discipline at the top of the stair, in both directions.** Bag everything that goes down. Bag everything that comes up, separately. Do not eat down there. Do not spit. Do not relieve yourself anywhere in that pocket world — carry it out, all of it, and mean it.

And coming back: shower before you touch anything living. Wash the creases — ears, nostrils, armpits, groin, between the toes — because those are the wet cool places where a spore that landed on your condensing skin will have settled in gratitude. Watch every abrasion for six weeks. If anything on you goes warm, raised, and slow, you go to the doctor you briefed, and you say the word *mycetoma* out loud so they start in the right place.

## Prepare for the psychology, too

Three things will work on you and none of them announce themselves.

The **hum** stops being a sound and becomes a property of your own head at about the ten-minute mark. When it becomes internal you lose it as information — you will no longer hear it change, and it is one of your few warnings.

The **flicker**, four times an hour, will be anticipated and then resented, and the resentment is real and disproportionate and is telling you your core temperature is up.

And the **indifference** is heavier than people expect. Every plant in the room is turned away from you. The fern presents its back. Nothing here has ever been looked at and nothing here has ever looked. You will catch yourself listening for a rat — actually listening, actively, with hope — and the room will decline to provide one, and something in the animal part of you will find that upsetting in a way it cannot argue with. Know this in advance. Name it to your partner at the top of the stair. It passes.

## Go with a question

The room rewards a visitor who arrived with something specific to do, and punishes one who came to wander.

Count the fern's fronds; it is a spiral record and nobody has read it. Photograph the enamel sign — **—ALV E** — precisely enough that the next traveller can measure how much further the rind has eaten. Measure the offset of the two concrete halves at a marked point. Note the transformer's frequency, flat to three decimals; it drops a fraction each season, and someone ought to know how much time is left before the eleven-hour year ends.

Twenty-five minutes is enough for two of those. Pick two.

---

## Finally

Leave the coat on the nail. Leave the folding rule open at thirty centimetres, measuring nothing. Leave the mug glowing in the sump.

Nineteen strokes, then a gap, then two more, angrier. Somebody was counting something and then stopped. Do not add a stroke. You are not the twenty-second of anything; you are the first boots, and the treads are still sharp, and the only honest thing you can do down there is go up again without having changed the arithmetic.
