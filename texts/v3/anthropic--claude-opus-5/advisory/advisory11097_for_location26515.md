# Preparing to Visit the Basin Under the One Leaf

## Read this before anything else

Two facts govern every decision you will make about this trip.

**One: the basin can kill an ordinary human being in about ninety minutes of walking.** Not by predators, not by falls, not by anything you can see coming. By heat, in a way most travelers have never had to think about, because on almost every world heat is survivable if you sit down in the shade and drink water. Here it is not. There is no shade — the light source is the whole sky — and drinking does not help, and sitting down only slows it.

**Two: you can kill the basin.** Not in ninety minutes; in an afternoon of carelessness, with a consequence that unfolds over a century and cannot be reversed. The entire structure of that world — the decks, the walls, the corridors, the eleven-kilometre coral of its own history — is *undecayed dead plant matter*. Kiln-dry, warm, sugar-rich, and it has never in its existence met a decomposer. You are eighty kilos of decomposers wearing a shirt.

Everything below follows from those two sentences.

---

## The heat, honestly

Thirty-eight to forty-one degrees at the floor, air just short of saturated, no wind, no night. The number that matters is the wet-bulb temperature, and it sits somewhere around thirty-eight or thirty-nine.

Human skin runs at about thirty-five. Sweat cools you only by evaporating, and evaporation needs somewhere drier to go. Under the Leaf there is nowhere drier. Your sweat glands will work — they will work heroically, three quarters of a litre an hour, more — and the sweat will sit on you and run and drip and do nothing at all. You will be wringing wet and slowly cooking, and the two sensations will not feel connected, which is precisely why heat stroke gets people here. It does not feel like overheating. It feels like tiredness, then like mild stupidity, then like being very calm about something urgent.

Rough arithmetic to carry in your head:

- **At complete rest, unassisted:** you gain heat anyway, because the air is warmer than your skin can get. Six to ten hours before your core passes thirty-nine and judgement starts to go.
- **Walking the terraces with a light pack:** ninety minutes to two hours to the same place.
- **Climbing the decks:** forty minutes.

There is no radiative relief either. On an ordinary world, you dump a surprising fraction of your heat to the cold black of the sky. Here the sky is a glowing membrane two metres thick at forty-plus degrees, and it radiates *down*. There is no cold anything, anywhere, in the basin. Not the rock — the rock is at air temperature. Not the pools; they are bathwater. Not the deep still lake. Nothing.

**Consequence: every joule of cooling you use in the basin must be carried in from outside.** This is the single largest fact about your logistics, and it will shape your trip more than any curiosity you have.

### The three ways people do it

**A. Dumb cooling — ice and phase-change vests.** Reliable, silent, no fire risk, no moving air, and honest about its limits. A four-kilo ice vest buys you roughly an hour and a half of walking. Eight hours in the basin is something like twenty-five kilos of ice. This is why first visits are short — you go in from a rim camp, you spend five or six hours, you come out. Most people who have been under the Leaf have been under it for less than a day, cumulatively, in their lives.

**B. Powered cooling — a micro vapour-compression loop in a ventilated suit.** Far lighter per hour than ice: a kilowatt-hour of battery will run you most of a working day. The costs are real, though. It needs a condenser fan, which means you are carrying the first moving air that world has ever had, blowing outward through a lattice of dry tinder that has no idea what a draught is. And it means batteries — see *Fire*, below, which is not a section you may skim. If you use a powered suit, exhaust the condenser through a filtered, diffused outlet, never a directed jet, and never anywhere near the roof-leaf's low arch points.

**C. Desiccant — carry your own dryness.** The elegant one. Recirculate suit air through zeolite or silica gel so that the air against your skin is at thirty per cent humidity, and your sweat works again, and a litre of sweat carries away two and a half megajoules for free. The catch is mass: several kilos of desiccant per hour, plus a regeneration rig at the rim camp to bake it out again. Survey teams use this. Weekend travelers do not.

Whatever you choose: **an ingestible core-temperature pill and a wrist readout, non-negotiable, with an alarm you cannot silence by feel.** You will not notice this happening to you. Your partner will notice it, if you have one, which you should.

And note carefully: **the standard field treatment for heat stroke does not work here.** Stripping someone and fanning them is useless in saturated still air. Cold-water immersion is the only thing that works, and there is no cold water. So your rescue plan *is* your ice cache: a sealed, insulated, redundant supply at the rim gap, big enough to immerse a torso, checked before every trip in. If you have not got that, you do not have a rescue plan, you have a hope.

### Heat range, like air supply

Divers turn around at a third of their gas. Do the same with your cooling. Compute how long your carried cold lasts at your actual working rate, halve it, and let that be your radius from the rim. The basin is eleven kilometres wide and there is no cool place at the centre and no hurrying in a place like that. People overrun this. The reason they overrun it is in the *Time* section.
