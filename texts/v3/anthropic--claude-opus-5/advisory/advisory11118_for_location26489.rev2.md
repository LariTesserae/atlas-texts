# Preparing to Visit the Kiln at the Ninth Turning

## First, understand what kind of trip this is

It is not a cave trip, though you will crawl. It is not a climb, though you will spend a day on scree. It is a **heat expedition into a sealed radiant chamber occupied by a living population that your body temperature kills on contact.**

Hold that last part in your head until it stops being an abstraction. In this room, you are not warm. You are the coldest object anyone there has ever touched. Their blood is a salt eutectic that sets somewhere around 55–60 °C; you walk in at 37 °C. Every individual that lands on your bare skin begins to harden within seconds, and its hooks leave that glassy residue as it goes, so it dies *welded to you*. You cannot enter the Kiln without killing. You can only choose the scale.

Plan for a total of forty to sixty minutes inside the main chamber, split into two or three pushes. Plan three months to earn those minutes.

---

## The numbers you are working against

These are estimates; calibrate on site with instruments, not with your skin, because your skin will lie to you in dry heat.

- **Outside, night:** −15 to −5 °C, thin air, no humidity at all.
- **Entry crawl:** 45–55 °C. This is your refuge and your airlock. It is also, for them, the border of death — the stiffened ones at its edge are the proof.
- **Upper (cool) end of the chamber:** 65–75 °C.
- **Mid-chamber:** 75–85 °C.
- **Low end, near the fissure:** 95–120 °C, with a brutal radiant component. Treat the last thirty paces as a place you look at rather than enter.
- **The hunters:** hotter than everything. Assume 130 °C-plus. Contact is a deep burn in under a second.

Your governing problem is not air temperature. Dry air at 80 °C is a sauna and people survive saunas. Your problem is that **every surface radiates at you from every direction**, so your body's usual trick of dumping heat by radiation runs backwards, and your only remaining cooling is evaporation — in a room where you must not spill water.

---

## Three months out: condition the body

**Heat acclimatization is not optional and cannot be faked.** Ten to fourteen consecutive days of ninety minutes' moderate exertion in dry heat, building toward 50–60 °C. The adaptations are real and measurable: plasma volume expands, you begin sweating earlier and more copiously, and your sweat gets less salty, which matters enormously here. An unacclimatized person in the Kiln has perhaps fifteen usable minutes. An acclimatized one has an hour.

**Altitude.** Thin enough to scrape. Mild hypoxia stacks with heat strain and makes heat exhaustion arrive without announcing itself. Arrive a week early and sleep high.

**Aerobic base and a strong crawl.** The forty-pace entry crawl is trivial on the way in and can kill you on the way out. Practice it: on your belly, heat-soaked, dehydrated, with a pack, in the dark, on a stiff-soled floor. Time it. Double the time. That doubled number is your safety margin.

**Salt-load and hydrate** in the days before, and drink 500 ml immediately before entry. You will lose 1.5–2.5 litres an hour.

**Cosmetic housekeeping with a purpose:** shave your head, face, forearms. Not for comfort — hair sheds, and shed hair is organic matter falling into a world that has never had any, onto hot nitrate salts that are powerful oxidizers. Trim nails. No contact lenses; they will dry onto your corneas. No skin oils, sunscreen, lip balm, or anything petroleum-based anywhere on your body or kit. Hot nitrate crust plus grease is a fire you do not want to be wearing.

## The gear, and why each piece exists

### The suit: hot outside, cool inside

The counterintuitive design principle: **make your exterior as hot as you can bear to make it, and insulate your body from it.**

A shiny, cool outer shell seems right — reflect the radiation, stay cool. It is right for you and catastrophic for them. A 40 °C exterior is a killing field: everything that lands on it dies. An outer shell allowed to sit in equilibrium with the room, at 65–70 °C, is *survivable footing* for the animals that will inevitably drift onto you. They will grip, feed briefly on the gradient into the ventilated air above your shoulders, and let go alive.

So: **an outer layer of aluminized ceramic felt or high-temp glass cloth** — loose, bagged, and deliberately allowed to heat-soak. Underneath, **a genuine insulating gap**, and inside that, **a ventilated cooling garment**: PCM packs or ice vest at your torso, plus a small pump moving air through the inner layer and out through a condenser.

Yes, this is bulky. Yes, the minimum viable version is just a loose aluminized coverall, a gaiter-sealed hood, and an ice vest, and you go in for twenty minutes and come out. That version works. It just kills more of them and gives you a third of the time.

**Gloves:** two pairs. Thick, high-temp, sacrificial outers that you will abandon; thin liners under. You will want to touch the wall. You may touch the wall for a slow count of three, and then you will have a glove full of cement and corpses.

**Boots:** stiff-shanked, thick low-conductivity soles — wood, cork, or ceramic felt sandwich. The floor is 70 °C+ nacreous fused bone that rings underfoot, thin and gallery-riddled over the hot fissure. Rubber will soften and take up residue; you will come out with a sole made of them.

### The mask

**A heat-and-moisture-exchange mask is the single best item you will carry.** It does three jobs:

1. Takes the worst edge off the inhaled air and prevents airway drying, nosebleeds, and the coughing fits that come from three breaths of this stuff.
2. Suppresses the sneeze. A sneeze is a shout.
3. **Traps your exhaled water.** You breathe out roughly 40 grams an hour of the only liquid water on this planet. Uncaptured, it falls as the first rain the Kiln has known. On a salt crust that has been dry since before anyone cut that spiral, a drop of water is not a trivial event — it dissolves, it deliquesces, it changes the cement. Catch your breath and take it home with you.

Seal the goggles too, and accept that you will be looking through fog. The fog is the point.

### Light — and why you should mostly not use it

They have no eyes. Light neither disturbs nor helps them, and it will not help you much either: a lamp in that chamber shows you a wall of pale wax-coloured plates two hand-spans away, and nothing else, and it flattens the place into a texture.

**The correct instrument is a thermal imager**, high-temperature-ranged and shuttered for an 80 °C ambient (most uncooled microbolometers will simply saturate and show you a grey nothing — confirm the calibration before you fly). In thermal, the Kiln resolves: the living coat *is* a heat map, and you can read the mountain's temperature by the depth of its fur, exactly as described. You will see the drapes as cooler catenary lines against a hot vault. You will see the hunters coming, which is the other reason to carry it.

Bring a red-filtered handlamp for footing and use it in short, low glances. Bring a second, sealed, and never use it unless the first fails.

### The line

You will be crossing 200 paces of slope in the dark with one landmark, and you will be doing it heat-stressed, which is to say stupid. **Run a guideline from the crawl mouth.** Use fine steel cable or aramid, not nylon — nothing that softens or creeps at 90 °C. Accept that you are leaving it; by the time you return it will be beaded with residue and half-adopted by the floor. Knot it at ten-pace intervals so you can count your way back by feel.

### Water, cool

Four to six litres, in a PCM-jacketed reservoir. Water carried in bare will reach 70 °C within fifteen minutes and scald your throat. Drink small and often, on a timer, before thirst — thirst arrives too late in dry heat.

### Everything else

Tethers on every single object, because a dropped canteen on a floor that rings is a cascade trigger. Throat mic and bone-conduction earpiece per person, so you can talk without speaking. A blunt ceramic or brass scraper for the shedding. A no-water burn kit: hydrogel pads, non-adherent dressings, a scalpel and forceps for extracting hooks. A probe — a light aluminium rod — for the floor near the fissure.

**Do not bring:** food, urine bottles, adhesive tape with organic solvents, anything that leaks, anything you cannot account for on the way out.

## The approach, and the threshold

Climb in the outside night. Two reasons. First, the cold air sinking in through the doorway pushes the population downslope, thinning the upper chamber and leaving a low, knee-deep river of merely-hot air flowing along the crawl and out across the floor — a survivable corridor that will not exist at midday. Second, the mountain's day heat on grey scree is its own problem.

The scree gives back half a step. Budget double the time and triple the water for the ascent, and cache your cold-weather layers, your radio, your spare everything, **outside** the doorway. Nothing out there will touch them.

At the threshold, stop. Do the arithmetic that everyone skips: you are about to step across a hundred-degree boundary in the space of one stoop. Your fingers may be numb; forty paces later your eyeballs will be drying. Your body will have no idea what is happening to it and will give you bad data for the first ten minutes. **Do not trust your subjective sense of heat inside the Kiln at any point.** Trust the thermometer, the timer, and the water counter.

Above you as you stoop: three courses of dressed basalt, a spiral nearly gone, five vertical strokes. Look at them. Add nothing.

## Inside: the rules

**One. Never enter alone.** One person goes in; one stays at the crawl mouth with the recovery kit, the cold cache, and the drag-line. Heat exhaustion in there does not present as heroic struggle. It presents as a person deciding, calmly, to sit down for a minute.

**Two. Silence is a physical safety protocol, not a courtesy.** A shout drops every drape in the chamber. You will hear the cascade travelling away from you and coming back, you will be standing knee-deep in bodies — and then, with the ceiling curtains gone, the vault's raw radiant load lands on you unshaded and unbuffered. That spike, not the bodies, is what would hurt you. Speak only through the throat mic. Cough into the mask. Set your equipment down, never place it.

**Three. Do not stand still.** Within one minute of stopping you are wearing them. Within five you are carrying kilos. Keep drifting.

**Four. Stay high and stay left of the fissure.** The last thirty paces are for looking at. Where the floor is soft and worked and dug through with galleries, it is roofing a hot chamber; probe before weighting.

**Five. Heat on the scalp means a hunter has passed overhead.** Move out from under the drape immediately and quietly. They are not interested in you — in their sensorium you are a hole, a moving absence — but they are arm's length, ten-legged, fast, and hot enough to burn through your hood on contact.

**Six. The niches are the west wall at standing height, spaced by a stride.** Eight bowls, three gaps. Look, image, measure, and **do not touch the salt.** The domes on those crusts were polished by hooked feet over a span you cannot make yourself feel. There is nothing you can learn by a fingertip that a photograph will not tell you better.

**Seven. Turnaround rule: 50% of water, 50% of time, or the first missed word on the radio.** Whichever comes first. The crawl out is uphill in your memory even if it isn't in fact.

## Shedding

Do this **inside the crawl, before the doorway.**

Stop in the cooler section. Scrape the outer shell, the gloves, the boots, the hood — everything — with the blunt scraper, and let what comes off fall where it falls. The ones still alive will find their way back upslope. The ones already hardened onto you will not, and they belong to the floor; let them join it. What you must not do is carry them out into the scree, where they can never become anything.

Then the second, uglier task: check your skin. Anything that got past the seals and hooked you is now a small glassy bead cemented to you, and it will need forceps and a steady hand. Do that outside, in the cold, with a headlamp, sitting down, after you have drunk a litre.

You will still, no matter how carefully you work, come out wearing a fine pale grit in every seam. Some of that grit was alive an hour ago.

---

## Afterwards

Expect to be wrecked for two days: heat strain, dehydration, a headache that sits behind the eyes, a nose that bleeds when you blow it, a strange metallic sweetness in the back of your throat for a week.

Expect, more than that, a specific and unfamiliar grief, and be ready for it, because travellers are almost never ready for it. It arrives about three days out and it is not about the dying. Things die everywhere. It is about the **quality of their not-minding** — that a drape being harvested makes one small sound like a fingernail on a comb and then the sift resumes; that the ones who stiffened in the night's cold are trodden into the floor by the returning tide and become, in a few thousand cycles, another fingernail's thickness of architecture; that you walked through the middle of all of it killing steadily and nothing shrieked, nothing fled, nothing acknowledged you at all.

And then you will think about the eleven niches, and the eight bowls, and the salt carried up a mountain into an oven, in the dark, by somebody who had reasons; and the five strokes over the lintel that mean either five visits, or five people, or five of something else entirely, cut by a hand that also cut a spiral and then went away and let the mountain take back the trail.

Go home. Write down what you saw. Do not add a sixth stroke.
