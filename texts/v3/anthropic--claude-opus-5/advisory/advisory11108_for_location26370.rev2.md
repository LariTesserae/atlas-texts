# Preparing for the Basin Under the Lid

## A traveller's brief

---

## I. Understand first what actually kills you here

Most visitors arrive braced for the wrong thing. They have read about the mothers and the things that hunt the listeners, and they buy armour. Armour is close to useless. Almost nothing in the basin wants to eat you — you are the wrong shape, the wrong temperature, the wrong sound, and you taste of a chemistry that has never existed here. Predation is item four on the list, at best.

What actually kills visitors, in order:

**1. The noise.** Not as an annoyance — as a physical dose. A continuous, structured, hundred-and-twenty-decibel field with a sub-audible carrier under it and an ultrasonic shear above it. It will deafen you in days, nauseate you in hours, and derange you over weeks. Every other hazard in the basin gets easier to survive if you manage this one, because everything you do here you do with a spike through your head.

**2. The floor.** It is a hollow bread-crust of accreted corpses over voids of unknown depth. It booms because there is nothing under it. People go through. Very occasionally they are found.

**3. The air in the low places.** The "sour breath of the deep stone" is not a metaphor. Hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide seep along the same fractures that feed the rinds, and both are heavier than air. They pool in the bowls. They pool *magnificently* in the gullets and throat-holes — which is precisely why the mothers are wedged there, immobile, bathing in it, and precisely why a curious visitor who climbs down for a closer look dies in ninety seconds, smelling nothing, because H₂S kills your sense of smell before it kills you.

**4. Getting lost.** There is no sky, no sun, no shadow, no horizon, no landmark, no compass reference you can trust, and no way to call out. Sixty kilometres of level pale rock that all looks the same in a shadowless grey-brown smear. Two people who separate by four hundred metres on the open pan have lost each other more thoroughly than two people separated by an ocean.

**5. Everything else.** Including the animals.

Plan against that order. Buy your kit against that order.

## II. Six to twelve months out: prepare the body

**Hearing.** Get a full baseline audiogram with high-frequency extension, and repeat it monthly against your return. Accept now — genuinely accept, not nod at — that you will come back with some permanent loss. Everyone does. Manage the magnitude, not the fact. If you already have tinnitus, do not go; the basin will hand you a companion note you will keep for the rest of your life.

**Vestibular training.** The infrasound will put your balance organs into a state they have no category for, and you will be walking on a crackling, uneven, occasionally-hollow surface with no visual horizon to correct against. Train balance blind. Practise walking rough ground with your eyes closed and ear defenders on, roped to a partner, until it stops being frightening.

**Tactile language.** This is the single most important thing you will train, and almost nobody trains it enough. On the pan you cannot speak. You cannot shout. You cannot whistle, signal, or be heard from two metres. Your entire social existence will be conducted through a gloved hand on a forearm, in the dark-equivalent, under vibration, while exhausted.

Learn a proper tap-and-squeeze code, not an improvised one. Forty to sixty terms is enough: *stop, back, void, gas, down, camp, listen, hurt, rope, how long, I am afraid, I am fine*. Drill it wearing four-millimetre gloves, through a suit sleeve, underwater if you can — anything that degrades the channel. Drill it until it survives panic. Two-person teams should be able to conduct a full argument by wrist.

**Cold-wet conditioning.** It is nine or ten degrees, always, with saturated air and permanent drizzle. That is not cold. That is worse than cold, because you will not respect it. Wet nine degrees, unrelentingly, for two weeks, takes people apart. Train in it. Learn your own shivering thresholds.

**Light deprivation.** Your circadian rhythm will have nothing to grip. No dawn, no dusk, no shadow-angle, nothing but two slow thickenings of the fog every day that you will come to read as tides. Spend a month living on a strict artificial schedule in constant dim grey light before you go, and bring that schedule with you as law. Travellers who let their sleep drift in the basin come apart quickly and characteristically: first they lose track of how long they've been there, then they lose interest in whether it matters.

**Psychological screening, honestly answered.** The basin does specific things to specific people. If you are prone to derealisation, claustrophobia under a ceiling, or intrusive rumination, the lid and the chord will find those seams like water finds fractures.

## III. The central doctrine: your acoustic identity

Here is the thing every veteran will tell you and every novice underestimates.

Nothing in the basin sees you. Everything *hears* you. What you must decide, before you take a single step onto the pan, is **what you will sound like** — because you are going to be one of the loudest, strangest objects this world has ever contained, and the shape of your noise determines how one hundred billion animals behave around you.

You have two bad options and one workable one.

**Option A: go quiet.** Wrap yourself in absorbent foam, felt your boots, pad your kit, muffle everything. This is the instinct of every visitor who has read the section about predators stalking in hush.

It is a catastrophic error. A human in full anechoic gear is a *walking quiet-well*. You return no echo. You are a moving amputation in the world-medium — the exact signature of the most frightening thing this ecosystem knows. Scrapers will break rank and flee in a bow-wave forty metres ahead of you. The listeners will orient on your absence from a kilometre away. And the things that hunt the listeners, which specialise in investigating hushes, will come and investigate yours, and they will not be deterred by the fact that you are the wrong shape, because they cannot tell what shape you are. You have made yourself the only thing in the basin that is inherently interesting.

**Option B: go naked-loud.** Clank, crunch, breathe, let your hollow-floor footfalls boom. You will be perceived, correctly, as a large, clumsy, loud, unidentifiable object. Nothing will mistake you for prey; nothing will mistake you for hush. The pan will generally open around you at a polite distance and close again behind you.

The problem is that you cannot actually do this, because you must protect your hearing, and every protective layer you add pushes you toward Option A.

**Option C — the working answer: wear a voice.**

Protect yourself as heavily as you like, then carry an emitter that fills your acoustic silhouette back in. A rasp-beacon: a broadband, irregular, low-information noise source tuned into the scrapers' band, mounted high on the pack, running continuously. It does not need to mimic anything. It needs to be *dull* — loud, featureless, unvarying, uninteresting. You want to read to the basin as a boulder that hums.

Rules for the voice:

- **It never stops.** Redundant units, redundant power, hot-swappable cells carried on the body where they stay warm. If your voice dies on the open pan you have instantly become a quiet-well with legs, and you now have a serious problem you did not have four seconds ago. Treat voice failure as an emergency on par with a breach in your breathing gear.
- **It never varies in an interesting way.** No pulses, no sweeps, no rhythm. Rhythm is information; information attracts attention. Boring is safety.
- **Everyone in the party carries one,** and they are detuned slightly from each other so you can, with practice, tell your partner's direction by the beat between your voice and theirs. This becomes your lifeline for maintaining formation, and it is worth a hundred hours of training on its own.
- **Never point your voice into a quiet-well.** Nothing will come out of it. But something may go in, following the only interesting sound in a world that has learned not to be curious.

There is also the question of **what you leave behind acoustically**. You will scatter ranks of scrapers that have grazed that seam for ten thousand years. The rank re-forms in an hour or so. It is not nothing, but it is close to nothing. What is *not* nothing is a broken party voice that hums on a dropped pack for nine years until its cells fail — a novel, permanent, information-bearing sound in a feedback-locked system that has reached its answer. Count your emitters in and count them out. Bring them home or destroy them.

## IV. Kit, annotated

### Hearing and head

- **Double protection, always**: deep-seated foam plugs under sealed muffs, with a gel cushion and a positive-pressure check every time you re-seat them. Assume 35 dB combined, not the 45 on the box; the field is broadband and your jaw conducts.
- **A rigid, padded chest plate.** This will look absurd. It is for the infrasound. You cannot block infrasound — it comes through your ribs, not your ears — but you can damp the sympathetic resonance of the chest cavity, which is where the nausea and the sternal pressure live. It reduces the dose from unbearable to merely awful.
- **Full skin coverage, including the face.** The clatter's ultrasonic component is far above your hearing but not above your tissue; at close range to a waking colony, exposed skin heats and aches, and prolonged exposure produces a specific headache and a specific dull vertigo that visitors call *the shrill*. Anyone who tells you they walked under a feeding colony bare-faced is lying or is now in a great deal of trouble.
- **A bite guard.** Bruxism is near-universal here. You will not notice yourself doing it. You will notice, after ten days, that you cannot open your jaw properly.
- **Noise dosimeters — two, on different parts of the body, logging continuously.** This is not optional and it is not a formality. Your own perception of the noise stops functioning within the first hour: you *stop hearing the roar entirely* and feel it only in your teeth. You will be certain it is quieter than it is. Your dosimeter is the only honest member of the party. Set hard limits before you enter. Obey them when they go off, especially when you feel fine, because feeling fine is a symptom.

### Breathing and gas

- **A four-gas detector on the chest and a second one on the ankle.** The ankle one is the one that saves you. H₂S and CO₂ pool low; you will walk into a shin-deep lake of it in a shallow bowl and your chest sensor will say nothing while your feet are in a grave.
- **A supplied-air or filtered system with sulphide-rated cartridges**, and a full-face seal. Do not rely on smell. H₂S anaesthetises the olfactory nerve at exactly the concentrations that matter; the classic sequence is *it stinks, it stopped stinking, thank god, it's gone* and then you are on the floor.
- **An escape hood per person**, worn, not packed.
- **Never descend into a gullet, a throat-hole, or any feature with a mother in it.** The mothers are wedged where they are because that is where the gas and the falling food are. They are not the hazard. They are the marker for the hazard. Treat a mother the way you would treat a skull-and-crossbones painted on a door.

### The floor

- **Load-spreading boots** — broad soles, oversized, something between a snowshoe and a marsh-plate. Your weight per square centimetre is the number that matters.
- **A probe pole, used constantly**, not occasionally. The lead walker probes ahead every step. This is tedious beyond description for the first day and then becomes as automatic as breathing. The crust sounds different over a void — a change in pitch, a hollower, longer boom — and you will learn to hear it through your boots, since you cannot hear it with your ears.
- **Rope, and the discipline of using it.** The party moves roped on the open pan. Always. Not because the voids are common but because the alternative — a party member through the crust, invisible, inaudible, unable to shout — is a death you cannot reverse.
- **Crushproof gaiters.** The surface gravel is hollow bone, shed plate and calcite husk, and it is *sharp*. It cuts boots. It cuts skin. And every cut here gets colonised by sulphur-metabolising organisms that your immune system has never met and your antibiotics were not designed for. Treat every laceration as serious on the day it happens.

### Grain management

The three-millimetre grain falls constantly, forever, like snow. It gets into every seal, every mesh, every hinge, every fold. And then the permanent drip cements it.

- **Coarse mesh pre-filters over every intake, changed on a schedule, not on failure.**
- **No zippers.** They will die on day two. Toggles, oversized buckles, magnetic closures, anything you can operate in gloves and clear by hand.
- **A stiff brush on every person's chest strap.** Descale yourself hourly. Descale your partner. This becomes a ritual and it is one of the few pleasant ones.
- **Eye protection with a positive seal and a wiper.** Grain in the corner of the eye, cemented by drip, is how people lose corneas here.
- **Never set gear directly on the pan and leave it overnight.** By morning it will have a rind of mortar on it. By the third day it is part of the floor. This is not an exaggeration; this is the mechanism by which the entire world is made.

### The biofouling problem

You are a warm, wet, mineral-surfaced object exhaling carbon dioxide in a world where everything is competing for a ten-centimetre-tall seam of chemistry. **Rind will try to grow on you.** It takes about thirty hours on a damp outer shell. Where rind grows, scrapers come — and a scraper's mouth is a file, and it does not know or care that the substrate is a human being standing still.

Nobody has been killed by this. People have had boots rasped through, packs opened, and — memorably — a sleeping bivouac sack worked open along the seam by four overlapping ranks.

- **Scrub down twice daily.** Outer shell, boots especially, anywhere water sits.
- **Do not stand still on a seep-flat for more than a few hours.**
- **Do not sleep on the open pan.** Which brings us to the most counterintuitive rule in the basin.

## V. Camp: the quiet-well doctrine

**You camp in the quiet-wells.**

This sounds like madness and it is the settled practice of every party that has gone in and come out.

Consider what a quiet-well is *to you*, as opposed to what it is to the basin. It is a bowl lined in soft, grey, sound-absorbing rock, dozens of metres across, into which no animal will ever willingly go and about which no animal is curious. The lining eats echoes. That means, inside it, **the roar drops away.** Not to silence — you will still feel the basin's chord in your sternum, because that travels through the rock and nothing stops it — but from a working quarry to something like a busy road. Inside a well, you can lift one muff and hear your own voice. You can speak. You can sleep. You can let your dosimeter run down.

Nothing follows you in. Nothing waits at the rim. The animals walk the edges and do not cross, and they have not crossed in ten thousand years.

It is the only refuge on sixty kilometres of pan, and the reason it is a refuge is the reason it is horrifying.

Rules for well camps:

- **Fix a line on the way in and leave it.** The lining is soft and treacherous and your ascent is your only exit.
- **Gas test the floor before you descend, and again at the bottom, and again in the morning.** Wells are bowls. Bowls collect heavy gas. Some wells are fine. Some wells are lethal from three metres down, which is very probably why some of them are so deep.
- **Do not camp in a well if rain is plausible.** They fill. They fill from every direction at once, and the lining turns to a grey slurry, and the fixed line is your entire life.
- **The floor is metres of compacted dead.** It is soft. It is the best sleeping surface in the world. It is made of tens of thousands of animals that went blind and mute in the same instant, circled, got quieter, and stopped. Some travellers find this unbearable and cannot sleep. Some find it restful in a way that worries them afterwards. Know which you are before you are lying on it at hour thirty.
- **Point your voices up and out, or shut them off entirely while inside.** Inside the well you are not a hush; you are simply absent, which is the correct thing to be there.
- **Leave nothing.** Absolutely nothing. A well lining is a permanent archive; anything you drop is still there in a thousand years, and it is the one place in the basin where a foreign object will not be ground down, eaten, or cemented into the general floor. It will just sit in the quiet.

## VI. Entering, moving, and the rain

### The rim walk

Do not helicopter into the middle. Enter at the rim and walk down over three days.

The last few kilometres inside the rim are nearly empty: brighter, quieter, the rinds failing, the grain absent, a light like true dusk. This dead margin is your airlock, and it does three things. It lets your hearing protection and your dosimetry get checked under increasing load rather than all at once. It lets your vestibular system meet the chord gradually rather than as an assault. And it gives you a psychological gradient — a sense that you came from somewhere and can go back to it — which sounds sentimental and turns out, at day nine on the open pan, to be the thing that holds people together.

Establish a rim cache. Make it findable by instrument, not by eye; the pan has no landmarks and your memory of "that hummock" is worth nothing.

### Navigation

- **Lay line.** Kilometres of it. This is the primary method and everything else is backup. On a shadowless level plain under a featureless lid, a laid line is the difference between an expedition and a disappearance.
- **Inertial navigation, two units, cross-checked, reset at every known point.** Expect drift.
- **Acoustic beacons at camps, in a band and pattern you can discriminate from the roar.** Accept that you are adding novel information-bearing sound to the world; keep it low, keep it brief, recover the units.
- **No dead reckoning by feel. Ever.** People circle here. It is not a legend. With no sun, no shadow, no horizon, and a mild permanent vestibular derangement, a competent navigator will walk a four-hundred-metre circle and be certain it was a straight line.

### Light

Here is a small gift the basin gives you: **use as much light as you want.**

Nothing here sees with light. Your lamp is not a beacon. It attracts nothing, warns nothing, alerts nothing. In a world where every other action you take is broadcast to a hundred billion listeners, your headlamp is the one thing you do that is completely private.

Bring the biggest lamps you can carry. Flood the pan. It costs you nothing but battery, and the ability to actually *see* the crackling ground you are walking on is worth an enormous amount.

And you should know, because nobody tells you beforehand: your lamp will throw the first shadows this basin has had in a very long time. Long, hard, moving shadows across the fluted pillars and the lens-perfect bowls. Not one creature in the world will ever know. Most travellers stop and look at this for a while. Most travellers do not mention it in their reports.

### The rain

Once in twenty days or so, the lid tears.

Watch your barometer. Watch the lid — the scallops sag and darken and the drip thickens into a grey wet mist for some hours beforehand. And watch the animals, who know long before your instruments do: the ranks stop rasping and settle, the columns of grain collapse, the clatter goes still in the underlid.

The rain is the great flattening. The noise floor rises so high that nothing in the basin can see anything at all, and the whole world lies down together on the pan — predator and prey within a body's length, motionless, breathing, waiting — and does not move until it stops.

For you, this is complicated.

**In its favour:** it is the only time you can cross open pan with no animal aware of you at all. Some parties plan their long traverses entirely around it. The hunting instinct is dormant. The ranks do not scatter. You are, for an hour or six hours, genuinely unremarkable.

**Against it:** the noise dose during heavy rain is the worst in the basin, worse than a waking colony. The crust softens. The voids fill and the floor becomes less trustworthy than at any other time. Water pours into the gullets and the throat-holes and the quiet-wells. And you will be walking, upright and moving, through a landscape of a hundred thousand motionless bodies laid down side by side, all of them blind, in a hammering white roar, and it will be necessary to keep walking.

Parties who do it say it is the most extraordinary experience available in any world they have visited. Parties who do it also come back with the worst audiograms. Decide in advance; do not decide in the moment, in the dark, with the chord in your chest.

## VII. Things not to do

- **Do not go quiet.** Ever, on the open pan, for any reason. If your voice fails, you have minutes of grace, not hours. Get a spare running or get to a well.
- **Do not enter a gullet or a throat-hole.**
- **Do not touch a mother.** Not because she will react — she almost certainly will not; she has not moved in decades. But her flanks carry their own rind, their own scrapers, their own small weather, and her surface chemistry is not something your skin should meet. Also: a five-metre soft-bodied animal that eats whatever falls into it does not need to have intentions about you.
- **Do not investigate a hush.** If there is a hole in the noise and it is not you, it is something that has evolved over ten thousand years to be a hole in the noise for one reason. Move away and keep your voice loud and boring.
- **Do not take specimens carelessly.** Everything here is calcium and sulphur chemistry your body has no defences for. Sealed, double-bagged, handled with the assumption that it is toxic and infectious.
- **Do not eat or drink anything from the basin,** including the water, which is fog-milk that has passed through fracture rock and sour gas and several hundred billion animals.
- **Do not let anyone go off-rope "just for a moment."**
- **Do not lie down on the pan to rest.** Lie down and you are lain upon — and after a few hours you are substrate, and after thirty hours you are rind, and after that the files arrive.
- **Do not leave anything.** Anything you leave becomes floor. This world's entire geology is the accumulated record of what fell into it, and it keeps that record perfectly, forever, and it is still writing.

## VIII. Leaving, and afterwards

Walk out through the dead margin at the rim. Take three days again. Your hearing protection comes off in stages, over hours, in the murmuring quiet at the edge, and it will be the loudest quiet of your life.

Decontaminate properly: everything scrubbed, everything sampled, skin and lungs and gut screened, every laceration cultured. Expect a persistent low-grade sinus and lung irritation for weeks. Expect the grain in your gear for years; people find it in seams a decade later.

Get a full audiogram within forty-eight hours and again at six weeks. The six-week one is the real one.

And then the thing nobody warns you about.

**You will bring the chord home.**

Not as a memory. As a sound. The basin's note — that thing at the bottom of your hearing that you first register as nausea, then as a pressure in the sternum, and then one evening simply notice has been there the entire time — does not fully stop when you leave. Most travellers carry it for weeks. Many carry it for months. A minority never entirely lose it, and describe it not as tinnitus, which is a whine, but as a *presence*: a low sustained note under everything, most audible in quiet rooms, most audible at night, that they are never quite sure isn't real.

This is treatable, mostly. Masking, therapy, time. But you should sign up for it knowingly. The basin is one very slow organ playing one very long chord, and it has been playing it for ten thousand years in perfect balance, and a human nervous system exposed to it for two weeks will take a little of the tuning home.

---

## IX. Why anyone goes

Because it is the bottom of everything: a whole world's economy in seams ten centimetres tall, running on sulphur and iron and the underside of fog, with no sun, no plant, no green thing, no tool, no fire, no built object — and it is not primitive and it is not struggling. It is *established*. It reached its answer and stopped. Every pillar, every lens-bowl, every fluted node is a feedback loop that solved itself and has been resting in the solution since before your species had language.

You go to stand inside a finished argument between stone and flesh, in a place where nothing has cast a shadow for a very long time, and to put a light on and cast one.

Then you gather up every last thing you brought, and you leave, and the chord closes over the space where you were as if you had never been there at all.
