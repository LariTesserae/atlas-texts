# Preparing for the Breathing Room

## First, understand that you are visiting three places, not one

The plain, the chimney, and the room are three separate environments, and each will try to kill you in an entirely different register. The plain does it with heat and light and thirst, loudly, the way deserts do — you will see it coming. The chimney does it with seven meters of greasy limestone and a body that has stopped cooling itself. The room does it kindly, with an invitation to sit down.

That third one is the one that gets people. There is no cliff in the room, no bad air you can smell, no animal that means you harm. What there is is a wet-bulb temperature at the edge of the human envelope and an environment so absorbing that you will not notice you are being cooked. Plan the whole trip around that fact and everything else falls into place.

Second thing to understand: **you are, by a very wide margin, the most dangerous thing that has entered this room in ten thousand years.** Not because of what you might break with your boots — though you will break things with your boots — but because of what is living in the cracks of your gear. A room of nine hundred amphibians and geckos in a closed, warm, wet, hundred-percent-humidity chamber with a single entrance is the most perfect disease trap that could be designed. If you carry in a chytrid analogue on a bootlace, you will not know it, and you will not be there when the frogs stop drumming. Weight your preparations accordingly. Biosecurity is not a formality here; it is the entire ethical content of the visit.

## Months out: the body

**Heat-acclimate for real.** Two to three weeks of daily heat exposure — sauna at 45–50°C for 30–40 minutes after light exercise, or equivalent — will expand your plasma volume, drop your sweat sodium, and lower the core temperature at which you start sweating. It buys you perhaps forty extra minutes in the room. Forty minutes is the difference between the visit you planned and being hauled out of a slot by your armpits.

**Understand the arithmetic.** The vents are 38°C at saturation. The far side is 31°C at saturation. Sweat does not evaporate in saturated air; it merely leaves you. This means your body has almost no cooling mechanism in that room other than conduction into the stone and the slow exchange of air up the slot. At 31°C wet-bulb, a fit, acclimated, resting adult can sit for a few hours and be miserable but stable. At 38°C wet-bulb, core temperature rises monotonically no matter what you do. There is no adaptation to this. There is only a clock.

So: **the warm east wall is the one place in the room you must not settle.** The room's own description invites you there, and the invitation is sincere, and it is a slow cooker. Sit on the northwest arc, on bare stone, well off the fissure lattice, and touch the vents only with a hand.

**Swallow a telemetry pill or wear a silent core-temp sensor.** Set a hard turnaround at 38.5°C core and honor it without negotiation, because by 39 you will be negotiating. Hyperthermia in dim green light with a beautiful ceiling ticking overhead does not feel like danger. It feels like grace.

**Get the boring medicine done.** Rabies pre-exposure series (bats above you, and something civet-shaped that uses your exit route). Tetanus. Whatever your crossing authority requires for a nonindustrial biosphere. Treat any athlete's foot, any fungal ear, any lingering skin infection *before* you go — a saturated 33°C environment is a culture incubator, and you will be inoculating your own macerated skin with everything on the wall.

**Fix your teeth and your feet.** Nothing in the room will heal. Everything in the room will bloom.

## Weeks out: the silence

This is the part travelers skip and regret.

**Practice being soundless for four hours.** Sit in a small room. No speech, no humming, no shifting weight, no scratching, no unwrapping. Notice how many involuntary noises a human body makes at rest: the click in a knee, a swallow, a sniff, the tick of a jaw. In the Breathing Room the dome returns every one of these four times from four directions. You will hear your own breathing come back at you and it will sound like something else breathing, and if you have not met that sensation before, you will make a sound.

**Audit every object you intend to bring for noise.** Not "quiet" — *soundless.* Velcro is out. Zippers are out or taped. Shell fabrics that crackle are out; wear thin cotton, silk, brushed wool. Buckles taped, carabiners taped or replaced with soft shackles and cord, spoons wooden, bottles gone entirely in favor of a soft bladder with a silicone tube you can sip through without a gulp or a cap. Remove every device that beeps, clicks, chimes, focuses with a motor, or vibrates against a hard surface. Anything metal you drop will ring in that dome like a struck bell — so bring nothing metal you can drop.

**Plan your sneeze.** You will need to cough or sneeze at some point in a humid room full of fig sap and guano dust. Carry a soft folded cloth, press it hard against your face, and do it in the chimney if you have any warning at all. If you have none, do it into the cloth and then stop moving for two minutes and let the room settle.

**Never, under any circumstance, bring anything capable of playback.** Not for a moment, not on a whim, not at the request of a curious colleague. A room that unlearned voices over a hundred thousand years should not have one reintroduced by a tourist, and there is a worse possibility than nothing happening.

## Weeks out: not being a vector

Do this properly or do not go.

- **Dedicate the kit.** Everything that will enter the room should be new, or should have been used only here. No soil, no seeds, no plant fragments, no water from another world in any seam, sole, cuff, or strap.
- **Sterilize what can be sterilized.** Bleach at 10% then a full rinse, or a virucidal like Virkon S at label concentration for ten minutes, then dry at 60°C for eight hours. Heat-kill boots and pack. Brush every welt and lug first — disinfectant does nothing to a clod.
- **Two pairs of footwear.** A tough pair for the plain, and a soft-soled, sterilized, felt-or-gum pair that goes on at the lip of the slot and never touches the surface. Change on a clean groundsheet, sitting, with your feet over the void.
- **Scent-strip yourself.** No soap, no deodorant, no perfumed detergent, no DEET, no scented sunscreen. Wash the clothes in plain water and sun-dry them. Use a mineral zinc block on the plain and wipe it off before you descend. The room is a place where courtship, predation, and navigation are conducted in pheromone plumes that pool under the ceiling; your scent will rise up the heat column and hang in that layer with the moths for hours after you leave. You cannot avoid being a disturbance, but you can avoid being a shout.
- **Nails short and filed.** Long nails tear fern tissue and catch on greasy rock. Rings off — your fingers will swell.
- **Do not shave the day you go.**

## Timing

**Consult the surface sky before anything else.** Four or five times a year a storm on the plain sends a brown column of water down that slot. It does not need to be raining where you are standing; the caliche pan sheds water like a table. If there is convective cloud anywhere upwind, do not enter the chimney. There is no second exit, no ledge, no refuge. This is the one hazard here that kills instantly and without warning, and it is entirely avoidable by cowardice.

**Season.** Late dry season is safest: pool low, seep quiet, floods improbable, the bone shelf exposed and legible. The fig fruiting is the great spectacle — bats and beetles and ants and the civet-thing all pillaging the crown at the volume of a page turning — but the chimney is then a busy corridor, and you do not want to be wedged in it, sideways, when something with claws decides to come down. If you go for the fruiting, be in the room before dusk and stay out of the chimney at the transition hours.

**Hour.** The wedge of sun crosses the west wall between the fourth and sixth hour and every gecko in the room repositions to intercept it — worth seeing. But the room's real event is the hour before surface dawn, when the plain has cooled and the ceiling gives up its water and the frogs climb the ferns to meet it. That means either a very early descent in the dark, or a night in the room, which for an unacclimated body is more than the clock allows. Choose one.
