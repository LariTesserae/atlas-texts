# Briefing: The Hummock

## The only question that matters first

Everything about this trip depends on one decision, and travellers who get it wrong waste the visit or end it.

**The city is eight millimetres tall.** A grown inhabitant stands about the height of a cranberry leaf. The mound is three paces across and hip-high — those are *your* paces and *your* hip. So there are two entirely different destinations sharing one set of coordinates:

**The Fen** — a cold flat nowhere you can walk across in an afternoon, where you will see grey till, standing water, sedge, and scattered dark cushions of moss, and nothing else, ever, no matter how long you look.

**The Hummock** — forty braided chambers on five levels, a drowned archive below them, a glowing log beneath that, and people.

You cannot have both. Choose before you book the scaling, and be honest with yourself about which one you can survive.

---

## A. If you visit at your own size

Understand that you will not meet anyone. You will stand in the wind above a civilisation and see moss.

**Kit:** treat it as a sub-arctic coastal traverse on a surface with zero fuel, zero shelter, zero wood, and no dry ground. There is *no firewood in this world* — the only wood in existence is the drowned pine under the mound, and it is not yours. Bring your own fuel, your own shelter, your own everything. The till is sharp: freshly ground stone flour and angular clasts, unworn, uncolonised. It will eat boot soles and it will cut you if you fall on it. Salt fog rolls in on still nights; the sea is close and the land is barely above it, so assume everything you own will be damp with saline condensate by morning. Fresh water is the bog water: acid, tea-coloured, drinkable after filtration, tasting of iron and sweetness.

**Conduct — and this is the whole point of the section:**

- **Do not step on a mound.** A boot lands on forty chambers, five levels, a granary, a nursery and six generations of laid-in dead. It is the most destruction an ordinary human can casually do anywhere in the multiverse. Walk the gravel, walk the black water, walk the sedge. Look where your feet go, every single step. The mounds are at roughly hundred-pace intervals and they are low, dark and easy to miss in fog.
- **Kneel to approach; don't loom.** Your footfalls arrive in the mound as a rolling ground-shock. The inhabitants have exactly one word for a very large noise coming from a direction with no door in it, and it means *shrew*. You will be heard long before you are seen, and what you will be heard as is an apex predator.
- **Don't breathe on it.** Your exhalation is a hot wet gale with a CO₂ spike, delivered at thirty-plus degrees above ambient onto a crown that lives at frost-point. Approach from downwind, hold your breath for close work, and never, ever sneeze.
- **Bring a 20× loupe and the patience to use it.** Lie on the till at dusk, brace your elbows, and watch one flank of a mound for an hour. What you are hoping for is a moving speck, or a dark regularity in the crown that is a doorway. Most visitors see nothing. Some see a comb-herder working springtails across the outer moss like a shepherd on a hillside, and never get over it.
- **Take nothing.** A pinch of moss is a district.

## B. If you go small

This is the real journey, and it is harder than it sounds, because you are about to be a warm wet fragile object in a place built by things that are not.

### 1. Heat. This is the trip's central engineering problem.

At eight millimetres your surface-to-volume ratio is roughly a hundred times worse than it is now. In a saturated, near-freezing, permanently wet environment, unmodified human physiology at that size loses core heat in *minutes*. The locals are not tiny humans; whatever they are, they solved this. You have not.

Insist that your scaling provider does one of two things: preserve your thermal mass by cheating (a bubble, a field, a non-scaling core), or give you a chemical heat source you can carry and refuel. Do not accept "you'll be fine, you'll be moving." You will not be moving in the drowning season; you will be sitting in a bail-line in cold water for nine hours.

Bring the means to make yourself warm privately, and then — **hide it**. Fire in this city is a civic event held twice a winter, guarded, brief, and remembered for months afterward as a miracle: the astonishing news that a thing can be warm and not wet at once. A stranger casually producing dry heat in a side gallery is not a guest. He is an event, and possibly a theology.

### 2. Water at your new scale

Surface tension is no longer a curiosity; it is structural. A film of water across a knot-hold is a membrane you must break deliberately. A droplet is a trap: it will wet you, cling, and roll you, and eight-millimetre you cannot reliably escape one. Rain is artillery. Never be on the crown in rain or thaw drizzle.

The first law here, which their children learn with their palms: *everything is holding water, and everything will let go.* The walls weep when pressed. Press harder and your hand goes through into cold. You will do this by accident within the first hour. Do it in a storage volume, not in someone's ceiling.

**You will never be dry.** Not once, except at the fire. Plan your entire medical regime around that sentence.

### 3. Skin, feet, acid

The bog water is acidic enough that meat does not rot in it. Good news: your cuts will not infect — sphagnum is antiseptic, it was packed into wounds in our own world's trenches. Bad news: your cuts will not *close*, and neither will your skin. Immersion foot is the single likeliest thing to end your visit. Bring barrier grease, bring more than you think, re-apply on a schedule, and treat the twice-a-winter fire as a medical appointment: that is your one chance to get your feet dry, and you must not monopolise it, and you must ask.

### 4. Lungs

Read this paragraph twice. A handful of the wall contains more animals than the city contains citizens: mites in the tens of thousands, springtails in drifts, nematodes in every water film, tardigrades folded in the leaf-hollows. You are going to sleep inside the densest mite habitat available to a human being anywhere. Mite protein is among the most potent respiratory allergens our species knows.

**If you are atopic or asthmatic, do not go small.** If you are clean, bring inhalers anyway, bring antihistamines, and expect a rough three days while your immune system decides what it thinks.

Also test the air in the lower galleries and the storage volumes before you sleep in them. Peat makes methane and carbon dioxide, and it makes them from beneath, and the braid-work is designed to hold water, not to ventilate.

### 5. Dark discipline

There is essentially no light. The city's illumination is fingernail chips of pine mined from a drowned log and colonised by a fungus that glows the colour of skimmed milk held against a lamp. A chip lasts a season. Four generations of braiding went into reaching that log.

- **Do not bring a torch you might be tempted to use.** One white LED in a mid-level gallery is not a convenience, it is a bomb dropped on an entire visual culture, and on the eyes of everyone in the room.
- If you must have light for a technical task, bring the dimmest red source that will do it, clear it in advance, and say what it is.
- **Train for the dark before you come.** Spend a week at home navigating your house blindfolded. You need to arrive able to move confidently in total blackness on wet knotted footholds, because you will spend ninety per cent of your time doing exactly that, and a panicking guest in a shaft is a hazard to everyone below.

Faces here are known by voice and by touch. Their word for *stranger* translates as **unhandled**. You will be read with fingers: face, hands, throat, the set of your shoulders. Consent to this warmly and early. Shave — everything. Your body hair at that scale is rope, and worse, it is habitat; and it makes you unreadable. Cut your nails. Take off every ring.

### 6. The chewing

Under everything there is a continuous grinding at the edge of hearing: the substrate being eaten by its tenants. It never stops. You will find it unbearable for two days and then you will stop hearing it, and that is the correct outcome, because **its absence is the alarm.** A silent wall is a dead wall, and dead walls slump. If the chewing stops near you, report it. Do not wait to be sure.

The other change in the sound is the **long noise**: a hollow rushing gnawing from a direction with no door. That is a shrew, longer than the widest gallery, unfightable, frantic, and warm-blooded with the metabolism of a furnace. There is no protocol but flight, then repair. Learn the evacuation routes of your level on your first day, by touch, in the dark, and rehearse them.

### 7. Objects: the rule of permanence

The acid keeps everything. Whatever you drop is not lost, it is *filed*. In four generations a diver will bring it up out of black water with a great-great-grandmother's flint comb, and it will become a relic, an omen, or a god.

Therefore: **tether every tool. Count every item in and count every item out.** No buttons, no beads, no wrappers, no fibres that aren't theirs. Nothing metal enters the mound — they have no metal, and a single lost steel needle in the wrong floor is a technological detonation with a fuse a century long.

**Gifts:** bring nothing that improves anything. No finer sieve mesh (they have sieves of every mesh, and mesh is their frontier technology; a better one from outside is not a present, it is a takeover). Bring perishables, or bring labour. The best gift you have is your back and your hands in a bail-line, and a song from somewhere else that they can keep or drop as they please.

### 8. The archive, and the drowning

The city stands perfectly still, climbing, forever: the crown grows a finger's width a year, the peat compacts, the water table stays where it is, so the lowest gallery drowns at exactly the rate the top gallery is braided. The past is directly beneath the present, and it is not gone. Sixty hands down the first chambers still stand, sieves still on their pegs, and the dead are in the floors with their hair and their fingernails and the expressions they went down with.

- **Do not ask to dive.** If you are invited, understand what you are being offered: not an adventure, a *clerical duty*. Cold shock at that temperature stops the chest. You will be holding a glowing chip in your mouth on a knotted line in water that is black, anoxic, and full of faces. Refusing is allowed. Nobody will think less of you.
- **Avoid the warm wet spring** unless you specifically want to witness the drowned city pressing upward — old floors giving, a sealed room of forty years opening into a working corridor, cold water smelling of iron and sweetness, and in the water something with a face. They have a phrase for it that means approximately *the low rooms have asked a question*. The answer is to bail, to braid, and to lay the risen one in the floor again, higher, where they will keep. If you are present, bail. Do not photograph. Do not recoil visibly from a face; everyone in that corridor is related to it.

### 9. You cannot leave

At your reduced size, the hundred paces between mounds is a hundred kilometres of open black water and rattling sedge under a sky that gives only a green aurora smear. There is no navigation, no shelter, no crossing. **The mound is the world.**

Consequently: fix your extraction anchor *inside or on* the mound, with redundancy, and rehearse the recall until it is reflex. Build your plan around the assumption that if extraction fails you stay put, in one place, for however long it takes — and negotiate that possibility with your hosts before you arrive, not after.

### 10. Contamination, both ways

Quarantine yourself before you scale down. You are a walking biome; one spore, one foreign mite egg, one nematode in a boot seam, into a closed island ecosystem that has existed for a handful of seasons on land that was ice ten winters ago. Come sterile. Go home through decontamination, because you will be carrying tardigrades, and they will survive anything you do to them.

---

## Two things to leave unsaid

**The chronology.** The land has been ice-free for ten winters. The city's archive goes down sixty hands, one floor per generation, with a great-great-grandmother thirty years under. That does not add up, and they will not be interested in your arithmetic. Note it, file it, and let it be the mystery you bring home.

**The future.** Do not explain succession to them. Do not explain that the fen will fill, or that the sea is close and rising, or what a raised bog eventually does to the mounds within it. They have two laws, and both are true, and both are enough.

---

## Final item: consent

If you die there, you will be laid in the floor, and the acid will keep you — your face, your hands, your expression — indefinitely, rising one floor per generation beneath a city that never moves. Some travellers find that the most frightening sentence in the briefing. Some find it the most beautiful.

Decide which, in writing, before you go. They will honour either, but they will ask, and in the dark, by hand, they will know if you are lying.
