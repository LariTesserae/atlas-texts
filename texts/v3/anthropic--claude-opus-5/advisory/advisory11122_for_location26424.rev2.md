# Preparing to Visit the Throat

## 1. Understand what you are visiting

Not a well. A machine, and an instrument, and a lung.

A machine, because every dimension in it was set by the weight of a full pot on a woman's head and the number of steps she can take before her neck fails. An instrument, because a hole in the ground with nine tiers of hard flat facing is a resonator, and everything alive inside it has adapted to being played. A lung, because it breathes warm from the bottom at dusk and takes in cold at dawn, and you will feel it do this on your skin.

If you go expecting "a historic water structure," the first evening will knock you down. Go expecting to live inside a throat that is always making a sound, and you will do better.

## 2. Timing

Arrive in the dry season, two-thirds of the way through it. The upper tiers will be hot and gritty, the wet line will be low and stable, and you will have weeks to learn the stairs while they are merely dangerous rather than lethal.

Plan to stay through the first rain and leave four or five days after it. The rain is the reason to come and the most likely thing to kill you.

Do not arrive on the day of a rain. You will be on the stairs, wet, new, and shoeless, in the dark, in a noise that removes the possibility of being warned.

## 3. Six months out: the body

**Feet.** This is the big one and the one travelers skip. Below the fourth tier, shoes are forbidden, and the prohibition is not ceremonial — it is a mortality statistic. You need six months of genuine barefoot walking before you go: gravel, hot stone, wet tile. You are building not just callus but *proprioception* — toes that splay and grip on their own, an arch that reads a surface through a film of water. Keep toenails short. Accept that you will pick up something fungal between the toes within a fortnight of arriving; the local cure is to go up and sit in the sun with your feet apart, and it works, slowly.

Do not harden your soles so much that you go numb. You want tough and *reading*.

**Hearing.** Ten thousand swifts in a reverberant stone shaft, twice daily, for an hour each. This is not atmosphere, it is an industrial noise exposure. A season unprotected will cost you high frequencies permanently. Learn, before you go, to shape earplugs from beeswax and a pinch of wool. There are bees in a crack on the sixth tier; wax is a local, legible, unremarkable material, and nobody will look twice at you plugging your ears with it. Do not bring foam plugs. Do not bring anything that looks manufactured.

**Voice.** Ordinary speech in the Throat is a bellow. You will be shouting from waking to sleeping, and if you shout from your throat you will be silent and humiliated by day four. Spend the months beforehand learning to project from the diaphragm on a flat, forward, carrying pitch — the way street vendors and sergeants do it. Also learn to face people when you speak: many residents past forty are half deaf and read mouths.

Learn also that **lowering your voice is socially loaded**. Quiet speech cannot travel and therefore means secrecy. Muttering marks you as somebody with something to hide. There is essentially no privacy of speech in the Throat, and people who try to make some are watched.

**Neck and shoulders.** You will be offered a pot. Everyone is. Carry a weighted vessel on your head for a few months beforehand — not to become competent, you won't, but so that your first attempt is merely funny instead of an injury. Competence takes a childhood. The attempt is what earns you goodwill; the spectacular failure is a gift to the community and you should give it graciously.

**Heat tolerance.** Sauna work, repeatedly, and specifically *wet* heat. Down there the air is saturated and blood-warm-plus. Your sweat will not evaporate. Your body's entire cooling strategy fails, and it fails without the usual warning signs — you will not feel parched, you will feel stupid, clumsy, and mildly euphoric, which are the same symptoms as being a tourist.

**Immunisation and gut.** Get everything your world offers. Then accept that you will be ill in the first ten days regardless. Budget for it. Do not be ill on the lower tiers.

## 4. What to bring, and what must never go down the stairs

**Bring:** undyed loose cloth you don't mind soaking; a broad hat for the plain above; a small fired-clay pot of your own (plain, not fine); a length of good grass rope; salt; dried figs; lamp oil; a bone or wood comb; beads.

**Do not bring, at all:** steel. A steel knife in a place where bronze is hoarded for mason's chisels is not a tool, it is a bomb. It will be seen, it will be wanted, it will restructure obligations around you within a week and you will not control the outcome. The same applies to glass, to anything with a lens, to any light source, to plastic, to a wheel or pulley of any kind. The Throat *is* the absence of the windlass. Introducing one is not a gift, it is an argument that their entire ancestral achievement was a mistake.

**Tether everything.** Anything dropped falls nine tiers into drinking water and cannot simply be fished out; retrieval is a small ceremony involving other people's day. Carry nothing down you cannot afford to lose, and nothing loose at the neck or wrist.

**Leave sandals at the lip,** even though shoes are technically permitted on the upper four tiers. It is the simpler habit and it reads well.

## 5. Arriving

You will be visible for hours before you arrive — a figure on a flat dust plain walking toward a rectangle. Do not try to approach unobserved; walk in plainly, slowly, in daylight, and stop at the lip without descending. Somebody will come up. The right posture is *asking*.

Have a story that is boring. Pilgrim is good. Better: a labourer who mixes plaster and carries stone. Maintenance is the community's chief occupation and chief pride, and the fastest route into the place is to be one more pair of hands keeping the decay at bay. Offer labour before you offer gifts.

Ask three questions on the first day, in this order: where the latrine is, where you may sleep, and which is the west flight. Get all three wrong and you will be a problem for weeks.

## 6. The first descent

Expect a physiological ambush somewhere around the second tier. The sound arrives from every direction at once, there is nothing natural to touch, the walls are all *made*, and your body reads the combination as an emergency. Travelers report a wave of near-panic, a lurch of the inner ear, a conviction of being swallowed. Sit down in a bay, put your back on a pillar, breathe, and let it pass. It passes in about ten minutes and rarely returns.

Mechanics of descending:
- **Hand trailing on the wall, always.** For your first fortnight, carry nothing that occupies a hand.
- **Weight on the ball of the foot, never the heel.** The treads are hollowed into spoons and the centres of the hollows on the busy flight are polished glassy. Wet, that glaze has no friction at all.
- **Do not look up** while moving. The rectangle of sky over nine tiers does something unkind to the balance of people who did not grow up under it.
- **Descend west, climb east.** You will get this wrong from pure instinct at least twice. It is the rule most immediately and loudly enforced.
- **A laden climber outranks everyone.** Step into a bay and flatten. Not to the outer edge — into the colonnade.
- **The eleventh step from the bottom is a finger too high.** You know this now and you will still stumble on it, because knowing is not the same as having it in your feet. Stumble, laugh, and let the children enjoy it. It is a small rite of entry and trying to be smooth about it looks worse than tripping.

Rule for your first week: **nothing below the sixth tier after midday.** The trap of this place is that descending is easy and the escape from the heat is upward, so you are at your least capable exactly when you most need capacity.

## 7. Water discipline

Drink at the bottom, before the climb, not after. Everyone does. Carry a small pot for the purpose.

Never wash in the basin, never enter it, never let anything of your body reach it. Ask explicitly where bathing happens — it will be on the wet stone, with a pot, at a specified place — and ask explicitly whether there are days or conditions (wounds, illness, menstruation) under which you should not go below the wet line. There will be. The rules will be stated flatly and without embarrassment.

Never relieve yourself below the lip. Never improvise a place. There is one place, it is above and downwind, and everybody knows exactly who has used it and when.

## 8. Things that will make you ill

- **The bat gallery, lowest west.** Several thousand bats and generations of guano. Ammonia, mites, and fungal spores that will give you a serious lung illness your world's doctors will not immediately guess at. The ninth tier is already forbidden for sleeping; extend that in your own practice to not entering the west gallery at all. Nobody will find this strange.
- **Bird mites** from the swift and dove niches. They will find your sleeping shelf. Shake your bedding daily in the sun on an upper landing, as everyone does.
- **Slaked lime**, if you do plaster work. It burns wet skin slowly and you will not notice until the evening. Keep a pot of water and rinse constantly; watch what the masons do with their hands and copy it exactly.
- **The water itself.** Ten thousand birds live above a spring. Refusing offered water is a serious insult; accept, sip, and rely on your immune preparation and your tolerance for ten bad days.

## 9. Sleep

There is no quiet, ever. The frogs work all night — a low woody *bomp* you feel in the sternum rather than hear — and the drips number about two hundred and each has a pitch. You will sleep badly for four nights and then you will sleep like a stone, and when you get home silence will keep waking you up.

Do not angle for a shelf on the ninth tier, however warm it is, however generously the shelves are cut. The stated reasons are the bats and the dreams. Both reasons are good.

## 10. The swift hours

Twice a day, at dawn and dusk, for an hour each, the swifts pour out and in and conversation ends. This is not an interruption of life here; it is an institution. No work is done. You sit. You cannot hear and you are not expected to try.

Treat these as the most important social appointments of your visit. Sit facing the way the others face. Do not fidget, do not write, do not tinker, do not use the time productively — being seen to work through the rest marks you as somebody who does not understand what the Throat is for.

You will also find that during the swift hour thought itself becomes difficult, that the shriek fills the space where your interior monologue lives. Some visitors find this the worst part of the trip and some find it the reason they came. You will not know which you are until it happens.

## 11. The two strange things, and the third

**The water moves in whole steps.** It goes up or down a full tread, overnight, always overnight, and no one has ever seen it in between. Do not stage a vigil. Travelers who have tried report, uniformly and slightly defensively, that they did not sleep and yet. Whatever the mechanism is, it appears to include your attention as one of its inputs, and the cost of testing it is a piece of your night you will not get back.

More practically: **do not schedule your extraction for the small hours.** If there is any place in the multiverse where you will lose an unaccounted forty minutes, it is here, at night, near the waterline.

Instead, spend an afternoon with one of the old women and the notched risers. The shoulder of each step carries tallies of how many seasons it has been the waterline; the whole basin is an archive of rainfall going back further than anyone's memory, kept in scratches. Ask to have it read to you. It will be the best afternoon you have.

**The shuffled echo**, fifth landing, third bay, back to the middle pillar. Shout four words above a certain volume and they come back in a different order — and, critically, the *same* different order every time for the same sentence. The arrangement belongs to the sentence, not to the sounds. Children keep a list of the best ones.

Bring a four-word sentence of your own. Contributing a famous one is the single fastest route to being liked by everyone under twelve, and the children are the ones who will actually explain the place to you.

But: the rearrangement attaching to *meaning* rather than acoustics should be taken as a warning label. Do not shout anything you would be unhappy to hear reassembled. Do not shout a lie. Do not shout the names of people you love. Above all, do not shout your own true name from your own world — a shuffled name is still a name, and here names stick.

**The sweat before rain.** The dry upper stone darkens faintly at the joints and goes cool to the palm a day or two ahead of weather. Everyone reads it, but they read it *comparatively* — the same stone, the same hour, day after day. So on your first morning, choose your patch of wall on the second or third tier, somewhere you pass anyway, and touch it at the same time every day. Within two weeks you will have the baseline, and you will know a day before the sky does.

This is your best safety equipment in the place. Use it.

## 12. The rain

When the sky over the rectangle goes bruise-coloured and the swifts come in early and low, you have minutes.

Get above the fourth tier. Get *behind* the colonnade, into a chamber with a corbelled roof. Not onto a landing — the rain falls as a single solid column exactly thirty paces by twelve, unbroken from the sky to the bottom, and it arrives with weight, and with whatever the wind has picked off the plain.

Then stay put and experience it. There is no name for the sound and there is no describing it afterward; eighty people, ten thousand birds, three thousand bats and an unknown number of frogs all shouting at once, inside a stone flute, under a waterfall. It is worth the entire journey.

Afterward is when the stairs are most dangerous. Two steps that were dry are now drowned, the moss line has moved up overnight into places you learned as safe, and everybody is elated and careless. Give it two days before you go below the sixth tier again.

## 13. Leaving

Climb out at dawn, before the swifts, or an hour after. Say your goodbyes on the way up and get them done early — the flights are narrow and you cannot turn around, and a laden climber behind you does not care about your farewell.

Leave something in the lap of the god that is worn into a smooth basin. A fig is correct. A coin is correct. Nothing clever.

Touch the faceless ones as you pass, with the flat of your hand, the way everyone does. They are faceless because of that hand, thousands of times over, for centuries, and adding your one rub to the erosion is the most honest participation available to you.

## 14. Afterward

Expect tinnitus for a week or two. Expect to shout at people who are standing right next to you. Expect silence — ordinary domestic silence — to feel like a held breath, a room where something is about to happen, and to sleep badly in it for a month.

Expect to touch walls. You will find yourself laying a palm on dry stone in other worlds, at the same hour each day, reading for weather that isn't coming.

And you will count steps. Every staircase you meet for the rest of your life, you will count, and somewhere around the eleventh from the bottom your foot will lift a little too high, all by itself, for a reason your body remembers and you have stopped being able to explain.
