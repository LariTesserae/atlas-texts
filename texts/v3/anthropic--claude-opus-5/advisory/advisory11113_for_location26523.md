# Preparing for the Shelf

## First, understand what kind of danger this is

The Shelf will probably not kill you. It has no predators large enough to want you, no weather worth the name, no cold, no thirst, no distance. Almost everyone who dies there dies of one of four things: walking off the rim in cloud, sitting down, drinking the water, or staying long enough to start listening properly.

What the Shelf is *good* at is being permanently marked by you. That is the axis your whole preparation should turn on. In most places you can be careless and time will clean up after you. Here there is no time in that sense — no rot, no wind-scour, no rain that erases, nothing that abrades. The Shelf is a place where consequence has no half-life. Every hair you shed, every fleck of paint, every drop of blood off a cut palm is not litter. It is a fossil, already, and it will still be legible in the flank of a mountain in eleven thousand years.

Prepare as if you were going to work inside a sealed reliquary that is also alive and also slightly hungry.

## Your body, in the six weeks before

**Altitude.** The Shelf is the highest thing for a long way and gives you no cues about it — no thin blue sky, no cold, no view. You will be warm and wet and short of breath and you will misread all three. Acclimatise properly beforehand. Spend a week high before you go up. Bring the drugs you'd bring for a mountain even though nothing about the place will feel like a mountain.

**Heat.** The stone is warm from underneath, the air is saturated, and you will not feel yourself sweating because sweat does not evaporate in cloud. This is the classic Shelf mistake, and it comes on as clumsiness first. Clumsiness on the Shelf means falling, and falling on the Shelf means being opened up by rims that have never been blunted by anything. Train in humid heat. Learn what your own early heat-stupidity feels like, in a gym, where the floor is soft.

**Hands and knees.** You will use them, and you should not. Do hand conditioning if you like, but the real work is behavioural: practise moving through a rocky environment without ever putting a bare palm down for balance. Practise it until it's boring. The stone here is not rock as your hands understand rock. Every lobe, fan, and drooping stack ends in a lip thin enough to shave with, because nothing has yet come along to take the lip off. The first cut is nearly free. It's the sixth, when your hands are stiff and slippery and you're tired, that ruins the trip.

**Teeth.** Have any loose metalwork in your mouth seen to. The mast on the high lobe hums at a frequency you feel in your molars, and people with old amalgam or a failing crown find that they cannot spend more than a few minutes in the upper basin without a headache that lasts two days.

**Ears.** Get a hearing test and keep the numbers. You will want the baseline afterward.

## Decontamination: outbound matters more than inbound

Every competent Shelf traveller quarantines twice, and takes the *return* leg more seriously.

Going in, you are the entire threat. You carry, in your gut and on your skin, the most sophisticated decomposition machinery in the multiverse. Nothing rots on the Shelf because there is nothing there to rot it. You are the missing thing. A single viable colony established in a warm wet seam is not a mess; it is the invention of decay on a world that hasn't got round to it. Erosion is waiting somewhere downhill, patient, holding its instruments — do not hand it a shortcut.

So: gut-clearing protocol before ascent, sealed-suit handling of all food, hard-shell latrine cartridges, and absolute discipline about what touches stone. Some services will not carry you up unless you've done a full course. Do it even if yours will.

Coming out, the direction of the threat reverses, and gets worse. The seeds take water, dissolved cloud, and the rubble of an older duller mountain, and they compose. They do not care about you. They also do not care what your home city is made of. A viable seed in a boot tread, in a trouser cuff, in the seam of a pack, is not a souvenir. Burn the boots. Genuinely: budget for footwear you will incinerate on arrival home, and do your final scrub in the transit lock with the machine that costs too much, twice, including your hair and under your nails, and then again.

Nobody has yet seeded a home world by accident. This is thought to be luck.

## Kit

**Footwear.** Rigid soles, hard rubber, replaceable. Bring two spare sets of soles and the glue. The rims do not wear your boots down; they *slice* them, in fine parallel cuts, and a sole can go from new to lace-deep in nine days of real walking. There is no grit anywhere, so nothing scuffs; everything is cut clean, including you.

**Gloves.** Cut-resistant liners under a smooth non-shedding outer. Never leather, never wool, never fleece, never anything with a nap — fibre-shedding fabrics are banned by every field code that exists, because a shed fibre here is permanent, and a hundred thousand permanent shed fibres in a translucent buttress look, from a distance, like mould. Someone did that in the seventies. You can still see it.

**Clothing.** Close-fitting, smooth-woven, sealed seams, nothing flapping, nothing hanging. Loose clothing on the Shelf snags on rims and the rims win. Hoods with a stiffened brim — a hat with structure to it — for reasons under *Fliers*.

**Kneepads and elbow pads,** hard-shelled. Not for impact. For the fact that you will eventually need to be low, and being low on new stone is like kneeling on a tray of scalpels.

**Water.** Do not drink the pools. They are the clearest water you will ever see, four metres deep and readable to the bottom, and they are working fluid: near-saturated with the material the crystal fronts are drawing up and locking down. Your gut is warm and full of nucleation sites. What happens is not fast and not dramatic and is very hard to treat. Drink rain, caught in the air on a clean sheet, twice a day when the cloud sags. Bring a distiller as backup and bring the fuel for it. Assume you will not be able to filter your way out of the problem; filtration does nothing about something that is dissolved and wants to stop being dissolved.

**Food.** Sealed, hard-shelled, crumbless. No shells, rinds, cores, bones, or nut hulls unless you enjoy the thought of a fig seed glinting inside a wall for ten millennia. Everything comes out in the same container it went in.

**Hearing protection — filtered, never occlusive.** This is the single most important gear decision you will make. The ambient sound is a dry brilliant sizzle, millions of lattices locking, plus the bells, the clonks, the tuned pinging, the frogs, the mason-ticks shrilling at dusk. It is loud enough to damage you over weeks. But you must not seal your ears, because the alarm on the Shelf is not a noise. It is the *absence* of the tick-song when the ticks go quiet all at once. Use attenuators that preserve the shape of the soundscape and just turn it down. Test them in advance in a room with a sizzling loop playing, and practise noticing when someone kills the loop.

**Line.** Fine, strong, high-visibility monofilament and a lot of it, plus stakes for the *old* stone only. Your route back to your entry point will not survive on landmarks. Landmarks here move a palm's width a day and a buttress a season. Lay line. Retrieve it on the way out — every centimetre, or it becomes an inclusion, and a lost line lying across a growth front is a wire embedded in a wall forever, which is exactly the kind of ugly nobody forgives.

**Light.** A great deal of night, and the days are grey chambers anyway. Two headlamps, one handlamp, spares for everything, and a red mode. White light in a grazing patch after dark stops the mason-ticks singing, which not only ruins your alarm system but makes the waders stop hunting, which changes what a whole basin does for the night. Small courtesies.

**A probe.** A light two-metre wand. You use it in cloud, ahead of your feet, near the rim. The rim is where the Shelf ends and the drop begins and the horizon is nine paces of vapour. There is no visual cue whatsoever. The waders know where the edge is; they come at dusk on legs like wire and stalk the shallows there, silhouetted against nothing. If you can see waders, you are already close enough.

**Notebook and pencil.** Graphite and rag paper. Ink runs in saturated air and, worse, ink fades, and nothing here fades. Write in something that will still be readable to the hand that comes after yours.

## Three specific hazards, and how to hold them in your mind

**Fronts.** Do not sit on a front. Do not lean on one, lay a pack against one, or leave a coil of rope touching one overnight. Growth is roughly the rate of a growing fingernail, which sounds harmless until you understand that it does not stop for objects and does not distinguish between wall and wrist. It will grow around you politely. There are two glass measuring pins four metres inside a translucent buttress, still glinting, still faithfully marking a surface that has moved on. They were driven into stone by careful people who thought they understood the timescale.

The corollary is useful: *the blue haze tells you where the stone is going.* Learn to see it — a blue no stronger than the blue in a shadow on snow, standing in the air with geometry in it, arches and ribs and stairs not yet arrived. Camp where the haze is not. Sleep where the haze is not. Put nothing down inside a plan.

**Froth.** The vents breathe out a pale froth like beaten egg-white that stiffens in minutes and holds your weight in an hour. Keep it out of open wounds and out of your boots and off your gear. It sets in a cut as readily as anywhere else, and it sets *into* you, and getting it out is a surgical matter performed by someone at least a week's travel away. This is a large part of why the cut-avoidance discipline matters more than it sounds like it should.

**Fliers.** A sheet-flier is a membrane the size of a cloak, no thicker than a wet leaf, and it comes down over a grazing patch like a closing hand, and it makes no sound at all. They are not interested in you. They are interested in what is small and flat and on the stone below them, and a person lying down to rest in a mason-tick patch is, from above, an ambiguous shape. The stiffened brim on your hood is because a flier settling on your face is a suffocation risk and a panic risk and a panic risk on razor stone is a bleeding risk. When the ticks go silent, stand up, and stand tall.

## Preparing your mind, which is the actual work

**The nine-pace horizon.** You will be inside a warm grey room three hundred paces across with walls made of vapour that occasionally thin to show you other shelves, staggered and pale, and then close again. There is no distance to rest your eyes on. Some people find this the most restful architecture they have ever occupied; some find that by day eleven they cannot stop turning around. Know which you are, if you can, before you're up there. Bring something small and dull to look at closely — a book with dense pages, a piece of work — as ballast.

**The ticking, and what it starts doing.** After a week or two, the polyrhythm of the bells and horns and paps and tuned tubes begins to organise itself in your hearing. It plays itself all night, and there is a great deal of night. Travellers hear names in it. They hear their own doorbells, their mothers, phrases repeated. This is ordinary pareidolia in an extraordinarily rich acoustic field, and it is harmless right up until you start answering. Plan a routine with fixed hours. Write in the notebook daily even when there's nothing. If you are going for longer than a month, go with somebody, and agree in advance that either of you can call the trip over without argument.

**The lens.** There is a walnut-sized object in a niche near the vents, wrapped in cloth, warm, and about nine times heavier than it should be. Hold it up against the blue haze and the plan resolves: arches, stairs, chambers, a second doorway that isn't there yet, and much further out and much fainter, the shape of a mountain that will take eleven thousand years and that is, by any measure you would care to apply, beautiful.

Prepare for this deliberately, the way you'd prepare for a drug. Not in your first week. Not alone if you can help it. Not for long — count breaths, five or six, then put it down. The problem is not what you see; the problem is the arithmetic that runs afterward, in which you are a fibre in a wall and your entire species is a haze that didn't set. Some people come down from that fine and even improved. Some come down needing years. Decide beforehand how much of the future you actually want in your hands, and then keep to it, because up there you will want more.

**The doorway.** In the middle of the basin, freestanding, arched, made of new stone, with a threshold worn smooth. It is the only worn thing on the Shelf, and it was not worn by weather. It was *in the plan*: something intended a threshold and intended it already used.

You are a multiverse traveller. You know what a door with a worn sill in a place with no wear is likely to be, and you know that you will be standing in front of it at some point with your hand on the stone. So make the decision at home, at low altitude, in a dry room, with people who love you present, and write it in your own hand in your own notebook, dated, and read it before you go out to the basin. Whichever way you decide is defensible. Deciding in the moment, at dusk, when the frogs have started and the bells have started, is not.

## What to leave

You will leave something. It is unavoidable; you will bleed at least once, and blood in a growth front is an inclusion like any other. Older travellers say you should go and look at your own smear when it has been swallowed, and give it a name, and stop thinking about it.

Beyond that, the code is simple and old: *whatever you leave, you leave forever, at the exact spot, becoming an inclusion — so leave carefully, and leave something worth looking at.*

Choose it at home. Take time over it. Small, inert, non-shedding, not organic, and beautiful in the specific way of surviving a long look: worked glass, a coin with a good profile, a bead of the right colour, a thing with a legible date on it. Not a name badge. Not rubbish with sentiment attached. Consider that it will be seen in cross-section, in an unfamiliar light, by something that may not be human, deep inside a mountain that is currently a suggestion in the air. Place it consciously, where the haze says a chamber will be, and write down where.

And add an entry to the notebooks in the fieldhouse. There are ninety years of dates in four hands and the last entry stops mid-sentence, and you should not finish that sentence — that is the one strong taboo among people who go there — but you should write under it. Date, weather, what the ticks did, which pins are still visible. It is the only continuity the place has that runs at human speed.

## Coming home

Expect the reversal to be harder than the arrival. You will come back to a world of dust, and grit in the low places, and soil, and rounded stones, and gullies, and everything gently coming apart, and for a few weeks that will read to you as something between untidiness and grief. Travellers describe an urge to clean that they know is stupid, and an aversion to sand, and a period of finding their own house unbearable because it is visibly being worn away by its inhabitants.

It passes. What tends to stay is the thing the last hand in the notebook was trying to write down at dusk with the frogs going and the bells ringing sympathetically — that you had been thinking of it as an unfinished place, which is wrong.

Pack for cuts, heat, and silence. Bring your own water. Take everything out. Leave one good thing.
