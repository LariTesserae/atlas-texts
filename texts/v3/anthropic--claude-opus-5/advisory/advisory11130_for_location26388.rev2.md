# Preparing for the Lower Cast

## First, the thing that will actually hurt you

It is not the depth, the dark, the ammonia, or the eighty-metre fall of a dropped spanner. It is the water in your clothes.

The Lower Cast runs two weathers a day, and they are perfectly designed to destroy an outsider's kit. Six hours of dry, hard, three-metre wind at four or five degrees, which pulls every gram of moisture out of you and out of everything you are wearing and makes you feel fine. Then six hours at saturation, in which everything you own — including the layers that felt dry — takes on water out of the air, and the ceiling drips on you from eighty metres, and you do not notice the transition because you have been working.

You cannot dry anything by hanging it up. The air will not take it. Nothing airs. Anything you wash is wet until somebody bakes it.

So the rule that governs all your packing is: **wool, never cotton; two of everything next to the skin; and never, ever sleep in what you wore.** Wool keeps some function wet. Cotton becomes a cold wet bandage with a person in it, and that is how visitors end up with the works' pneumonia, which they are very good at treating and very tired of treating.

Bring a vapour-barrier liner for your sleeping arrangement, whatever it is — a bag liner of coated fabric, even a well-taped sheet of plastic. It feels clammy and horrible and it will keep your insulation from soaking through in four nights.

And learn the local trick immediately, because it is the only drying technology in the cavern: **the cure.** A wall between about eight days and four weeks old gives off a steady, low, patient heat. Crews dry socks on them. Find the date stamp, count the days, put the back of your hand against the wall, and if the frost line stands off the base in that clean bright half-metre, you have found the warmest place in four kilometres. Do not use anything younger than eight days. Do not lay anything against anything younger than about ten days that you intend to put back on your body — young concrete is caustic, and cement burns don't hurt until they are deep.

## Your body, before you go

**Give yourself a fortnight of cold.** Not extreme cold; wet cold. Walk in the rain at four degrees with wet hands until you stop flinching about it. The Lower Cast's cold is unremarkable in figures and merciless in duration: there is no coming in from it. There is no room in the works that is warm. There is only the cure, and the mess huts, and the grow-lamps over two bad lemon trees.

**Get your hands used to being useless.** Everything there is done with numb fingers. Practise the things you will need to do — lacing, buckling, striking a light, writing — with hands you have deliberately chilled. Visitors with soft hands end up doing everything one and a half times.

**Train stairs, not distance.** The terraces are a staircase four kilometres long. You will climb more in a day there than in a month anywhere else, at an altitude that does you no favours and in air that is either scouring you or drowning you.

**Bank sleep, and accept that you are about to lose the twenty-four-hour day.** The place runs on a twelve-hour cycle: a windy loud working half and a fogged blind half. Your body will fight this for roughly ten days and then give up and go biphasic, and the giving-up is accompanied by a flat, stupid gloom that has nothing to do with the place and everything to do with your pineal gland. Know this in advance. Write yourself a note now, in your own handwriting, saying *the gloom is the clock, it will pass around day ten*, and put it where you will find it on day eight. Travellers who don't do this frequently conclude that they are having a spiritual crisis about the nature of enclosure. They are having a circadian one.

**Bring vitamin D.** No sun to speak of. The shaft-light is grey and exhausted and does nothing for you biologically. It does a great deal for you otherwise, which is a separate matter.

## The beat

Twelve ammonia machines, four beats a second, out of phase, producing a swelling and dying pulse on an eleven-second cycle that you feel in your sternum rather than hear.

You will get headaches for about a week. Everyone does. Nothing prevents them; paracetamol or its local equivalent helps a little; hydration helps more than you would think, because the dry hours dehydrate you faster than cold weather ever suggests.

More usefully: **low-frequency pressure at this level produces unearned dread.** You will experience, somewhere in your first three days, a sudden conviction that something is wrong, that you are being watched, that you should leave. It is physiological. It is the beat. It arrives most often in the fogged hours and most often when you are alone near the east terrace where the halls are loudest. Expect it, name it when it comes — out loud, if you like — and it loses most of its grip.

Do **not** bring high-attenuation earplugs. Two reasons. First, they won't touch the compressors, which are not really operating on your ears. Second, and far more importantly, you need to be able to hear, and the works will notice that you can't. Bring filtered plugs of modest attenuation for the fan houses and the grating-howl near the shaft-heads, and take them out the rest of the time.

**Practise the cadence before you arrive.** People here speak in the troughs of the pulse. It is a real rhythm: roughly four or five seconds of usable quiet, then a swell in which nobody bothers. You cannot fake it by talking faster. You must learn to hold a thought, deliver it whole, and then wait — with your face still engaged — through the swell for the answer. Outsiders find the waiting unbearable and fill it, and filling it is the single most irritating thing a visitor does. Practise at home with a metronome or a timer: eleven seconds, talk in the first five, silence and eye contact in the rest. Do it until the silence stops feeling like failure.

## Kit

**Light.** A lamp is not for seeing. A lamp is for putting islands of orange around yourself in a place that has decided, correctly, that light is heat and heat is the enemy. Bring something dim, warm-toned, hooded, with a long burn. Bring a red option for close work and for the fogged hours.

Do not bring a bright white torch, or if you bring one, keep it sealed for emergencies and never use it socially. Sweeping a modern high-lumen beam across the Lower Cast is roughly equivalent to firing a shotgun indoors. You will ruin the dark-adaptation of everyone within two hundred metres, you will be visible from a kilometre away, and you will be talked about for the rest of your stay.

One compensation worth knowing: **your lamp charges the phosphor.** Walking a stair or a gallery with a lamp lays the white nosings and bollard-edges in for the dark hours behind you. This is considered a small courtesy. Walk the long way round occasionally, and lay the lines for the next shift.

**Feet.** Soft, sticky rubber, not hard lugs. The hazards are, in ascending order of stupidity: black mould in the tooled grooves of old bays; algal felt at the sunward end of the basins; the ice rind at the basin edges; and — the one that gets everybody — **mould-release oil on a new bay.** Water beads on it and will not wet. A fresh bay in the fogged hours is a sheet of polished glass with a film of unattached water on it, and it looks exactly like a floor. People break wrists and elbows there weekly. Learn to identify the shine, which is unmistakable once you have seen it: the bay looks like a lake laid flat and hardened, because that is what it looks like.

Bring strap-on studs or chains for the basin shoreline, and take them off before you walk a cured bay, because steel points on good concrete is the sort of thing that gets remembered about you.

**A hat.** This is a rule and you should not treat it as a quaint one. In the wet hours the ceiling rains, cold, wide-spaced, from eighty metres. It will not injure you. It will, over six hours, take something out of you that is very difficult to put back. The rule says *not for injury, for morale*, and the rule is correct. A brimmed hat, proofed, that sheds water off your collar line. A hood alone is not enough; you need the drips off your face.

**Skin.** Alternating scour-dry and saturation, plus cement dust, plus alkali. Your hands will crack. Bring a lanolin-heavy barrier cream and use it before the damage, not after. Bring a small bottle of dilute acid rinse — vinegar water, citric solution, whatever travels — for cement contact. Alkali burns are painless until they are serious; if you have knelt in slurry, rinse and change, don't wait to feel it.

**Respiratory.** A wool or silk buff to pre-warm the dry-hour air. Everyone develops the works cough; you will too; lozenges and a scarf reduce it to a nuisance.

**Calories.** You will burn far more than you expect and your appetite will lie to you in the cold. Bring fat. Bring a flask you trust.

**Something to do in the fogged hours that does not require light.** This is real advice. Six hours, twice a day, in which the lamp-strings go to smears and then to nothing, and there is dripping, and there is the beat. Bring memorised things. Bring an instrument you can play badly. Bring cards you can read by touch. The travellers who suffer most in the Lower Cast are the ones who packed for the work and not for the weather.

## Ammonia

Twelve machines running continuously, and the whole reason the place exists. Leaks happen.

Before you have been there two hours, ask — this is a legitimate and welcome question from a newcomer — **where your mask hangs, and what the alarm sounds like against the beat.** Then go and touch the mask where it hangs, in the light, so you can find it in the dark.

Learn the smell. Not the industrial description; the actual sensation, which is a sharp assault at the back of the nose and in the eyes, long before it is dangerous. Ammonia in low concentration is a warning you cannot ignore, which is the one merciful thing about it.

Know that a leak in the windy hours clears down the trough and a leak in the fogged hours does not clear at all, and that in fog it will come to you dissolved in water droplets and go for your eyes. Know which way is down-trough from wherever you are standing. In practical terms: the water in the channels runs west and down, and so does the wind. Go against the water, and up the terrace stairs, and get onto a ventilation adit.

## Manners

**Do not ask what is past the Fourteenth Bay bulkhead.** Not directly, not obliquely, not by implication, not by the innocent-visitor route of wondering aloud. The crews know what the cold is for, or they know that they do not know, and either way the question marks you permanently as someone who came to find out rather than to be there. You will be answered politely once and then handled.

**Obey the seventh-day rule absolutely.** Do not enter a bay before the seventh day, for any reason, including a very good reason. Around day eight or nine a big bay will sometimes give a single deep report like ice on a lake. It is normal. It is also not a sound you want to hear from inside the bay, and it is not a sound you want to be alone for the first time you hear it.

**Learn to check a wall properly, because you may have to report one.** Back of the hand, not the palm. Warm is right. Cold is right — it is old, it is done. *Neither* — the in-between wall, the one that stopped curing early — is the one you report, immediately, to the nearest crew, with the bay number and the date stamp from the corner. Know the number of the bay you are standing in at all times. This is not paranoia; it is how you make yourself worth having around.

**At the changeover, stop and stand still.** Whatever you are doing. Whatever you are carrying. Stand until the note has gone all the way up or all the way down. This is the only moment the whole works speaks with one voice, and the crews are listening four kilometres away with their whole bodies. A visitor who keeps talking through it has committed the local equivalent of shouting in a church, except that churches are not load-bearing.

**You will flinch at the changeover for about a week.** This is how they know you are new. Do not try to suppress it; the suppression is more obvious than the flinch, and faking a local's ease is worse than being visibly a newcomer. Newcomers are forgiven everything. Frauds are not.

**On the heron: have no opinion.** There is an argument about whether feeding it is cruel, conducted eleven seconds at a time, and it will not be resolved, and it is not yours. Whatever you say about it will be attributed to you for the length of your stay and possibly repeated to people you never meet. If you are handed food and pointed at the second basin, take it and do it and say nothing.

**On gifts:** dry socks, good ones, in quantity, are worth more than money. Salt. Citrus, if you can carry it — the two lemon trees in oil drums are doing badly and are kept alive as a point of principle, which tells you exactly what a lemon is worth down there. Bring fertiliser for them if you know how to ask without implying that anyone has been doing it wrong.

**Do not bring seeds, spores, or anything alive that you intend to plant.** In a place where every organism is known — the crickets, the rats, the two deaf dogs, the heron, the wretched grass under the shaft-heads that someone has bricked a kerb around — introducing something is not a kindness, it is an event. If you have brought a plant, give it to the mess hut and let them decide.

## Two things to do that have no operational purpose

**Stand in a shaft-light circle and eat something.** Everyone does. Eighty metres of concrete cylinder and a column of grey, exhausted daylight arriving with nothing left in it, illuminating nothing but the water falling through it and a circle of floor. If you are lucky, snow comes down it, turning slowly, melting before it lands or not. There is no reason to eat there. Eat there.

**Stand beside a weir in the dry hours.** The cast hydrology is the accidental beauty of the place — glass-clear dead-cold water moving fast in graded channels nobody designed to be looked at — and at a weir drop the small white noise will take out the compressor beat entirely for as long as you stand there. It is the only silence available.

## Coming back

Budget three days on the far side.

The beat does not stop when you leave it. You will feel the eleven-second swell in your chest for two or three days, most noticeably when lying down, and you will hear it in machinery that isn't doing it. It passes.

Sunlight after sodium is genuinely painful; bring dark glasses for the surface, not for the works.

And the smell of the world — wet soil, growing things, exhaust, bread, anything — will hit you much harder than the light does, because four kilometres of the Lower Cast smells of cold concrete, ammonia, wet steel and nothing else, and you will not have noticed the absence until it ends.

The last thing: for a while afterwards you will run your hand along walls, looking for the seam of a formwork panel, the ridge where two casts met, the chamfer somebody's drawing specified. You will find them in surprising places. This is not a disorder. It is just that you have been taught to read a surface, and the lesson does not come off.
