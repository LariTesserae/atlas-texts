# Preparing for the Standing Water

## First, understand what will actually kill you

Travellers rank the dangers here wrongly and die of the wrong thing.

The glass will cut you — badly, constantly, and without your noticing. The heat will unbuild your joints if you go deep. The falling water will take you if you're careless within ten paces of any lip, and the fall has no bottom that anyone has reported from. All of that is ordinary mountain business, and ordinary mountain discipline handles it.

The acoustic damage is worse and less avoidable. You will trade hearing for this visit. Accept that now, in advance, at home, where you can grieve it properly.

But the thing that empties the basin of visitors is none of these. It is **duration**. This place does not attack you. It holds you, the way a held note holds a room, and it is very patient, and its patience is the only infinite resource in the entire basin. The birds are not killed. The birds *stay*, and then they go up. Every discipline below is really about one thing: being the sort of creature that can leave a sentence unfinished.

## The hard rule

**Four days in the basin. Six absolutely. Under no circumstances nine.**

Plan the whole expedition backwards from this. The ninth or tenth day is when people sit down at the lip with their legs over the drop and their hair going upward, and by then they are not making decisions any more, they are being polite to something that is talking.

Do not trust yourself to enforce this. On the fifth day, staying will be the most reasonable thought you have ever had. It will arrive with excellent supporting arguments, all of them true. This is not madness; that is the trap. Madness you could notice.

So: **hire your extraction.** Someone who does not enter the basin. Someone who waits below the cloud, at the lip of the cloud, in the warm upward rain, with rope and two strong friends, and who comes up on the morning of the fourth day with written instructions — signed by you, notarised if your world does such things — that read approximately:

> *Remove me. Do not negotiate. Disbelieve everything I say, and be most suspicious of whatever sounds sensible. If I have written anything since arriving that contradicts this letter, that writing is void. If I have asked for one more day, I have already been kept too long.*

Choose someone who does not love you enough to be persuaded. Love is a liability at the Standing Water. Employees are better. A creditor is best.

## Physical preparation (three to eight weeks)

- **Altitude.** Birds arrive here of the wrong species for the altitude, exhausted. Take that seriously: this is high ground. Acclimatise properly on the way up, and know that blood-warm water will completely mask the usual early signs of altitude sickness. You will not feel cold, you will not shiver, you will not sweat visibly in the spray. You will simply become stupid. Have a companion whose job is to notice you becoming stupid.
- **Heat tolerance.** Train in warm water. Learn what your own hyperthermia feels like from the inside, because here you lose every ordinary cue: no cold, no sweat gradient, no thirst-signal you can trust. Weigh yourself. Bring a scale if you can. Drink on a schedule, not on desire.
- **Swimming, badly, in armour.** If you enter the water at all — and you will — you must be able to move prone and feet-first above a bed of glass blades with the current doing the deciding. Practise this over an obstacle you must not touch. Practise not thrashing.
- **Noise conditioning.** Spend hours in the loudest ugly place your world offers: a boiler house, a turbine hall, a stamping mill, beside a genuine cataract. Not to toughen your ears — you cannot — but to learn that you can still think, count, tie knots, and read a slate inside a wall of sound. Most people discover in the first hour at the Standing Water that they have never had a thought in noise before, and they lose that hour, and they cannot afford it.
- **Neck and core.** You will spend days on unstable, blade-edged, freshly-cooling ground with a heavy pack and no reliable sense of down. Falls here are not bruises. Falls here are lacerations to the depth of the bone.

## Skills to acquire before you go

**1. Signs.** Speech is impossible in the roar. Agree on no more than thirty hand signs and drill them until they are reflex, in noise, in low light, in gloves, with a bleeding hand. The essential ones:

- *You are bleeding.*
- *Null behind you. Null under you. Null moving.*
- *Down is wrong here.*
- *I have lost time.*
- *I saw the figure.*
- *Count your things.*
- *We are leaving now* — and this one must be an order, not a suggestion, and any member of the party may give it to any other, once, without appeal.

**2. Plumb reading.** Your single most important instrument is a weight on a string. Down is not toward the centre of the earth here; down is toward the loudest place. In the roar those nearly coincide and you notice nothing. Near a null they diverge, and you fall sideways with no warning from your inner ear whatsoever. Practise until reading a plumb line is as automatic as glancing at a watch. Hang one from your pack, one from your wrist, one over your bed.

**3. The blood check.** Fresh glass cuts painlessly and cleanly and you will not feel four wounds in a palm. In blood-warm water you cannot feel yourself bleeding. Establish the ritual now, at home, so it survives the noise: every hour, both palms up, both forearms, both shins, look behind you at your own footprints. In water: look at the water, not at yourself.

**4. Being wrong without panic.** This is the strangest item of preparation and the one that saves people. Have a friend, over several weeks, quietly alter small possessions of yours and then insist calmly that they were always so. Practise saying *I accept the correction* and meaning it, and going on with your day. At the Standing Water you will at some point be *revised*, and the revision will include your memory, and the discrepancy will be checkable, and the only two responses are equanimity and disintegration.

## Kit

- **Boots**: stiff-soled, cut-resistant, with a second pair sealed in the pack. The glass eats footwear. Bring more laces than you think mad.
- **Gloves**: three pairs, leather over mail if you can get it. Not for warmth. For palms.
- **Shins, forearms, belly**: hard plates or thick leather. Everything that will drift over the blades.
- **A respirator or, at minimum, cloths you can keep damp.** The fine white silt never settles, and the rain that falls upward past your face is full of it. Ten days of breathing that is a lung dose you will carry for life. This is the injury nobody warns you about because it doesn't hurt until later.
- **Ear protection**: proper plugs, plus muffs. They will not save your hearing and they will not change your relationship to gravity, because the roar is not something arriving at your ears, it is something you are standing inside. Wear them anyway, and understand the cost: plugged, you lose the loudness-gradient that was doing half your balancing. Plugged, you *must* use the plumb bob.
- **The null alarm.** Cheap and essential: a plumb bob hung inside a metal cup, string tied to your wrist. If down shifts while you sleep, the bob touches the cup and the vibration reaches your wrist. Sleeping through the arrival of a null is how sandals acquire extra straps.
- **Water and food for the entire stay, plus a third.** Do not drink the lake. It is silica-laden, hot-spring water off a mountain that is still being born; assume fluoride, assume arsenic, assume the silt. Do not eat the whip-weed. Do not eat the shrimp. Do not, under any circumstance, eat the eel.
- **A mechanical watch, and a knotted tally cord.** One knot per meal, one overhand per sleep. When the watch and the cord and your memory disagree, you have measured how much of you was taken. Do not argue with the cord.
- **Slates and chalk, carried on the body.** Never leave written records lying on the ground. Paper journals are for below the cloud.
- **Rope, and the discipline of tethering to each other**, since nothing here has roots. Count the strands of your rope daily. Yes, really.
- **A dull task with clear increments** — knitting, a translation, a decade of beads, long division. Something with a countable middle and an actual end. This is medicine, not recreation. See below.

## Protocols in the basin

**Party of three.** One alone will not come back. Two will agree with each other, which is worse than being wrong. Three still argues.

**Camp on the highest lobe, near the ringing boulder** — not touching it, but within a dozen paces. Nulls are made of the fall's sound cancelling itself; the boulder puts a second voice into that arithmetic and the ground around it is the most reliably solid down in the basin. It is also the least restful place to sleep in any world. Take the trade.
