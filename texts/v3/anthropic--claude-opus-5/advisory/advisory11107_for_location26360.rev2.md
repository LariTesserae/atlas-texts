# Going to Nýgres: A Traveller's Preparation

## First, understand what kind of place this is

Most destinations in the multiverse punish you for one thing. Nýgres punishes you for four at once, gently and continuously, and none of them will kill you. That is the trap. It is a place where the danger is *attritional* — skin, lungs, sleep, boot-leather, patience — and travellers who prepare for drama and not for attrition come back after ten days looking like they have been sanded.

Hold two facts in your head before you pack anything else:

**Nothing here dries, and nothing here is fixed.**

Everything in the list below follows from those two sentences.

---

## Timing and duration

The boat is twice a week. That is the whole of it. Mis-time your arrival and you lose four days on the old coast in a room above a fuel shed; mis-time your departure and you lose four days you had not budgeted for, with your visa or your beacon window or your body's own tolerance running down.

**Minimum stay: ten days.** Below that you spend your entire visit in the sleep-deprivation phase (see *Sound*) and you will remember Nýgres as a headache. The turn happens around day six or seven. It is worth waiting for.

**Season matters enormously:**

- **April–August** is when the place is fully itself: cliff roaring, terns at full war, green thickets at maximum, midges. It is also when you are under attack every time you cross open rock.
- **Late May to early July** is peak tern. Go then only if you want the real thing.
- **September–March** is quiet in a way that locals describe as a convalescence and outsiders find bleak. The fog is worse. The light is shorter and even flatter. The works still runs.
- **The re-levelling**, four days after the frost goes out — usually late April or early May — is the single best and single most socially demanding time to be there. If you go then, go as a worker, not a spectator. More on this below.

---

## Skin: the first and most constant problem

The rock takes skin. Everyone repeats this and everyone underestimates it. Sixty-one-year-old basalt has edges like broken bottle glass, and a stumble on it does not bruise you, it *removes* a patch of you and leaves grit and volcanic glass in the wound.

Then, because nothing here dries, that wound sits in permanent 95% humidity for a fortnight and macerates. Small abrasions in Nýgres go bad at a rate that surprises people from drier worlds. The nurse — there is one nurse — has seen it a thousand times and will not be impressed by your injury, only by whether you cleaned it properly on day one.

**Pack:**

- **Gloves you wear constantly.** Not for warmth — for palms. You will catch yourself with your hands a dozen times a week. Leather-palmed, and bring three pairs, because they get destroyed and they never dry.
- **Long sleeves and long trousers always**, even in the warm hollows. The thickets are full of nettles and of angelica with wrist-thick stems whose sap will sensitise your forearms; you will push through a wall of it to get into a hollow and regret bare skin for a week.
- **A serious irrigation kit.** Saline in quantity, a syringe or bulb for pressure irrigation, forceps, good antiseptic, and enough dressings to change them twice daily. Dressings that stick in a dry world do not stick here. Bring the tape you don't like carrying.
- **Barrier cream** for hands and forearms — this does double duty against fibre itch.

## Lungs: the stone-hair

The works spins rock into fibre and the fibre gets out. Always. The west wind carries it across the plain and it is in the moss, in the willow, in the sixty-year white stratum under the soil, and on certain mornings it is hanging in the air in a way that catches light and looks, briefly, lovely.

Locals have been breathing it their whole lives and have opinions about that which you should not solicit on your first evening.

**You are not acclimatised and you should not try to become so.**

- **A proper half-face respirator with P3/P100 filters.** Not a dust mask. Not a cloth thing. Wear it in the bale shed without exception, in the furnace hall, at the quarry face, and outdoors whenever the west wind is up and the bales are being moved.
- **Sealed eye protection.** Fibre in the eye is the classic visitor injury — it gets in, you rub, and you scratch your own cornea with a stone splinter.
- **Do not wear fleece or any napped synthetic as an outer layer.** Fibre tangles in it permanently and you will carry a personal cloud with you into bed. Smooth-faced shells, tight-woven cotton, oiled canvas, hard-finish wool.
- **Bring a change of "outside" clothing you can leave outside.** The town already works this way. There is a porch, a rail, a brush. Use them.
- **Barrier cream, and cold water first when washing.** Hot water opens the pores and drives the itch deeper. Every local child knows this; no visitor does.

## The birds

Three separate problems, ranked by how badly they will ruin your trip.

**Terns.** From late May they nest on the open rock, which is exactly where the paths are, because the paths are the only place you're allowed to walk. You will be attacked. It is not a metaphor; they strike the highest point on you, hard, repeatedly, and they draw blood from the scalp.

- **Carry a stick.** A metre and a half, light, held straight up, with something pale bound to the top. You will be lent one on arrival; it will be old and well-balanced and it is a real loan. Do not lose it, do not lean it in the wrong rack, and return it.
- A brimmed hard hat is not cowardice. Take one.
- Learn the walk: hunched, stick up, straight line, no stopping, no photography. Stopping in the colony makes you a feature rather than a passage.

**Fulmars.** Do not approach the cliff ledges. Fulmars defend themselves by projectile-vomiting stomach oil, and that oil is one of the most ruinous substances an ordinary traveller can encounter. It is rancid beyond description, it does not wash out, and the smell will still be in the garment in two years. A hit garment is a destroyed garment.

- **Bring one sacrificial outer shell** if you intend to do any cliff work. There is a shed at the edge of town for oiled clothing. You will understand why when you smell it from the outside.
- Keep it out of your hair. Getting it in your hair is a shaving offence.

**Midges.** Columns of them in the lee of every thicket, in numbers that hum. A head net that you actually can eat and drink through. Repellent. Cuff closures. They are not dangerous, just erosive.

**And the birds' side of it:** every nest here is stone wool. Do not take one. Do not touch the cliff quilt. The ornithologists count them like tree rings and the count is a civic record, and in Nýgres interfering with a record is close to the worst thing you can do.

## Water, food, contamination

The town drinks from one collapse pit: a three-metre pool of polished black walls, unvarying, unfailing, and faintly alive with translucent amphipods that got there by some route nobody knows.

Three things follow.

1. **The amphipods are fine.** Drink the water. If you must filter, filter privately. Making a performance of filtering the town's only water in front of the people who drink it is the single most reliable way to be disliked here, and they will be too polite to say so.
2. **Nobody entirely trusts the pool** — not because of the animals but because it has never varied, and in a town obsessed with records, a number that never changes is a number nobody has caught out yet. Do not reassure them about it. It is not your water.
3. **You are a biosecurity event.** Nothing has reached that pool. No fish. Nothing above the amphipods. You are arriving from another universe with an entire foreign microbiota in your boot treads, your trouser cuffs, your pack straps, and your gut. *Decontaminate before you travel.* Scrub and dry boots and poles to bare surface. Freeze or heat-treat fabric gear if your kit allows. Carry nothing living, nothing seeded, no soil, no fruit with viable pips. Do not swim in the pool. Do not wash in it. Do not put your hand in it.

Food: the canteen feeds the works and will feed you. Bring your own supplementary calories anyway — the cold and the wind and the walking will take more out of you than you expect — and bring things that keep in damp. Anything in paper is a mistake.

**Do not feed the fox.** The blue-morph foxes have already worked out the canteen. Adding yourself to their map makes you briefly popular with the foxes and permanently unpopular with everyone else.

## Sound, sleep, and the thing under your boots

There is no quiet in Nýgres. The cliff cackles day and night for five months. The terns scream. The floor hums — the whole vesicled plain playing a broad grey note somewhere under a cello whenever the wind exceeds a walking pace. And the compressor thumps every eleven seconds, and that thump goes into the rock and travels, so that two kilometres out you are being percussed through the soles of your boots without knowing it.

**Expect a week of bad sleep.** Everyone gets it. Then it turns, and you sleep better than you have in years, and this is the thing people actually come back for.

- **Bring good earplugs** — the flat-attenuation kind, not the ones that only kill high frequencies, because your problem is low.
- **Bring ear defenders as well** for the furnace hall. The spinners produce a frequency that arrives as pressure rather than sound, and plugs alone are not adequate.
- **Be aware of the infrasound.** A minority of travellers respond to the eleven-second thump with nausea, dread, or a persistent low-grade anxiety they cannot locate. If you are one of those people, you will find out on night two. There is no cure except distance and time; the far end of the plain is meaningfully better.
- **Learn to navigate by sound.** In fog — and fog is most mornings until ten or eleven — the locals steer by the direction of the compressor thump and the volume of the cliff. It works. Practise it on a clear day so you have the reference.
- **The four-day shutdown.** When the works stops for re-levelling, the sudden absence is genuinely disorienting. People sleep badly *again*. Some cry, quietly, and are embarrassed. Do not remark on it.

One more thing, which is the strangest piece of local weather-lore and is true: **on still days the town is in a foul mood.** No wind, no floor, no bass line — everything feels thin and wrong. Bright calm days are socially the worst days in Nýgres. Save your favours and your awkward questions for a grey blowing afternoon.

## Light, sight, and moving over the ground

The light here is flat, even, sourceless and honest. It shows every colour exactly as it is and it shows almost no shadow, which means that on a plain of uniform black rock you *cannot read the terrain*. Edges vanish. A twenty-centimetre drop and a two-metre drop look identical. This, not the sea and not the cliff, is how visitors break ankles.

- **Bring amber or yellow contrast lenses.** Not sunglasses — you rarely need darkening. You need edge recovery. It transforms the walking.
- **A pole**, which is also your tern stick, which is also your probe for hollows under moss.
- **Boots with stiff, thick, replaceable soles**, and a spare pair, and repair adhesive. New lava eats footwear at a rate that will shock you. Locals resole constantly and there is a man who does it.
- **Stay on bare rock.** The moss mats are a hand's-breadth of woolly *Racomitrium* that swallows a footprint and holds it for a month. Walking across a mat is vandalism, visible for weeks, and everyone will know which way you went. The cruel joke of Nýgres is that the ethical route — bare rock — is exactly the tern colony. Take the beating. That is what the stick is for.
- **Respect the thicket boundaries.** Each hollow is its own room, its own weather, warmer and wetter and louder with insects. Push in slowly, arms covered, and expect to be in a completely different world two steps from the wind.
- **Hard hat and awareness at the quarry face.** The narrow-gauge loco is small, fast, quiet under all the other noise, and does not stop well.

## Kit that is specific to this place

- **Pencils, not pens.** And waterproof notebooks. Ink and fog do not get on.
- **A drying strategy.** There isn't one, really, except the furnace hall, which functions socially as the town's drying room. Bring less clothing than you think and more wool, which is warm wet. Two pairs of boots so one can be in the hall.
- **Spare batteries and desiccant.** Condensation forms by contact here, not by falling. Anything with a lens or a seal will fog internally within days.
- **A small brass or steel punch, or a good instrument.** See below.

## The people, and how not to embarrass yourself

Four hundred people, one factory, and an obsession with records that is not a quirk but a survival adaptation. The land rises eleven millimetres a year and the only way to know it is to write it down. So Nýgres writes everything down: the tide-post, the sixty-one brass marks, the levels, the channel depths, the nest counts, the birth dates, the ages of things.

**Practical manners:**

- **Ask the age of things.** It is the local form of politeness. Everyone knows exactly how old everything is, to the year, sometimes the month, and they like being asked. "How old is that thicket?" is a warm question here.
- **Offer to hold the staff.** During re-levelling the children hold the surveyor's staff and it is a small honour. A visitor who asks to work — carrying the bar, reading the level, writing numbers down — becomes a person rather than a tourist in about an hour.
- **Bring a gift that measures or records.** A precision level. A good thermometer. A set of number punches. Archival paper. Something calibrated. They will value it far above alcohol or delicacies, and they will put it into use immediately and tell you, years later, what it measured.
- **Secondary gifts:** coffee, fresh fruit, batteries, books, news of elsewhere.
- **Do not raise the lupine.** Somebody's grandmother introduced it and nobody can remove it and the town has been arguing about it for two generations. There is no neutral position. Nod and move on.
- **Do not offer opinions about the fibre and the lungs** unless asked directly, and then answer honestly and briefly.
- **They will ask what your ground does.** Answer plainly. Understand that "ours doesn't move" is, to them, a very strange thing to boast about — faintly pitiable, like saying your family has never had to learn a skill. The response you want is curiosity, not comparison.
- **The saying is *we are screwed down, not planted.*** Do not repeat it back to them as if you have understood something. You haven't yet.

## The traveller's particular problem: anchors

This is the part that is specific to us and that the guidebooks for ordinary tourists do not cover.

**Do not set a spatial anchor, beacon, cache, or return-mark to the ground at Nýgres.** The ground is going up eleven millimetres a year and — worse — it is not going up evenly. Hollows lift faster than slabs. Slabs tilt. The entire town stands on screw jacks and re-levels itself every spring precisely because a thing set solid here tears itself apart inside twenty years.

If your method of return depends on a fixed relation to local ground, Nýgres will drift it out from under you, slowly, patiently, and in a way you will not notice until it matters.

**Instead:**

- Reference the **tide-post at the jetty**. It is the one object in town whose entire purpose is to be a record of change rather than a thing pretending to be stable. Take your mark against the brass for the current year and *write down the year*. If you return, you will need to count.
- Or reference the **scoria spine**, which is the closest thing to a datum, and even then log the date.
- **Re-check before departure.** Whatever you set on arrival, verify it before you leave. Ten days is not long enough for eleven millimetres to matter, but a hollow can settle in a week and it is a cheap habit.
- **Leave nothing on the ground.** The white stratum under the moss is sixty years of stone-hair that does not rot, does not burn, does not blow away. Anything you drop joins it, permanently, and will be found by someone cutting a spade into a hollow in the next century, and they will know exactly which year's layer it came from. They keep records. They will work out that it was you.

---

## Leaving

You will be tired in a specific way — skin scraped, hands itching, ears ringing pleasantly, and sleeping better than you have in years, which you will lose within three nights of getting home.

Wash everything twice in cold water first. Expect to find stone-hair in your gear for months; expect to find it in gear you did not take.

And take one last hour on the spine at midday, in the flat honest grey light, and look at the whole of it — black plain, green hollows going off like fireworks, white drift in the willow, one stack shimmering, terns in a loose blizzard over everything, and the sea getting slowly, patiently, imperceptibly further away.

That is the thing you actually came for, and no preparation is required for it except being there and not talking.
