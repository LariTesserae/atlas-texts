# Preparing for the Sill

## First, the honest briefing

Three things on this world will kill you outright: **the heat**, **the noise**, and **the gas**. Three more will take you apart slowly: **the glass**, **the damp**, and **your own mind**. Nothing here is hostile to you. Nothing here has noticed you. You will die of ordinary physics in a place that has no interest in the outcome.

Understand the heat first, because every traveler underestimates it and the mistake is not recoverable. Under the canopy at slack hour the air sits at blood temperature and is completely saturated. That means your sweat does not evaporate. That means you have no cooling mechanism at all. A human being at rest, naked, in still air at 37°C and 100% humidity, will die — not become uncomfortable, *die* — in something under a day, and much faster if working. You are not going for a walk in a hot jungle. You are going for a walk inside a lung.

There is no cold on this world. None. The rock is warm, the litter steams, the trees are body-temperature at the base, the water is warm, the air is warm, and the sky is a lid. There is nowhere to put your heat. The only heat sinks in the entire system are **altitude** (the cinder cone flanks, three hundred metres up, in whatever wind gets under the ash veil) and **the sea** (four kilometres east, past the glass shore and the screaming colony).

Plan your entire visit around this single fact and you will probably survive. Ignore it and you will be found — if anyone bothers to look — dead of heatstroke, unmarked, in a place with more life per cubic metre than anywhere you have ever been.

## The body, beforehand

**Heat acclimatization: eight weeks, non-negotiable.** Not sauna sitting. Progressive work in humid heat, two hours a day, until your sweat rate is high and your sweat is dilute and your plasma volume has expanded. An acclimatized human holds core temperature nearly a degree lower under the same load than an unacclimatized one, and on the Sill that degree is the entire margin.

**Cardio, with load, in a mask.** You will be working in a respirator with double hearing protection in blood heat, blind. Your subjective effort will be double your actual effort. Train in the mask until breathing through resistance is boring.

**Get thin and get strong.** Body fat is insulation you cannot remove. Muscle mass is a furnace. There is an optimum here and it is lean, wiry, and not tall — you will be moving through fern-layers you have to *wade*, under three tiers of canopy, along paths worn by people a metre and a quarter high.

**Feet.** Whatever you do to your feet in preparation, do it now, because in that place there is no dry surface and you will never dry them again. Toughen the skin over months. Treat every existing crack, blister, fungal nail. Maceration begins at hour twelve and trench foot at about hour sixty. You are on a clock from the moment you step off.

**Dental and ear work done, healed, and cleared.** A perforated eardrum in a 118 dB environment is a disaster. Any wax occlusion will make your plugs fit badly. Have them molded to your ear canals by someone competent.

**Vaccination is theatre and you should do it anyway.** The Knappers are probably people. If they are people, they are a naive population, and you are a walking reservoir of respiratory viruses that would go through twenty thousand of them like a fissure through a hectare. You will wear a filtering respirator the entire time you are within a kilometre of them, for *their* sake, and you will accept a quarantine before you go. Whether their microbes can touch you is unknowable in advance; whether yours can touch them is a coin-flip with a genocide on one face.

## The training you cannot skip

**Darkness.** Three weeks minimum of blind work. Not "dim room" — blackout hood, functional blindness, learning to move, eat, assemble gear, and handle sharp objects by touch alone. Cavers and blind-rescue instructors run this. The specific skill is *not panicking when you lose the up-down reference,* which you will, repeatedly, because there is no horizon and the ground is uneven and the noise is coming from every direction at once. Vestibular disorientation in total dark with saturating sound is the commonest cause of a traveler sitting down and refusing to move.

**Noise.** Spend time — real time, hours, days — in 110 dB continuous broadband. Industrial noise labs will do it. You need to learn the thing nobody believes until they feel it: *you cannot think at 118 dB.* Working memory collapses. You will forget what you came to do, mid-sentence, with no sentence available. Everything you intend to accomplish on the Sill must be decided beforehand, written on a wrist card in raised characters you can read by fingertip, and executed as a checklist like a dive plan. Improvisation is not available to you there. Your improvising self stays home.

**Percussion.** Learn to strike stone. You need perhaps four signals: *here I am*, *coming closer*, *stop*, *distress*. Learn them from a recording of Knapper traffic, badly, and accept you will speak with a thick and idiotic accent. This is worth more than any weapon you could carry. A hand that can knock is the only credential you have.

**Diving-style discipline.** Buddy pairs, hand-signal contact through a tether, turn-around times enforced by a vibrating timer, and an absolute rule that the first person to say "back" is obeyed without argument. Treat the plain like an overhead environment, because functionally it is.

## Kit, by system

### Thermal — the system you will live inside
- **A cooled base.** Not optional. A small insulated shelter on a cone flank at two to three hundred metres, with a vapour-compression cooler and a serious power supply. The cooler will run badly — there is nowhere warmer than ambient to dump heat into — so oversize it by half and expect it to pull the interior to a merely survivable 28°C at 60% humidity. That shelter is your only real dryness in the world. Treat it as an airlock: a heated drying box for socks, liners, and optics, run continuously.
- **Phase-change cooling vest**, with enough spare inserts to cycle. 15°C packs, not ice — ice against skin in this climate causes vasoconstriction and *reduces* your heat shedding. Recharge them at base. Each vest gives you roughly two to three hours of moderate work on the plain. That is your excursion length. Set the timer, obey the timer.
- **Core temperature telemetry.** Ingestible pill, readout on a wrist unit with a vibrating alarm at 38.5°C. You will not notice yourself overheating. Nobody does. The alarm exists because your judgment will be gone before your legs are.

### Acoustic — the system that decides whether you come back with your hearing
- **Custom-molded plugs plus over-ear muffs**, worn together, for a real-world attenuation of about 35 dB. That brings 118 dB down to a survivable 83. Sustained 115 dB causes permanent damage in **under thirty seconds** unprotected. You will not get a warning. Hearing loss does not hurt.
- **Electronic ears over the plugs** — compression-limited, ambient-passthrough, so you can still use the world's medium. Without these you are sensorily castrated in a place where sound is the only sense that works.
- **A geophone spike and headphones.** The plain is one continuous stone and the Knappers' entire civilization runs through it. Drive the spike, listen, and you can hear a message struck twelve kilometres away arrive in your skull before it arrives in the air. This is the single most useful instrument you will carry and the one that will teach you the most.
- **Earplug spares, sealed, many.** You will lose them. They will be slick with condensation. Losing both plugs three kilometres out ends the excursion immediately.

### Optical — the system that mostly will not work
- **Thermal imaging: bring it, but do not rely on it.** Everything here is within a few degrees of everything else. Trees, ground, litter, animals, you — all one warm smear. Thermal gives you nothing in this environment except the vent-mouths and the occasional fissure-glow. It is the great disappointment of the kit.
- **Near-infrared illumination and image intensification.** This works, and it is how you will see. 850 nm floods, invisible to human eyes and invisible to the pit-sensing snake-analogues (they read far-IR, around 10 µm, i.e. your face). Some things here will see it anyway; assume a few do.
- **Heated, purged optics.** Every lens you bring will fog permanently within minutes unless it is heated above ambient and dry-gas purged. This is the difference between an imaging system and a paperweight. Budget power accordingly.
- **No white light on the plain.** Ever. Light is an event here. A torch makes you the fissure: every animal within range freezes, then screams, and the things that hunt the frozen come to you. Near a Knapper group, white light is worse — their vestigial eyes read only fissure-glow, and you would be announcing an eruption. If you must use visible light, do it inside the shelter with the door sealed.

### Contact with the ground
- **Boots with a full-length puncture plate and an aggressive, self-clearing lug.** Fresh basalt glass will open a foot to the bone through anything soft. But you will also be walking on a living carpet — crabs, isopods, that flat meter-long thing — a surface that *clicks and gives*, and slides when crushed. You need grip on wet crushed carapace, which is like walking on oiled gravel.
- **Hard-shell gaiters or greaves to mid-calf.** Against glass edges, against arm's-length centipedes, against the crabs, which will climb you.
- **Knee pads and elbow pads.** You will fall. Falling onto unweathered basalt is a laceration event.
- **Gloves: cut-rated, thin, grippy, in quantity.** One arm's sweep touches six species and at least one of them will be a mouth.
- **Nothing natural.** No leather, no cotton, no wool. It will be furred with fungus inside three days and stinking inside five. Synthetics, silver-treated, and a full change per excursion, dried at base.

### Air
- **Full-face respirator with acid-gas and particulate cartridges**, plus a multigas detector on the chest strap calibrated for **H₂S, CO₂, and SO₂**.
  - **H₂S is the killer.** It smells of rot at low concentrations, and then — critically — *your nose stops working at exactly the concentration that kills you*. Olfactory fatigue at around 100 ppm. Lethality not far above. You cannot smell your way to safety. Only the detector knows.
  - **CO₂ and H₂S are both heavier than air.** They pool in hollows. The plain is made of hollows. Every lobe-margin, every collapse pit, every low place in the basalt is potentially a reservoir on a still night. **Never enter a depression you have not gassed. Never sleep below the general level of the ground.** The corpse-carpet you find in some of these pits — crabs, moths, an occasional bat — is the only warning sign the world provides, and by the time you're close enough to see it in IR you're in it.
  - Fissure mouths have updrafts and are often *safer* than hollows, but they are also where the bats roost, which means guano dust, ammonia, and whatever fungal spore this world's equivalent of histoplasmosis is. Cartridge respirator, always, near a fissure.

### Water and salt
This is subtler than it looks and it has killed travelers.

The condensate on the pale leaves is, functionally, **distilled water**. The trees sweat it out of steam. It is among the cleanest water in any world you will visit — *except* that volcanic steam carries dissolved metals, and the leaves condense those too. Iron, certainly; that's the taste. Mercury, very likely, because geothermal steam usually carries it. Arsenic, possibly.

- **Activated carbon plus a heavy-metals-rated filter, many cartridges.** Drink from leaf-cups through the filter, always.
- **Electrolyte concentrate, in industrial quantities.** You will sweat one to one and a half litres per hour and none of it will evaporate; it will simply run off you. That is an enormous salt loss. If you replace it with near-distilled leaf water you will develop hyponatremia — confusion, seizure, death — while being perfectly well hydrated. **Every litre gets salted.** Make this a checklist item, because you will not be capable of remembering it out there.
- Carry two litres on your body and plan to refill constantly. Carrying a day's water is not possible; you need eight to twelve litres a day.

### Food
**Eat nothing from this world.** The entire food web is built on chemosynthesis at a volcanic seam — sulphur, dissolved iron, whatever else comes up the crack. Heavy metal bioaccumulation in a system with a biomass turnover of *weeks* will be extraordinary. The crabs will be full of arsenic. Bring all your calories: dense, cool-eaten, openable one-handed, in packaging that doesn't rustle audibly at close range.

### Medical
- **Wound kit oriented entirely to lacerations.** Copious sterile irrigation — a litre-scale pressure irrigator — because obsidian cuts drive glass fragments deep and clean-looking. Skin adhesive, closure strips, hemostatic gauze.
- Assume any wound in a blood-warm, saturated, biomass-saturated environment will be colonized within hours. Whether alien organisms can actually infect you is unknown; assume yes, and accept your antibiotics may be irrelevant.
- **Debride and close everything, immediately, in the shelter.** "I'll do it at the end of the excursion" is how travelers lose feet.
- Antifungal, antihistamine (moth scales, in quantity, in your mouth and eyes and lungs), eye irrigation, and a lot of tape.

### Navigation
- **Your compass will lie.** Fresh basalt carries strong remanent magnetization; sixty-year-old lava is a magnetic mess. Do not trust a needle anywhere on this plain.
- **Inertial navigation unit**, drift-corrected at known points.
- **A line reel.** Cave-diving practice. On your first three excursions, lay line from the shelter and follow it home. It is not cowardice; there are no landmarks, no sky, no shadows, and the paths belong to everyone including things that will use them behind you.
- **Natural references, learned and trusted:** the sea to the east as a pressure in the sternum; the cone ring as the place where the ground tilts up; the vent-glow patches, which are semi-permanent; and the *paths* — faint grooves worn into basalt by forty years of the same feet.
- **On beacons, think carefully.** The obvious solution is an automated tapper at base, knocking a pattern into the rock that you can find from anywhere with your geophone. But struck sound is *speech* here. An automated tapper is a machine shouting nonsense into a language, every minute, all night, forever, across a plain twelve kilometres wide. The Knappers will come and find it. Every animal that reads the substrate will react to it. There is no empty channel on this world — the audible band is full, the ultrasonic band is full and belongs to the bats and the moths, and the substrate band belongs to the people. **You cannot enter this soundscape without displacing someone.** If you must use a beacon, use it sparingly, use a simple non-lexical rhythm, and understand that you are broadcasting into a commons that was full before you arrived.

## Protocol on the ground

**Work at slack hour.** When the frog-sheet thins and the cicada patches fall out of phase, the noise floor drops perhaps fifteen decibels and you can think a little. That window is short and it closes the way a rain-front hits — the frogs restart *all at once*, and the plain goes from 100 to 118 in a single breath. If you are still out when that happens you will lose several seconds to pure physiological shock, and several seconds is a long time to be standing still on a moving floor.

**Never sleep on the ground.** Fungal shelves are wide enough to sleep on, and something is already living on them, and the litter is a carpet of appetites. Sleep in the shelter, strapped into a hammock, cool, with the cooler running, and take a sedative if you need one, because 118 dB penetrates walls.

**Enforce a clock.** There is no day here — the ash veil turns the sun into a grey smear and the moon into nothing at all. Your circadian rhythm will free-run within forty-eight hours and take your judgment with it. Vibrating alarms, fixed sleep block, melatonin on schedule. The Knappers' slack hour is your dawn; use it as one.

**If the forest goes silent, freeze.** Do not walk toward the quiet. A hole in the noise means something cleared it. It is either a Knapper drum-floor — in which case you are about to walk into a ceremony — or it is the thing that imitates the sound of a fissure opening, which eats well in silences it makes itself. Stand still, gas-check, listen through the geophone, and wait for the noise to come back.

**When real light happens, stop moving.** A fissure opens somewhere once or twice a night and for a few minutes the whole forest is visible — streaming, crowded, enormous — and every animal in it goes silent and then screams. This is the only sightseeing available to you and it is genuinely one of the sights of the multiverse. It is also when predators hunt the frozen. Look, and do not be the only thing moving.

**Believe the ground.** Roughly once a month the seam opens somewhere and takes a hectare of everything down. The Knappers have a call that means *the ground is about to do something*, and they are usually right. If you hear it come up through the rock — and you will feel it in your feet before you hear it — go, immediately, without asking why, in whatever direction the small people are going. A Knapper within earshot is the best safety equipment on this world, and the only one you didn't have to carry.

## If you meet the Knappers

You will hear them long before anything else happens, and they will have heard you much longer before that. Your footfalls are wrong: you weigh three times what they weigh, your soles are hard, your gait is slow and stupid and doesn't match anything. They will know exactly where you are.

**Announce yourself by knocking.** Do not arrive silently. Silence is the signature of a predator here; a thing that moves through the forest without making the forest react is a thing that has arranged for the forest not to react. Knock badly and honestly and keep knocking as you approach.

**Never use light.** Their eyes are two dark buttons that register fissure-glow and nothing else. A torch is an eruption. It would be the most violent thing you could do with your hands.

**Never touch a tuned plate.** It is a section of hollow heat-tree, found among nine rejected, scraped smooth on the inside with a basalt flake over a period of *years*, and it is the single most valuable object on this world. It isn't owned. It stays where it grew. They come back to it. If you chip it, or strike it with anything metal, or strike it at all, you have done something for which there is no repair and no apology in a language of ninety animal-names and no abstractions.

**Do not stand inside a drum-floor.** The polished circles of bare rock, eight metres across, worn down through the litter. That is where twenty or thirty of them hammer for hours with no message in it, carving a hole in the forest's noise that every other animal flees — and inside that hole, for a while, they can hear each other breathe. It may be the only privacy available on this world, maintained by labour. You are not in it.

**On gifts, be very careful.** Obsidian is worthless to them; it's everywhere and they lose blades constantly because they can always make more. What is scarce here is *dry, dense, resonant, durable* material. A steel bar would ring forever, never dull, never need retuning, and could be heard across the whole plain. It is also, therefore, a bomb. It would reorganize a society whose entire material culture is acoustic, in one afternoon, in a direction nobody could predict and nobody there could refuse. Carry nothing of the sort where it can be found or taken. If you want to give them something, give them your absence and your respirator.

## Leaving, and afterward

**Budget three nights.** Maybe four. Not because of danger exactly, but because the cumulative load — heat strain, sleep debt in 118 dB, macerated skin, cartridge burn-through, and the slow grinding of a place where you are constantly wet, constantly deafened, and never once able to see — takes travelers apart on a very predictable schedule. Most people who stay a week come home wrong.

**Decontaminate seriously.** You are leaving a world of unclassified organisms with a body full of cuts and a bag full of warm wet fabric. Burn what you can. Quarantine yourself for whatever period your home protocol demands, and then a bit longer.

**Get audiometry immediately, and again at three months.** Hearing loss from this trip does not announce itself. It shows up as *"everyone mumbles now."*

**Expect the return to be strange in a specific way.** Two things get people. The first is that ordinary rooms will feel *empty* — not quiet, empty, like a missing organ — because you have been living inside a sound that filled every cubic centimetre and did all your seeing for you, and now there's nothing there and you can't tell where anything is. That fades in a week or two.

The second is the horizon. You will not have seen more than two metres in any direction for days, in a forest so dense that one arm's sweep touches six species, and then you come out somewhere with a sky and a distance in it, and a great many travelers sit down on the ground at that point and are not much use to anybody for an hour.

That's normal. That's the Sill leaving you. Let it.
