# Preparation Notes: The Chamber Under the Gutter

## First, the one thing you must get into your bones before you go

You are about to enter a place where the highest virtue is *not changing anything*, and you are, by nature, a change. You arrive from a careless world; you are the first thing ever to come down into that chamber and speak. Everything in the preparation below is downstream of a single discipline: **leave no trace that a great-grandchild could read.**

Not "leave no litter." Leave no *record*. The city keeps its history in the polish of stair-treads and the dark tracks where hands have rubbed away the glowing film. If you lean on a wall for an hour out of tiredness, you have written on their only archive. Train your hands to hang at your sides.

---

## Body preparation (begin eight to twelve weeks out)

**Hearing.** This is the whole problem. Assume 100–110 dBA standing anywhere in the eastern third, 85–95 through the dwelling clot, and near-normal conversational conditions only inside the acoustic pockets. Unprotected, the eastern floor will cost you measurable permanent threshold shift in a single long visit.

The cruelty is that the roar *stops existing* for you within the hour. Your auditory system will adapt it into silence, and the damage will keep accruing inside that silence. **Therefore: never dose your exposure by comfort. Dose it by clock.** Wear a mechanical watch (see below) and keep a hard log — minutes in the roar, minutes in the clot, minutes in a pocket.

Get custom-moulded attenuators with swappable filters: a heavy set (~30 dB) for crossing the eastern corridors, a light musician's filter (~15 dB, flat) for working the Screens and for living in the clot, and nothing at all inside a quiet pocket. The flat filter matters more than the number, because you will be navigating by the *shape* of the sound field, and a cheap foam plug turns that shape into mud.

Condition beforehand. Spend afternoons in front of industrial fan banks, in pump rooms, at the base of a real waterfall. You are training two things: the ability to keep working while your ears have quit, and the ability to **not open your mouth**. People in loud environments lose all calibration of their own volume. You will walk into a stranger's quiet room after four hours of roar and bellow at them from two feet away. That single mistake can end a visit. Practice until near-silence is your default posture and speech is something you have to decide on.

**Cold and wet.** The temperature is the cool that beads and never shivers — call it twelve to fifteen degrees at total saturation, and you will be standing in moving water from ankle to shin for every waking minute. This is not dramatic. It is a slow machine. Over three days it will take your core down, your fine motor control first, then your judgement, and you will not notice because nothing ever changes there and so you will not think to check.

Train by standing in cold water for hours. Learn what your own hands do at the edge. Then bring: thin neoprene or modern equivalent from the knee down, wicking non-shedding layers above, and — non-negotiable — a **dry sleeping arrangement**. You cannot sleep wet in that cold and wake up competent. More on this under *Hosting*.

**Feet.** Immersion foot will end a long stay more reliably than anything else. Twenty-four hours a day in cool water and your soles macerate, split, and infect. You need a nightly ritual: off, dry, air, powder, inspect, fresh dry socks, feet elevated on stone. Budget an hour of every sleep period for your feet. Bring three times the socks you think you need and a way to keep them dry that does not involve fire, because there is no fuel.

**Neck, back, knees.** Most ceilings force a slight duck; the tunnels force worse. You will spend days in partial flexion on slick, uneven, wet limestone with no handrails. Do the boring work: posterior chain, single-leg balance, loaded carries in a crouch. **The single most likely way you die here is a fall.** A broken femur ninety fathoms down, in a city with no wood for a splint and no route out but the one you came by, is a death sentence with a long tail.

**Eyes.** Practise in the dark. Spend evenings in a room lit to the level of "a door ajar three rooms away," with a faint green cast if you can rig it. Learn to read faces at four paces under *omnidirectional* dimness — there are no shadows there, no modelling light, so all the cues you normally use for lip-reading are absent. Faces are flat pale coins. Lip-reading a flat pale coin at four paces in green gloom is a skill; acquire it before you arrive, not after.

## Skills to carry in, in order of value

1. **A sign language, and the humility to abandon it.** Learn one fluently — any one — not because they will understand it but because your hands will already know how to *be* a language. Then arrive expecting to be illiterate. Spend your first day watching hands and doing nothing else. Copy. Get things wrong at the level of a small child, which is a safe thing to be.
2. **Foot-talk.** They speak through the resonant stone. You will not learn the grammar quickly, but you can learn the courtesy: *announce yourself by footfall before entering anything.* Never appear silently in a doorway. In a world without voices, the unheralded arrival is the worst manners there are.
3. **Cord literacy.** Do not touch a cord. Ever. Not to move it out of your face, not to steady yourself. The ceiling is a nervous system and every one of those hundreds of lines is somebody's sentence to somebody. A traveller who tugs a cord for balance has rung a bell in a birthing room. Learn to read the knot-and-bead patterns by eye if you're allowed to, and keep your hands down.
4. **Stillness.** The ability to sit on a stone platform for three hours, doing nothing, without fidgeting, without narrating, without producing. This is the local baseline of virtue. It is also how you will be judged.

## Kit

**Bring:**

- Attenuators, three filter sets, plus spares. Losing them ends the visit.
- A **mechanical** watch, wound, plus a second as backup. No day, no night, no weather, no season, no hunger cues you can trust — your sense of time will dissolve inside forty hours. The watch is your one anchor and also a small private blasphemy, so keep it under a cuff.
- Non-shedding clothing. Seamless where possible. **No fibres.** Every fibre you shed goes downstream into the nine Screens, and the Screens are the world's stomach. A fleece jacket is an act of vandalism.
- Soft, non-marking, thin-soled wet footwear. Two pairs. Scrub them sterile before entry and never bring in grit — grit scores flowstone and flowstone remembers.
- Water: a mechanical filter and chemical backup. You will be drinking Gutter water, which is surface runoff that has been through limestone, which means it is cold and clear and carries whatever the careless world upstairs put in it. Boiling is not an option; there is nothing to burn. Do not, under any circumstance, use their lamp oil — it is rendered from a blind crustacean that breeds grudgingly, and the whole city's herd would fit in two pots.
- Your own calories, all of them, for the entire stay, plus fifty percent. In dense, odourless, sealed form. **You must not eat one mouthful of anything from that chamber.** One crustacean is not a snack; it is a theft from the closed system, and their entire ethics is built on not taking two where one was owed.
- Medical: wound care, antibiotics, splinting material that is not wood, rehydration salts, and something for a perforated eardrum. Assume any break in your skin gets infected in a saturated cave.
- A slate and a nail, or nothing. **No paper.** Paper is plant matter; plant matter is Gutter debris and food; also it clogs Screens and also it disintegrates. No wax tablet either — wax is fat and fat is lamp oil and lamp oil is wealth, and writing on wealth in front of them is obscene. Learn to keep your notes in your head and transcribe after exit.
- One small dead animal. See *Gifts*.

**Leave behind:**

- All fragrance. Soap, deodorant, laundry residue, mint, tobacco, insect repellent. You will already smell overwhelmingly of the surface world — of leaves and grass and rot-that-grew — and that smell is *food-smell* in a chamber where food falls from the sky. Wash with unscented water for three days before you go and wear nothing that has met detergent.
- All electronics. Total saturation at low temperature kills them and the condensation will bead on everything you own within minutes. If you must record, bring something potted and mechanical and hide it.
- **Any tool that would improve their lives.** A steel blade. A needle. A file. A synthetic cord stronger than stone-flax. A mortar. A lens. A lighter. Each of these would be received as a miracle and would end the world you came to see, and it would not even be a kindness — you are not staying to manage the consequences. Consider it the traveller's oath: *nothing that improves.*
- Any light brighter than a candle. Read the next section twice.

## Light discipline

Your headlamp is a weapon. It will strip the dark-adaptation from every eye that catches it and leave them functionally blind for the better part of an hour, in a city of unrailed platforms over moving water. It is also, more subtly, an insult: it shows them their own home as a thing badly lit.

Bring one dim, warm, hooded, downward-facing source, hand-shielded, for emergencies and for inspecting your own feet inside your own borrowed room. Use it never in the open. Train beforehand until you can move confidently at "faces recognisable at four paces, shapes beyond that." That is the operating specification of your eyes for the whole visit. Meet it.

## Waste

Here is the question most travellers fail to ask, and it is the one that matters most.

The chamber is a closed nutrient system with a single input. Everything that enters it becomes silt, becomes paste, becomes crustacean, becomes lamp oil. Therefore **your body's output is not waste there. It is either food or it is poison, and only they can tell you which.** Your gut flora is a foreign biota entering a system whose only animals are one blind crustacean, one inedible grazing insect, and a film. You could plausibly be the end of them.

**Protocol: pack everything out.** Sealed, doubled containers, carried on your back for the duration, urine included if you can manage it, and at absolute minimum never anything released upstream of the Screens. This is heavy and undignified and it is the price of admission. Do it without commentary. If a host indicates a designated place, follow the indication exactly and do not improvise.

Same logic for quarantine on the way in: two weeks clean, current on everything, and if you have so much as a cough, do not go. You would be an epidemic with a face.

## Etiquette and the hard taboos

- **The quiet pockets are private property, inherited, fought over, and roofed.** They are the only rooms in the world where anything can be said. Never enter one uninvited. Never sit down in one. Never raise your voice in one. If you are led into a pocket, understand that you have been handed something closer to a sacrament than a chair.
- **Never touch a drip-figure.** The stalactites are in exact balance, dissolving as fast as they accrete, and that balance is the local model of all correct behaviour. To snap one is not clumsiness; it is a public statement against the nature of the world. They have built rooms around these things rather than break them.
- **Never touch the Screens, the netting, the frames, or the tanks.** Not to help. Not to look closer. The Screens are the work of the world, and they are tuned by people who have tended them since before you had a name.
- **Do not rub the film.** Do not idly scrape it, write in it, or take a sample home. (If you cannot resist a sample: it is the finest Screen's catch, i.e. food, and it is also a novel organism that should probably not cross worlds in either direction. Resist.)
- **Do not investigate the rumour.** There is something long and grey at the edge of the lamplight in the outflow slot, and nobody has caught it, and everybody has agreed to consider it a rumour. That agreement is load-bearing. You with a net and a curiosity could resolve a question that they have deliberately left standing for centuries. Walk away.
- **Do not go near the outflow slot at all,** two hands wide at the base of the wall, with the whole chamber's water accelerating into it. And do not enter the plunge basin. Dark green-black, four times a man's height of falling water on top of it, and a sound that makes rescue signalling impossible.

## Gifts, payment, and the problem of being worth hosting

You cannot buy anything. There is nothing to buy with and almost nothing to buy. But you will need shelter inside a quiet pocket within your first waking period, or you will not sleep, and if you do not sleep you will be a hazard to yourself and everyone around you.

Two things you can offer, and only two:

**Calories.** One small dead animal — a rat, a fish, unremarkable, nothing they haven't seen come down the Gutter. Handed over plainly, from your pack, without ceremony, and then let them decide what it means. Do not try to fake a Gutter delivery; that is a lie about the structure of the world and the structure of the world is all they have. Do not bring a sack of grain. A windfall too large to be Gutter-shaped is not a gift, it is a theological crisis.

**Labour.** This is the better offering. Stand at the Screens and let someone show you the sorting, and then do it badly, and then less badly, for hours. Carry silt. Comb flax by feel at the north wall until your fingertips are raw. Add your share of polish to the stair-treads. In a place where history is measured in wear, honest wear is the one mark you are permitted to leave.

## What you must not say

They have no word for plant except a word for debris. No weather, no season, no day, no eras, no upstream news. Their word-stock does not have a slot for most of what you are.

Prepare two sentences and nothing more:

1. An honest, minimal answer to *what is above* — that there is a careless world, that it is not better, and that you came from it and will go back into it. Say this once if asked. Do not elaborate. Do not describe the sun. You have no idea what describing the sun does to a people whose only cosmology is *nothing changes*, and you will not be there when it lands.
2. *I don't know.* Learn this one first, in their hands, and use it generously.

And the thing you must never say, in any grammar: **any sentence containing the idea of *better*.** To ask what could be improved there is a category error — like asking what a stone weighs in the dark. It is not offensive so much as unparseable, and the attempt will mark you permanently as something that arrived broken.

## Arrival protocol

Do not translate in at the eastern end. Not into the basin, not into the roar, not into a house, not into a tunnel you cannot see the end of. Aim for the slack shallows in the middle third, well clear of the Screens, standing in six inches of glassy water with the dwelling clot glowing faintly to your west.

Then, **first hour**: stand still. Heavy filters in. Let your eyes go. Let the roar become silence, and note the watch, because that silence is a lie you are now inside. Find and memorise your exit point by touch and by the shape of the sound in that spot — not by any mark you leave, because you will leave none. Sound-shadows are the local map; your exit is a place with a particular acoustic feel and you will be able to find it in the dark once you trust that.

**First day**: announce yourself by footfall, hold your hands still, watch, be led, be useless politely, hand over the rat, work the Screens badly.

## Sanity

Constant temperature, constant light, constant sound, no horizon, no day, no season, no news. This is a sensory deprivation tank the size of a room with three hundred houses in it. The known effects arrive on schedule: time distortion by hour thirty, mood collapse by day two, hypnagogic intrusions and mild depersonalisation by day three or four. Countermeasures: rigid clock-anchored schedule, real physical tasks, the foot ritual, the log, and — this is important — permission to sit inside a quiet pocket and hear your own heartbeat without treating it as a symptom. After a day of roar, your own blood is astonishingly loud. Travellers have panicked at it. It is just your body, finally audible.

Cap your first visit at four days. Cap any visit at ten.

---

## Departure and aftercare

Expect tinnitus. Expect it to be permanent in some small degree, and decide in advance whether that price is acceptable to you, because you cannot decide it later.

Expect worse than tinnitus: the inversion. You will come out into a world of variance — wind, birds, colour, people who change their minds — and it will read as *noise* in the moral sense. Many travellers describe months of flat affect and an obsessive craving for stillness, and a few never quite get their appetite for novelty back.

Decompress deliberately. Forty-eight hours in a dim, quiet, unchanging room. Reintroduce colour before you reintroduce crowds. Do not go straight to a market, and for the love of everything do not go straight to a forest — the sheer green riot of it, after that place, has broken people.

Then write it down, all of it, from memory, in one sitting.

---

## The short version

Ears plugged, hands down, mouth shut, lamp hooded, feet dry, waste in your pack, watch on your wrist, one dead rat in your kit, nothing that improves, nothing green, nothing that sheds, no paper, no scent, no curiosity about the grey thing in the slot.

You are going to a completed place. It arrived at itself a long time ago and has had no reason since to be anything else. Your entire job is to visit it and leave it completed.
