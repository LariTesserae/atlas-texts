# Preparing for the Shoulder

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are not visiting a volcano. You are not visiting a geological feature. Those categories will mislead you at every step, and the misleading will be practical, not philosophical — it will get your boots cut open and your tarp taken.

The most useful working frame, and the one used by everyone who has spent real time up there, is this: **the Shoulder is a workshop with the maker temporarily out of the room.** Stone is being laid. Water is being plumbed. A tree is being kept. One garden was installed last April. The work is ongoing, unhurried, competent, and indifferent to you in the specific way that a mason is indifferent to a pigeon on the scaffolding — not hostile, not welcoming, simply engaged in something else.

Prepare the way you would prepare to spend a week standing in someone's workshop without permission and without being able to ask any questions. Touch little. Leave nothing. Watch your feet.

## The shape of a sensible visit

Do not attempt to live on the terrace. The Shoulder sleeps two people, possibly three, and only because of the fig and two pegs. Everything else about it — the heat, the acoustics, the sharpness, the slow appetite of the floor — degrades a human being steadily and without drama.

The standard pattern is a forest camp below the bare rock, at or just under the treeline, and four to seven days of ascents. One or two nights on the terrace, if you have a hang point and a reason. Longer than six days on the Shoulder itself is where the reports start to get strange, and the strangeness is not in the reports, it is in the reporters.

## Before you leave your own world

**Heat-acclimatise. This is the single most valuable thing you can do and almost nobody does it.** Ten to fourteen days of daily heat exposure — sauna, hot bath, exercise in layers, whatever you have — before departure. It expands your plasma volume, drops your sweat sodium, and lowers the heart rate you'll be running at on the terrace by fifteen or twenty beats. People who skip this spend the cloud hours lying in a hammock with a headache, seeing nothing, and go home saying it was hot.

**Altitude.** Twenty-eight hundred metres is modest, but it stacks badly with heat. Two nights around 2,000 m on the approach. If you can't, go slow and expect a poor first night's sleep whatever else you do.

**Fitness, specifically:** load-carrying on broken angular ground, and the ability to stop. Most of the injuries are slips. The rock does not abrade, it cuts; a fall on the Shoulder is a fall onto a floor of shallow knives.

**Medical.** Whatever the forest below demands for tropical prophylaxis — take local advice, the local field people will know. Beyond that: a wound kit far beyond what the duration justifies. Irrigation syringe, sterile saline in volume, adhesive closures, non-stick dressings in quantity, and a course of broad-spectrum oral antibiotics with a clinician's instruction on when to start it. A cut in saturated 38-degree air with mineral water running over it will not behave the way cuts behave at home. Assume anything you open goes bad in thirty-six hours unless you are disciplined.

**Dentistry and anything else deferrable — do it now.** There is no evacuation from the Shoulder that is quicker than two days of being carried down a mountain by people who will be furious with you.

**Quarantine your gear, both directions.** Eleven gardens and one tree. Do not be the person who introduces a weed to a biosphere with a total vegetated area smaller than a tennis court. New boots or boots boiled and scrubbed; no soil in the treads; pick out the Velcro; freeze your tent bag if you can. Coming home, do the whole thing again in reverse, and declare the silica.

**Mentally: decide in advance what you believe about the ornament, and then hold that belief loosely.** The fossils are real. They are also four days old. If you go up expecting to resolve that, you will spend your week irritated. If you go up expecting to *see* it, you will have a good week.

## The crossing, and the coordinates

The Shoulder moves. Seven metres seaward and seven metres up, every year. A fixed arrival point is a decaying arrival point: it drifts into open air on the seaward side or into solid rock on the landward side at about ten metres a year of combined error, and the rock is the one that matters.

**Never set an arrival point on the terrace itself, or above it.** Set it low — in the forest, on ground that has been forest for longer than the mountain has existed, where nothing is being extruded. Walk up. The walk is part of the visit anyway, and it puts you at the field camp, where the people are.

If you are returning after a gap, re-survey before you commit. The cheerful local phrase for the alternative is "going in as an inclusion," and the people who use it are not entirely joking.

Comms will not cross. Whatever satellite messenger you carry is for the local world only, and coverage on a seaward flank in permanent afternoon cloud is what you'd expect. Your return window is your lifeline. Write it down in three places and give one to someone at the camp.

## The approach

Below the terrace there are long slopes of new stone: pale, angular, hot, weeping, and unweathered. There is no scree and no soil, which means there is nothing to slide on and nothing to grip either. It is walking on a staircase built by someone who had heard about staircases.

Start before dawn. You want to be on the terrace by nine and off it or under shelter by two. In the cloud, on those slopes, with twenty metres of visibility and every surface running, the descent becomes genuinely unsurvivable for a solo traveller.

Gaiters. Trekking poles with carbide tips and spare baskets — the tips will blunt visibly over a week. Long trousers. And walk with your hands empty.

## Clothing and footwear

**Boots are consumables.** Whatever you bring will come back with the sole opened. Take the stiffest full-rubber lugged sole you can walk in, and carry a second pair, and carry a repair kit (contact adhesive, Aquaseal, a stitching awl, Dyneema thread, duct tape as the confession of failure). People who work up there resole twice a season. Some wear cheap boots and consider them ammunition.

**Gloves at all times on the terrace.** Not leather — it never dries and it goes hard and it stinks. Nitrile-dipped work gloves, four pairs, and accept that you'll lose one to a plate edge. You will put your hands down. Everyone puts their hands down.

**A foam kneeling pad,** small, on a lanyard, hanging from your harness. Never set down and left.

**Clothing:** light, long, synthetic or light wool, three sets, none of which will ever be dry. This is not a problem you can solve; it is a problem you manage. The only dry hours are between five and seven in the evening when the cloud has gone over and the terrace steams. That is laundry hour and foot hour. Strip, rinse, hang in the steam and the low sun, and put on the least-bad set.

**Feet get a ritual:** every evening, off, dried properly, powdered or creamed with an antifungal, fresh socks, and the day's socks rinsed in vent condensate. Skip this twice and you will be limping by Thursday.

**Sun.** Before two o'clock, at 2,800 m, on pale reflective stone, the UV is savage. Wide brim, buff, long sleeves, high-factor sunscreen reapplied because it will be sweated off within the hour. You can also burn *inside* the cloud. Sunglasses that you are prepared to see fog permanently.

## The sleeping system, and the only rule

*Do not sleep on the ground.* Everyone tells you this. Fewer people tell you the corollaries, which are more important because they are the ones you'll break by accident.

**Nothing of yours rests on the stone overnight.** Not the pack, not the boots, not the water bladder, not the pole tips, not the corner of your tarp. Hang everything. Bring far more cord than you think — six millimetre, abrasion-resistant, plus abrasion sleeves for every point where line crosses an edge, because a plate corner will part a rope in a night of wind-free dripping. Bring carabiners, bring hanging bags, bring a light collapsible hook-stick for getting things up into the fig.

**Nothing rests anywhere for longer than a working day.** Rotate your gear between surfaces. Move the cook kit. Move the water cans. It is almost certainly excessive. The eighth structure's tarp took eleven years to grow a centimetre and a half of interleave. The boot took nobody knows how long.

**Run a calibration object.** On your first morning, set something you don't want — a coin, a plastic spoon, a piece of your snapped pole basket — on a marked plate in the open, and photograph it, and look at it every morning: the underside, the edges, whether the plastic has gone dull, whether there is grain in it. This costs nothing and it is the only instrument you have that measures the thing you actually fear.

**Hammock,** with a structural ridgeline so the sag is repeatable, a light bug net, and an under-tarp *that does not touch stone.* You will not need insulation. This is the one gift the mountain gives you: the floor runs at blood heat all night and the air never drops. Bring a sheet. Bring nothing warmer, and use the weight for water.

Check your hammock lines every morning. The terrace rises two centimetres a day past the fig's roots, which means your anchors and your tree-hang are diverging at that rate. Over ten days it's twenty centimetres. You can watch your camp levitate.

## Water

There is water everywhere and very little of it is straightforwardly drinkable.

The Sump is 44 °C at dawn, thick with dissolved silica, floored in gel, and contains two to three eyeless crabs. It is a wonderful thing and a poor water source; silica will blind a hollow-fibre or ceramic filter in a couple of litres.

**The good answer is condensate.** Rig a simple still over a vent: a cone or a sheet, a gutter, a collection bottle, all of it off the ground and none of it left overnight. You get warm distilled water at a useful rate with no fuel. Bring the sheet, the tubing, and two litres of headroom in your containers.

**The second answer is dew,** which is heavy enough to harvest and which certain leaves up there seem to have been shaped to harvest, which is either convenient or a hint.

**The third answer is carrying it up,** which is heavy and reliable.

Whatever you drink, **it will be nearly ion-free, and you will be sweating continuously without noticing** because you are already wet through. Bring electrolyte salts and use them from the first hour. Hyponatremia is the classic Shoulder casualty: a fit, careful, well-hydrated person who becomes confused at four in the afternoon and cannot say why. Weigh yourself morning and evening if you can. Watch your urine. Eat salt with everything.

## Food and fire

No fire, no fuel, no stove flame. You don't need warmth, and open flame in a place that has no wind and no smell of its own is an act of vandalism you will feel in your chest.

Warm your food in the Sump or hold a sealed pouch in a vent. Bring things that eat cold and salty. Bring more salt than seems reasonable. Bring something sharp-tasting — pickles, chilli, vinegar — because appetite dies in wet heat and you will otherwise under-eat by half.

**All food waste leaves with you. All waste leaves with you.** There is no soil except what eleven fern gardens make out of their own collapsed fronds, and there is no decomposition budget for your orange peel. Faeces out, in doubled sealed containers. Urine out, or well down the flank, never on the terrace. This is not sanctimony; it is the second-order version of the boot rule. Think about what you want entering the record under your name.

## Tools, instruments, and records

Assume electronics die. Saturated air, 38 degrees, mineral aerosol. Keep the camera in a sealed hard case with more desiccant than you think, open it only in the dry morning hours, and let it equilibrate before you crack the seal or you will shoot a week of fog. Spare batteries, sealed separately. A second camera that you don't care about.

**Pencil, not pen. Waterproof paper.** Number your pages before you go, because you will otherwise lose the sequence.

Bring a folding scale for photographs, a soft brush, a hand lens, and a thermometer. Bring a small spirit level and a length of marked line if you want to measure the rise yourself; it is the cheapest and most affecting experiment on the mountain, and it takes four days.

Do not bring a drone unless you have a reason you'd be willing to state out loud on the terrace. The place's total acoustic output is a drip and a hiss and a tone. You will not want to add to it. That instinct is worth trusting.

## Your body on the terrace: the day

- **05:30–09:00.** The best hours. Cool by local standards, dry-ish, the stone still exhaling from the night, the Sump at its hottest and the light flat. Do your walking, your looking, your photography, your rigging.
- **09:00–13:30.** Working hours. Hydration discipline. Hat. Rest in shade — there is shade only under the fig and in the shelter, which is the entire reason the eighth structure exists.
- **13:30.** Be where you intend to be for the next three hours. Rig anything that needs rigging. Get water containers under drip lines.
- **14:00–16:30.** The cloud. Do not move around. Visibility twenty metres, every surface running, every edge an edge, and the heat thickens rather than lifting. Lie in the hammock. Drink. Write. This is not lost time; it is the most characteristic experience the place offers, and most people remember it above everything else — the white ceiling coming *up* past you, and then the world reduced to a hot glass room with a soft wall.
- **17:00–19:00.** Steam hour. Wash, dry feet, hang clothes, eat, walk the terrace in the low sun with the ribbons standing off the plates. The best light of the day by a distance.
- **After dark.** Heavy dew, blood-warm ground, the tone. Hammock. Headtorch with a red mode, used sparingly.

## Your mind on the terrace

Three things will work on you, and it is much better to have been told.

**The acoustics.** New stone is dead. It does not ring and it does not return anything. Your own voice will sound wrong to you — flat, close, unsupported — within an hour, and some people find this profoundly unpleasant in a way they can't locate. It is the same effect as an anechoic chamber, and the standard mitigation is the same: talk to someone. Go in a pair. If you must go alone, read aloud, and accept that you will hate the sound of it.

**The tone.** The stoups produce something at the bottom of hearing, more felt than heard. Infrasound and near-infrasound do reliable things to people: a sense of pressure, low-grade nausea, unease without object, and — well documented, entirely mundane, still upsetting — the feeling of being accompanied. **Expect to feel watched. Do not treat the feeling as evidence, and do not treat it as nothing.** Note it in the log with a time. Earplugs will not help; distance will, slightly.

**The ornament.** This is the one that gets people on day four. You will pick up a plate edge and find bedding laminations that were not there yesterday, laid down top-first. You will stand over an ammonite in stone that was blank when you arrived. And a small door opens in the back of your head with *how do I know anything is old* written on it, and for some people it does not close again for months. Prepare for it the way you'd prepare for a strong drug: know it's coming, have someone to talk to, don't make decisions during it, and give it a week on the other side before you decide what it meant.

## Conduct: the manners of the place

1. Do not sleep on the ground, and do not let your things sleep on it either.
2. Do not walk in the vent-gardens. There are eleven. The total vegetated area of a nineteen-year-old mountain is a few square metres and it is all right there.
3. Do not climb the fig. Do not cut it, prune it, sample it without permission, or hang more than the agreed load. It is the only nesting surface on the mountain and the swifts have priority.
4. Do not attempt to open a stoup. The seventh is already a corpse; the filaments dulled and stopped within minutes of air. We have exactly one look inside a working mechanism and we spent it. Do not spend the second.
5. Do not scratch, carve, paint, or tag anything. You can't mark a stoup anyway — a scratch closes overnight — which should tell you how the place feels about being written on.
6. Do not bathe in the Sump for more than fifteen minutes, do not bathe in it with an open wound, do not bathe in it dehydrated, and **do not fall asleep in it.** It is 44 degrees, you are at altitude, and the floor is a soft gel that takes an impression and keeps it. Whatever the rule about the ground is, that gel is the ground's best offer.
7. Do not harm, move, or count-on the crab. There are two or three. If you see four, write it down with a time and tell the camp.
8. Do not chase the frog. It will not be where the sound was.
9. Take nothing without asking the field people. Yes, the fossils are four days old and there will be more next week. That is not the point; you are in a record that someone is keeping.
10. **Take nothing to the terrace you would mind seeing in grey stone with the stitching preserved.**

## Signals that mean go down

- A new vent within a few metres of your camp or your route. They arrive fast and the garden follows in weeks; the heat arrives first.
- The stoup-tone changing pitch, volume, or rhythm. It doesn't. If it does, that is new information and you should be somewhere else while you have it.
- Your calibration object going dull, or losing its edges, or acquiring grain.
- Any of your own gear you cannot account for.
- Confusion, apathy, or a headache that isn't thirst — in yourself or in your partner. Wet-bulb heat illness presents as bad judgement before it presents as anything else, and the person it happens to will argue with you. Agree in advance, at the forest camp, that either of you can call the descent without debate.

## Departure: the inventory

Make a written inventory of everything you carry up. Not a mental one. Written, itemised, including the small things — the spoon, the spare basket, the lens cap, the roll of tape.

On your last morning, before you touch anything else, check it. Lift every item and look at its underside. Look at the plate it was standing on.

If something is missing, **spend twenty minutes looking for it in the floor.** Walk the terrace in a grid with the sun low. Look for a shape standing slightly proud, pale grey, with the detail intact.

If you find it there, photograph it, note the plate and the date, and leave it. It is not yours any more. It is four centimetres of the record, and in eleven years someone will stand over it and be very glad that you wrote down what it was and when it stopped being an object.

## Packing list, condensed

**Sleep:** hammock, structural ridgeline, bug net, light sheet, under-tarp, 40 m of 6 mm cord, abrasion sleeves, 8 carabiners, 4 hanging drybags, hook-stick.
**Feet:** two pairs stiff-soled boots, resole/repair kit, gaiters, six pairs socks, antifungal cream, foot powder.
**Hands and knees:** four pairs nitrile-dipped gloves, kneeling pad on a lanyard.
**Body:** three light long sets, buff, wide brim, sunglasses, high-factor sunscreen, electrolyte salts (double what you'd estimate), salt for food.
**Water:** condensation sheet and tubing, 4 L of containers, filter plus chemical backup plus filter spares, dew cloth.
**Food:** cold-eat, salty, sharp-flavoured, five days more than planned.
**Medical:** oversized wound kit, sterile saline, irrigation syringe, closures, non-stick dressings, oral antibiotics with instructions, blister kit, oral rehydration, analgesia, tweezers, a mirror for looking at your own back.
**Record:** pencils (six), waterproof notebook, hard case with desiccant, camera, spare camera, spare batteries, scale bar, hand lens, soft brush, thermometer, spirit level, marked measuring line.
**Waste:** doubled sealed containers, more bags than dignity requires.
**Miscellaneous:** trekking poles with spare tips, red-mode headtorch plus spare, small hand-line for cloud navigation between fig and shelter and Sump, one calibration object you are content to lose.
**For the camp below:** fresh fruit, batteries, dry socks, and news. They are the ones who know things, and none of it is written down.

## Last word

Most people go up with a question and come down with a temperature. Set the question aside. You are not going to solve the Shoulder in a week; four years of survey have produced a betting pool and a working phrase that nobody likes.

What you can do, and what is worth the whole expense and all the discomfort, is to be standing on the eastern point in the last hour of light, with the cloud gone over the summit and the entire terrace exhaling, and the stone at blood heat through your ruined soles, and the ammonite four paces away in rock that will be five years old in November.

Be there. Don't talk. Let the tone come up through your feet.

Then go down before dark, because the ground is patient and you are not made of anything it can't use.
