# Preparing to Visit The Bell

## First, the honest hazard ranking

People preparing for a place like this tend to prepare for the wrong things. They prepare for the grandeur. The grandeur will take care of itself.

In descending order of what will actually kill or maim you:

1. **Hydrogen sulfide.** Not the heat. The gas.
2. **Heat stress**, and the confusion it causes before you notice it.
3. **A fall** — on glazed, dished, unrailed steps, into a sixty-pace shaft with a glowing grate at the bottom.
4. **Fire**, which you will bring in with you, in a battery or an oxygen line, without meaning to.
5. **Hearing loss**, which is not dramatic and is entirely permanent.
6. **The surface**, on the way in or out, if your arrival anchor drifts by even a few miles.

Everything else on the list — the sulfur sap, the salt crust, the psychological effects of standing for hours inside a chord — is real but survivable with ordinary care. The first six are not forgiving.

---

## Timing: learn the three clocks before you go

The Bell keeps three rhythms, and your entire trip plan is really a decision about where in those rhythms you want your body to be.

**The nine-second breath.** This is your metronome, your watch, and — once you're inside and cannot speak — your unit of communication. Practice counting it before you arrive. Nine seconds is long enough that untrained people lose it under stress. Train until you can hold a breath-count while doing something else, because you will need to say things like *wait four* and *I'm going up in twelve* with a hand.

**The five-hour swell.** Temperature rises, gas output rises, pitch rises a quarter-tone, silent flues start to speak. This is the governing constraint on every excursion. **Plan every descent to begin after a crest and end well before the next one.** Know where you are in the swell at all times; if you have to guess, guess pessimistically. The swell is not a curiosity — it is a 20–30% change in the thing that is trying to cook and poison you.

**The annual week.** Do not be inside for it. The shaft runs hard and hot; the exhaust through a shoulder-width crack becomes a jet; the crystal fans at the lips shake themselves to powder. The stair is the only way out and during that week the stair is a flue. Be on the plain, lie down, and feel the hum come up through your ribs. That is the correct way to experience the annual chord, and it is also the only way you will experience it twice.

Budget at least a week on the surface before your first descent, simply to sit near the crack, watch the plume, and take gas readings across a full swell and a full day-night cycle. You want to arrive at the gallery already knowing what the place does.

## Air: the non-negotiable

**Do not plan to breathe the Bell's air. Ever. Not for one breath, not to "check."**

The exhalation is carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and a little hydrogen. Any one of those is sufficient. Hydrogen sulfide in particular is the classic killer of confident people, because at the concentrations that matter it *stops smelling*. Olfactory paralysis sets in above roughly a hundred parts per million; the rotten-egg stink vanishes and the naive interpretation is *good, it's cleared*. What has actually happened is that you are now in the lethal band and have lost your only free alarm. Above about seven hundred ppm it is a knockdown poison: one or two breaths, unconsciousness, then death by apnea while your partner watches.

So:

- **Closed-circuit or supplied air, full-face, positive pressure.** Cartridge filters are useless here; they cannot manufacture oxygen and they saturate against H₂S in minutes.
- **Two independent gas monitors** on your person, logging, with vibrating alarms (audible alarms are a joke in there), plus a third on the gallery as a fixed station. Set thresholds absurdly low. Believe the instrument and not your nose — your nose is the thing that will lie.
- **Redundancy for the stair, specifically.** The stair is the most dangerous forty metres of the trip and almost nobody expects it to be. The whole Bell's exhalation is squeezing up that shaft and out a shoulder-width crack. It is the hottest, fastest, most concentrated gas anywhere in the system, and it is the only exit. Carry an independent escape supply — separate cylinder, separate regulator, separate hose — sufficient to climb twenty-two body-lengths at exhausted-panicked speed, and double the number you think that is.
- **Never solo.** Two minimum, three better, with one always above and one always at the surface with a radio and a haul line rigged.

### The oxygen paradox

Here is the part that catches experienced people: **the most dangerous flammable object entering the Bell is your own oxygen supply.**

You are proposing to carry pressurised oxygen into a hot, dry, sulfur-dusted, hydrogen-bearing room with a glowing grate at the bottom and a total, ancient prohibition on fire. A leaking O₂ fitting in that atmosphere is not a leak, it is an ignition event. Sulfur dust is explosive in suspension. The canes burn blue and quick and there is nothing in two hundred miles to put them out.

Use **compressed breathing air** rather than enriched or pure oxygen wherever the mission permits. If you must use a closed-circuit rig, use one with an inherently limited oxygen flow, service every fitting before the trip, leak-test with the correct solution (not soapy water — see below), and *never* purge, bleed, or vent near vegetation or near the grate. Treat your own exhaust as a hazard to the room and route it deliberately.

## Heat: the second clock in your body

The upper gallery air feels "like a hand pressed flat on your face." Call that 60–75 °C, rising as you descend, with hard radiant load from a cherry-glowing grate below — which means the lower terraces are not merely hotter air but a *radiator*, and reflectivity matters more than insulation down there.

- **Acclimatise for real.** Two weeks minimum of heat-exposure training before departure: daily 60–90 minute sessions of light exercise in heat, progressing to full kit. Acclimatisation is worth more than any piece of equipment you can buy. It lowers core temperature, raises sweat rate, conserves salt, and — critically — it buys you *minutes of clear thinking* at the top of your heat load.
- **Active cooling, water-free.** Phase-change cooling vests (paraffin or salt-hydrate, sealed, not ice) with spare packs staged on the gallery in an insulated case. Reflective aluminised outer shell for any work below the upper terraces.
- **Core temperature telemetry.** Swallowed pill thermometers if you can get them. Otherwise a hard time limit, which is worse but better than nothing.
- **Hard turn-around rules, written down and agreed before you descend**, not negotiated in the room: a core temperature number, a gas number, an air-supply number, a clock number, and a swell-position number. Whichever comes first, everyone goes up. No exceptions, no "just this flue."
- **The buddy question.** Heat stress announces itself as confidence, irritability, and fumbling. Every ten breaths at the top, every five below, one of you asks the other a question with a non-obvious answer — a number, a name, a sum. A wrong or slow answer ends the day for *both* of you. Write this into the plan and practise it at home, because in the room you will not want to.

Plan short shifts: 45–90 minutes on the gallery, far less below, with long recovery on the surface between. This is not a place where you push through.

## Ears: the problem you will be tempted to solve wrongly

The Bell is "as loud as a river in flood." Call it 95–105 dB broadband, continuous, for as long as you are in there, with heavy low-frequency content and an overtone that seems to originate from the empty centre.

Continuous exposure at that level does permanent, cumulative, un-treatable damage in hours, and you will want to take your protection off, because **the entire point of the place is the sound**, and muffling it feels like visiting a gallery in a blindfold.

The solution is not to endure it and not to plug it. It is:

- **Electronic hearing protection** — active compression earmuffs under the suit hood, which reduce the dangerous peaks while passing the structure of the chord. You will hear it *better* than unprotected: less masking, more detail.
- **Binaural recording** at the gallery, on a shock-mounted rig, so that you can sit in a quiet room at home later, at a safe level, for as many hours as you like, and actually *listen*. This is how you experience the Bell. Not there. Afterwards. Accept that now and your trip will be far better and your hearing will still exist.
- **A baseline audiogram** before you go and another two weeks after you return.
- Expect the low end anyway. It comes in through your chest and skull, not your ears. Nausea, chest flutter, blurred vision on the swell's crest, and a persistent low-grade feeling of being watched from the middle of the room are all normal reactions to infrasound and near-infrasound in dim light. Know this in advance so that when it happens you correctly file it under *physics* rather than under *something is here*.

## Getting in: the crack is a door and a chimney and a filter

The entrance is a slot no wider than a shoulder, with a hot gas column coming out of it and salt crystal fans growing on its lips.

Design consequences:

- **Everything must fit through a shoulder-width gap, or be haulable separately.** You cannot go down wearing a bulky backpack rig. Plan a **pass-down**: person descends slim, gear follows on a line, gets donned in the stair or on the gallery. This means your breathing rig must be doffable and re-donnable in the dark, in gloves, hot, deafened — practise it a hundred times until it is boring.
- **The crystal fans will shred fabric.** You will break them going through; they regrow in days, so the damage is trivial to the Bell and potentially fatal to your suit. Pad the lips with a sacrificial mat. Inspect every suit seam after every passage, with a light, on the surface, before you consider going back in.
- **Anything you drop near that crack does not fall. It is taken.** A glove, a lens cap, a notebook page — snatched up and out onto the plain, or blown back down into the shaft. Everything on a lanyard, starting at the surface, not at the gallery.
- **The stair is worn into glazed bowls.** Twenty-two body-lengths, two turns, no handrail, in a hot updraft carrying dust. Rig a fixed line and go down it on a hand ascender/descender the way you would any other vertical hazard. Do not free-climb the stair because it looks like a staircase. It is a staircase the way a wet roof is a floor.

## Movement inside: the hole is the instrument

Two rules above all others, and they are the same rule twice:

**Do not enter the central void. Do not put anything into the central void.**

The masons spent generations keeping that volume empty, because a bridge would have damped it. A body, a dropped pack, a tripod that topples off a terrace, a drone — each one is a damper. The fall is fatal, the retrieval is impossible, and the object that lands on the grate does not rot away: it glows, it cooks, and it stays, a foreign thing in the throat of the instrument, for as long as the Bell lasts.

Which means:

- **Lanyard discipline is absolute.** Every item tethered, including the items you think are too big to drop. Count your items in a written manifest at the surface, count them again on the gallery, count them again before you leave the gallery, count them at the surface. The manifest is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between *visited* and *permanently altered*.
- **No drones. None.** I know. The empty middle is the most drone-shaped space in any world, and the footage would be extraordinary. But rotors generate tones, a drone in the column is a damper, the updraft will take it, and if it lands it lands on the grate and burns. If you need the geometry, laser-scan from the terraces and stitch.
- **No leaning out for the shot.** The updraft strips, cooks, and sheds anything that leans too far into it. That is the documented behaviour of plants that have had two thousand years to learn better.
- **Assume every surface is slippery.** Glaze, dust, and unscraped ashwort mats on the treads. Soft, high-friction, non-marking soles; three points of contact; move on the inhale when the air is slackest and hold still on the exhale if the flow is pushing you.
- **Stairs "at angles chosen for reasons that were not walking."** Do not assume a stair goes anywhere. Do not assume a doorway is a doorway — hundreds of them are shallow niches that simply stop. Navigate conservatively and never take an unexplored route on the way *out*.

## Light: the dimness is a law, and you should obey it

There are no lamps in the Bell because fire is prohibited, and the prohibition made the dark. That dark is not a deficiency in the site. It is a two-thousand-year-old policy, and a visitor who fills the room with white LED light has done something rather like shouting in it.

Also, practically: the inhabitants — if any remain — read the city by the faint greenish phosphorescence of the old glazes before they read it by shape. That skill takes hours to acquire and one flashlight to destroy.

So:

- **Dark-adapt on the surface at night, then descend.** Thirty to forty minutes, no screens, no white light. Red goggles on the walk to the crack.
- **See by near-infrared and image intensification**, not by illumination. IR illuminators on your helmet are invisible in the room and give your cameras everything they need.
- **If you must have visible light, make it deep red, make it low, and point it at your feet.** Match the cherry glow from below. You will find, after an hour, that you barely want it.
- Your instrument displays should be dimmable to near-nothing and red-shifted. A bright screen in that room is a small sun.

---

## Power, and the fire you will bring

Lithium cells above 60 °C do unpleasant things, and the Bell's ambient will exceed that everywhere you want to work. A cell venting and igniting on a terrace is the precise disaster that the oldest law in the place exists to prevent, and there is nothing to fight it with — no water for two hundred miles, and an atmosphere that will happily carry a sulfur-dust flame through eleven stacked terraces of resinous canes.

- **Insulated, cooled, hard-cased battery containment**, with PCM packs, carried on the body where you will feel it if it gets hot, never set down on vegetation, never left behind.
- Prefer chemistries tolerant of heat; prefer small cells, many, separately housed, over one large pack.
- **No spark-producing tools. Antistatic everything.** The Bell is bone dry and full of moving dust; static charge accumulation is guaranteed. Conductive fabrics, bonded gear, no synthetic fleece, ground yourself to the masonry at intervals.
- No pyrotechnics, no flares, no chemical light sticks, no hand warmers, no smoking, no leak-testing with anything flammable.

---

## Water: you are the wet thing

There has been no water in the Bell for two thousand years. That fact *is* the place — no rot, no mould, no frost, no rust, no collapse, nothing decaying on a legible schedule. The city has not survived despite conditions; it has survived because there is no mechanism for it to end.

You are about to walk into it carrying several litres of liquid water, breathing out water vapour, and sweating.

Take this seriously in both directions:

- **Sealed hydration, drunk through a suit port, never opened in the room.** A spilled bottle on a sulfur plant at 140 °C is a steam explosion and a dead plant. On a glaze it is the first drop of rain in two millennia, and it leaves a mark that will still be legible when everyone reading this is a fossil.
- **Contain your own exhaust and sweat.** A closed suit is the right answer for the Bell's sake as much as yours. Do not exhale on the grinding stones, whose dust has been sitting at its angle of repose since before your civilisation had writing.
- **Nothing organic leaves your body in the room.** Plan your excursion lengths accordingly; pack out everything, including sweat-soaked liners, in sealed bags.
- On the surface: you are in one of the driest environments that can exist. Your consumption will be enormous and your thirst will lag behind your need. Drink on a schedule, with electrolytes, and measure your output. Dehydration and heat illness are the same problem wearing two hats.

## Sound discipline: mind what you say

This is the rule most visitors never think of, and it is the one with the longest consequences.

**Every species in the Bell drops its pollen when shaken at a particular pitch and no other.** The room is a keyboard and the calendar is the score. Which means an arbitrary loud tone at the wrong time of year is not noise. It is an instruction, issued out of season, to a plant that will then have nothing to breed with.

Therefore:

- **Do not whistle. Do not sing. Do not hum.** Not even quietly; you are standing inside a resonator that was built to amplify exactly this.
- **No test tones, no sweeps, no acoustic characterisation with speakers.** If you want the transfer function, measure it passively from the room's own drive.
- **No playback, ever, anywhere in the room** — including the "let's see if this recording sounds right" instinct at the gallery. Listen on the surface, or at home, or never.
- **No unnecessary impacts.** Dropped tools ring. Hammering rings.
- **Keep motors quiet or absent.** Fans, pumps, and rotors all sing, and your rig sings at a fixed pitch for hours. Check what your equipment's tonal signature is *before* you go, and choose gear that is broadband-hissy rather than tonal.

## What not to touch, which is nearly everything

- **The wedges.** Thousands of them, sorted by thickness into niches. They are not hardware. They are the notation — the accumulated written decisions of generations about what this room should say and when. Moving one wedge is editing a manuscript with your thumb. Do not pick them up "to look." Do not re-sort a niche you think is untidy. Photograph, measure by eye and by scan, and leave them where they lie.
- **The tools on the pegs.** Adzes, rasp, plumb-bobs, the bundle of graduated rods. They were set down. Where a thing was set down is data.
- **The dust on the grinding stones.** Angle of repose, undisturbed, for an unknown number of centuries. It is the most fragile object in the room.
- **The copper beads in the thresholds.** The only metal in the world you are standing in. Taking one would be the single most destructive act available to you for its weight.
- **The plants.** The sap is liquid sulfur at 120–150 °C. A broken cane does not "bleed"; it sprays a hot liquid that wets, clings, holds its heat, and then glasses over — on masonry, on your suit, on your skin. Thermal-contact gloves and sleeves, and simply don't brush past canes. Keep off the ashwort mats where you can; they creep slowly and were scraped by hand for a very long time, and now are not.
- **The three rods and the plumb-bob leaning against the wall in the western quarter, and the fresh chips on the tread below them.** Someone put those down intending to come back before the swell. It may have been last month. It may have been forty years ago. It is not determinable and it is not yours to determine by moving anything.

And: **leave nothing.** No flagging tape, no chalk route marks, no cairns, no survey pins, no plaque, no "we were here." In a place with no decay, anything you leave is permanent, and worse, anything you leave *near a flue* will eventually be read as a tuning mark by someone trying to reconstruct the register. Your trash would become scripture. Navigate by scan, by photograph, by the phosphorescent glaze outlines, and by a partner posted at the branch.

## If someone is there

Assume they might be. Two flues have been re-wedged. Plan for the encounter rather than improvising it at 100 dB in a respirator.

Understand what you look like. You arrive out of a place they do not have a word for, at a body temperature they would find impossible, with a faceless shining head, carrying metal — the substance their whole world contains six beads of — and drinking water in a land where water does not exist. Nearly everything about you is, to them, either a miracle or a violation, and you cannot control which.

So:

- **Get low and still, immediately.** Sit down on the terrace. Put your hands where they can be seen and empty. Do not stand between anyone and their wedge-niches, and do not block a stair.
- **Do not speak or signal with tones.** Gesture slowly. Gestures are safe; pitches are not.
- **Show your face if the atmosphere outside the room permits** — on the surface, later, not inside. A face is worth more than any gesture. Inside, a visor is a wall.
- **Give nothing.** Not water, not metal, not fire, not a light, not medicine. Every one of these is a bomb in a place organised around their absence, and none of them is yours to introduce on a three-day visit. The urge to give something will be very strong when you understand what has happened here. Resist it.
- **Take nothing**, including images of people who have not understood what an image is.
- **Follow them.** If they scrape a mat, stand back. If they descend, do not follow unless invited. They know which stairs go nowhere.

## The temptation, and what to do about it

You will work out, within an hour or two, that you could probably fix it.

The small bores have abraded wide. The top of the chord has gone flat and blurred. There is a stand of tall, glossy, perfectly healthy plants on the north terraces that inflate and slacken with everything else, grow a finger a day, and have not shed pollen in living memory, because their note is no longer in the room. They cannot even die properly. They will simply continue, lush and sterile and loud.

And you have calipers, a spectrum analyser, a laser scanner, and a wedge-shaped rock is a wedge-shaped rock.

**Don't.**

Not because the impulse is wrong — it is the most human impulse available in that room — but because you do not know the score. You know pitches; you do not know the *order*, or the weeks, or which notes are meant to be silent, or whether the sterile stand on the north terrace is a failure or a retirement or a deliberate famine imposed for reasons recorded in a wedge-niche you cannot read. A note added in the wrong week releases a species into an empty room. A note added into a chord you have not fully mapped will move other notes you did not intend to touch; that is what a coupled resonator *is*. You would be performing surgery on a two-thousand-year-old instrument, on a three-day visit, in a respirator, with a hard turn-around time, from a civilisation whose relevant expertise is in a field that was invented here and nowhere else.

**What to do instead, and do it thoroughly:**

- Measure every flue mouth you can safely reach: bore, chamfer, depth, wedge state, plug state. Photograph the wedge-niches with their sorting intact.
- Record the chord, binaurally and with a calibrated array, through at least one complete five-hour swell and, if you can manage a long expedition, across the annual cycle from the surface.
- Model it. Build the acoustic reconstruction at home, where you can run it a thousand times and be wrong for free.
- Work out, if you can, which pitches have gone flat and by how much, and what wedging would restore them — and then *write it down*, in a form that could be given to someone who has the right to use it.
- Carry the record out. Do not leave a document in the Bell.

If there is a decision to be made about the north terrace, it belongs to whoever left three rods and a plumb-bob leaning against a wall, intending to come back before the swell. Your job is to make sure that when they come back — this year or in four hundred — the information exists.

## Above ground

Do not treat the surface as the safe part. It's the part that kills the underprepared on the walk back from the crack.

- **The crust.** Room-sized polygons curling at the edges like old paint, over a void that has already slumped once. Sound ahead of you, stay roped where the crust sounds hollow, and never walk the curled edges. Establish one path from camp to crack, flag it with something you can retrieve entirely, and use only that path.
- **Salt glare.** Albedo like snow, with none of the associated cold. Full-coverage glacier goggles, spares, and sunblock on the *underside* of your chin and nose, because the light comes up at you.
- **Temperature swing.** Evaporite plains bake by day and lose heat brutally at night. Pack for both; you will want the night for surface work anyway.
- **Stay upwind of the plume.** The column from the crack is the Bell's exhalation: hot, sulfurous, and carrying abrasive flakes of dead leaf. Site your camp crosswind and well back, and wear monitors even on the surface. The gas concentration at the lip is a real hazard and the nicest place to sit is exactly the worst one.
- **Water and the two hundred miles.** Everything you will drink must arrive with you or be cached. Your arrival anchor is therefore a life-support asset. Mark it in a way you can find in a whiteout of blowing salt, verify it daily, and hold a reserve — water, air, and shelter — sufficient to survive a missed departure window by a wide margin. In a place with no water in any direction, a two-mile navigation error and a delayed return are the same event.

---

## Coming out

- **Decontaminate off-site.** Sulfur compounds and fine sulfide dust will be on everything. Ventilate, wash down, bag the liners, and inspect all elastomers and steel — H₂S attacks both, and the failure it causes in steel (sulfide stress cracking) shows up as a sudden fracture in a part that looked fine.
- **Do not bring a cutting out.** Not a leaf, not a chip of cane, not a flake from the plume. A chemolithotroph running on liquid sulfur has nothing to fear from your world's ecology and your world's ecology has no idea what to do with it. The reverse case — a spore of yours taking hold on a terrace — is the sort of thing that ends two-thousand-year-old systems.
- **Medical follow-up.** Audiogram, and a genuine one. Respiratory check. Note any lingering headache, eye irritation, or cough — low-level H₂S and SO₂ exposure has a nasty habit of being retrospectively obvious.
- **Expect the rhythm to stay in you.** Most people who spend more than a few hours on the gallery report that the nine-second cycle keeps running for days afterward: they find themselves breathing on it, walking on it, waiting for a swell that isn't coming. And they report that home sounds broken — too quiet, too irregular, too full of small pointless noises. This passes. It passes faster if you sit down with the binaural recording at a safe level and listen properly, which is the thing you went for and could not do while you were there.

## Minimum manifest

Slim enough to pass a shoulder-width slot; everything tethered; everything counted twice.

**On the body:** full-face positive-pressure breathing rig with independent escape supply · two gas monitors, vibrating alarm, logging · PCM cooling vest · aluminised outer shell for lower work · thermal-contact gloves and sleeves · antistatic, non-melting under-layers · soft high-friction non-marking boots · active compression hearing protection with bone-conduction intercom · core-temperature telemetry · lanyards on all of it.

**Carried:** shock-mounted binaural recorder and calibrated mic array · IR illuminator and intensified camera · laser scanner · calipers, non-marking · deep-red low-output hand light · written manifest and grease pencil · sealed hydration · containment case for batteries · spare visor films · sacrificial mat for the crack lips.

**Rigged and left at the surface, not in the room:** fixed line down the stair, haul line at the crack, fixed gas station on the gallery (retrieved on exit), shaded camp, water cache, radio, a partner who does not descend.

**Left behind in the Bell, at the end:** nothing. Not one thing.

---

## A last note on the right frame of mind

You are not visiting a ruin. Nothing here has fallen; nothing here can. You are visiting a working machine with almost no one left to work it — a keyboard played by the planet, maintained by hand for two thousand years, going slowly and irreversibly out of tune by a grain a year, in the dark, at volume, with the plants still breathing on the nine-second cycle all around the walls.

Go quietly. Measure everything. Touch nothing. Carry the record out.

And when you are back, and someone asks you what it was like, you will find that the honest answer is the one from the description you were given: *it is only a room*. Forty paces across. You could shout across it. That it is only a room is the thing that will not stop mattering.
