# Preparing for the Young Range

## The one thing that will kill you before anything else does

You cannot step through a door onto that marsh.

This is the whole problem with portal travel into high country and it needs to sit at the front of every plan you make. The valley floor is at forty-nine hundred metres. That is above the altitude at which humans have ever established permanent settlement, and it is roughly the altitude at which an unacclimatised sea-level body begins to fail inside twelve hours — headache by hour two, vomiting by hour six, and then, if you are unlucky in the way that a small fraction of people are unlucky, fluid in the lungs or swelling in the brain, in a place with no evacuation, no descent route that is short, and no one who will find your body.

So: **you acclimatise on the origin side, before you ever open the transit.**

Three to five weeks. Either a real mountain — live at three thousand, sleep-high day-trips to four and a half — or a hypoxic tent, which is worse and slower but works if you're disciplined about it. You want your haemoglobin up, your ventilatory response retuned, and your resting saturation at simulated five thousand metres to be sitting comfortably in the high seventies without you feeling clever about it. Take acetazolamide starting two days before entry and keep taking it. Carry dexamethasone and nifedipine and know, cold, at three in the morning, in the dark, what each one is for.

And then still plan the first three days as if you're ill, because you will be. Nobody is sharp at forty-nine hundred. You will sleep badly — Cheyne–Stokes breathing, the awful cycle of waking up gasping — you will lose your appetite exactly when your body needs four and a half thousand calories, and you will lose weight steadily the entire time you're there, because at that height human tissue is being spent, not maintained. There is a clock on your visit and it isn't your food supply. Two weeks is a reasonable trip. Three weeks and you are noticeably degrading. A month and you are a different, thinner, stupider person.

**Write your safety rules down at sea level.** Turnaround times, camp-move criteria, the saturation number below which you descend. Laminate them. At altitude you will find beautiful reasons to break every one of them, and the reasons will feel like insight.

## The second thing: it is wet, and it is never going to dry

Most high-altitude travel is a desert problem. Thin dry air strips water out of your lungs, your lips split, your gear dries in an hour on a rock.

None of that applies here. The air sits at its dew point most of the day. This inverts your entire kit list.

Practical consequences, in order of importance:

**No down.** Not in your jacket, not in your sleeping bag, not in your booties. Down in a saturated atmosphere at plus-two degrees does not fail dramatically; it fails slowly, over four days, until the night you notice you've been shivering since midnight and cannot remember when it started. Take synthetic. Take more of it than looks reasonable. It will be heavier and it will not care that it is damp.

**Vapour barriers.** This is the counterintuitive move and it is the correct one. Because nothing evaporates and nothing dries, you must stop your own sweat from ever entering your insulation. Vapour-barrier liner socks. A VBL shirt against the skin under the fleece. A VBL liner in the sleeping bag. It feels clammy and unpleasant and it is the difference between a sleeping bag that weighs the same on day fourteen as on day one and a sleeping bag that has quietly gained two kilos of your own condensed breath and stopped working.

**Trench foot is your most likely injury.** Not frostbite — the temperature hovers just above freezing, which is precisely the non-freezing cold injury band, and you'll be standing in silty meltwater. Three pairs of boots is absurd; two is not. Dry socks live in sealed bags and are treated as a controlled substance. Every single night, no exceptions, no matter how tired: feet out, dried, inspected, powdered, into dry sleep socks. Look at your toes. Actually look at them, with a light.

**Everything soft is stored in dry bags, all the time, even in the tent.** Assume any object left in the open for two hours is wet. Assume any object left out overnight is glazed.

## The third thing: the dust will eat your equipment

Rock flour. Ground finer than talc, in suspension in every stream, dried on the flats, lifted by the afternoon wind. It is not dirt. It is abrasive, freshly fractured mineral with sharp edges at the micron scale, and it gets into zippers, stove pumps, camera focus rings, bearings, watch crowns, tent pole ferrules, your teeth, and your eyes.

- Zippers: wax them, and expect to lose two.
- Optics: bring lens caps you can operate with gloves on, and never wipe a dusty lens dry — you'll grind the coating off. Blower first, always.
- Water: **do not run glacial melt through a membrane filter.** It will blind in about four litres. The flour will not settle out of still water for weeks. Instead — and this is the single best local knowledge you can carry in — **drink from the seep lines.** The wall bases weep continuously from bedding planes; that water has come through kilometres of fractured limestone and comes out clear and cold and hard. Collect from the seeps, boil to be sure (remember the boiling point at this altitude is around eighty-four degrees, so hold it, don't just flash it), and leave the milk-coloured river alone. If you must use the river, carry alum or a similar flocculant and settle it overnight in a black bag. Silt-water gives you a grinding, low-grade gastroenteritis that at altitude is genuinely dangerous.
- Your body: sunglasses or goggles on the flats in the afternoon, not for glare but for grit.

## Light, and what its absence does to you

Understand what you're walking into optically. Eleven kilometres of cloud, a colourless blue-shifted daylight at maybe the brightness of an overcast room. It never gets brighter and it dims through the afternoon.

Two separate problems.

**Terrain reading.** With no directional light there are no shadows, and with no shadows you cannot see relief. You will step off a forty-centimetre drop you never saw. You will misjudge a slope by fifteen degrees and a distance by a factor of three. Bring amber or rose contrast lenses — they help more than you'd think — and bring trekking poles and *use them as feelers*, tapping ahead, the way you would in fog. Move slower than your legs want to. Nearly every injury in flat light is a simple ankle or knee, and a simple ankle at forty-nine hundred metres with no rescue is a fatal event with a two-week delay.

Also: bring UV protection anyway. Thin atmosphere, high cloud, snow albedo. The light is dim and it will still burn your corneas and the underside of your nose.

**Circadian collapse.** Ambient light of a few hundred lux will not entrain your body clock. Within four or five days you will be sleeping in fragments at strange hours and feeling a flat, ashy, unmotivated misery that you will misattribute to altitude or homesickness. It is neither. Carry a small high-output LED panel — ten thousand lux at arm's length — run it for twenty minutes at your chosen morning, every morning, without negotiating with yourself. Keep the battery in your jacket against your chest; lithium cells at plus-two deliver maybe half of what they promise, and cold batteries that read empty come back to life warm.

Wear a watch that you trust and keep a written log with times in it. Time distorts badly in that valley. The log is your anchor.

## Moving through it: the hazard clock

The valley runs on a diurnal cycle and if you learn it you can travel more or less safely. If you don't, you're rolling dice several times a day.

**Night into pre-dawn (frozen).** The safest ground conditions. Marsh is firm, channels are low, the walls are locked. But it's dark and it's the coldest, and you can't see, so you mostly don't move.

**Mid-morning, roughly nine to eleven (the thaw release).** The most dangerous hours near any wall. Everything the night froze now lets go: the ice aprons on the seep lines detach and come down in slabs, and the blocks that freeze-thaw quarried overnight get released from their sockets. **Do not be within rockfall range of a face between eight and noon.** Plan your traverses of the talus for the afternoon or the early morning, cross fast, cross one at a time, and wear a helmet whenever you can see a wall above you. Note that in this flat light you often cannot see a falling stone until it's close, and the acoustics here are strange — you'll hear three or four rockfalls an hour and be unable to say from where. Do not rely on hearing to save you. Rely on not being there.

**Midday to mid-afternoon (peak melt).** Braided channels rise, sometimes by half a metre. Crossings that were ankle-deep in the morning are chest-deep, opaque, and moving. **Cross water early; get to the side you want to sleep on before eleven.** Milky water hides its own depth and hides holes. Probe with a pole. Unclip your hipbelt.

**Late afternoon (wind and dimming).** The flats start exporting dust. Cloud forms *in* the valley at eye level with no warning — a white body sliding along a contour, and visibility goes to fifteen metres in ninety seconds. The rule for this is simple and you should adopt it as doctrine: **when the cloud comes, sit down.** Don't navigate in it. Don't try to reach camp. Put on your jacket, mark your position, wait. It dissolves as fast as it forms.

### Two hazards that will not give you a clock

**Outburst floods.** Those turquoise lakes between the rubble ridges are impounded by ice and moraine and some of them fail completely in an afternoon. When one goes, the entire braid plain reorganises. **Never camp on the flats.** Camp high on a lateral moraine, above the highest strandline you can identify — look for the trimline, the boundary between rock that's fresh and rock that's slightly less fresh, and get above it with margin. Do not camp in the mouth of a side gully either. Accept the wind and the awkward ground; you are buying the right to sleep.

**The marsh itself.** Saturated silt over rubble, in places behaving as a liquid. It sucks. A leg in to mid-thigh with a heavy pack, alone, in near-freezing slurry, is a survivable event only if someone can pull you out. Travel roped or at least paired on the flats. Never travel the flats alone.

## Navigation in a landscape that won't hold still

Whatever map you carry is a rumour. The channels move weekly, the lakes are temporary, the moraine ridges shift, and cairns get buried under half a metre of fresh grit. Assume no satellite constellation. Assume the schist bands throw your compass by a few degrees.

What works: skyline. The walls and the arêtes and the notch of the col are stable on your timescale and everything on the floor is not. Navigate by ridgeline bearings and profile sketches, not by ground features. Keep a pace count. Photograph your camp site from a hundred metres out in four directions so you can recognise it in flat light. Leave your route on paper with your partner and stick to it.

## What you owe the place

This is a pioneer system in the first century of its existence. The crustose lichen on that boulder is growing at a millimetre a decade. The moss cushion in that flush took eighty years to form and the flood will take it anyway — but your boot will take it too, and your boot has no business doing so.

- **Walk on rock, ice, and gravel. Never on the moss flushes.** Detour. Always detour.
- **Pack out everything.** Everything. There is no soil, no decomposer community, no oxidation to speak of. An orange peel here is not compost; it is litter with a projected lifespan in centuries. Human waste goes into sealed containers and comes home with you, and this is non-negotiable, because you are carrying a gut flora that this world's meltwater has never encountered.
- **Sterilise your gear before entry.** Boot treads, Velcro, tent floor, pack seams, pole baskets. Seeds, spores, tardigrades, springtail eggs. You are the single most efficient vector of biological introduction this valley has ever had access to and its inhabitants have no competition experience whatsoever. Freeze-thaw your soft gear, scrub the hard gear, and do it properly.
- **Assume cross-world microbiology is a two-way problem.** You have no immunological history here and neither do they. Full-face protection is overkill; basic hygiene, no bare-hand contact with the algal snow or the springtail mats, and a hard rule about not eating anything local is proportionate.
- **Do not camp on the ablation zone grooves.** That's the springtail city. A hundred thousand animals to the square metre, and your tent floor is two square metres.
- **The blue sheep and the wolf are doing arithmetic and you are a new variable.** Watch from four hundred metres with glass. Do not skyline yourself above the sheep. Do not follow the wolf. Do not leave food where either can find it, ever, once.
- **Leave the fossils in the wall.** You will find them at fifty-two hundred metres, ammonites on edge in a tilted slab, and you will want one very badly. They have been up there a few thousand years and they will not last; taking one changes nothing geologically and everything about what kind of visitor you are. Photograph it. Sketch it. Put your hand on it. Walk on.
