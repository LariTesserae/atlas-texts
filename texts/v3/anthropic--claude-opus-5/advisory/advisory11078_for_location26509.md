# Briefing: Visiting the Maintenance Plain

## Preliminary Advice

Read the whole of this before you buy anything. The Plain kills people, and it does not kill them with the things they were afraid of. Nobody has ever been eaten there. Several have died of drinking too much water. More have died of standing still.

Budget six to ten intervals on the surface for a first visit. Longer stays are possible; they are not recommended, and the reason is in the last section, not the first.

---

## 1. The Central Problem: You Cannot Cool Yourself

Everything else is logistics. This is physics.

Thirty-four degrees at the edge of saturation means the wet-bulb temperature is thirty-four degrees. A human body sheds heat by evaporating water into air that has room for it. This air has no room. Your sweat glands will run at full output for as long as you are conscious and the sweat will simply *sit* on you, or run off, and it will remove almost nothing.

At rest you generate about a hundred watts. Walking, three to five hundred. On the Plain you have essentially nowhere to put it. Untreated, an unacclimatized adult walking slowly on flat plate goes hyperthermic in two to four hours and does not recover by sitting down, because sitting down does not help. This is the mechanism that has killed most of the people the Plain has killed, and it kills them while they are comfortable, unhurried, and taking photographs.

**But the Plain is a cooling system, and it will cool you if you ask it correctly.**

Note the detail in every survey: *the floor beads.* Every horizontal surface is below the dewpoint. The plate is being held a few degrees under air temperature by the coolant galleries beneath it, and plate is dense, sintered, and thermally conductive. It is a continent-sized cold plate maintained at constant temperature by machinery that will not notice your hundred watts if you add them for a century.

So:

- **Bring a body-cooling loop with a plate-coupled heat exchanger.** A liquid-cooled garment (the sort worn under hazard suits) plumbed to a flat conductive pad you lay on bare plate, weighted, with thermal paste at the interface. This is the single most important item you will carry. Two of them. Two pumps, three sets of tubing.
- **Bring a dehumidifier — the largest one you can move.** Inside a sealed shelter, drying the air is what makes you human again: it restores evaporative cooling, it keeps your skin from rotting, and its condensate is the cleanest water on the world. Dump its heat into the floor.
- **Phase-change vests** (paraffin, 28 °C melt) for excursions, recharged in the shelter. Rotate three sets.
- **Move slowly and rest often, but rest lying down on plate, with maximum skin contact.** Face down, arms out. It looks absurd. It is the local posture and you will adopt it by the third interval without embarrassment.
- **The dew tide is not a cool period. It is the most dangerous four hours of the interval.** When fog condenses on your skin it *delivers* latent heat directly into you — roughly 2,400 joules per gram of water that forms on you. During tide you are being gently heated by the weather. Be inside, or under a shed layer that condenses on its own outer face and drains clear of your body. Never work through a tide. Nobody experienced does.

Acclimatize before you go: two weeks of heat-and-humidity work in a chamber, ten to fourteen days, until your sweat runs salt-poor and your plasma volume is up. It buys you an hour a day of function. Take it.

## 2. Water and Salt

You will lose four to eight litres per interval doing almost nothing, and every drop of it runs off you into the seams instead of cooling you. That loss is nearly all water *and salt*.

**The classic Plain fatality is hyponatremia.** A well-equipped, careful traveller distils beautiful clean water from the fog, drinks nine litres of it, doesn't feel hungry in the heat, and has a seizure on the fourth interval. Do not let this be you.

- Electrolyte concentrate, dosed by measured schedule, not by thirst. Thirst is a liar at saturation.
- Sodium target: budget 8–14 g/interval and weigh yourself twice per interval. Body mass is your instrument. A digital scale is not optional kit.
- **Never drink seam water or gutter water, unprocessed or "filtered."** It carries dissolved reduced sulfur and trace metals — it is the rime's food supply, which is to say it is coolant seepage. Filtration does nothing to dissolved metals. Distil, then run it through chelating resin and activated carbon, then test. Or, simpler: take the dehumidifier condensate, which is atmospheric and much cleaner, and treat it anyway.
- **Do not eat anything on the Plain.** Not the rime, not the beetles, not the snails, not the crabs, which look like food and are not. The fauna have machined ossicles and catalytic gut mesh; their tissues concentrate exactly the metals the rime concentrates. Assume every animal is a small heavy-metal accumulator with unknown proteins. All calories are imported: dense, high-fat, low-residue rations, and expect to eat less than you should. Force it on a schedule.

## 3. Air

Test before you breathe. The Plain's atmosphere is the exhalation of a coolant system and it is not guaranteed.

- **Four-gas monitor minimum**, with a dedicated hydrogen sulfide head, worn at the collar, plus a second at ankle height (H₂S is heavier than air and pools in seams, gutters, and the lee of welds). The rime metabolizes reduced sulfur; the sulfur has to come from somewhere; the seams are where it comes out. Sulfide anaesthetizes the sense of smell within a minute or two — the moment you stop smelling rotten eggs is the moment you are in real trouble, and your nose will not tell you.
- **Escape hood** on the belt, always. Full SCBA if you go anywhere near an intake throat, and see §8 about not doing that.
- Note that water vapour displaces about five percent of the atmosphere at saturation. Your oxygen fraction is already reduced before anything else goes wrong. Combined with heat strain, this shortens your working ceiling more than you expect.
- **Never enter a gutter-line, a throat, or the lee-side base of a weld without gas-checking the space first**, even if you have checked it before. Especially if you have checked it before — the Plain's regularity makes people stop checking, and that is the mechanism.

## 4. Light, Depth, and the Absence of Shadow

The uniform ceiling does three specific things to you, and all three are worth training for.

**It removes shape.** Without shadows, you cannot see form. A ten-centimetre step and a two-metre drop look identical. Casting-flash frills, seam edges, the knife-crest of a weld — all invisible until you touch them. Carry a **strong single-point handlamp at hip height and use it constantly**, in "daylight," not for illumination but to manufacture shadows. You are bringing your own sun. It feels ridiculous for about ten minutes and then becomes the thing you would not go outside without.

**It removes distance.** Uniform luminance with no shadow, no aerial-perspective cues you can trust, and a haze that varies unpredictably — you will misjudge ranges by factors, not percentages. A stack you judge at two kilometres is at eleven. Carry a **laser rangefinder** and use it before every commitment of energy. Also expect *empty-field myopia*: with nothing to focus on, your eyes relax to about a metre and you become functionally blind to the middle distance. Countermeasure: lenses with a faint etched reticle, and a habit of refocusing deliberately on your own outstretched hand, then pushing out.

**It removes time.** There is no hour. See §7.

Take neutral-density tinting: not for brightness, but because a whole-field uniform glare with nothing to rest on produces a specific grinding headache within a few hours. Some travellers bring lenses with a printed horizon line. It helps more than it should.

Photograph with raking light or you will bring home nothing but grey rectangles.

## 5. Walking, Footing, and the Voltage in the Seams

**Boots:** broad, soft, smooth-soled, high-volume, insulating. Not cleated, not aggressive-lugged. Three reasons: the vitreous flanks of welds are wet glass and lugs skate on them; cleats abrade plate and the Plain notices abrasion and answers it; and every square centimetre of tread you press down kills animals. You will kill thousands of animals per interval regardless. Do not add to it out of vanity.

**Insulation matters for a second reason.** There is an electrical potential between adjacent plates — the rime lives partly on shorting it. Nobody has measured a lethal step-voltage, but travellers have reported stinging through wet gear when straddling a seam with conductive kit, and metal tools bridging seams corrode fast and behave oddly.
