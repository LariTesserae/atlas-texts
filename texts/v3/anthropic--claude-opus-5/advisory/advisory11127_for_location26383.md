## Preparing for the Stanchion

### 1. Understand what you are actually going to do

You are going to spend a small number of hours standing inside a musical instrument the size of a cathedral, at an altitude where you cannot think properly, in air that will not let you cool down, surrounded by a plant community older than your nation, and you are going to pay for it with part of your hearing.

That is not hyperbole and it is not a metaphor. It is the entry fee, and the tenders think of it as a fee rather than an injury because they have made peace with it. You have not. Decide now, at home, in silence, whether you are willing to trade the top of your hearing and the rest of your life's quiet for this. If the answer is no, that is the correct answer for most people, and there is no shame in reading about it instead.

If the answer is yes, prepare properly, because casual preparation here produces a permanently deafened tourist with a fungal lung infection and no memory of what they came for.

### 2. Home-world preparation (start three months out)

**Get a baseline audiogram.** Full range, both ears, including the high frequencies your doctor usually skips. You want a document of what you had. Get another one two weeks after you return. This is partly for your own care and partly because the honest literature on the Stanchion is thin, and one more clean before-and-after is worth something.

**Acclimatise.** The plug sits above four thousand metres and the approach ridge is higher. You need at least a week of graded altitude before you begin the walk in, ideally two. Altitude does two things that matter here: it makes you stupid and it makes you clumsy, and you will be doing fine motor work on wet iron above a three-hundred-metre drop while being physically vibrated. Do not arrive fresh. Do not arrive sick. Do not arrive hungover.

**Get fit for load-carrying, not for speed.** You will carry your own water in and your own waste out. See below on why.

**Learn the hands.** There is a working grammar in use at the Stanchion, and it is not any sign language from your world. You will not become fluent. You should nonetheless arrive with thirty to forty signs solid: *yes, no, stop, wait, come, go down, go up, help, sick, cold, hot, hungry, thirsty, I don't understand, again, slower, thank you, sorry, careful, rope, hold, let go, water, food, sleep, how long, where, mine, yours, ask,* and the four local terms that have no translation — *the pattern has slipped*, *the bearing is telling a new story*, *the drum is thick today*, *go to the cage*. Get them from a tender or from a traveller who has been, and drill them until they are reflex. In there, a sign you have to think about is a sign you do not have. Practise them wearing gloves.

**Learn to write on your own forearm** with your non-dominant hand, legibly, without looking. This sounds absurd. You will use it constantly.

**Decide what you are going to say in the cage.** Do this early and do it soberly, weeks out, written down, in your own language, in your own handwriting. The people who go out on that line without having decided are the people who come back having apologised to somebody, and who cannot afterwards explain to themselves why. Whatever your intention, the middle of that tower will find the thing you have been not-saying and hand it to you, and you will say it out loud to no one, in a voice you have not heard for days. Better to have chosen.

### 3. The approach

The ridge is old-paper dry. It will take moisture out of you without ever feeling humid, and the high sun will burn you through cloud-glare off the cloud sea below. Standard high-desert discipline: cover everything, glacier-grade eye protection, lip and nose grease, drink on a schedule rather than on thirst.

**Carry all your own water.** This matters more than anything else on the walk in. There is one pipe leaving the Stanchion, a forearm thick, and everything in it is already owed to a plain that cannot see the mountain. Drinking from that pipe is not resourcefulness; it is taking a swallow out of a city. Nobody will stop you. Everybody will know.

**Time your arrival deliberately.** If you want the autumn valve cycle — the one minute per year when the fundamental sags a whole tone, the pressure eases, all the swifts come off the wall at once and the crown light strobes with them — you must be there in the right week and be willing to wait, indoors, doing nothing, for days. It is the fastest thing that happens in the Stanchion and it is over in about sixty seconds. Many travellers go for it. Be aware that it is also the most dangerous minute of the year, for reasons in §7.

**Quarantine your gear before the last day's walk.** Turn every seam inside out. Brush and wash your boots, then dry them, then brush them again. Discard anything organic: no seeds, no nuts in shell, no dried fruit, no untreated wood, no soil, no leaf litter from home, no plants pressed in your notebook. Inside that drum is a closed garden that has been arranging itself for centuries with nine bands and nine scars and nothing else. A single burr in your bootlace is the most destructive thing you could possibly bring, more destructive than a hammer.

### 4. The door, and the first twenty minutes

The sign at the base reads, in iron, in a language nobody speaks: **THE DRUM IS SPEAKING. DO NOT ANSWER.** Do not spend energy on translating it or on deciding whether it is mystical. Take it as operational instruction, because that is what it is, and see §8.

It is a pressure door. Clear your ears going through. The interior sits a little above the outside and you will feel it in your sinuses; if you have any congestion at all, do not enter, wait a day.

Then, inside the plug corridor, before you go anywhere: **stand still for twenty minutes.** Do not walk in. The plug's concrete drops the composite to something merely industrial, and this is where you find out how your particular body handles the fundamental. Thirty-one cycles per second is below the ear's competence and above the body's tolerance. It arrives in the sternum, the sinuses, the fluid of the eye, the fillings of your teeth. Some people get nausea. Some get vertigo. Some find their vision softens so that they cannot read their own writing at arm's length. Some get a specific and unpleasant sensation of being *examined*. Find out here, in the corridor, at ninety-odd decibels, with a door behind you — not on the fourth gallery at a hundred and thirty with a rail in front of you.

If it makes you sick in the corridor, it will incapacitate you on the floor. Go back out. This is not a character failing; it is anatomy, and the anatomy of the middle ear and the sinuses varies enormously between people.

### 5. Hearing: the honest arithmetic

Bring both plugs and muffs, and wear both, always, from the door onward, with no exceptions and no "just for a second." Well-fitted foam plugs under a good muff will get you something like thirty-five to forty decibels of real-world attenuation in the range where your ear is fragile — which means the thin white shriek off the throat vents and the rasping four-forty from the failing bearing come down to something survivable, and those are the two things most likely to take your hearing outright.

What no protection on any world can do is stop the fundamental. Below about a hundred cycles, sound arrives through your skull and your chest, and your inner ear hears your own bones. There is a floor to attenuation set by your skeleton, roughly forty to fifty decibels below the airborne level, and against a hundred-and-thirty-decibel bass that floor is somewhere in the eighties. Which is to say: perfectly protected, you are still standing in a machine shop, and you are standing in it through your ribs.

Therefore: **budget time, not comfort.** Total exposure inside the drum proper — beyond the plug's quarters — should be counted in single-digit hours per day, with hours out for every hour in, and a hard total for the whole visit. Six hours in the cylinder across four days is a real visit. Six hours a day for four days is an audiogram you will not enjoy reading.

Bring spare plugs. Bring more spare plugs. In that humidity, foam plugs go limp and slimy and stop sealing after a couple of hours; flanged silicone lasts better but slides. Check your seal by yawning and swallowing every few minutes; you will learn to feel the fundamental change character in your sinuses when the seal breaks, which is a better indicator than anything you can measure.

### 6. Reading the wall

The single most useful skill in the Stanchion is knowing where the quiet is, and the wall tells you for free.

Green means survivable. The nine hanging jungles sit at the pressure nodes; the plants are there because that is where the air is not being torn. Bare pale rind means the antinodes: sterile, dry, scoured, and acoustically murderous. The nine plates of standing dust hover level at the nodes for the same reason.

So: **the garden is your sound map.** Stand level with green. Do your looking, your writing, your resting, your thinking at the ledges and the galleries that the builders — who knew exactly what they were doing — placed at the nodes. Crossing a bare band on a ladder is a thing you do briskly, breathing out, not a thing you do while admiring the view. The difference between a node and an antinode is a few metres of climb and something like ten to fifteen decibels, which over a visit is the difference between keeping the top of your hearing and losing it.

A note for the arithmetically inclined: the numbers do not close. Nine bands across three hundred and eighty metres does not sit comfortably with a thirty-one-cycle fundamental in a ninety-metre cylinder under any air you are likely to have brought assumptions about. Several visitors have burned a whole trip on this. The tower does not care about your figures. Read the wall. The plants have had three hundred years to be right.

### 7. Body, and moving

**Lungs.** Warm saturated air, fern and liverwort spore in quantity, three centuries of swift guano on the bare bands, and a knee-deep bed of fermenting seed pulp in the second band. Whether the local biology can colonise you depends on how close this branch is to yours; assume the worst. Wear a well-fitted half-mask with the highest-grade particulate filters you can get, and accept that it will fog your eyes, chafe against the muffs, and make you feel like you are suffocating at four thousand metres. Practise wearing mask, muffs and glasses together, at altitude, before you go. If your travel service offers antifungal prophylaxis, take it.

Do not linger low in the second band. Fermentation makes carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide pools in warm still channels. Carry something that tells you — a cheap monitor, or at minimum a candle you can crouch and watch.

**Heat.** The interior is warm as breath and saturated. You cannot evaporate sweat. Every exertion is thermally unforgiven. Dress in the vestibule: strip to the minimum before you go in, leave your mountain layers at the door, and understand that you will be climbing ladders in a sauna wearing a respirator.
