# Preparing for The Sheet
### A briefing for first-time visitors

---

## 1. Understand what will actually hurt you

Almost everyone who prepares for The Sheet prepares for the wrong things. It looks benign. It *feels* benign — that is the trap. Here is the honest order of risk:

1. **Heat you cannot feel.** The air and the water and your skin are all near the same temperature, and the humidity is total. This means your sweat does not evaporate, which means sweating does you no good at all, which means you have no cooling system. You will not feel hot. You will feel pleasantly warm, then talkative, then stupid, then confused, then you will sit down in an inch of water and die in the sky. There is no shiver-and-shade warning. **Hyperthermia here is silent.**
2. **Your feet.** Continuous immersion plus quartz veins standing proud like blades plus glassy pocked slag. Macerated skin splits, a split becomes a wound, a wound in warm nutrient-rich brine goes septic in a day and a half, and you are four days' walk from anywhere with a floor.
3. **Lightning.** You are the tallest vertical object for a kilometre in every direction, standing in a conductive film, under a daily afternoon anvil.
4. **Navigation failure.** No horizon, no landmarks, mirage, magnetic iron crust. People walk in circles here with perfect confidence.
5. **Everything else** — snakes, crabs, the big grazers, a twisted ankle in a crab burrow under waist-high fog — is real but ordinary.

Prepare in that order.

---

## 2. Prepare your body first, months out

**Heat-acclimatize in humidity, not in dry heat.** A desert sauna teaches you nothing useful. You want ninety minutes a day, for two to three weeks before departure, of moderate work in a steam room or a hot humid chamber — enough that your body learns to start sweating earlier, sweat dilute, and hold plasma volume. It will not save you, but it buys you an hour a day of function, and an hour a day is the whole trip.

**Learn your own sodium loss.** Sweat-test if you can. On The Sheet you will sweat constantly for no thermal benefit, and you will drink constantly because water is literally underfoot. That combination produces hyponatremia — the classic, avoidable, lethal beginner's death here. You must know your numbers and pre-package electrolytes by the day, not by guess.

**Get very fit in the least glamorous way.** You will walk slowly through resistance, in an inch of water, for six hours, carrying everything. The gradient is nothing; the drag is everything. Train by walking in shallow water or wet sand with a pack. Your calves and hip flexors will tell you whether you did.

**Dental and skin check.** Nothing heals here. Go in with no open cuts, no cracked heels, no untreated tinea, no dodgy filling. Trim your toenails short and square two weeks out, not the night before.

**Vaccinate and carry broad-spectrum antibiotics** appropriate for aggressive soft-tissue infection, plus a topical antifungal you trust, plus a second one in case the first does nothing. Alien biochemistry cuts both ways: local pathogens probably cannot use you, but *opportunistic colonization of dead tissue does not require co-evolution.* Something will move into a wound. Assume it.

---

## 3. Biosecurity — the part that matters more than you do

This is the single most important paragraph in the briefing.

The Sheet is finished. It is flat, closed, soil-poor, with no upstream, no refuges, no altitude for anything to retreat to. Its whole ecology rests on the fact that **the plain will not hold soil.** A single hardy grass in your boot tread — something that traps silt and builds a root mat — would not merely alter this place; over a few thousand years it would end it. The tussock islets are the size of a hat for a reason. Do not be the one who makes them bigger.

- All gear new or sterilized. Not "cleaned." Sterilized.
- Boots, poles, pack, straps, tent floor: brushed to bare material, then chemically treated, then sealed until arrival.
- No untreated organic material whatsoever. No wooden pole, no leather, no cotton, no cork, no dried fruit with a seed in it, no wool.
- Freeze or irradiate soft goods if your service offers it.
- Human waste **comes out with you.** All of it. Packed, sealed, carried. There is no soil to bury anything in and there is no dilution — every gram you leave goes into an inch of water that concentrates by evaporation. Bring far more waste bags than dignity suggests.
- Antibiotics in your body will exit in your urine. Ideally, do not take them here unless you need them; if you do, that waste is hazardous and goes home with you too.

You are the first tool-user this world has ever had. Every object you drop is the oldest artifact in its history, and it will lie there, un-eroded, on a surface where a mound takes two hundred years to melt.

## 4. Choose your window

There is no season, only gradients — but the difference matters.

**The less-wet months** are better for a first visit. The iron crust hardens to a ringing pavement, which means firmer footing, fewer preserved footprints, and less risk of you grinding the algal mats. Animals concentrate at the seeps and upwellings, so you see more by sitting still. The water is shallower and warmer — a heat risk, but a predictable one.

**The wet months** give you the frogs at full strength, which is the reason many people come, and the resurrection ferns, and the storm walls at their most theatrical. They also give you greasy crust that holds your footprint for a season, more lightning, deeper water, and a midge hatch that has to be experienced through a head net to be believed.

The connoisseur's answer is **the turn**: the last weeks of the less-wet, when the first storms arrive but the pavement still rings. Go then. Accept that you may have mistimed it; nothing here announces itself.

## 5. The kit, annotated

**Optics and skin — the double sun.**
The glare comes from above and below simultaneously. You will burn under your chin, inside your nostrils, on your eyelids, on the roof of your mouth if you walk with it open. A hat brim is half a solution.

- Glacier-style sunglasses with side shields, **and** a second pair, because losing your only pair is a trip-ending injury. Polarized helps you see into the water; it does not solve the glare. Anti-fog treatment matters enormously in saturated air — goggles fog and stay fogged.
- Broad hat *plus* a light hood or neck drape *plus* zinc under the chin, under the nose, on the ear lobes.
- Long, loose, light, hooded UPF synthetics. Not cotton — it will mold in three days and it will chafe. Not leather anywhere: leather rots and so do the feet inside it.

**Feet — the crux.**
- Wading boots that *drain*: mesh uppers, firm sole, closed toe, hard enough to stop a quartz blade. Not sandals. Not neoprene socks.
- Four to six pairs of thin quick-dry liner socks. Rotate daily whether or not they look dirty.
- A **mandatory dry-foot ritual** every single night: bare feet, elevated, one to two hours minimum, antifungal powder, inspection of every toe web with a light. A missed night is how it starts.
- Ankle support and two trekking poles. The poles are not for balance on a flat plain — they are for probing. Under waist-high dawn fog you cannot see a crab chimney or a lungfish hole, and a rolled ankle out there, alone, is a death sentence dressed as a nuisance.

**Sleeping — you need to get off the ground.**
There is no ground. There are three beds on The Sheet: a whaleback, a very wide-footed camp cot, or a hammock strung between fan palms (rare, and you should not be lashing loads onto the tallest living things in the world).

- A cot with broad feet that will not punch through soft crust, plus a groundsheet under it purely to keep your dropped items out of the water.
- A tight-mesh inner tent or a full bug bivy. No-see-um mesh, not mosquito mesh. The midge hatch is the reason the dragonflies darken the sun.
- **Whalebacks are the best campsites and you should mostly not camp on them.** The seep lines are cool, permanent, and *everything comes to them.* You would be sitting on the only water source for a square mile. Camp a hundred metres off, on bare crust, and visit.

**Water.**
There is water everywhere and almost none of it is safe.

- The mats are the problem. Vast cyanobacterial mats mean cyanotoxins, and **a filter does not remove them.** Boiling does not remove them. Concentrate them, if anything.
- The tea-coloured pools are tannic, iron-loaded, acidic.
- The white rings are brine and soda. Never. Not filtered, not diluted.
- **What you want are the upwellings** — the cool columns against the ankle, water that has spent years inside the stone. Groundwater, low algal load. Scoop with a wide shallow bailer to avoid stirring sediment, or better, open a small hollow in the crust at the upwelling and let it clear for ten minutes.
- Treatment chain: prefilter cloth → hollow-fibre filter → **activated carbon** (this is the non-negotiable stage, for toxins) → chemical or UV. Carry spare carbon. Carry a hand-pump desalinator as an emergency backup that also handles most toxin load.
- Electrolytes, pre-measured, in daily sachets. More than you think. Weigh yourself morning and evening if you can carry a scale; if not, watch urine colour and volume obsessively and know that clear copious urine in this place is a warning, not a triumph.

**Food and fire.**
There is no fuel. Nothing here burns and nothing here should be burned. Carry a sealed stove and canister fuel or eat cold — and given the heat, you will want to eat cold anyway. Everything must be retort-sealed or freeze-dried; there is no refrigeration and no cool place and an opened packet is finished within hours. Plan for appetite loss: humid heat kills hunger, and you will need to schedule eating like a chore.

**Against the rot.**
Nothing dries. Ever. Plan around it.
- Dry bags inside dry bags. Sealed hard cases for electronics.
- Large quantities of desiccant, double-bagged, single-use — you cannot recharge silica gel in a world with no dry air and no fire.
- Titanium or quality stainless only. Carbon steel rusts visibly in a day.
- Spare batteries sealed individually; contacts greased.
- Expect at least one camera to fog internally and stay fogged.

**Ears.**
Filtered earplugs — the kind that attenuate evenly and let you still hold a conversation. The insect shrill is constant but your brain will file it as silence. The frogs are different: ten thousand pure-tone callers become a *pressure*, felt behind the eyes, for six hours, and there is no shelter from it because it is not really sound by then. You will not sleep without protection. You will also not hear a grazer approach, which is the argument for the sleeping site being open and visible in all directions.

**One strange item.**
Bring three or four chemical instant cold packs and ration them.

Nothing here is ever cold. Not the night, not the water, not the shade, not the stone. Most travelers report that somewhere around day five the absence of cold stops being a curiosity and becomes an obsession — a genuine physical craving, like thirst. A cold pack held against the back of the neck for ten minutes is, out there, a serious piece of psychiatric medicine. Do not spend them early.

## 6. Navigation: assume every instrument lies

- **Your compass will lie.** The crust is the iron the rock sweated out. Expect local deflection, sometimes gross, sometimes subtle and consistent enough to feel trustworthy. Never navigate on a compass alone.
- **The termite mounds do not lie.** They all stand the same way: thin edge to the noon sun, broad face to the morning. A field of mounds is a field of meridians. Learn to read them on day one; they are the most reliable direction-finder in the landscape, and they are everywhere, and they are free.
- **Sun compass and shadow stick.** Know the local solar declination. Practise before you arrive.
- **Pace count and time**, logged religiously. You will rise the height of your shin in an hour, so altimeters are decorative, but pace counting on flat ground is unusually accurate.
- **Redundant satellite/inertial positioning**, two units, separately sealed, separately powered.
- **Distrust your eyes entirely.** Mirage and looming are constant. Objects invert, double, detach from the ground, and float. A fan palm looks like a standing person at a distance and like a person at any distance in fog. A two-metre termite mound can loom into a tower. Binoculars will confidently show you things that are not there and will not show you their true range.
- **Morning is the clearest navigation window** and also the foggiest: the fog lies waist-high and flat, so your feet are invisible but the *tops* of everything float above the milk with perfect clarity. Navigate by treetops and mound caps at dawn. Probe with poles. This is one of the great strange pleasures of the place.
- Mark your insertion point with a staked, high-visibility marker that you *remove on departure*, and record its fix in three independent places.

---

## 7. The daily rhythm you should adopt

The plain has one schedule and you should surrender to it.

- **0300–0430 — wake.** It is as cool as it will ever be, which is not cool.
- **0430–1030 — travel.** Through the fog and into the clear. This is your entire productive day.
- **1030–1200 — make camp, eat, dry feet, log the day.** Be settled before the sky organizes.
- **1200–1700 — do nothing, in shade you brought with you, low.** The cloud stacks. The thunder arrives twenty minutes behind the flash. The wall comes across at walking speed and hits you warm as spit and is gone in a quarter of an hour. Then the low-slant sun ignites every drop on the entire plain at once and you will not be able to look at anything but your own feet.
- **Dusk — sit still.** The frogs begin. This is the reason to come.
- **Night — sleep with plugs in.** Travel at night only in emergency: the stars are reflected in the sheet, up and down dissolve, and the vertigo is genuine and nauseating.

## 8. Lightning: read this twice

There is no good answer and you should not let anyone sell you one. You will be the highest point in a conductive film on a plain with no shelter, and this will happen essentially every afternoon.

What you can do:
- **Be finished travelling before noon.** The storm builds from the morning's fog. You can watch it assemble from an hour away.
- **Know the wall.** It advances at walking speed with a visible edge. This means you can see it coming for twenty minutes and, if you must, walk *with* it rather than through it.
- Drop your poles and pack a distance away. Get low, feet together, not lying flat. Crouching in an inch of water over conductive crust is poor; lying full-length in it is much worse, because step potential across your length is what kills.
- Do **not** shelter beside a termite mound or under a fan palm. They are the only tall things in the world and they are the strike points.
- Whalebacks are higher ground and therefore worse ground.

Mostly you manage this by scheduling, and by accepting an irreducible residual risk. Say so out loud before you go, so that you have actually decided it rather than drifted into it.

## 9. Manners, and how to actually see anything

The Sheet is a place where **the spacing is the design.** Everything alive has agreed, over an unfathomable stretch of time, to stand very far apart and be extremely loud. You are a violation of that agreement: a large, dense, moving, silent thing.

- When you move, the alarm crosses the field of waders as a visible wave, faster than the rain wall, and subsides behind you. That wave is a public announcement of your position to every animal within a mile. You cannot sneak. You can only *stop.*
- The technique is furniture. Choose a spot near a seep or an upwelling, sit down before dawn, and do not move for three hours. The field re-accepts you. Then the plain happens around you, which is the only way it ever happens.
- Walk on bare crust and hard pavement. **Avoid the mats.** The stacked cushions and columns are millimetre-per-century constructions and a bootprint through one is a casual thousand-year vandalism. Walking through the shallow mats also makes the water fizz at your calves, which is one of the finest sensations available anywhere, and which is worth doing exactly once, deliberately, in a place already disturbed.
- Do not pour water on a resurrection fern to watch it unfurl. You will be tempted. It costs the plant a cycle it budgeted for rain.
- Do not climb a mound, alive or dead. Do not open one. The dead ones are housing.
- Give the big grazers three hundred metres. They are quiet, they are enormous, they walk on their own reflections, and they will not hear you over the frogs.

## 10. Prepare your mind, specifically

The two things that break people here are not dangers. They are **monotony** and **exposure of a kind that has no physical remedy.**

There is no horizon, so your eye never rests. There is no shade you did not carry, no cold, no privacy, no hiding place, no vertical, and no silence. There is a sky underfoot. The standing-in-the-sky effect is beautiful for a day and dissociative by the fourth; several travellers describe a persistent, low-grade sense of not being quite located in their own body, which is exactly what the terrain is doing to the sense of self and is exactly what the description warns you about when it says *you lose track of where you end.*

Countermeasures, all of them boring and all of them effective:

- **Go with one other person.** Not four, not alone. Two is the number: mandatory hourly cognitive checks (name the date, count backwards from a hundred by sevens), which is also your only real defence against silent hyperthermia, since the first symptom is that you stop being able to notice symptoms.
- **Keep a hard written log** with times. The place has no calendar — "gradients so weak that the animals do not schedule themselves by the year." You will lose the day count faster than you believe.
- **Impose ritual.** Foot inspection at the same hour. Water at the same hour. One page written before sleep. The plain has no structure; you supply it.
- **Carry one dense, cool, hard, human object** — a stone from home, a metal tool, a book with a stiff spine. Something with an edge, that was made, that is not the colour of the sky. People end up holding it.
- **Talk out loud**, even alone, even if it feels foolish. The insect shrill will otherwise be filed by your brain as silence, and true silence for a week in a place with no horizon does strange things.

And prepare, also, for the opposite: that you will not want to leave. The long empty shining stretches with one palm in them, or one bird, or nothing, are not emptiness — they are the plain's actual substance, and after a week you will begin to read them as *full.* That reading is correct. It is also very hard to give up.

---

## 11. Leaving

- Recover your marker. Recover every peg, tie, wrapper, fibre, and filter. Sweep your campsites on hands and knees.
- Carry out all waste, all of it.
- Decontaminate on the way out as thoroughly as you did on the way in — for your own world's sake, and because nothing about a closed, finished, undefended ecology should ever travel.
- Log your position and your route so that the next visitor can go somewhere else. A single repeated track across soft crust becomes a trail, and a trail catches silt, and silt is soil, and soil is the one thing this world has spent an unimaginable patience refusing.

Ten thousand years from now the frogs will be shouting from the same flats and the quartz veins will stand a hair less proud. Prepare so that the only trace of you is that you were, for a week, standing very far apart from everything, and reasonably quiet.
