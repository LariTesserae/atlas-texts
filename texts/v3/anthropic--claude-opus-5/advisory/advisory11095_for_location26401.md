# Preparing for the Standing Water

## First, the thing that will kill someone else

Two hundred people in a closed bowl at three thousand metres, with no road, no metal, and no immunological history that includes you. A head cold you would carry through your own week without comment can empty the high town.

So: quarantine before you cross. Not a token three days — two full weeks of isolation, with a second traveller checking you, because you are the worst judge of your own sore throat. Full vaccination against everything respiratory that exists in your home line. No arrival with a cough, a fever, a cold sore, a stomach upset, or a child. If you arrive on the northeast lip and find you are sniffing, turn round and walk back out. There will be another window. There are only two hundred of them.

Everything else in this document is about your comfort and survival. This part is about theirs, and it is not negotiable.

## The body you need

You are going to be a partly aquatic animal in twelve-degree water at an altitude where the air is two-thirds of what your lungs are used to. Give yourself six months. Nine is better.

**Cold habituation.** The single most useful thing you can do. Get into twelve- to fourteen-degree water three or four times a week, dressed as you will be dressed there — that is, in wet wool and grease, not neoprene — and stay in until you are shivering, and then get out and shiver properly instead of jumping into a hot shower. What you are training is not toughness. You are training away the cold shock response: the involuntary gasp on entry, which underwater is a drowning, and the panic-tachycardia that makes you surface badly. After twenty or thirty exposures the gasp stops. That gasp is the reason most visitors' first serious dive is their worst one.

**Breath-hold, conservatively.** You want to be comfortable and unhurried at fifteen metres so that you can work sanely at six. Static apnea tables, CO₂ tolerance work, and above all equalisation — hands-free or Frenzel, practised until it is boring, because the mid town is a place where you go up and down thirty times a day. A cold, blocked sinus disqualifies you from diving entirely; make peace with this before you go, because you will be congested a great deal and you will be tempted to push it. A reverse block at eight metres in a flooded room with a stone belt on is a very stupid way to end.

**Aerobic base and acclimatisation.** Arrive at altitude by stages if the crossing allows it. Two or three days at two thousand metres somewhere before you come up over the rim. Diving on the first day at three thousand metres, cold, dehydrated from the climb, is how travellers black out at four metres in front of people who have to fish them out.

**Dentistry, feet, skin.** Have your teeth done. There is no dentistry at the Standing Water and a hot molar in a cold wet place with no analgesia beyond what you brought is a route to going home early or not at all. Clear up any fungal infection on your feet before you leave: in permanent damp it will not clear up, it will spread and colonise. Cut your nails short and file them. Do not shave anything for a week before arrival — every nick is a door.

## Language, learned loudly

Learn the tongue the way it is actually used, which is: shouted across water, or spoken face-to-face with a jaw that is shaking.

Practise your vocabulary at volume. Water swallows speech; people here talk very loudly and stand very close, and a soft, careful accent reads as either illness or evasion. Then practise the twenty phrases you cannot afford to lose — *I am cold, I am going down, come up, help, I don't know, thank you, whose is this?* — while you are genuinely mildly hypothermic, because that is the state in which you will need them, and cold makes you slur and cold makes you stupid, in that order. Anything you know less than perfectly, you will not have.

**Learn the knocking before you learn the words.** At minimum: two-and-one, *come up*; the long roll, *a body in trouble*; the slow repeated triplet, *a birth, keep off the wood.* You must be able to recognise the long roll while asleep. And you must break, in advance, your species' habit of idly drumming on things. Every stem near a dwelling is a telephone. Tapping a paddle-spot because your hands are bored is not eccentricity, it is shouting nonsense down every wall of every house in a mile-wide town, and it is the first thing that makes visitors disliked. Bring no mallet. Do not tap. If someone eventually shows you a paddle-worn place and hands you a root mallet, that is a considerable event; treat it that way.

## Kit

The governing rules: nothing that outperforms local tools so obviously that it distorts them; nothing that cannot become part of a house; nothing that sinks.

**Bring**

- **Wool and greased felt.** Two sets of everything, both of which will be damp forever. Wool, not cotton, not linen. A greased felt overshirt cut for swimming in.
- **Lanolin and rendered fat, a lot of it.** For your shoulders, your ears, your seams. This is your insulation and your waterproofing and half your medicine.
- **Stone.** A cord belt you can hang river stones from, adjustable. Get your weighting right at home in fresh water with wool on.
- **Eyes.** This is the one real compromise. Underwater, unaided, the mid town is a green blur. A small low-volume mask is transformative and it is also the most covetable object anyone there has ever seen. If you can have goggles made with horn or wood rims and ground glass lenses — something that reads as an exotic trade good rather than a miracle — do that. Whatever you bring, bring two, expect to give one away, and expect that gift to have consequences you did not intend.
- **Obsidian.** Raw blanks and struck flakes. Obsidian already comes up over the rim; it is legible, valuable, and it changes nothing. This is your money. Also: beads (they will end up as caddis masonry, which is the correct fate for a bead), salt, and fat. Bring calories, because you are going to eat like a sled dog and the basin's larder is two hundred people's larder.
- **Water discipline.** The basin is the town's drink and the town's sewer and sixty metres of silt. Collect rain off the leaves in the afternoon into a gourd, filter through cloth, and treat it — chemically, in unmarked containers, quietly. Do not plan to boil. Fire here lives in clay bowls and is guarded like a small animal; asking for fire to boil water is asking for a real sacrifice.
- **Medical, honestly considered.** Antiseptic, iodine, acetic-acid ear drops (you will get otitis externa; everyone does), fungal cream, needle and thread, and a decision made in advance about antibiotics: whether you carry them, whether you use them on yourself only, and what you will say when someone's child has cellulitis. Make that decision at home. Do not make it at three in the morning under the frogs.
- **Floats and tethers.** Everything you own gets a gourd or a cord. A dropped knife is gone into thirty fathoms of silt in under a minute.
- **Wax earplugs**, understanding that you may only wear one.

**Leave behind**

Metal fish-hooks. Batteries and anything containing them — this is a closed basin whose water is drunk. Plastics. Nets. Recording devices: the whole acoustic surface of this place is private speech, and sitting under it with a machine is eavesdropping on a mile of houses at once. Any instrument for measuring the water level. They know the water is rising. They have known for four hundred years. You have nothing to add and a great deal to spoil.

## Arriving

Come in over the northeast lip, in the ankle-deep overflow, in the middle of the morning before the rain. Two reasons. First, you will be seen coming from a long way off, on the one path that everybody's grandmother also used, which is the polite way to appear. Second, you get the transition in the right direction: the noise arrives over about ten paces, and it is much better to walk into it than to be dropped into the middle of it.

The polished stone is exactly as slick as it looks. Barefoot or soft grip; do not come down that tooth-coloured sheet in hobnails.

Then sit down at the edge of the water and wait to be found. Do not swim out among the columns. The columns are somebody's walls.

## What the days are actually like

Nobody is ever warm. That is the shape of life here. You work in short bouts — ten, fifteen, twenty minutes in the water — and then you spend forty minutes shivering under felt with your hands in your armpits, next to other people doing the same, and this is when the talking happens. The rhythm is not diving-and-resting. It is diving-and-society. If you treat the shivering as downtime and go off to be alone with your notes, you will spend a month in the middle of a town and learn nothing.

You will be alternately the most examined object in the world and completely alone. Two hundred people in a bowl a mile and a quarter across is nobody. You can swim ten minutes and meet no one, and then round a stem into four women hauling a trap who all talk at once, at volume, and will still be talking at volume an hour later.

Sleep will be your worst problem for the first ten days. The frogs start at dusk and do not stop, and it is not a chorus, it is a scatter of hard reports, near ones cracking and far ones flattened, which is far harder to sleep through than any drone. Under it and through the wood is the knocking, which never finishes. Do not fight it. Do not drug yourself insensible on the second night. Take the insomnia, expect the habituation somewhere around day eight, and keep one ear clear for the long roll.
