# Going to the Throat: A Traveler's Preparation

## 0. First decide whether you are allowed to go

Not by their law — by your body's.

Do not go if you are pregnant, if you have any cardiac arrhythmia, if you have migraine with aura, if you have Ménière's or any vestibular disorder, if you have asthma or reactive airway, if you have an untreated perforation or tube in either eardrum, if you are prone to panic in enclosed spaces, or if you have ever had a dissociative episode. High-amplitude sound near sixty cycles is not "loud" in the way a factory is loud. It is a mechanical event that happens inside your chest wall and your abdomen and the jelly of your eyes. Your viscera will resonate. There are people for whom this is merely horrible and people for whom it is a medical emergency, and you cannot find out which you are at the bottom of a pit with no water in it.

Go to a good audiologist and get a baseline audiogram, both ears, out to 8 kHz, plus otoacoustic emissions. Keep a copy. You will want it afterward, and not for compensation — for knowing.

Understand and accept, before you commit a single day of travel, that **you will come back with less hearing than you left with, and with tinnitus you will have for the rest of your life.** The residents are stone deaf by fifty. You will not be there thirty years, but you cannot buy your way out of the tax entirely. Hearing protection has a hard ceiling: once you exceed roughly 130 dB, sound enters your cochlea through the bones of your skull no matter what is jammed in your ears. Plugs and muffs do not stop this. Nothing stops this. Your total exposure is a budget, it is spent in minutes, and it does not refill.

If you are already deaf, you are the ideal visitor to this place, and they will know it, and the terms of your welcome will be different and better. Note that you will still take the whole-body dose — the chest, the gut, the eyes — and you should read the rest of this as written for you too.

## 1. Timing

Ask, at the plain's edge, whether the seventh-harmonic cells are coming into quiet. They come twice a year, for about nine days, and they are used for dying in. If your visit overlaps, either postpone or arrive with the explicit understanding that you will be nowhere near the fourth terrace and will not ask what is happening there. A stranger gawking at the two rooms where people go to die is the sort of thing that gets a city to decide it does not host strangers anymore.

Beyond that: go in the cool season if there is one. The drift is a function of air temperature, so a cooler year is a slower, gentler, more forgiving lattice, and the shifts are less frantic.

## 2. The body you must arrive in

- **Heat acclimatization**: two full weeks of daily exertion in dry heat before departure, until you sweat early and sweat dilute. This is not optional. The floor of the pit bakes bread.
- **Vestibular and balance work**: you will get vertigo out in the loud (loud sound producing eye-flicker and a sense of the world tilting is a real and common phenomenon), and you will get it while standing on a reed-stem bridge over a sixty-pace drop. The actual cause of death for outsiders here is not deafness or gas. It is falling.
- **Rope work**: be genuinely competent with a harness, a short tether, a prusik, and soft anchors — in gloves, in glare, in noise, without being able to hear a partner or yourself.
- **Silence practice**: spend some hours, before you go, in an anechoic chamber or the deepest quiet you can find. The stills are not quiet rooms. They are the sudden and total subtraction of the world. People who have never heard their own carotid pulse before tend to meet it badly, and meeting it badly for the first time in a stranger's house, three paces from a drop, at forty degrees, is poor planning. Learn what your interior sounds like on your own time.
- **Diet discipline, starting a week out**: in a still, your body is the only sound there is. Digestion is a public performance. Drop legumes, dairy, and carbonation well before arrival. This is not a joke; it is hospitality.

## 3. The single most important thing in this document

**You can very easily destroy this city with a spark.**

Consider what is actually in the pit: free hydrogen in the exhalation. Terpene sap in every stem, volatile and fragrant, with vapor collecting under the noon in still corners. Drifts of fine elemental sulfur lying along every ledge like pollen — and fine sulfur dust in air is not merely flammable, it is a classic dust explosion. Dry insulating gypsum dust everywhere. Forty-one lives in an unventilated stone bell with one exit, sixty paces up.

And you, arriving in a synthetic jacket, scuffing across dry dust, carrying lithium cells.

So:

- **No synthetics.** Nothing that generates static: no fleece, no nylon shell, no polypropylene base layer, no plastic pack fabric. Wear cotton and linen and leather, ideally garments woven with conductive thread, and wear a dissipative wrist strap or a trailing grounding tail that touches stone as you move. Ground yourself deliberately, with the back of your hand to bare rock, before you touch anything of theirs.
- **Declare and surrender every ignition source at the lip.** Matches, striker, lighter, ferro rod, magnifying lens, anything pyrotechnic. Do this in the open, visibly, into someone's hands. They will have a procedure. Follow it exactly and do not attempt to retain a "safety" backup — the backup is what kills everyone.
- **Electronics**: if you bring any, bring the smallest possible energy in the safest possible chemistry, in a sealed, vented, non-sparking enclosure, and declare each item. Assume you may be asked to leave all of it topside. Plan a trip in which that is acceptable. If you cannot do the trip without a battery, you are not ready to do the trip.
- **No tools of steel.** Bring beryllium-copper or aluminum-bronze if you bring tools at all. A steel chisel struck on silica-stiffened reedwood throws sparks.
- **No paper, no cardboard, no loose fiber.** Wax tablet and stylus, or a slate.

If you internalize nothing else, internalize this section. Everything else on this list risks you. This risks them.

## 4. Kit

### Ears
Deep-insertion custom-molded plugs *plus* over-ear muffs, worn together, all the time, from the last day of the approach onward. Bring three sets of plugs; you will lose one and one will crack in the heat. Understand that plugs are nearly useless below 125 Hz — you are protecting against the harmonic stack and the shriek, which is what actually burns out a cochlea, while the fundamental goes straight through you regardless.

Bring a **noise dosimeter** if you bring anything electronic at all, and treat its number as the real clock of your trip. It, not the sun, tells you how much visit you have left.

### Eyes
Glacier goggles with side shields, very dark, plus a spare pair. The gypsum plain delivers the sun twice, from above and from underfoot; the burn is on the underside of your chin, the insides of your nostrils, the roof of your mouth if you walk with it open. Sunblind on that plain is a death sentence because you cannot find the lip and you cannot find your way off. Inside the pit the selenite windows will still cook your eyes over days, and hydrogen sulfide causes a specific, painful, gritty conjunctivitis that the locals simply live with.

### Lungs
A half-mask with P100 particulate filters *and* acid-gas cartridges, plus many spare filters — silica dust from reedwood work, gypsum dust, sulfur dust, and H₂S. Know the critical fact about hydrogen sulfide: **above roughly 100 ppm it paralyzes your sense of smell.** The stink going away is not good news. It is the last warning you will get. Carry an H₂S monitor if you carry anything, know that high concentrations poison the sensor, and bring two. Carry an oxygen/CO₂ meter as well. The pit exhales mostly carbon dioxide; it rises because it is hot, but it does not rise everywhere, and there are dead corners, old honeycombed cells, and the cool hours before dawn when it lies down.

Never enter a disused still on a low terrace. Never. Those plastered drums are exactly the shape of a gas trap and they are full of eight hundred years of nothing.

### Heat and water
There is no water in that country. None. Not underground, not in the plants, not in the air to condense. Everything you drink, you carry or you cache.

Plan on 1 to 1.5 litres an hour of active loss. A two-day crossing is fifteen to twenty litres per person, and that is before you live there. This is not a solo carry; it is a cart, a beast, a cached depot, or a supply arrangement through whatever door you came in by. Work the arithmetic honestly and then add a day. Salt and electrolyte in everything — sweating that hard on plain water will drop your sodium and confuse you, and confusion is how you walk off a lip.

### Ropes
Harness, short tether, two lanyards, soft slings, non-sparking hardware. **You clip in whenever you are out in the loud.** Always. Also: their bridges are laid reed-stem lashed by hand, sized for small, spare, permanently underfed people. You are almost certainly heavier than anyone in the city, and with a pack you may be twice a resident's mass. Ask, every single time, before you put your weight on anything. Ask in signs, and wait for the answer.

### The no-resonance rule
Anything hollow that you carry into that bell becomes an organ pipe, and an organ pipe that wasn't there yesterday detunes houses. Cap or fill every tube on your pack frame. No open-mouthed bottles, no hollow trekking poles, no instrument, no drinking horn, no empty canister. Solid gear only, or plugged.

And pack **quiet**. This is the inversion nobody anticipates: out in the roar nothing you carry makes a sound, and then you step over a threshold into absolute silence and your jacket is a thunderstorm. No velcro. No crinkling wrappers. No jingling hardware. Tape your buckles.

### The alarm
Buy, borrow, or improvise a shallow drum with sulfur sand on the skin, exactly as they use. Have it by your head every time you sleep. If you have any electronics left, build a redundant backup: a sound-level sensor that vibrates a band on your wrist above a threshold. Two of them.

This is the classic way a visitor dies or is ruined: you fall asleep in a null in the cool hour, the node walks out through the window, and you wake — if you wake — into a hundred and thirty decibels at point-blank range with your ears unstopped. They say it has been fatal. They say, worse, that it has been permanent. Believe both.

## 5. The crossing

Hire a guide. There is no version of this where you find the place yourself. The plain throws the roar off its own polished face; hot ground bends the sound upward into shadow zones; at midday the noise appears to fall out of the entire white sky. You will walk toward it for two days and arrive nowhere.

Cross at night and in the hour before dawn, when the air inverts and the sound lies down along the ground and tells the truth. Lie up under shade cloth through the middle of the day. Understand that this means you will be moving in the dark across a floor that ends without a rim, without a hill, without a single warning stone — so the last approach is made at low sun, when a lip finally casts a line of shadow, roped together, the lead probing with a staff.

Your guide will pull their plugs for a count of three, every hundred paces, to taste the gradient. Do not imitate this. They are spending hearing they have already decided to spend. Their nose will bleed by evening. Let them do their work and keep your ears stopped.

## 6. Arriving

**Do not arrive unsponsored.** Forty-one rooms. Forty-one people. There is no spare quiet. Every hour you spend in a still is an hour someone else spends out in the thunder, and for an old resident that may be an hour they could not afford.

So: go first to the settlement at the plain's edge, stay days, be seen, learn some signs, and get someone to speak for you. Arrive as a known thing. Arrive having already asked the question *what does my being here cost you, and what can I bring that pays it.*

And come prepared to spend most of your time out in the loud, so that you take as little quiet as possible. Your superior hearing protection is precisely what lets you be a light guest rather than a heavy one. This is the whole ethic of visiting here in one sentence: **bring your own silence, or don't come.**

## 7. Living on the drift

Your day will not have hours. It will have temperatures. The lattice tracks the speed of sound, the speed of sound tracks the air, and so the city's whole calendar is thermal. A cell good at dawn is thunder by mid-morning; the cell three paces down and out has inherited the quiet. People move all day, carrying bedding, following the nulls around the inner face like beads of mercury tipping on a plate.

Practically:

- Sleep is broken into pieces. Expect four or five short sleeps. Accept it; do not fight it with stimulants in that heat.
- Plan in the quiet, execute in the loud. You will discover that you cannot think sequentially out in the roar — the noise eats working memory, reading, arithmetic, and planning, and at sixty cycles your eyeballs are vibrating so hard you cannot focus on anything smaller than a hand. Write your task list on your forearm in a still and go out and do it by feel.
- The transitions are the dangerous minutes. Going in: the world shuts off like a hand closing, your ears sing, your balance lies, and you will stagger. Going out: the wall of sound hits like a physical shove and people step backward off things. Move slowly through every threshold, with a hand on stone, clipped.
- Do not attempt to make your own quiet. Your fancy hearing protection works on your ears. It does not work on your lungs or your gut or your inner ear, and the local rooms are not soundproof in any sense you would recognize — they are thin plastered shells that work only because the lattice has already cancelled the pressure that would drive them. Off the node, a still is just a drum you are inside of.

## 8. Manners

**The stills are the only privacy that exists in this world.** Speech in a still stays in the still. That is not a curiosity; it is the founding fact of the settlement and the reason anyone tolerates the noise. Everything a human being needs to say in secret — grief, confession, bargaining, love, treason, dying — happens in those little plastered drums. The rest of life is conducted in bright light, in enormous visible hand signs, across gaps, in front of everyone.

Therefore:

- Never cross a threshold uninvited. Wait at the lashing and be seen and wait.
- Never, ever repeat outside a still something said inside it. Not to another resident, not in signs, not in your journal, not at home afterward with names changed. If you cannot promise this absolutely, stay out of the stills, and say so at the lip, and they will respect you for it. Note also that you may be told things *precisely because* you are leaving and taking them out of the world. Treat that as the most serious trust anyone has ever given you.
- Do not take a child into a deep still, and do not leave one alone in one.
- The seventh-harmonic cells are for dying. Do not go near them, do not photograph or sketch them, do not ask who is in them.
- **Do not touch the reedwood.** Not a leaf, not a stem, not a lean, not a snapped-off souvenir. Every hollow stem is a pipe in a gale and the garden's collective shriek is the upper half of the city's note. One cut thicket slides the whole harmonic stack and empties eleven houses. Pruning here is regulated more tightly than marriage, and it is decided by deaf old men arguing in signs about a hand's length of stem, and they are right and you are not.
- The blind six-legged thing gripping a stem out in the thunder: leave it. It eats the borers. It never enters a still. It is not dangerous to you and you are extremely dangerous to it.
- Do not carry a borer moth, a grub, or an egg out of the pit — check your kit, seams, straps, and boot welts on departure. And bring nothing in: no seed, no soil, no insect, no fruit. A rival organism in that garden would be the end of the city, and a borer loose in some other world would be your doing.

**Learn the signs before you go.** Their language of the hands is the main language: large, high-contrast, made to be read across three paces of empty air in blinding light. Their elders are deaf from childhood habit and thirty years of the roar and cannot lipread a foreigner's mouth. Carry a wax tablet and stylus, because scratched marks on plaster are the other half of their communication, and they will find a tablet perfectly natural.

Also be ready for the thing they will find uncanny about you: you are an adult who can still hear. Depending on age and manner, that will read as childlike, or as miraculous, or as faintly obscene.

## 9. Eating, washing, waste

Bring all your own calories. A forty-one-person city's food supply has no slack in it, and the grubs are their only meat. If you are offered roasted borer grubs — resinous, salty — eat them, thank them properly, and do not ask for more. The bread comes off the hot black floor-stones near the Throat with a taste of struck matches; take it.

Pack food that needs no water and no fire: fats, nut pastes, cured things you can chew dry, sugars. Anything freeze-dried is dead weight in a country with nothing to reconstitute it with.

There is no washing. None. Bring a sensible amount of dry cloth and unscented oil and accept being filthy. Bring sealable bags and **carry out everything you produce.** Do not foul a terrace and do not put anything organic or combustible down the Throat.

Sulfur dust plus sweat is mildly acidic and will chew the creases of your body. Gaiters, long sleeves, powder your folds, check your feet daily, and mind the smallest abrasion, because there is no water to clean a wound with.

## 10. Gifts, and thinking hard about them

They have copper soft enough to sharpen on a stone, lime plaster, lashings, and two centuries of measuring the invisible with sand. They are not poor; they are exactly matched to their circumstances. Be very careful about what you hand them.

Good gifts, in rough order:

- **Non-sparking tools.** Beryllium-copper and aluminum-bronze chisels, files, and picks. Hard, fine, and safe in the atmosphere. This is the one obviously right gift: it makes the masons' work easier without changing the shape of their world.
- **Water.** Plainly.
- **Hearing protection for the surveyors.** Good custom plugs for the young ones who must pull the plugs to check that the sand is not lying. Understand what you are doing, though: this pushes deafness back by years, and their entire social structure rests on the young hearing and the old not. You would be handing them a change to their generations. Hand it over and let them decide; do not decide for them.
- **A slate, wax tablets, styluses.**

Gifts to think about for a long time before offering:

- **A thermometer and a table** letting anyone predict node position from air temperature. This is a trivial object and it could gut a two-hundred-year craft in a generation. Maybe they should have it. Maybe it should be their choice made by their elders and not a present you leave at the lip like a coin.
- **Any electronic acoustic instrument.** Same argument, more so, plus a battery you are leaving in a room full of hydrogen.

Bad gifts, absolutely: steel, fire-making, alcohol, anything hollow, anything that grows, anything with a resonant cavity, and anything you would not be willing to still be responsible for in a hundred years.

## 11. If it goes wrong

- **You wake to the sand dancing**: plugs in first, muffs second, then move. In that order, always, even if the room is already roaring. Ears first, then feet.
- **You lose your node with nowhere to go**: get low, get your back into a wall pocket or an old honeycombed shell *above* floor level, protect your ears, and wait to be found. Do not wander in the loud looking for quiet; you cannot navigate by ear when the ear is saturated, and everything here is a ledge.
- **The stink of rotten eggs stops**: hold your breath, get high, get out. Your nose has been switched off, not the gas.
- **Fire**: there is nothing to fight it with. There is no plan. There is only the sixty paces up and the rope, and you will be climbing through burning terpene. This is why section 3 is section 3.
- **Somebody falls or is hurt**: know in advance that there is no evacuation. Two days of gypsum in either direction, no water, and a patient you would have to carry. Bring a real trauma kit, know how to use it, and know that its actual purpose here is to make an outcome bearable rather than to prevent one.

## 12. Coming home

Get out at dawn, roped, following your guide. Repeat the audiogram within a week and again at three months.

Expect the tinnitus. Expect, for a while, that ordinary rooms will seem to be humming. Expect — this is the part nobody warns about, and the residents know it perfectly well, which is why they say people come out of a deep still with their expressions rearranged — that you will have spent hours sitting in a two-metre plastered drum with nothing in it but your own pulse and the grinding of your jaw and a high pure tone that belongs to your ear and not to the world, and that this will have told you some things about yourself that you had arranged your entire life to avoid being told.

Arrange, in advance, for someone to be waiting on the other side of the door. Not to hear about the trip. Just to be there, making an ordinary amount of noise.
