# Preparing to Visit the Bubble in the Nine-Week Rock

## 0. The first decision, made at home, before anything is packed

You cannot go in. Settle this before you spend money.

The room is 340 °C in its coolest working gas and 600 °C in the floor seams. It is pressurised and depressurised every thirty-eight minutes by a column of magma. Its atmosphere is steam, carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen fluoride, in a volume of roughly two and a half cubic metres, of which perhaps a third is occupied by rubble and another third by insects. A single unprotected breath there would close your larynx and cook the tissue behind it; the fluoride would finish what the heat began, over the following two days, by stripping the calcium out of your blood.

So "visiting" here means one of three things, and you must choose which one you are actually preparing for:

**A. The remote visit.** You go to the mountain, you live on the dome for thirty to forty hours, you put a 50 mm hole into the pocket and you put your eyes, ears and instruments down the hole. Your body stays outside in the snow. This is the visit that is actually available, and it is the one this document assumes.

**B. The cooled-shell visit.** You go in bodily inside an actively cooled hard capsule, having first enlarged the entry to something a human can pass. This requires removing perhaps a cubic metre of the dome, destroying the eastern taper, venting the pocket, killing the whistle permanently, and collapsing the thermal margin in which every living thing here lives. You would get four to eight minutes. You would see, through a fogged port, a room you had already ruined. I list it only so you can reject it in writing.

**C. The patient visit.** You wait. Year five: the seams go black. Year twelve: the engine is dead, the gas is gone, the pocket is cold, dry and silent, and you can crawl in wearing ordinary overalls with a headtorch and touch the drips with a bare hand. Everything that made the place itself will be gone — the sound, the breathing, the deposition, the four hundred beetles — and you will be standing in a black glass room full of dead flies. Some travellers prefer this. It is honest about what it is: a visit to a corpse, which is what most geology is.

The rest of this is for A.

---

## 1. When

The crust hardened in October and the flow has been dead for nine weeks. You are arriving in late December or early January, at 4,100 metres, on the northeast flank. That is midwinter on a high cold mountain that is also, in patches, 200 °C underfoot.

You will be working in wind-driven snow on ground that melts it. Expect permanent fog: the dome sublimes its own weather. Expect to lose sight of your own gear at four metres. Expect the snow to be acidic enough to pit a watch glass overnight, and to turn your teeth rough if you let it blow into your mouth.

Do not schedule this for "a day." Budget two weeks on the mountain for three working days on the dome.

---

## 2. The body

**Altitude.** 4,100 m is about 62 kPa. You will have roughly 62 % of the oxygen you are used to, while wearing 30 kg of equipment and operating a drill. Acclimatise properly: ten to fourteen days staged, sleeping progressively higher, with at least three nights above 3,500 m before you set foot on the dome. Acetazolamide from day one if you tolerate it. Learn your own Lake Louise score and be ruthless about it — the single most common way this trip fails is not the volcano, it is a competent person making a confident bad decision at 4,100 m with a headache they have decided to ignore.

Note that you will be breathing bottled gas for most of the working period anyway. Do not let this fool you into thinking you are acclimatised. When you take the mask off to eat, the mountain comes back.

**Hearing.** The 30 Hz fundamental enters through the rock, not the air, and once your hole is open, the 800 Hz whistle will have a direct path to your head for nineteen minutes in every thirty-eight. Bring plugs *and* muffs and wear both. Understand that the low end is not stopped by either — you will feel it in your sternum and in the fluid of your eyes, and after a few hours your vision will smear slightly at the edges and you will feel mildly, chemically nauseous for no reason you can point to. This is normal. It is not altitude sickness and it is not fear. Rest for one full cycle out of every four, downslope, behind rock.

**Skin and lungs.** Assume you will be dosed. Not badly, but dosed. Pre-trip: no facial hair of any kind, because nothing seals against stubble; trimmed nails; no contact lenses, ever, for the duration. Bring your own prescription inserts for the mask. Treat any open cut on your hands as a disqualifying injury until it is closed, because fluoride finds them.

**Fitness.** The specific fitness required is the ability to stand still, in a mask, holding a drill steady, on a 20° slope of hot loose glass, for nineteen minutes at a time, for ten hours. That is not cardiovascular fitness. Train it as isometric work.

## 3. The mountain will kill you before the pocket does

This must be said plainly, because everyone fixates on the hole and forgets the roof.

A nine-week-old dome on a mountain "still deciding its own shape" is not a landform, it is an event in progress. It is oversteepened, gas-charged, and loaded with a cooled carapace over a plastic interior. Its failure mode is not rockfall; it is sudden collapse of a flank into a block-and-ash flow moving at 30–100 m/s with an internal temperature of several hundred degrees. There is no escape from one on foot. There is no shelter from one behind a boulder. Survivability at your working position is zero.

Therefore your preparation is entirely *avoidance*:

- Get the seismic record. You want at least three weeks of continuous data and someone who can read it. Look for rockfall signal counts, for the drumbeat tremor that says the dome is extruding, for any change in the 38-minute period.
- Establish the current extrusion rate and where the active lobe is. Work on the oldest, coldest, most buttressed part of the dome you can reach that still gets you over the pocket. Eleven metres is eleven metres; the pocket does not care which azimuth you come from.
- Post an observer, off the dome, with line of sight and a radio, whose *only* job is to watch and call. Agree the abort word in advance and agree that it is never discussed, never questioned, and never proven right or wrong afterwards.
- Know your wind. All acid gas from the borehole and from every fumarole on the dome goes downwind. Work upwind. Have a cross-slope retreat that does not require you to go down a gully.
- Probe the ground ahead of you with a steel rod as you walk. Nine-week dome crust hides voids. Falling through into a 400 °C cavity with your legs is how people die here, and it kills slowly, in a helicopter, hours later.

**Footwear:** rigid-soled mountaineering boots with a bolted-on aluminium or ceramic-fibre oversole. Standard rubber-soled boots will soften and skin off on hot ground within minutes and then you are barefoot at 4,100 m in December.

## 4. The hole

This is the technical heart of the trip. Treat it as a small well, not a drilling job.

**Geometry.** Do not drill vertically down into the crown. Directly beneath a vertical hole is the ceiling — the field of frozen drips, mirror-smooth on the underside, hair-fine at the tips. It is the best thing in this world and it is the only thing in this world that a single dropped cutting can destroy irrecoverably. Angle the hole to intersect the eastern half of the pocket near the taper, so that it breaks through into wall, above the rubble floor. Cuttings will then fall onto rubble that collapsed in the first hours anyway. You will be adding to a mess rather than making one.

**Method.** Air-flush diamond coring, 50 mm. No water. Water is forbidden, absolutely, and not for sentimental reasons — a water-flushed hole breaking into a 340 °C pressurised gas pocket gives you a steam explosion up the annulus and into your face, and it puts liquid into a world whose first rule is that there is none. Use a vortex-cooled air supply and accept that you will burn bits. Bring four times as many as you think you need. Expect 1–2 m/hour in glass, slower in rubble zones, and expect to lose the hole at least once and have to offset and restart.

**Casing and valve.** Case as you go with Inconel or high-nickel tubing. Set a mechanical packer with graphite seals at 8–9 m, above the breakthrough point, and terminate at surface in a valve. You are building a wellhead. When the bit punches through, you want to be able to close the pocket again on command, with a quarter-turn, from three metres to the side.

**Timing the breakthrough.** Use the engine as your clock, because it is a better clock than your watch and it is the only one that matters. The cycle: roughly nineteen minutes of outflow (whistle at 800 Hz, steady), then the slide down a whole tone and the stop, then three or four minutes of cracking, then intake. **Punch through during intake.** The pocket is drawing at that point, pressure is at its minimum, and your first contact will be a suck rather than a jet. Have the geophone coupled to the rock and a set of headphones on your driller's assistant: they will hear the tone quit through the ground a second or two before anyone feels anything, and that second is your signal.

If you get it wrong and break through on the stroke, you will have a 340 °C acid-gas jet out of a 50 mm pipe at chest height. Nobody stands in line with the hole. Ever. Not for a moment. Rig the drill so it can be operated from the side, and rig a deflector plate before you are anywhere near depth.

## 5. Instruments, and the particular problem of looking at this place

Everything you send down will be attacked by heat, by acid, and — the one nobody plans for — by deposition.

**The lens will grow crystals.** Ammonium chloride sublimes at about 338 °C. Your borescope window will be cooler than the gas by definition, which means it is a nucleation site, which means it will frost white with sal ammoniac in seconds. Solution: a dry nitrogen purge flowing continuously past the objective, at a rate high enough to keep gas off the glass and low enough that you are not blowing a two-metre jet into the sublimate gardens and knocking down the fans. Practise setting this rate on a bench before you leave. You will have about one chance to get it right.

**Bring:**

- A 4 mm fibre borescope with a sapphire window, a nitrogen sheath, and at least six metres of usable insertion length. A second one, complete, in a sealed case. The second one is not a spare, it is the actual instrument; the first one is the one you will destroy learning.
- A cooled-sensor stills camera on a rigid probe, for long exposure. The red seams are at 600 °C, which is very deep red indeed — around 700–800 nm, at the ragged edge of human sensitivity. A long exposure will show you the floor almost exactly as a human eye would fail to. Consider also shooting with the near-IR filter removed, to see the room as the beetles effectively "see" it, and understand that this image is a translation and not a photograph.
- A thermal camera. This is the honest imaging tool here. The pocket is a heat landscape, not a light landscape, and thermal is the only sense in which the place is actually bright.
- A probe microphone: a thin Inconel waveguide tube down the hole to a capsule outside in the cold. Also a rock-coupled geophone for the 30 Hz and a wideband acoustic emission sensor for the cracking. Record every cycle you can, continuously. You are listening to the beginning of a thirty-year decay and every cycle is fractionally quieter than the last; a long unbroken recording is the single most valuable thing you can bring home.
- Gas sampling: evacuated Giggenbach bottles with caustic solution, a drawn-in condensate line kept above 200 °C to the last possible moment, and alkaline filter packs for HF/HCl. Personal monitors on every body for SO2, H2S, CO, O2 — and know that no personal monitor made will warn you about HF at the concentrations that matter.
- A fine-mesh sleeve that can be fitted over the wellhead. What comes out will not only be gas.

**On light:** the crystal gardens on the west wall have never been lit. If you illuminate them, that fact ends, and it cannot be restored. Whether this matters is a question about you and not about the rock. If it does matter, there is a defensible compromise: image the gardens thermally and by touch-probe profilometry, and reserve visible light for the ceiling and the floor. Some travellers deliberately expose exactly one photograph of the west wall and then never look at the rest. I have no strong view. I only ask that you decide before you are standing there with your thumb on the lamp.

## 6. What will come out of the hole at you

Prepare your people for this or they will run.

The moment the pocket is open and the stroke reverses to outflow, your 50 mm tube becomes the lowest-resistance path out of a room containing four hundred fire-chaser beetles and several hundred thousand ash flies. The flies come first and they come as a **column** — a dense grey rope of insects standing out of the pipe into the snow, going instantly quiet and dropping dead in a cone around the wellhead, because the outside air is minus fifteen. Within an hour you will be working in an ankle-deep drift of them. They stick to the frost on your mask.

The beetles are slower to find it and much worse to have. *Melanophila* are drawn to infrared and to hot metal, and you are the warmest thing on that slope. They will land on your hood and your gloves and crawl into any fold, they will pack into the joint between your glove cuff and sleeve, and they bite. They are ten millimetres long, hard, and extremely determined, and there is no repellent that means anything to an animal that flies *toward* fire. Preparation: smooth outer shell garments with taped cuffs, no webbing, no loops, no velcro, nothing for a tarsal claw to hook. A wide-brimmed shield over the mask's exhaust so they are not drawn to your own warm breath. And a settled understanding, agreed in advance, that a beetle inside your suit is not an emergency and you do not break the seal for it.

Fit the mesh sleeve. Not to collect specimens — though you should, three vials, no more — but because a wellhead that is 60 % blocked with insect bodies will change the whistle, and you want your recording to be of the pocket and not of your own interference.

## 7. Contamination, both directions

**Into you.** Standard high-hazard practice: full-face positive-pressure supplied air with an escape bottle, hooded suit, double gloves, boot covers, and a decontamination line set up thirty metres upwind before the first hole is drilled. Everything comes off in one order, every time, and someone watches. Sulphur and chlorides get into fabric and out-gas in a warm tent at night; bag it outside. **Calcium gluconate 2.5 % gel in the kit, at least six tubes, and someone who has actually rehearsed applying it.** HF burns do not hurt at first. That is the whole problem with them. Any unexplained tingling in a fingertip is treated as fluoride until proven otherwise, and "proven otherwise" happens in a hospital and not on a dome.

**Into it.** Harder, and more permanent.

This pocket has no artifact in it and never has had. Rule Three is the strangest fact about the place — that the hexagonal columns of the east wall *look* made and are not, and that in the entire history of this small world nothing has ever been placed, dropped, lost or left. You are about to end that condition, and you should be clear-eyed that you cannot avoid it. A 50 mm steel tube through the wall is an artifact. So is the powder of your own cuttings on the rubble. So is one nylon fibre off your sleeve, which will not decay, which will be sealed in cold glass in thirty years, and which will still be there in eleven thousand when the landslide opens the roof for ninety seconds.

What you can do:

- Use metal, glass and ceramic down the hole. No polymers past the packer. No lubricants. No tape.
- Never let anything ungrasped enter the pipe. Everything on a tether, every tether load-tested, every tether counted in and counted out, on a written list, by a second person.
- When you leave, retrieve the borescope, retrieve the mic tube, and plug the casing with a machined graphite bung. Do not attempt to seal it into invisibility. You have made a hole; pretending otherwise is worse than the hole.
- Accept that you have shifted the margin. Cold mountain air now enters the pocket through your casing during every intake stroke. The survivable shell, which was creeping inward at about a centimetre a month, will now creep at some slightly different rate on the eastern side. The animals will follow it, as they follow everything, without knowing. You have changed the migration path of an entire population and you will never know by how much.

## 8. Psychological preparation, which is not optional here

Three things catch people out.

**The first is that the place is loud and nothing moves.** Your instinct will insist that a 30 Hz roar and a nineteen-minute whistle and a percussive ceiling mean something is happening, and nothing is happening. The only movement in the entire mineral world is the smallest sublimate towers drifting like grass. Everything else changes on a decade. You will find yourself waiting, physically braced, for an event that the room is not going to provide, and after six hours of bracing you will be exhausted in a way you cannot account for.

**The second is the poverty of remote presence.** You will never smell this room. You will never feel its shape with your hands or know what the drips sound like when you say a word under them. You will look at it down a four-millimetre tube on a screen in a tent, through a nitrogen purge, in false colour, with your ears full of foam. This is a genuine loss and it does not get easier by pretending it isn't. Go in expecting to feel cheated at the exact moment of success.

**The third is the arithmetic of thirty years.** This world is nine weeks old. It will be dead before you are. Nothing you do can extend it, nothing you do can preserve it, and nothing you record will be verifiable by anyone who comes later, because there will be nothing to come to. You are visiting the only interesting phase of an object that has one. If that lands on you badly on the second night, at altitude, in a tent, with the whistle coming up through the ground under your sleeping mat every thirty-eight minutes — and it does, and it lands badly on most people — then know in advance that it is coming, and know that it lifts by morning.

## 9. A note on arrival and departure vectors

If you are able to choose an insertion point, **do not insert into the cavity.** Two and a half cubic metres cannot absorb a human body without a pressure transient, and in any case you would be dead before you had catalogued a single sensation. Insert on the snow, on a stable older lobe, at least four hundred metres from the active front, and walk.

More importantly: **do not anchor your return point inside the pocket.** In thirty years this is a sealed void in cold glass eleven metres inside a mountain. An anchor there is an anchor in solid rock, and everything that implies. Anchor it low on the flank, below the dome, on bedrock that predates the eruption — and confirm before you leave that the bedrock you chose was not, in fact, emplaced last spring. Mountains that are still deciding their own shape are careless about which parts of themselves are old.

## 11. The last preparation

Before you drill, sit on the dome for one full cycle and do nothing.

Thirty-eight minutes. You will not hear the whistle from outside; it never leaves the room. You will feel the 30 Hz in your boots, on and off, on and off, and you will be sitting on eleven metres of two-month-old rock with a lit room under it that has never been lit, packed wall to wall with four hundred beetles and a hundred thousand flies and an orange felt of mites, all of them slowly migrating inward, a centimetre a month, after a warmth that is leaving.

Then decide, one more time, whether to put the hole in.

Either answer is defensible. Only one of them is reversible.
