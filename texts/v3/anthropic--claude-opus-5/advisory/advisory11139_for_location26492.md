# Preparing for the Shelf of Combs

## First, understand what kind of place this is

It is not a destination that rewards effort. There is no summit here, nothing to reach, nothing to bring back. The shelf will not test you in any way that produces a story. It will simply keep the conditions it keeps, and you will be inside them, badly adapted, for a few days.

Almost everything that can kill you here is quiet and unspectacular: hypoxia, sunlight, the edge in fog, and the very ordinary business of not being able to call for help. Meanwhile almost everything you can destroy, you can destroy by accident in half a second, and it will take three hundred years to come back.

Prepare, therefore, for two separate disciplines. One is keeping yourself alive in the death zone. The other is being an unusually careful guest in a place where nothing decays.

---

## The air

Take the sound seriously as a measurement. A shout dying to a whisper in thirty paces is not atmosphere; that is roughly a fifth of sea-level density, perhaps less. You are arriving somewhere in the vicinity of 12,000–15,000 metres of equivalent pressure. An unacclimatised human dropped there is unconscious inside a quarter of an hour and dead within the day.

**Do not gate directly onto the shelf from a sea-level world.** If your transit is fixed and you cannot stage, then you must arrive already on oxygen, mask sealed, flowing, before the transition — treat it as a summit push out of a pressurised aircraft, not as a walk through a door.

Better: stage. Spend five to six weeks in an intermediate world at 4,500–5,500 m, with the usual rotations, and then treat the shelf itself as an extreme-altitude excursion of no more than three or four days. Even fully acclimatised you will not be *comfortable*; you will be functional.

**Bring:**

- Supplemental oxygen with a wide margin. Budget 1–2 L/min at rest and sleeping, 3–4 L/min for any movement through the grove, and then double the total. Cold kills regulators. Carry two, and keep the spare in your jacket against your body.
- A closed-circuit or high-efficiency system if you can get one; the mass savings matter more than they usually do, because the approach is described by the people who made it as *a very hard climb*.
- Acetazolamide as standing prophylaxis; dexamethasone and nifedipine in the emergency kit; a portable hyperbaric bag if you have anyone to carry it.
- A heat-and-moisture-exchange mask for any period off oxygen. The air is not only thin, it is savagely dry. Your lungs will lose litres a day to it.

Expect your judgement to be poor and know that you will not notice. Write your decisions down in advance — turnaround times, fog protocol, weather triggers — and obey the paper rather than yourself.

## The light

Two suns. The one above is violet-edged and unfiltered through a very short column of air; the one below is a two-thousand-foot cloud-sea returning almost everything it receives. You will be irradiated from every direction at once, including from beneath.

- **Glacier glasses are not sufficient.** You need wraparound Category 4 lenses with solid side shields *and* a lower shield or a deep nose-piece. The reflected component comes up under ordinary frames and burns the sclera. Carry two spare pairs; a broken lens on day one ends the visit.
- Snow blindness from below has a distinct pattern and a distinct cruelty: it develops while you feel adequately protected.
- Sunburn will appear in unlikely places — the underside of the chin and jaw, the septum and inner nostrils, the roof of the mouth if you breathe with it open, the undersides of the eyelids and brow. Use a heavy zinc-oxide barrier, not a chemical screen, and apply it to the places you would never think to.
- Lip protection with high UV block, applied hourly. Lips split, then bleed, then freeze.
- **The shadowlessness is a mobility hazard.** Relief vanishes. You cannot see a six-inch step, a hole in the cemented floor, or a change of angle. Carry a trekking pole or a light probe and use it to read the ground constantly, the way a blind person does. On the open rim, walk with the probe ahead of you every time.
- The apparent hovering of standing figures is harmless. The false horizon of the convex cloud-sea is not. Do not stand near the drop looking outward for long; people lean. If you want to look at the white, sit down first.

## Cold, damp, and the twice-daily tide

The shelf is permanently damp and never wet. Twice a day a boundary of thaw crosses it and then re-freezes behind you. This produces the specific hazard of a thin, invisible verglas on fluted stone, and the specific misery of clothing that wets slightly and then freezes slightly, four times a day, for as long as you stay.

- **Tight-woven shell fabrics, top and bottom.** Nothing fuzzy.
- **Vapour-barrier liners in the boots**, and boots with high, sealed uppers — the water here arrives as films running down curved surfaces, and it will find the top of a low boot.
- **A stationary parka.** This is the single most important garment. The best of this place happens while you are sitting still for an hour at fifteen thousand metres in the cold, and you cannot do that in what you walk in. A vast down or synthetic belay parka, a closed-cell foam sit-pad, and insulated overmitts that you put on *only* when you stop.
- Handwear in three grades, and never bare skin on stone. Not only for you — see below.
- Nose and cheeks: the frost tide will be at head height and you will want your hood off to hear it. That trade is real. Take it deliberately, for a bounded time, with a warm face-covering to put back on.

## Water, food, fuel

There is no fuel. That sentence is the reason no human has ever spent a night here, and it is the whole logistics problem in five words.

If you intend to melt ice, you must carry every gram of the fuel to do it, and then you must reckon with what burning does in a place where nothing weathers away. **Soot on sinter is not dirt; it is stratigraphy.** A film of it will be sealed under next season's lamina and will still be legible in three hundred years as a black band, dated to your visit, running through the whole eastern end of the grove.

So:

- Burn nothing outside a tent. Use a canister stove with a regulator and an inverted-canister preheat, not a liquid-fuel stove that must be primed with a yellow flame. Cook in the vestibule with the door oriented away from the grove, and accept the CO risk management that implies.
- Better still: **carry your water.** For a three-day visit you need perhaps twelve to eighteen litres per person at this altitude, which is brutal, but it is honest.
