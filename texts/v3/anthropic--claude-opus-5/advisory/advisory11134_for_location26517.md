# Preparing to Visit the Lantern Under the Hill

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are not visiting a room. You are visiting a *moment* in the life of a machine that is only just switched on. The plaster is nine months old, maybe eighteen. The lime is already beginning to bury it. Everything you will find remarkable — the glare, the hardness, the screaming vent, the sugar-frost starting on the arris of the lowest step — is a condition of this decade and no other. If you come back in eighty years you will find a warm, dim, softly fingered, half-silted grotto that smells of algae and is full of frogs. That will be a good place too. It will not be this place. Pick your century and don't be greedy.

Second, understand that this is a working site with a crew on it. You are stepping into someone's instrument, at the stage where they are still calibrating it — that crossed-out arc on the stair wall is a man's mistake, still fresh enough to sting. Behave accordingly.

## Medical honesty, before anything else

The water sits somewhere around 39–41°C and the air above it is the same temperature and completely saturated. There is no cool place inside. You cannot sweat there; sweat has nowhere to go. Every minute you are in that room your core temperature is rising, and swimming makes it rise faster. The only heat sink in the whole chamber is the stair, and the only real one is the dry hillside.

Do not go if you are pregnant, if your heart is unreliable, if you have a seizure disorder, if you take anything that blunts thirst, sweating, or judgement. Do not go having drunk alcohol — not the night before, either. Do not go dehydrated: put half a litre of plain warm water into yourself an hour ahead, and a pinch of salt with it. Do not go having just walked up the hill in the sun; arrive cool, sit in shade for twenty minutes first, get your skin down before you take it into that.

And do not go alone. Not because of drowning, though that too. Because of fainting. You will be immersed to the chest in hot water, thoroughly vasodilated, and then you will stand up and climb eleven paces of stair. That is a textbook faint, and a faint on the fourth step under the water is a death. Someone must be at the stair-head who can reach you.

## Conditioning

Three things are worth training, and none of them is fitness in the ordinary sense.

**Heat tolerance.** Two weeks of daily hot immersion at home — hot bath to the chest, fifteen minutes, then out and cool — will make an enormous difference to how much of your attention the heat consumes. Untrained, the heat will take everything; you will spend your whole visit as a body complaining. Trained, you can spend it looking.

**Swimming blind and swimming calm.** Practise treading water and slow breaststroke with your eyes shut and your ears plugged, in warm water, for ten minutes, without your breathing rate climbing. You will need that composure. Practise also swimming with one hand always extended in front of your face, palm out. Make it a habit until it is stupid. In there, it is the difference between meeting a wall and meeting it with your teeth.

**Breath discipline, not breath-holding.** You do not need to dive. You need to be able to breathe slowly in air that is hot, wet, thick, and only a hand's breadth deep. Panicked breathing in that lens is the single most likely way to get hurt.

## What to bring

Nothing.

I want to be exact about this, because every traveller argues. There is no ledge. There is no shelf, no niche, no dry corner, no hook. The floor is under water and the water is being drained daily through a hand-wide grille. Anything you set down is gone, and worse, anything you *lose* becomes a permanent feature of a place that is currently laying down a geological record at a fingernail a season. A dropped brass button will be sealed into the floor within a decade and will still be there in a thousand years, wrong, in a room where the only worked metal is one bronze sluice plate.

So: no camera, no watch, no light, no lens, no bottle, no cord of your own, no coin, no ring — take the ring off at the top of the hill, hot water swells fingers. No goggles or mask: they are the hardest thing to give up and the most conspicuous object anyone in that century has ever seen, and the room does not need them; the water is clearer than glass. No contact lenses; alkaline water under a lid is misery. No sunscreen, no oil, no ointment, no scented anything — the water is a chemistry the builders are reading, and a slick of grease on that surface will show up as an insult under the light column.

Two exceptions I would defend:

**Beeswax earplugs**, softened in the hand and pressed in on the dry stair. Kneaded wax is period-plausible, cheap to lose, and the noise in there is genuinely injurious — a continuous cataract roar in a burnished masonry dome with no soft surface anywhere. You will come out with your hearing dulled and a ring in it for a day regardless. Plugs turn "a day" into "an evening." Take them out before you climb back up so you can hear the crew.

**A body**, ideally uncovered, or wrapped in undyed linen if the crew's modesty requires it. Dyed cloth bleeds. The bleed will be visible. Someone will chalk it on the wall.

If you must carry something out — and you should not — carry it in your head.

## Skills to acquire in advance

**Their hand-sign.** Learn it from the crew above ground, at the spring-head, over a couple of days, before you ever go down. It is small and practical: *rising, hold, out, more, wait for me, are you well.* You will not be able to ask for it once you are inside, and inside is where you will want it. Do not improvise gestures; in that room a wrong sign is not misunderstood, it is obeyed.

**The rope code.** One tug *rising*, two *hold*, three *out now*. Learn to feel three tugs through a line held loosely in a hand that has gone numb-warm. Learn to *give* three tugs without letting go of the rope. Practise on a washing line at home until it is reflex, because the moment you need it your hearing will be gone, your eyes will be full of white light, and your language will not work.

**Counting surges.** The room's clock is the siphon: every hundred-and-some breaths the flow trebles, the voice drops to a thud, the level lifts a finger, and the vent shrieks. Learn the count from the charcoal tallies on the dry wall. Learn to count it in your own body, at your own resting breath rate, and check yourself against the crew. Your permitted stay is measured in surges, not minutes — two, on a first visit. Three if the person on the rope knows you.

**The sluice hour.** Ask. Ask twice. Ask a different person. When the bronze plate is wound open, the pull inside is gentle, wide, unhurried, and directed at a grille the width of your hand, and it does not tire. There is no signal for it inside the room except that the water has an opinion about where you should be. Never on a sluice hour.

## Timing your visit

Mid-morning to mid-afternoon, or there is no lantern — only a hot black cistern with a roar in it, which is a different and much more frightening experience, worth having second and not first.

If you want the noon disc, you need the right weeks of the year, and you need to be already in the water and already acclimatised and already counting, because it lasts about forty breaths. Do not plan to arrive for it. Plan to be present for a while, and let it happen to you. People who go down specifically to see the coin on the forge spend the whole time looking up the shaft and squinting and miss the actual event, which is not the disc — it is the way the entire room, floor and dome and your own underlit hands, brightens together, as if something answered.

And do not stare up the bore. It is a forearm-wide hole full of sun above a white burnished funnel and your pupils will already be wide from the descent. Look at what the light *does*, not where it comes from.

## The descent, step by step

Undress and sit on the dry grass a few minutes. Drink. Wax the ears. Look at the shaft from outside, because you will not understand it from inside: a dark square in dry ground, silent, with fern round its lip and goats somewhere, and nothing coming out of it. Fix that silence in your mind. You will want it later for comparison.

Go down slowly. The steps are new — no rounding, no lip, no wear, a sharp arris on every tread and a plaster surface that is beginning to take a bloom and is therefore slippery in a way that looks like it isn't. Take the caper seedling four steps above the water as your landmark and *do not step on it*; it is the second-oldest living thing in the building and there are only three.

At the waterline, stop. Sit on that step for a full surge cycle before you commit. This does three things: it lets your body meet the temperature without shock, it lets your ears and eyes recalibrate to a roar and a glare that will otherwise simply overwhelm your ability to notice anything, and it lets you hear one whole surge from safety — the drop into bass, the shriek from the southeast haunch, the wet gulp of the air coming back — so that when you feel it out in the middle of the room you know what it is and do not think the hill is failing.

Enter on the ebb. Take the rope.

## Inside: the four rules that matter

**Keep your face out of the lens.** You will want to swim to the crown, put your palm on the brick, and lie in that hand's breadth of hot air under your own mirrored reflection. It is the most extraordinary sensation available to a human body and I am not going to tell you not to do it. But: that thin pocket, immediately above hot mineral water that is fizzing gas out continuously, is where the heavy gas sits, and the chimney draught up the shaft is the only thing clearing it. Go up to the dome briefly, breathe the upper air near the bore, not the air at the surface in the far haunches, and come back down to the stair. If you feel a flush, a headache behind the eyes, a sudden inexplicable urge to breathe harder — leave. Not "leave soon." Leave.

**Never be at the dome on a surge.** At the surge the lens compresses and a swimmer at the crown is pressed up into the brick with a firmness that is not violent and cannot be argued with. Count. Be back at the stair with a hand on stone before the count runs out. If you lose the count, go to the stair and start again; nobody has ever been harmed by cowardice in that room.

**Distrust distance.** The water is so clear that distance stops working, and the light is so shadowless that there is no second cue to fall back on. The far wall is an arm away or fifteen paces and you will not know until you arrive. Swim with one palm out, always. Use the two real cues the room gives you: the temperature gradient — the two coolest corners with the bruise-coloured felt in them are furthest from the inflow — and the line strung from the stair-head to the vent, which is not decoration, it is the only straight thing in there. Hold it. There is no shame; the crew hold it.

**Touch nothing you are tempted to touch.** The ghost-hatchings at the corners, the herringbone burnish, the thumbprint that went in and was smoothed over but not quite, the felt patches still deciding whether to stay — these are the room's only history and it has about ten years of it left before the lime buries the lot. Do not scrape. Do not rub the felt to see the colour better. Do not add a stroke to the tally, do not scratch an arc, do not carve anything anywhere. Everything you do to that plaster will be *set in stone*, precisely, within a human lifetime, and read by people who will have no idea what it means.

## Coming out, and after

Come out on an ebb, hand over hand, and come up the stair slowly with your weight on the rail-less wall — this is the faint moment. Sit at the top, in the dry, before you stand.

Expect the following, and do not be alarmed by any of it:

Your hearing will be flattened and there will be a fine ring in it, and the roar will continue in your head for some hours after you leave and will come back at the edge of sleep for two or three nights. Your skin will be squeaky-taut and faintly chalky, because you are wearing a molecule of the same lime the room is putting on itself; you have been briefly included. Your hair will feel like straw. Your eyes will be tired in the way snow tires them. You will be very thirsty and slightly stupid for an hour; drink, salt, shade, don't make decisions.

And you will find the hillside unbearable. That is the real aftereffect and nobody warns about it. You come up out of a room made of noise and light into dry grass and goats and a silence so total that the world seems to have been switched off, with a dark square in the ground beside you that could be stepped into by anyone not looking, and no sound coming out of it at all. Sit with that for a while before you go anywhere. It is the actual experience; the swim was only how you got to it.

## What you cannot prepare for

That you will not be able to speak. Not "it's hard to hear" — voices arrive as shape without word, and a room that removes speech from a human being removes something people do not know they are carrying. Some travellers find it a relief so profound they weep in there, which is invisible and unhearable and therefore permitted. Others find it a small horror. You will not know which you are until the second surge.

And that the room feels like a held breath before a very long exhalation, and that you will be unable to shake the sense that you were let in early, before it was ready, by people who knew exactly what they were building and knew they would not live to see it soft.

Go on the ebb. Carry nothing. Stay two surges. Come back up.
