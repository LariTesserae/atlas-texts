# Preparing to Visit the Throat

## 1. Understand what you are entering

Not a well. A well is a hole with water in it. The Throat is a **machine made of stairs**, and it is also, unavoidably, an **instrument** — a nine-tiered resonant cavity with a hard floor, hard walls, a hard lid of colonnades, and a small rectangle of sky for a mouth. Every preparation you make should follow from those two facts.

Because it is a machine, it has tolerances, and you are the wrong size for them. The treads, risers, flight widths, and landing intervals were all derived over centuries from a single unit: a woman with a full pot on her head, climbing without setting it down. You are not that unit. You will be slightly wrong everywhere, in the way a left-handed person is wrong in a right-handed kitchen. Expect to be corrected. Expect the correction to be shouted.

Because it is an instrument, you cannot be private in it, and you cannot rest your ears in it. This is the part travelers under-prepare for, every time. They train their legs and they ruin their hearing and their voices and their nerves in nine days.

## 2. The body: begin six weeks out

**Feet.** No shoes below the fourth tier. This is not a courtesy; wet polished stone plus a sole has killed people, and they will not let you test it. So you need soles. Start walking barefoot daily on progressively rougher and wetter ground — gravel, wet tile, stone steps — until you can take a hundred stairs down and up unshod without thinking about it. Trim your nails short and square. Treat any fungal infection *now*; you are going into a warm, wet, permanently damp environment shared by two hundred and eighty pairs of feet, and a cracked heel becomes a septic problem in a week. Get a tetanus booster. Consider that a torn toenail on the fifth tier is the end of your visit.

**Neck and gait.** If you intend to carry anything on your head — and carrying is the entire moral economy of the place — you need eight weeks minimum of loading. Start with two kilos, a folded cloth pad, and level ground; progress to stairs; progress to eight or ten kilos. Learn to keep the load still by keeping your *spine* still, not by using your hands. You will still be visibly bad at it. Accept that. But there is a better plan, which is:

**Carry something other than water.** The Throat has an inexhaustible appetite for sand, slaked lime, firewood, brick, rope, and the masons' grit. Nobody's proportions are sacred for those. If you go down empty and come up loaded with something a mason wants, you will have paid your rent, and you will have paid it in the only currency the place recognizes: **upward labor.**

**Stairs and heat, together.** Train stair climbing in heat and humidity, because the bottom of the Throat is a wet-bulb trap. The spring rises a little above blood temperature and the air at the lowest tiers is saturated. Your sweat cannot evaporate. You will not cool down by resting there; you will only cool down by *going up*. Rehearse that until it is reflex: **when you feel strange, climb.** Learn your own early symptoms of heat strain — the specific way *you* get stupid — and give a companion permission to overrule you.

**Voice.** Ordinary speech in the Throat is a bellow. Untrained visitors are hoarse by day two and voiceless by day four, and a voiceless person in the Throat is functionally absent. Work with anyone who teaches stage projection: breath support, open throat, forward placement, volume from the diaphragm and not from squeezing the larynx. Learn that **whispering is worse for your cords than shouting well.** Bring honey; bring nothing that dries you out. And learn the local hand signs on your first day — there are always hand signs where there is noise, and they will be doing them without noticing that they are.

**Eyes.** Below the fourth tier the light is oil-lamp and reflected daylight. Practise moving in near-dark by handrail and tread-count. Learn to count. The whole community thinks in counts — steps, tiers, waterlines — and a person who can say "twenty-second step" and be right is a person who has arrived.

## 3. Medical, in order of how likely it is to actually ruin you

1. **Falls.** Overwhelmingly the main hazard. Glassy stone, three-finger-deep hollows, no railings in places, wet, dark, loud, and a right-of-way system you don't have instincts for.
2. **Heat.** See above. Salt and electrolytes. The heat here is not the heat you expect from a hole in the ground, and that expectation is itself the danger.
3. **The water in your nose.** A warm freshwater spring is exactly the habitat for organisms that get into a human via the nasal passages and go somewhere unsurvivable. **Never** submerge your face. Never dive. Never dunk your head to cool off, however desperate you are. No nasal rinsing, ever, with any water from below the lip. If this rule feels excessive, keep it anyway; it costs you nothing and its violation has no treatment.
4. **Drinking water.** Two hundred and eighty people, ten thousand birds, three thousand bats. Have a plan that is either invisible (chemical tablets used out of sight, a filter concealed in an ordinary clay pot) or defensible (you boil everything, because you drink a bitter infusion for your health — this reads as an eccentricity, and eccentricities are permitted).
5. **The bat gallery.** Enclosed spaces with thousands of bats and generations of guano carry a fungal lung disease that is contracted by breathing dust, and can be serious and delayed. Do not go into the lowest west gallery. If you must, go with a damp cloth over your nose and mouth — which, conveniently, is a completely period-plausible thing to do, unlike a respirator. Do not handle bats. Rabies pre-exposure vaccination before departure is cheap insurance in a place where several thousand bats jostle a hand's breadth above people's heads.
6. **Hearing.** Discussed next, because it is not really a medical problem. It is a social one.

## 4. Ears: the central problem

Ten thousand screaming swifts in a hard-walled resonant shaft, twice a day, for an hour each. Frogs you can feel in your sternum. Bees like a held note. Rain that arrives as a solid column the shape of the well's mouth. You should assume genuinely hazardous exposure, repeated, daily.

And yet: **the swifts' two hours are the community's rest.** You sit. You cannot hear. It is understood that this is what happens. So if you turn up wearing bright foam plugs, or worse, sit through dawn visibly *able to hear*, you have made yourself strange during the two hours of the day when everyone is looking at everyone.

The solution is beeswax. Soft wax plugs, warmed and rolled and pressed in, are completely unremarkable, are available on the sixth tier, and work. Bring a starter supply and learn to make more. Wear them constantly below the lip and remove them only when someone is speaking directly to you. During the swift hours: plugs in, sit down, look at the middle distance, and let your face do what everyone else's face is doing. You will find, after a few days, that the swift hours are the best part of the day.

Then prepare for the aftermath. Expect tinnitus. Expect to come home shouting at people for a fortnight. Expect the return to a quiet world to feel like a bereavement — travelers report this consistently and are always surprised by it. Arrange a soft re-entry: somewhere with running water and birds, not somewhere silent.

## 5. Kit, and the rule that governs all of it

The Throat contains: dressed stone, brick, lime plaster, timber, grass rope, fired clay, hide, and bronze reserved for chisels. That is the whole material world. Anything you bring is either inside that list or it is a visible artifact from elsewhere.

**The governing rule: nothing you carry may be capable of falling out of reach.** This place is *maintained*. The basin corners are rounded so sediment can be swept. Everything that goes to the bottom is eventually recovered by someone whose job is recovering things. A dropped lighter, pill blister, or lens is not lost — it is *found*, and kept, and shown around, and remembered for four generations. Lanyard everything. Sew pockets shut. Carry nothing loose above water.

Practical notes:

- **Clothing:** unbleached natural cloth, no synthetic sheen, no elastic, no zips, no bright dyes unless you know the local dyes. Hair bound up. Leather rots below the fourth tier; whatever sandals you bring for the plain will be carried, not worn, and will be ruined anyway.
- **Eyeglasses:** the single hardest item. Metal-framed lenses are unexplainable. Prefer contacts with a hygiene regime you can maintain in humidity, or a prescription you can survive without, or accept being The One With The Eye-Glass and let it become your attribute, like the god with the jar.
- **Light:** oil lamp, or nothing. A torch below the lip is a catastrophe you cannot walk back.
- **Recording devices:** see §7. Think very hard.
- **Water gear:** a good clay pot, bought locally on your first day, and a head pad you have made yourself.
- **Trade goods:** salt. Good needles, sparingly. Dye. Medicinal seeds. Honey. Fine whetstones. Give **consumables and skills**, never durable wealth, and never, under any circumstances, bronze — bronze here is chisels, chisels are the maintenance of the entire structure, and a gift of bronze is not generosity but an intervention in politics you cannot see.
- **Not brought:** anything that would let you fix, improve, or shortcut the Throat. No pulley. No rope-and-bucket. No windlass. The absence of the wheel over the well is not an oversight or a poverty; it is the reason the place exists in this form, and it is the reason eighty people live in a cool honeycomb with galleries and gods instead of hauling from a narrow hole. If you cannot promise yourself you will keep your improvements to yourself, do not go.

## 6. Arrival, hour by hour

Come across the plain on foot, mid-morning, dusty, laden. Not at dawn or dusk — arriving during the swifts is arriving during someone's sleep. Not in rain, and not in the two days before rain, when everything loose is coming up and everyone is busy.

At the lip: **stop.** Do not walk to the edge and look down while reaching into your bag. Sit on the yellow stone in the shade of the fig, where you can be seen from four tiers, and wait to be come to. You will be come to. The Throat has two hundred visitors a day and a well-developed instinct for strangers.

Then, in order:

- Ask permission to descend. Ask who to ask.
- Take off your shoes at the fourth tier without being told. Someone will notice that you knew.
- Do not offer gifts at the top. Offer labour at the top and gifts on the third day.
- Learn, that first hour: which flights are west (down) and which east (up); where people relieve themselves — it is not in the well, it is somewhere out on the plain, and you need to know where, and what people do at night, *before* you need to know it at night; where clothes are beaten; and where you must never put your hands or feet in the basin.

**You will stumble on the eleventh step from the bottom.** It is a finger too high, on purpose, to brake the hurrying. Every stranger stumbles on it, and it is the community's welcome and its joke and its diagnostic. Do not try to pre-empt it or perform your way through it. Stumble, laugh, look up, let them have it. You cannot pass as local in the Throat; the architecture itself is designed to detect you. Your only viable identity is **honest stranger, useful, quiet, quick to learn** — and that identity is well provided for. There are chambers behind the colonnades used for nothing but shade and argument. Strangers are argued at. This is a form of hospitality.

## 7. The three peculiarities, and how to behave around them

**The water that moves in whole steps.** It rises and falls overnight, one tread at a time, never caught partway up a riser. You will want to watch for it. Everyone does; children do; there are old men who have watched for decades. So watching is not a transgression — but *investigating* is, if you do it in a way that says *your neighbours' account of their own home is unreliable*. Watch it the way they watch it: by sitting up, at the twenty-second step, with a lamp, on a night when you feel like it, and failing, and being teased. Do meanwhile note the number. **Ask, on arrival, which step the water stands at, and how the year is named.** *The year we went to nineteen.* That count is the only calendar in reach, and it is how you will place this visit in relation to any other.

**The shuffled echo, fifth landing, third bay, back to the middle pillar.** Four words go out; the same four words come back rearranged, and the rearrangement is stable for a given sentence, and the children keep a list of the good ones. Go and hear it — it would be rude not to, it is the local wonder, and being shown it is an honour. Learn some famous ones. Contribute one, if you can.

Do not go in intending to solve it. And think carefully before you take a recorder in.
