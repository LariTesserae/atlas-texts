# Going Down the Kettle: A Traveller's Preparation

## First, understand what you are

You are not the one in danger here. You are the danger.

Four hundred and ten people live in a sealed pocket of rock with one hole in it, breathing each other's air twice a day, drinking condensate off a mesh, with no rats and no flies and no immunological history in common with anything outside. A traveller arriving from another world is a walking ecosystem: gut flora, skin flora, follicle mites, the seeds in your cuff-seams, the spider that laid an egg case in the frame of your pack three towns ago, and — worst by an enormous margin — whatever respiratory virus you are incubating and do not yet know about.

Read the plate at the shaft foot again. Five of the six rules are about not wasting things. The sixth is *Log everything you bring down*. And the line scratched underneath is not poetry. It is the founding threat assessment of the settlement.

Prepare accordingly. Everything below flows from that.

---

## Three months out: the body

**Heat acclimatisation.** Ambient forty-three, up to fifty in the Low Quarter. This is survivable, but only by an adapted body. Spend the eight weeks before travel building heat tolerance deliberately: daily sessions of moderate exercise in heat — sauna, hot room, whatever your world provides — worked up from twenty minutes to ninety, five or six days a week, sustained right up to departure. Adaptation begins in four days, matures in about two weeks, and decays in about the same. Do not arrive "generally fit." Arrive *hot-fit*. The physiological changes you want are increased plasma volume, earlier sweat onset, and — critically — dilute sweat. In the Kettle salt is free and water is not. Losing less salt per litre means you can get by on plain condensate and a pinch from the pans, instead of demanding electrolyte stores nobody has.

**Grip and ladder endurance.** Four hundred metres of ladder. That is a hundred and thirty storeys, in rising heat, with a load. Train it specifically: dead hangs, farmer's carries, step-ups with a pack, and if you can find a ladder, climb it until your forearms fail and then climb it again tomorrow. The thing that kills people on long ladders is not cardiovascular collapse. It is the hand opening without permission.

**Fungal housekeeping.** Treat and clear every fungal infection you own before you travel — feet, groin, nails, scalp. Forty-three degrees and permanent damp is the best fungal habitat a human body can be placed in. Anything smouldering in you now will become a serious, mobility-limiting injury by day five.

**Teeth.** There is no dentist. There is a four-hundred-metre ladder between you and any surgeon. Get everything fixed.

**Hair.** Cut it short. Long hair holds heat, holds salt, and is an unanswerable biosecurity argument at the shaft head. Beards likewise — and a wet collar-cloth seals badly against a beard.

**Nails short, skin intact.** Every callus and hangnail is a place for salt to get in.

## The quarantine, which is not optional

Budget **seven to ten days at the shaft head before you descend**, in isolation, with minimal contact. Not because they'll make you — though they will — but because you should want to. A common cold in a closed chamber that exchanges air twice a day will move through four hundred and ten people in under a fortnight, and some of them will be old, and all of them will be dehydrated, and the fleece-field does not run itself.

Expect, at the shaft head:

- Everything you own, baked. Assume anything that cannot take an hour at a hundred and thirty degrees will not be coming with you. Test your gear against this *at home*. Do not discover at the top of the shaft that your boots delaminate.
- Wooden objects refused outright, or shaved and baked. Wood carries borers.
- Anything organic — fresh food, leather, down, feather, fur, unmilled cloth — refused, argued over, or destroyed. Bring nothing you would grieve.
- A seam-by-seam inspection of your pack. Do this yourself first, with a light, unhurried, the day before. Turn the pockets inside out. Pick out the grass seeds. You will find some. Everybody finds some.
- Your body inspected for parasites, and a frank conversation about your gut.

Volunteer more than they ask for. Open things before you're told to. This costs you nothing and buys you a reputation in a town of four hundred and ten people where reputation is the only currency that doesn't weigh anything.

## What to carry, and the arithmetic of the back

Every kilogram is a person's back on a ladder. Take this personally.

**Wear or carry:**
- Two or three collar-cloths, plain woven, fast-wetting. You will live with a wet rag on your neck and you will come to love it.
- A smooth under-layer of your own. Basalt wool is itchy as sin and you have not spent four years growing indifferent to it.
- Two pairs of thin, tough gloves. Salt-crusted catwalk rails cut palms. So does the floor, if you put a hand down.
- Hard-soled boots that grip glass. And learn to **lift your feet**. Scuffing takes a flake off the crust, and the flake stays sharp for years, and residents can hear an outsider walking by the sound of the drag. Walk flat, place, lift. It will feel absurd and it is the single quickest way to be read as respectful.
- A folded sitting mat, hand-sized. Rock at forty-eight degrees will burn the backs of your thighs through cloth in under a minute.
- A shielded lamp with a red or deep-orange filter. Never a white beam. Light is heat and the whole town's dark-adaptation is a shared resource; sweeping a bright torch across the third tier is like shouting in a library that is also an oven.
- Your own drinking jar, marked, and the iron habit of never setting it down beneath anybody's gutter.

**Do not carry:** seeds. Soil. Anything alive or recently alive. A pet, obviously, but also the dried flowers you forgot in the lid pocket. And no gift of food.

**Gifts that actually land** (small, dense, inorganic, useful): fine copper or steel tubing for fleece coils. Fine wire. Needle files. Good mesh. Lens glass and goggles. Sewing needles. Medicines with long shelf lives. And, if your world has such a thing, **a decent dew-point instrument or sling psychrometer**. The Kettle's whole economy is condensation and their instrumentation is homemade. A traveller who can hand over a working hygrometer and half an hour of honest explanation about dew point and surface temperature will be remembered for a generation.

And one small thing that costs nothing: **a fine soft brush, squirrel-hair or better.** Give it to Osme. Say what it's for and then stop talking.

## The descent

Go down on an in-draft if they let you — cold air pouring past you makes the last hundred metres bearable.

Expect two to four hours with rests. Expect the heat to climb as you drop, not steadily but in layers, with pockets of warm air that feel like walking into a wall. Expect your hands to be the problem. Expect the light above you to shrink to a coin, and then to a star, and then to nothing when the shaft bends. Expect, somewhere around the three-hundred-metre mark, a moment of pure animal panic at the amount of mountain over your head. It passes. Keep climbing down.

Use whatever fall-arrest they offer. Do not carry weight on your back that you could send down separately; a swaying pack pulls you off the rungs.

Cache a **dry, warm layer at the shaft head** for your return. You will come up soaked and the mountain air will take you apart.

---

## The first six hours: learning the two weathers

The Kettle has two climates a day and they demand opposite behaviour from your body. Residents don't think about this. You must, for about four days, until you stop needing to.

**Out-draft (dry, salt blooming).** The air leaves, the walls grow white fur, and the air is dry. Your sweat and your wet cloth evaporate, and evaporation is your cooling. This is when the wet cloth *works*. Re-wet religiously. Drink ahead of thirst — at forty-three degrees your thirst signal lags your loss by hours, and by the time you're thirsty you are already down a litre and a half.

**In-draft (cold column, fog from the ankles up).** Air temperature drops, which feels wonderful, but the chamber saturates. Evaporation stops. Your wet cloth now cools you only by being *colder than you are*, not by drying. This is the trap: the fog makes you feel cooler while removing your best cooling mechanism. If you work hard during in-draft — and in-draft is the working time, and everyone around you will be working — you can overheat inside a pleasant white mist without any of the usual warnings.

So: **get a buddy, and brief them.** The signs of heat illness are things you cannot observe in yourself. You stop sweating. You get goosebumps and chills in an oven. You become irritable, then cheerful, then confused. Tell someone: *if I get chatty and stop sweating, sit me down at the shaft foot and pour water on me.* Then do the same for them. This exchange, offered on your first day, will do more for your standing than any gift.

No alcohol. No caffeine for the first three days.

---

## Water: you are extremely expensive

One fleece yields nine litres a day and belongs to a household. You will drink eight to twelve litres a day, more if you work. Do the sum: an unacclimatised visitor consumes an entire household's daily water.

Settle this before you descend, explicitly, in numbers, with whoever hosts you. Bring something that pays for it. Do not improvise, do not assume hospitality will cover it, and above all **do not be gracious about water** — do not refuse a drink to be polite, and do not accept one without knowing whose fleece it came off. Getting this wrong in either direction is worse than being blunt.

Never rinse in the Weep. Never drink it. Brine on cloth crystallises and turns your collar into sandpaper.

---

## Your own waste

Ask about this early and without embarrassment. Nothing is thrown away; the compost feeds eleven beds of ash-cress and one fig tree. Whether they want an outsider's gut flora entering that loop is their decision and it may well be *no*. Be prepared to use a sealed system and to carry your own waste four hundred metres back up the ladder. If that sentence makes you reconsider the trip, reconsider the trip. It is the most honest test of whether you should go.

The same logic applies to menstrual management, wound dressings, hair clippings, and nail parings. Bring sealed disposal. Log it. Take it out.

---

## Hazards, in the order they will get you

1. **Heat.** Covered above. It is first because it is first.
2. **Gas.** The floor has slumped basins and breathing fissures. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and pools in low places, and there is no bird, no rat, no insect anywhere in this chamber to die on your behalf. There is no canary in the Kettle because there is nothing alive in the Kettle. Never lie down on the floor. Never sleep low. Never climb into a basin to retrieve something. If you feel suddenly short of breath and slightly euphoric at ankle height, stand up and walk uphill immediately. Carry a small flame or a simple indicator and know the local rule about which basins are avoided.
3. **Glass.** The floor is a field of knives that have never been weathered. Falling is a laceration, not a bruise. Salt in the laceration. Wounds heal badly in heat and damp and there is no surgeon.
4. **Contact burns.** *Do not touch bare rock below the third tier.* Fifty degrees will not burn you instantly but it will burn you in seconds, and you will do it reflexively while catching your balance. Keep gloves on below the third tier. Keep your hands in front of you, not out to the sides.
5. **Fog navigation.** During in-draft, visibility on the floor drops to twenty metres and the fleece-field is four hundred identical grey panels. Do not enter the fleece-field alone in fog in your first week. Learn the guide lines. Count ranks.
6. **The rock.** It ticks. It groans. Three times in four years it has opened a new vent. Accept this consciously before you go down, the way you'd accept it before boarding a ship. If you're not willing to die in a rockfall for the sake of seeing this place, don't see this place.

## When the Listener calls

Go up. Immediately. Without gathering anything. Without asking why.

Six of the last nine calls were wrong. That is the point, not an argument against it. The discipline only works if it is unconditional, and a visitor who hesitates, or who asks a question, or who goes back for a bag, is breaking the one social technology holding this town together.

On your **first day**, before you unpack, before you eat: walk your quarter's evacuation route to the Spine, twice. Once with a light, once without. Learn it with your hands. Learn where your quarter's ladder-run is relative to your hammock. Learn the sound of the call. Ask someone to make it for you so you know it in your body, not in your notes.

## Manners

**Never touch a fleece. Never touch a frame.** Not to steady yourself. Not to sweep behind. Not a hand's width. This is the expulsion offence and you are four hundred metres from the surface and there is no appeal. Walk the fleece-field with your hands at your sides.

**Sweep your frontage.** If you lodge with a household, you take a broom twice a day and you sweep, badly at first. Twenty minutes, four hundred people, the soft sibilant chorus, and then it stops all at once — you will not know when to stop, so watch your neighbour's shoulders, not your own patch. Being part of the sweep is the fastest way into the town.

**The sun-walk.** For your first several days, don't stand in it. Watch. Be invited. When you are invited, count thirty and step out, and step out a little early. Don't bring anything into the light — no notebook, no instrument, no sketch, no work of any kind, and that includes the work of recording. It is the only unmanaged thing they have. Don't manage it.

**Learn names.** Four hundred and ten is a learnable number. Make the effort visibly. In a town this size you are being observed constantly and there is no privacy; accept that, and behave as though everything you say will be repeated by evening, because it will.

**Don't lend water. Don't borrow water.** Don't get involved in any dispute concerning a fleece, a gutter, or a frontage boundary, even to agree with someone.

## The fig, and the urge to help

You will want to fix the fig. Everyone does. Nine years old, four metres, never fruited, hand-pollinated with an inherited brush by a woman who has never seen a squirrel.

Do not arrive with a solution. Do not bring pollen. Do not bring a cutting, a wasp, a graft, a soil amendment, or a theory that you deliver in front of other people. Whatever you know about caprifigs and *Blastophaga* may be exactly right and it is still not yours to deploy. That tree is not an agricultural problem. It is the town's only relationship with something that isn't them.

If you have knowledge, offer it to Osme, privately, as a question. *May I tell you something I know about figs? You may not want it.* Then abide by her answer, including if the answer is no, and including if the answer is no because she already knows.

Touch the trunk on your way to the ladder, like everyone else.

---

## The children, and the surface

Some of them were born down there. Some of them have never seen the sun as anything but a coin crossing a field of grey mesh.

They will ask you about animals. They will ask what rain is like. They will ask whether birds are loud.

Before you answer anything, ask their parents what they'd like said. Then tell the truth, plainly, without wonder and without pity, and without performing your own nostalgia. Do not describe the sky as though it were an argument for leaving. These people made a town out of a gas bubble and their children are not a tragedy.

And do not promise to come back.

---

## Sleep and the mind

You will sleep badly for three nights. Heat, hammock, the unfamiliar rhythm of two weathers a day, the pumps at eleven strokes a minute, and the rock cracking overhead at random — a knuckle-pop, then a long tearing groan, then nothing for an hour. Your nervous system will treat every report as a starting gun until it stops.

Don't use earplugs. You need to hear the call.

Learn the trick everyone has: keep water within arm's reach of the hammock and re-wet your collar without waking. You will be clumsy at this and then one night you'll do it in your sleep and realise in the morning that you've become slightly local.

**The absence of animals** will take you about a day to consciously identify and about a week to stop feeling. It is not silence — it is the missing periphery. Nothing moves at the edge of your vision. Nothing scuttles when you lift a board. There is no life in the corners. Some travellers find this restful. Most find that around day three it produces a specific, hard-to-place dread, and the honest thing to know in advance is that the dread is normal and it passes and the residents find the same stillness soothing, and by the end you may too.

**The fleece-field's shrug** — four hundred panels moving at once when the chamber breathes — will get you at least once. Stand and let it.

**The tone on the out-draft.** If you are alone in the field when the shaft draws and the pitch starts sliding as the salt closes the cracks one by one, you will hear words in it. Everyone does. The young workers have a whole vocabulary for what it says. Here is the advice: don't be alone out there for it in your first week, and when you do hear it, be very careful who you tell and how. Saying *it spoke to me* to the wrong resident is a joke. To the right one it is a confidence. To a third it is something close to trespass.

Everyone agrees it is not a voice.

---

## Leaving

Go light. Trade for what packs small: a scarf of basalt cloth, a jar of pan salt, the Listener's log copied out if they'll let you. Not a bolt of fabric. Not a slag brick. Every kilogram is a back.

The climb up is worse than the climb down and there is no fog to help you. Start early. Drink more than you think, at the bottom, before you start, because there is no water on the ladder.

At the top: dry layer, immediately, before you speak to anyone. You will be soaked through and the mountain will be thirty degrees colder and a wet body at altitude loses heat catastrophically fast. People have survived the Kettle and then been hurt by the first ten minutes of the surface.

---

## Afterwards

Two things will happen and you should be ready for both.

You will find, for about a week, that ordinary air feels thin and that ordinary rooms are absurdly, wastefully large, and that you keep reaching for your collar.

And at some point in the first day or two — a fly on a window, a dog, a bird landing on a rail — something small and alive will move at the edge of your vision, and you will not be prepared for what that does to you.

Most travellers cry at the first insect. It's fine. Let it happen. Then wet your cloth out of habit, find you're not wearing one, and go home.
