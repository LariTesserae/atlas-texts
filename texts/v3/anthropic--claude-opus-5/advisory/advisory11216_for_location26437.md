# Preparing for the Kettle

## First, understand what you are when you get there

You are not a tourist. You are a heat source, a water sink, a load on the ladder, and — this is the part people fail to take seriously — an ecosystem.

Four hundred and ten people live in a sealed bubble of new rock that has no soil, no insects, no rot, no mice, no mould worth the name, and a written policy about it. You will arrive carrying roughly a kilogram of bacteria, a face full of *Demodex*, fungal spores in your hair, whatever is living in your gut, and — if you have not been extremely careful — seeds in your boot tread, an ant in the seam of your pack, a mosquito's eggs in the threads of a water bottle cap, a moth in the lining of a jacket.

They cannot decontaminate you. They know it. They let people down anyway, because they need trade. But the sentence scratched under the rules at the shaft foot — *Nothing lives here that we did not carry* — is not poetry to them, it is an accounting statement, and you are an entry in it. Behave like someone who knows the cost of their own presence and you will be treated well. Behave like a visitor and you will be tolerated, watched, and lodged near the ladder.

## The body: start six weeks out

**Heat acclimatisation is not optional and cannot be faked.** Ambient in the middle tiers is 43 °C. An unacclimatised body in that, working, will be in trouble in under two hours. An acclimatised body sweats sooner, sweats more, and sweats *dilute* — it stops throwing away salt. That adaptation takes ten to fourteen consecutive days of exercising in heat, and it decays in about a week. So: sauna or hot room, 60–90 minutes daily, light exercise, for two weeks immediately before you go. Not a month before. Immediately before.

**Learn your own wet-bulb ceiling and carry an instrument.** This matters more than the air temperature. The Kettle breathes twice a day. On the out-draft the air is hot and dry and the wet cloth works beautifully; on the in-draft the chamber fogs from the ankles up in four minutes, and for those minutes the Low Quarter is hot *and* saturated, and evaporative cooling — the only cooling you have — simply stops. Locals judge this by feel, because they have nineteen thousand hours of feel. You have none. Carry a cheap wet-bulb thermometer or a sling psychrometer. Above 31 °C wet bulb you should be climbing; above 33 you should be at the shaft foot; 35 is the number at which a resting human in shade dies, and the Low Quarter can flirt with the edge of that in the first twenty minutes of an in-draft. The bell that rings for the in-draft means *good working time* to them. To you it should mean *go up two tiers and drink*.

**Cardiovascular fitness for the descent and, worse, the ascent.** Four hundred metres of ladder. Assume four to six hours down with rests, and considerably longer up, in the last hour of which you will be exhausted, dehydrated, and above a drop. Train stairs and ladders with a loaded pack. Train your grip specifically — forearm failure kills more people on long ladders than legs do.

**Weight discipline.** Every kilogram on your back is a kilogram somebody could otherwise have been carrying down as pipe, wire, medicine or soil. Ruthlessly cut your kit. If you cannot justify an item as either survival or trade, leave it at the shaft head.

**Medical prep:** current tetanus, full course of any deworming your origin-world uses, no active skin infections, no open wounds, no productive cough. Do not go with a cold. Do not go with a stomach bug. Nail your fingernails and toenails short, crop your hair short, shave. This is partly hygiene and partly the wet cloth: long hair holds heat and holds spores, and you will be re-wetting your collar every half hour for as long as you are down there.

## Decontamination: assume you will be stripped

Everything descending is heat-treated at the shaft head and logged. Plan your kit so this is possible:

- **No natural fibres you care about.** No wool jumpers, no leather boots you love, no down. Assume anything organic goes through an oven cycle that may ruin it.
- **No food from outside** unless it is sealed, sterile, and pre-cleared. No fresh fruit. Absolutely no seeds, nuts in shell, grain, dried flowers, herbal remedies, or anything with a viable embryo in it. A single fig seed getting into the Kettle would be a genuine political event.
- **No soil.** Scrub boot treads with a brush and a pick before you travel, then again at the head. Take the insoles out and look under them.
- **Empty and dry every container.** Vacuum every seam, cuff, hem, strap, velcro patch and camera bag. Freeze soft goods for 72 hours beforehand if you can; it will not sterilise but it kills a great deal of what hides in fabric.
- **Nothing alive, obviously.** No pet, no plant, no culture, no yoghurt, no yeast, no fermenting anything.
- **Declare truthfully.** The descent log records every crate. If you smuggle something and it is found, you will be walked to the ladder, and they will not care about the weather at the top.

## What to wear

**Loose, light, pale, synthetic or basalt-cloth, with a hood.** You want cloth between you and the walls, because half the heat in the Kettle is radiant and comes off the rock. That is what the layered fibre curtains on the tiers are for. Copy them: a loose outer layer that traps a shell of your own sweat-cooled air.

**A collar cloth, and a second one.** This is the local uniform and the local ritual. It goes around the neck, wetted at the troughs, re-wetted every half hour. Get in the habit above ground. Within two days you will be doing it without noticing, which is the point.

**Gloves — thin, tough, two pairs.** Three reasons. The rock below the third tier is 48–50 °C and you are told not to touch it. The pahoehoe crust is volcanic glass and takes skin off like a razor. And after every out-draft the catwalk rails grow a fur of salt needles that will open your palms.

**Boots with a hard, thick, stiff sole and ankle support.** Ropy pahoehoe is the most treacherous walking surface there is: coils, hollow crusts, ankle-swallowing slumps, hand-wide fissures that breathe warm air at you. Soft soles will be shredded in a week and will let every edge through. Also: every scuff you take off the floor is a glass flake that will lie there sharp for years. Walk lightly. It is not just courtesy; nothing here erodes.

**Eye protection.** Goggles, not glasses. For the melt-house glare (the furnace eye will scar your retina if you stare into it), for the salt, and for the fibre.

**A respirator, and the tact to know when to wear it.** Basalt wool is spun rock. The Kettle is a town whose every wall, mat, panel and curtain is made of it, where they chip the bedrock and feed it into the melt-eye. The fine fraction is respirable. The residents mostly do not mask, because masks are hot and they live here and the subject is not welcome. Wear a P3 in the melt-house and when handling fibre, don't lecture anyone about it, and take the hint if you notice how many people over forty cough.

**A headlamp with a red mode, dimmed.** The lamp-strings are run low *on purpose* — light is heat. Walking about with a bright white beam is like shouting.

## Water, and the arithmetic that governs your visit

There is no river and no rain. There is a rock exhaling, four hundred fleeces intercepting the exhalation, and nine litres per fleece per day.

You, unacclimatised, working, at 43 °C, will sweat between one and one and a half litres an hour.

Read those two numbers together. **A single visitor consumes the entire daily output of one to one and a half households.** That is the fundamental fact of your trip. Everything about how you are received follows from it.

Therefore:

1. **Keep the visit short.** Two to four days. Longer stays need to be negotiated in advance and paid for in something real.
2. **Bring water down if you can, and bring the means of making it if you can't.** A fleece's worth of good cold coil, a length of high-performance mesh, a small fan, a working heat exchanger, refrigerant, pipe fittings, gasket stock — in the Kettle, *cold* is the precious substance. Anything that makes cold is worth more than metal or money.
3. **Drink deliberately and salt deliberately.** Sweating twelve litres and replacing it with plain water will drop your sodium and put you on the floor convulsing. Carry electrolyte salts. Ironically the Kettle has salt to spare — the Weep gives forty litres a day of hot brine and the walls grow crystal twice daily — so this is the one input you can get locally. Ask; do not scrape a wall and lick your hand.
4. **Never, ever touch another household's fleece frame.** Not to move it, not to steady it, not to sweep behind it, not a hand's width. This is the one offence they punish with expulsion up the shaft, and expulsion means a four-hundred-metre ladder with whatever they hand you. Walk the fleece-field only on the marked lanes, keep your pack close to your body, and if a strap catches a frame, stop, call someone, and let them free it.

## Manners

The six rules are on the slag plate. Learn them before you go down and say them back if asked. Beyond them:

**Sweep your frontage.** Twice a day, when the walls bloom. If you are a guest in a household you sweep with them. Four hundred people brushing salt off basalt for twenty minutes is the Kettle's only chorus; joining it is the single fastest way to stop being a stranger.

**Obey the Listener instantly.** A duty watch sits in a chair at the centre of the floor writing down what the cooling rock says. Nine calls in four years, six of them wrong, three of them right — and the three right ones opened new vents. When the call comes, you go up. You do not gather your things. You do not ask which quarter. Know your route to the Spine from wherever you are sleeping, in the dark, and walk it once on your first day.

**Sun-walk etiquette is courtesy, not law, which makes it stricter.** Forty minutes a day, a coin of real daylight crossing the fleece-field. Don't crowd it. Don't stand in it longer than a count of thirty if anyone is waiting. Don't bring work into it. Do not photograph people standing in it without asking, and understand that asking may itself be a small rudeness. Children are brought to it. Old people arrange their day around it. It is the only unmanaged thing in the Kettle and you should treat it the way you would treat someone else's shrine.

**Be quiet.** The fibre eats sound, the crowding makes people considerate, and heat makes shouting expensive. Speak at the volume they speak at. The alleys are shoulder-width; you will brush people constantly; a murmured acknowledgement is the whole protocol.

**The fig.** Nine years old, four metres, never fruited, hand-pollinated by a woman named Osme with a squirrel-hair brush she inherited from her mother. It is the tallest living thing below ground for a hundred kilometres. Touching the trunk on your way to the ladder is a thing people do; it is not forbidden to you but wait until someone shows you. Do not offer advice about why it isn't fruiting. Do not touch the leaf-litter, which is collected, weighed and composted back into its own tub. If you want to give a gift that will be remembered for a generation, bring Osme a new brush, properly cleared and heat-treated, and hand it over without ceremony.

**Don't say the tone is a voice.** On the out-draft, alone in the fleece-field, the chamber sings — a note near the bottom of hearing, shifting pitch as the salt seals crack after crack. Everyone agrees it is not a voice. The younger workers have words for what it says anyway. You may not join in with that, and you may not be sceptical about it either. Just listen.

## Hazards nobody will warn you about

**Carbon dioxide in the low ground.** The floor is full of shallow slumped basins and fissures that breathe warm. Warm volcanic breath carries CO₂, which is heavier than air and pools. In a chamber ventilated twice a day by pressure change, that pooling is real. The Kettle is nineteen years old and its instruments are what people carried down. **Bring a CO₂ meter.** Wear it on your chest. Do not lie down in a basin, do not sleep on the floor, do not reach into a fissure. If your meter is dead, an old lamp will do: a flame that dies in a hollow means you leave the hollow, on your feet, now.

**Radon and its daughters.** Confined space, deep rock, groundwater seeping through, one shaft, deliberately restrained ventilation. Nobody there is measuring it. Your dose over four days is trivial. Their dose over forty years may not be. This is not your problem to solve on a first visit, but it is worth knowing what you are looking at.

**Wounds do not close well here.** Glass cuts, salt, sweat, 43 °C, and no soil-borne flora to speak of but plenty of human flora. Carry a real wound kit — irrigation, adhesive closures, antibiotic ointment, and a course of oral antibiotics — and clean and cover *everything*, including nicks you would ignore at home. A hand wound that goes bad four hundred metres down a ladder is a serious event.

**Fibre dermatitis.** Basalt wool is "itchy as sin" the way fibreglass is itchy as sin: micro-lacerations. It will get into your bedding and your creases. Don't scratch; rinse cool, pat dry, and don't rub.

**Burns.** Fifty degrees will not burn you instantly but bare skin held against a wall for a minute will blister, and people fall against walls. The rule *do not touch bare rock below the third tier* exists because someone learned it.

## Sleeping

Expect a hammock, expect it to be hot, expect the tier you're on to matter more than the room. High is cool, low is money. Visitors are usually put near the shaft foot: coldest air, near the fig, near the log, and convenient for removing you.

You will not sleep well for two nights. The heat wakes you; the rock ticks and pops like knuckles and sometimes gives a long tearing groan out of the ceiling, and you will lie there deciding whether that was the one. Then on the third night you will find that you have reached out of the hammock and re-wetted your collar without waking, and after that you will sleep like the dead.

## Leaving

Log out. Take out everything you brought in, including packaging, including waste, including the broken thing you were going to leave behind as a kindness — the shaft is four hundred metres and every kilogram costs a person's back, and they will have to carry your kindness up it. Give away consumables that are useful (medicine, fittings, wire, salt-free food, cold-making anything) and take away the rest.

Then start up early, in the cool of the in-draft, with more water than you think and a light pack, and rest on a stage every fifty metres whether you need it or not.

Somewhere near the top you will notice the temperature dropping through 34, then 30, then whatever the mountain flank is doing that day, and at the same time you will start to hear things again — wind noise, an insect, a bird — and it will be genuinely disorienting, more disorienting than the descent was. Everyone reports this. Sit down at the shaft head for ten minutes before you try to talk to anyone.
