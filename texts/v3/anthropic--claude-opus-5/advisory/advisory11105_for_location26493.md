# Preparing to Visit the Chamber Under the Pan

## First, understand what you are actually being asked to do

You are not visiting a cave. You are being introduced into a closed measurement.

That room has spent a thousand years averaging the sky above it into a single number and holding it. It has no weather, no seasons, no predators, no decay. Its total inventory of events is: a root advancing a finger's width per decade, a grain of silt falling now and then, and a breath of air moving in and out twice a day because the whole atmosphere leans on it.

Into this you will bring roughly a hundred watts of continuous heat, forty grams an hour of water vapour, twenty litres an hour of carbon dioxide, a moving column of turbulent air, several thousand shed skin cells, some hair, and light.

Everything you prepare for should follow from that single fact: **in that room, you are the weather.** You are not there to survive it. It cannot hurt you much. You are there to be its largest event since the Neolithic, and the whole discipline of the visit is about being that event *deliberately, briefly, and cleanly.*

---

## Screening yourself

Be honest before you commit, because the shaft is a shoulder-width tube ten or eleven metres deep and it is a very poor place to discover things about yourself.

- **Dimensions.** Shoulders rotate; chest depth does not. Measure your chest front-to-back at full inhale. If you are above about 30 cm you should test the passage with a rigid gauge on a string before you commit your body to it. Empty every pocket. A low-profile harness, no bulky belt, no chest-mounted anything.
- **Claustrophobia.** Test it in advance, at home, in a tube of the same diameter, in the dark, for twenty minutes, while a friend times you. A panic attack at eight metres down a straight shaft is one of the few genuinely lethal outcomes available here.
- **Rope competence.** You must be able to ascend ten metres on a single rope in a confined space, without swinging room, with slightly numb fingers, on the second attempt, tired. Practise until it is boring.
- **Sensory tolerance.** The chamber is thermoneutral, silent, and dark. That is a flotation tank with rock around it. Expect time distortion, visual noise, hypnagogic imagery, the sense of a second presence. These are normal outputs of a human nervous system deprived of gradients. Know that in advance and they are interesting. Do not learn it there.
- **Tinnitus.** If you have it, you will meet it properly for the first time. Decide now that this is acceptable.

Do not go alone. Not because the chamber is dangerous, but because ten metres of vertical shoulder-width rock is, and because there is nobody else in that world who is coming.

## Timing

Three clocks matter, and none of them is your wristwatch.

**The season.** Go in the shoulder weeks — not the burning months, not the two weeks of frost. Not for the chamber's sake, which does not care, but for the surface camp, which has no shade but one waist-high fig that holds its leaves shut like a fist.

**The daily bar of light.** It arrives late — it reddens and tilts up the wall as it ends, which means it belongs to the afternoon. Work out the shaft's azimuth from the surface before you descend (a plumbed string and a compass, or the shadow of a stick over an hour). Then be down, seated, still, and dark-adapted at least forty minutes *before* it arrives. The bar itself is a few minutes long and it is the only scheduled event in that world. Arriving mid-way through it, panting, with a headlamp on, would be a small tragedy.

**The chamber's breath.** The twice-daily sigh from the shaft is the atmospheric tide — the whole air of that planet flexing on a twelve-hour rhythm. Pressure peaks mid-morning and mid-evening, troughs in mid-afternoon and the small hours. The room inhales as pressure rises and exhales as it falls, and the flow is strongest halfway between, so roughly early afternoon and around one in the morning. If you want to hear it, plan a long, motionless afternoon sit. Bring a barometer with a fine resolution; watching the needle drift and then hearing the shaft answer it is one of the better experiences available to a human being.

Consequence of all this: **you will be climbing out at dusk.** Plan for that. Rig your ascent in daylight, pre-place your surface lights, and accept a night on the pan.

## The surface: navigation and camp

The pan erases distance. There is one tree and it is shorter than you are; at three hundred paces it is a smudge, at eight hundred it is heat shimmer, and there is nothing else at all. This is the single most underrated hazard of the trip.

- **Read the pebbles.** Coarse at the rim, fine at the centre. This is a working compass for the one bit of information you need: am I going in or out? Kneel, look at the grain size, and you know. Learn to read it in five seconds before you rely on it at dusk.
- **Back-bearings and pacing.** Every departure from the fig gets a bearing and a pace count, written down, not remembered.
