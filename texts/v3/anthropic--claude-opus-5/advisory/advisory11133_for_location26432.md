# Preparing for the Warm Level

## First, understand what will actually kill you

Not the dark. Not the fall. Not the bats.

The thing that kills visitors here is **heat**, and it kills them by making them stupid twenty minutes before it makes them unconscious. Thirty-nine degrees at saturation means the wet-bulb temperature is within a degree or two of the point where a resting human body can no longer offload its own metabolic heat by any mechanism at all. Sweat does not evaporate in air that is already full. It just runs off you, taking your salt with it, and does nothing. You are, functionally, in a covered pot.

So the entire preparation for this place is organized around one number — **ninety minutes** — and around making sure that you are not the reason someone has to carry a body up two hundred steps of cut rock in the dark.

Everything else is secondary. Take it in that order.

## Four to six weeks out: the medical gate

You cannot do this trip next week. If someone tells you that you can, they are planning your funeral.

**Rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis.** Three doses, day 0, day 7, day 21 or 28. Under a ceiling of three hundred thousand bats, with pups falling into the guano, with grounded animals crawling on the catwalk at dusk, you will eventually have contact you didn't notice. Pre-vaccination does not make you safe; it makes the *post*-exposure treatment simple and survivable in a hamlet with one clinic, and it means you do not have to explain to a rural pharmacist in some other world's 1970s what immune globulin is.

**Tetanus** current. Everything metal down there is on its third life and every edge of it is rusted and organically filthy. **Hepatitis A**, because of the hand-to-mouth route off guano-contaminated everything.

**Talk to a doctor about your lungs and your heart, honestly.** If you are on beta blockers, anticholinergics, antihistamines, diuretics, SSRIs, stimulants, or anything that blunts sweating or drives heart rate, you are at materially higher risk of heat collapse and you need to know that before the third landing. If you have any degree of immunosuppression — steroids, chemotherapy, HIV, a transplant — **do not go**. Disseminated histoplasmosis from a bat cave is a genuinely lethal disease and this is one of the highest-inoculum environments on any world.

**Heat acclimatization: ten to fourteen days.** This is not optional and it cannot be faked. Ninety minutes a day of real exertion in real heat — a sauna with a stationary bike in it, a boiler room, a summer roofing job. Your body will learn to sweat earlier, sweat more, and conserve sodium. An unacclimatized person and an acclimatized person in that chamber are having two different experiences, and only one of them is walking out.

## Gear

Bring the minimum. Every kilogram you carry down converts to heat you cannot shed.

**Hard hat with a chinstrap.** Rule two. Not a bump cap. The drips are acidic and the domes shed.

**Two headlamps plus a third in a pocket, all sealed.** Hydrogen sulfide destroys electrical contacts — it blackens copper and silver in hours. Anything with an exposed spring terminal will fail. Bring spare cells in a doubled zip bag with silica gel and change them topside, never below.

**A personal H₂S monitor, clipped at the collar, and a hard rule about it.** The badge the crew wears is a colorimetric strip, which is a record, not a warning. You want something that screams. And understand the trap: **hydrogen sulfide destroys your sense of smell at exactly the concentrations that begin to matter.** When the match-smell fades, that is not good news. That is the worst news the room can give you. If the yellow duct stops moving air, you leave — not after you finish what you're doing.

An old trick worth carrying anyway: a strip of polished silver in your breast pocket. It will be brown by the second landing and black by lunch. It tells you nothing quantitative and it will make the crew like you.

**Clothes you intend to throw away.** Cotton, cheap, loose. Trousers tucked into boots and taped at the ankle with a wrap of duct tape — the crickets are harmless and you will still lose your composure the fourth time one goes down your collar, and losing your composure on a swaying catwalk with three missing panels is the actual hazard. Not synthetics you love; the drips will put brown-edged holes in them. No leather that matters. No wool.

**Boots that can be scrubbed and disinfected.** Rubber will parboil your feet in an hour; a synthetic-upper work boot with a scrubbable sole is the compromise. Steel toe if the crew wears it.

**Gloves — two pairs.** Thin nitrile against the acid and the biology, a cheap cotton or coated work glove over the top for the grating and the rail. Change the nitrile when it tears. **Do not steady yourself on the wall,** gloved or otherwise; that paste is not plaster, it is the wall's dissolution product, and behind it there is nothing structural.

**Eye protection, and a decision about your glasses.** Your glasses will fog on the first landing and stay fogged. There is no anti-fog coating that beats saturated air twenty degrees above your lens temperature. Options: go without and accept blur, or bring a cheap pair you can wipe constantly with a chamois inside a plastic bag. **Do not wear contact lenses.** Acidic condensation aerosol, filthy hands, and a lens you cannot remove in a place where you cannot wash — that is how people come home with a corneal ulcer.

**Respiratory protection, understood honestly.** A P3/N100 with a good fit is the correct answer for histoplasmosis, and wearing one at thirty-nine degrees and one hundred percent humidity is genuinely miserable and adds real cardiovascular load. The compromise the professionals make: wear it for anything that disturbs the guano — walking the floor, clearing the intake screens, moving spoil in the tipper — and pull it down when you are standing still on the catwalk at the far end. Note that it does **nothing** for H₂S. Two entirely separate problems.

**Water and salt.** A litre in, a litre out with you, and use the crew's salt-tablet cooler at the head of the stair the way they do — before descending, and again the moment you surface. Not alcohol the night before. Not a heavy meal.

**A camera, if you must, prepared correctly.** Seal it in a bag at the surface and *do not open the bag until it has warmed to cave temperature* — twenty minutes at least. Open it cold and moisture will condense inside the lens and on the sensor board and it will never work again. Expect to lose it anyway. One-tenth of the light your eyes want means a tripod, and a tripod means standing still on a swaying catwalk, which means arranging permission.

**No rings.** Your fingers will swell. Rings plus grating plus a fall equals a degloved finger, and silver rings will be black anyway.

## The three rules, and the one they don't state

**Ninety minutes.** Set a timer you cannot ignore and treat it as an alarm, not a suggestion. Better: weigh yourself before and after, dry, on the crew's scale if they have one. Two percent of body mass lost is your ceiling for the day. Most first-timers do fifty useful minutes and spend the rest getting down, getting back, and sitting against the maroon pumps breathing.

**Hard hat, always,** from the third landing down.

**You do not walk the catwalk during exodus.** Find out *that day* when it starts — it moves with the season and the crew know it to within five minutes. Forty minutes of dry roar you feel in the sternum, a pressure change, lamps strobing behind bodies. Get to the wall, get your back against something that isn't dissolving, keep your mouth shut, and let it happen. Do not try to walk up the adit against the current: the snakes are out of their crevices with their forebodies in the air, striking blind, and they will not distinguish your ankle from a myotis.

And the unstated one: **do not lean on anything, do not touch your face, do not eat or drink below except at the pumps with washed hands.** The route into you is your hands.

## Arriving as an outsider

This is a working pump station, not a site. Three people are down there on a shift and they have a shift book with four decades of entries in six hands, most of them four words long. You are an interruption in that.

Come with a reason that fits the world you have arrived in — hydrology student, inspectorate, spa company, a photographer with a commission. Come with the local paper to support it. Have local currency, and the right decade of it.

Bring something. Coffee, cigarettes if that is the local vice, a decent candle for the flange. Ask before you photograph the shrine, and do not photograph the taped picture of the daughter at all unless someone offers her name first. Do not write in the shift book. Do not make the joke about the ferns; ask who started watering them, and listen.

Accept the towel around the neck. Take your shirt off if the crew have. There is no dignity to be preserved down there beyond trousers-in-boots, and standing on a catwalk in a clean pressed shirt while three shirtless men clear a screen is its own kind of insult.

## Coming out, and the week after

**Surface slowly.** Two hundred steps in that state, with your heart already running high, is where the cardiac events happen. Rest on the landings. Drink and salt at the top before you do anything else.

**Decontaminate.** Bag the clothes at the head of the stair; wash them separately in the hottest water available or burn them. Scrub the boots — soles, seams, laces — and disinfect them. This matters in both directions: you may be the vector that introduces a white-nose-type fungus to a bat population in a world that has never met it, and multiverse travelers are structurally the worst biosecurity risk that has ever existed. Clean your gear before you go down as thoroughly as after.

**Shower. Ears, nose, nails.** Fungal infections of the ear canal and the toenails are the most common souvenir.

**Then watch yourself for three weeks.** Histoplasmosis incubates one to three weeks and presents as flu — fever, dry cough, chest ache, headache, drenching sweats. If it comes, you must be able to say the sentence *"I inhaled aerosolized bat guano in a cave three weeks ago"* to whatever doctor you can reach, in whatever world, in a language they speak. Write it on a card and carry it. Without that sentence you will be treated for pneumonia and it will not work.

**Any bite, scratch, or unexplained puncture, or waking up having slept anywhere a bat could reach you: post-exposure rabies treatment immediately, even fully pre-vaccinated.** There is no observation period on this. There is no waiting to see.

## What to expect of yourself

The chamber is not frightening in the way people prepare for. It is not tight. It is a cathedral. What gets people is the *combination* — the blindness of your fogged glasses, the impossibility of ordinary speech over the granular chatter, the constant fine motion of droplets in the beam, the sensation of small legs on the back of the neck, and the ninety-minute clock ticking behind all of it. People do not panic in the Warm Level. They go quiet and slow and slightly foolish, and then they make one wrong step near a taped panel.

So rehearse the small things. Rehearse working with useless vision. Rehearse not flinching. Rehearse the discipline of one hand on the rail at all times and never the wall.

And build in the one thing no one plans for: after you surface, you will be soaked for an hour, shaking slightly, ravenously thirsty, and completely unable to describe the ceiling to anyone. Give yourself the evening. Do not schedule your jump out for that night. Sit above the town and watch the column come up out of the raise and spiral out over the roofs, which is the part you actually came for, and which you cannot see from inside.
