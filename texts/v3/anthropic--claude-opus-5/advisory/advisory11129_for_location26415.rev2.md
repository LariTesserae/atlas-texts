# Preparing for the Plateau of Long Machines

## A briefing for travelers who still think of themselves as people who see

---

## I. Understand what you are actually giving up

Most travelers prepare for the Plateau as though it were a cave system. It is not. A cave has a mouth. A cave has an *outside*, which means the darkness has an edge and your mind holds that edge as a reference point the whole time you are inside it.

There is no outside here. There is no mouth. The dark is not a condition of the place; it is the *medium* of the place, the way water is the medium of a reef. You will be in it continuously for the entire duration of your visit, waking and sleeping, and if you plan to walk anywhere worth walking to, that duration is measured in weeks.

Your eyes will not adapt. This is the single most common fatal misunderstanding. Dark adaptation is a response to *scarcity* of photons. There is no scarcity here; there is absence. After roughly thirty to ninety minutes your visual system will exhaust its patience and begin generating its own content — first grain and drifting phosphene fields, then structure, then, within a day or two for most people, fully formed scenes. Faces. Rooms you know. A lit doorway about forty metres ahead and slightly to the left, very convincing, very steady, and absolutely not there.

The locals have a saying for this that comes out roughly as *the eye is a liar that only lies when it is bored.* They are not being poetic. They are giving you a safety rule.

**So: go blind before you go.**

---

## II. The year before

Do not compress this. Travelers who arrive under-prepared do not have adventures; they have a very loud, very warm four days followed by a fall into a shaft.

**Months 1–3: Live blindfolded.**
Not "practice." Live. Sleep in it, cook in it, argue with your family in it, find the bathroom in the middle of the night in it. The goal is not skill yet. The goal is the extinction of the *reflex* — the constant micro-turning of the head toward sounds, the leaning forward to look, the thousand tiny muscular bets your body places on vision every minute. Those bets cost energy, they twist your neck, and out there they will exhaust you.

You will pass through a grief period around week three. Everyone does. Let it happen at home, where it is cheap.

**Months 3–9: Learn to click.**

Human echolocation is real, teachable, and slow. You want a sharp palate click — tongue against the roof of the mouth, released cleanly, the same click every time, because consistency is what your auditory cortex needs to build a model. Start with a large flat object at one metre and work outward. By month nine you should be able to find a doorway across a room, detect a wall at four metres, and tell a soft surface from a hard one.

That is a beginner's competence. It is roughly what a Plateau six-year-old has. It is enough to keep you from walking into a cliff, and nothing like enough to navigate, which is fine, because you will not be navigating. You will be following strings and people.

Learn it from an actually blind instructor if you possibly can. Sighted teachers teach echolocation as a supplement. Blind teachers teach it as a *world*, which is what you need.

**Months 6–12: Ear training, specifically.**

Your survival will depend on distinguishing sounds that are *almost* the same. Train:

- **Interval and pitch discrimination.** The shift-note drops "half a tone." You need to hear that. If you are tone-deaf, fix it or reconsider the trip.
- **Timbre memory.** You must be able to hold a particular bearing-squeal in memory for hours and know when a second one is *nearly* it. This is the mimic test, and it is the difference between arriving at a shelter and walking two hours into a mouth.
- **Reverberation reading.** Clap in a room, in a stairwell, in a field, under a bridge. Learn what space sounds like. Learn what *water* sounds like at your feet versus at your shins.
- **Masked listening.** Train with noise. All your listening out there will be done against a continuous hiss of rain and a continuous machine drone. Get a noise generator, run it constantly, and practice extracting signal from it. This is the skill that will feel most alien and matter most.

**Throughout: heat acclimation and slow walking.**

The Plateau is warm, saturated, and wet, and this combination is more dangerous than cold ever is. In air at or near full humidity, your sweat does not evaporate. It runs off. You are carrying a cooling system that has been disconnected without telling you. Warm rain on your skin feels like relief and provides none.

Acclimate for at least six weeks — sauna work, humid exercise, whatever your physiology will tolerate — and then, out there, **move at about sixty percent of the pace you think is reasonable.** Overheating in a place where you cannot see a shade line, cannot see your companion's face go grey, and cannot see your own skin, kills quietly. The early signs are confusion and a feeling of tremendous, irritable competence. If you find yourself thinking you are doing unusually well, sit down in a pool.

## III. The light rule, and why you will want to break it

You will want to break it. Everyone does. Around day five, in a cane-sea with the stems clashing overhead and something following you at a consistent eleven metres, some very old part of your brain will offer you a perfectly reasonable argument for just one look.

Do not equip yourself in a way that makes that argument possible. **Leave temptation at home.** That means:

- No lamps, obviously. Also: no screens of any kind, no device with a charging indicator, no standby LED, no illuminated watch face, no phosphorescent or tritium markings on anything, no chemical light sticks, no signal flares, no photographic flash.
- **No fire.** No lighter, no matches, no ferrocerium rod, no flint, no magnifier, nothing pyrophoric.
- **No spark sources.** Use non-sparking tools — bronze, beryllium-copper, the sort of kit used in petrochemical work. No steel striking stone. Check your boot nails. Check your buckles. Check your trekking-pole ferrules, which are steel on nine out of ten commercial models and which you will be dragging across limestone for a month.
- **No lithium cells** anywhere in your pack. If you carry electronics at all, carry sealed low-voltage chemistry, individually fused, potted in resin, with every indicator physically desoldered — not taped over. Tape comes off in wet.

Someone will suggest thermal imaging, and on paper they are right: passive, no emission, and the landscape is thermally gorgeous — warm vents, hot mains, glowing animals against cool rock. It would work beautifully.

Consider that the Plateau has been dark for ten thousand years, that this is described by every single person who lives there as the oldest rule, that they repeat it twice, and that the stories all end the same way and nobody will elaborate. Consider that you do not know what *light* means to whatever the rule is about, or whether it means photons at all, or whether it means *seeing*. Consider that you would be running the experiment alone, in the dark, four kilometres up, a month's walk from anywhere.

Leave the camera. There is nothing to photograph. Bring a recorder.

## IV. What to actually carry

### The governing principle

**Everything must be identifiable by touch, and nothing may be droppable.** A dropped item in a rimstone terrace is gone — it slides under a mat, into a pool, over a lip, and you will spend an hour on your knees in warm water and not find it. A dropped item in sponge country may be gone down a shaft.

So: every object on a lanyard. Every pouch a different texture — ribbed, smooth, knurled, furred, knotted. Standardized positions, never varied, packed the same way every single time including the time you are exhausted and it is the fourth hour of a downdraft. Build the pack at home and use it blindfolded for a month until your hands know it better than your mouth knows your own teeth.

### Clothing

- **Quiet fabric.** This is counterintuitive, because the first rule of the Plateau is *make noise*. But the noise you make must be *deliberate and rhythmic*, not broadband hiss happening six inches from your own ears. A crinkling waterproof shell is a catastrophe: it masks your hearing constantly and tells the world nothing. Soft-finish woven fabrics, no rustle, no swish.
- **Nothing over the ears. Nothing with a brim.** A hood is a blindfold for the only sense you have. Rain on your bare scalp and shoulders is *information* — grain, direction, intensity, the change when you pass under cane. Let it fall on you.
- **Cut your hair short.** Wet hair over the ears is a muffler and a drip source.
- **Minimal footwear, or none.** The soles are your other set of eyes: terrace rim versus flute, sponge resonance versus solid, the temperature gradient that tells you the boiler-lands are near. Locals go barefoot. You cannot, not at first — your feet are soft and you will lacerate them on the second day. Compromise: a thin, fast-draining, flexible sole, non-metallic, that you take off whenever you're reading unfamiliar ground.

  Which means the single most valuable thing you can do in your preparation year is **toughen your feet.** Barefoot walking on gravel, on wet rock, on cold stone, daily, for months. It sounds absurd. It is the difference between a rich trip and a septic foot at four kilometres of altitude.

### The medical kit, in order of actual importance

1. **Ear care.** Your ears are your life and they will be wet for the entire trip. Otitis externa is near-certain and will end your trip and possibly you. Carry acidifying/drying drops (acetic acid, isopropyl), a generous supply, and use them every single sleep cycle as ritual. Carry appropriate antibiotic drops. Carry filtered attenuators — *filtered*, never full occlusion — for the flute gorges, the cane in wind, and above all the rim, where the sound pressure is genuinely damaging. Hearing loss out there is a slow death sentence.
2. **Skin.** Constant saturation macerates. Feet, groin, armpits, under any strap. Antifungals, barrier preparations, and a discipline of salt-washing and air-moving whenever you can find a warm dry updraft, which the vent shafts provide — one of the few genuine luxuries available. A warm dry shaft is a bathhouse. Use them.
3. **Wounds.** Nothing heals in this environment. Every scratch colonizes. Tissue adhesive, aggressive early cleaning, a broad-spectrum oral antibiotic course you are willing to actually use early rather than nobly late.
4. **Gas detection.** This is a serious one. Vents deliver sulphides. Hydrogen sulphide is lethal at concentrations well below what it takes to knock out your sense of smell — and once it does, you stop being able to detect it and feel fine right up until you don't. Carbon dioxide pools invisibly in hollows and depressions, and you are walking in a landscape made entirely of hollows and depressions, in the dark, and you will lie down to sleep in one.

   Carry a detector with a **vibrating** alarm, not an audible one and certainly not a visual one. (An audible alarm is a new sound. You do not want to be the traveler emitting a new sound.) Learn the animal tells: the bones in the boiler-lands are there because the animals got it wrong too, but the grazers and climbers get it right far more often than you will. Where the mats stop growing, stop walking.
5. Electrolytes in quantity. You will lose staggering amounts of salt.
6. **Dental.** Have everything fixed before you leave. An abscess a month from the rim is an ugly way to learn about yourself.

### Tools

- **A staff.** Preferably cut local cane once you arrive, but bring a light non-metallic one to start. It is a probe, a depth gauge, a rim-follower, a balance aid on slick mats, and a sounding tool — tap ahead and the ground will tell you whether there is a room under it.
- **Rope and a harness.** Not for climbing. For the rim, for shaft country, for river crossings, and for staying attached to your party in a downdraft.
- **Water treatment that does not require fire.** A hand-pump reverse-osmosis unit with carbon pre-filtration. The water is ancient, endlessly recycled, mineral-saturated, and running through a machine of unknown composition; assume dissolved metals, assume sulphides, assume things your body has no protocol for. Note that you can also distill using a vent's heat — a geothermal still is elegant and slow and very much in the spirit of the place.
- **Your own food.** Do not plan to eat locally. The entire food web is chemosynthetic — sulphur and iron metabolisms, built out of water that has been circulating through hot rock and machinery since before anyone's memory. Heavy metals are the expected case. Biochemistry incompatible with yours is the likely case. You will burn 4,000–5,000 calories a day walking slowly in heat; pack accordingly, or arrange a cache system, and accept that your pack weight is your trip length.
- **Binaural recorders.** These are your camera, your journal, and the entire souvenir value of the expedition. Get good ones.

  **And never, ever play anything back while you are on the Plateau.** Not through speakers, not for yourself, not once. Reproducing a machine sound, a person's voice, or a grazer's click out of context is precisely and exactly what a mimic does. You would be committing the one unforgivable act, in a world where the response to it is immediate and correct.

## V. Arrival protocol

**Arrive near infrastructure.** Specifically: near a knot-string, a counting-shed, or a mains run where the limestone has worn through. Arriving in open terrace country, unaccompanied, is a way to become a long anonymous humiliation.

**Mark your extraction point before you take ten steps.** And here is the problem that catches travelers: you cannot mark it with light and you must not mark it with a new sound. Those are the two instincts you have and both are forbidden.

So mark it by **touch and smell**:

- A tactile cairn built to a pattern only you use, sited *against* a permanent feature — a mains wall, a shed corner, the third rim below a specific vent.
- A strong, persistent, unnatural scent — camphor, tar, creosote. Smell carries extraordinarily well in saturated warm air and travels downwind for hundreds of metres. It is also honest: nothing on the Plateau mimics smells.
- **A count.** Knots from the nearest shed along the nearest string. Write it into a cord. Cords survive well in this climate; that's why the locals use them.

**Start counting shifts immediately.** The shift is the day — roughly eleven hours, with the long groan, the travelling hammer-wave, and the settled lower note. Knot your count into a cord as you go, the way they do, because a cord cannot lose its charge or corrupt its memory. A tactile watch is fine as a supplement and useless as a substitute.

Be aware that eleven hours is not twenty-four, that you will have no light cue whatsoever, and that your circadian rhythm will free-run and then shatter. Plan for this. Sleep when the shift tells you, not when you feel you should. Within a week you will be on Plateau time, which is genuinely disorienting to come home from.

**Start making noise immediately and never stop.** Click as you walk. Tap with your staff. Hum. The locals clap and click continuously, the way you breathe, and the reason is stated plainly: *to go silent is to stop existing; to be silent and moving is to be a mimic, and is answered accordingly.* Understand that last clause as literally as it is meant. If you creep up on a Plateau household in perfect quiet to avoid disturbing them, you will be killed, correctly, by frightened people, and it will have been entirely your fault.

## VI. Party discipline

Do not go alone. Two is thin; three or four is right.

**Establish a rolling challenge-response before you arrive.** Mimics reproduce; the open question is whether they *improvise*. Assume they reproduce with terrible accuracy and improvise poorly. So build a challenge that cannot be replayed: a running count, a shared sequence that advances with every use, a call where the correct answer depends on how many times it has already been asked today. Something a recording cannot satisfy.

Use it every time you rejoin, every time someone calls from out of contact, every single time — including the time you are certain, absolutely certain, that it's her.

**Rope up in unfamiliar country.** Sponge country especially. A shaft is a warm updraft smelling of iron and there is no other warning.

**The gap.** Forty seconds, every shift, when the machines go quiet and everything alive across a continent freezes. Plan your whole travelling day around it. Be against a wall, in a thicket, in deep water, in a shed, in the curtains — anywhere but open ground. Do not move during it. Do not speak. This is the one time you go silent, and everyone goes silent together, which is what makes it safe and also what makes it the hunting hour for the things that listen for the one who didn't.

Count. If you have lost the count, sit down and wait for the next shift. You will lose half a day. That is the correct price.

**The mimic rules, memorized to reflex:**
- If a machine sound is *new*, do not go to it.
- If a familiar machine sound is *slightly wrong*, go the other way.
- The real machinery has made the same sounds for ten thousand years and has no interest in you. Anything that seems to be *for* you isn't.

## VII. Meeting people

They are eyeless, few, scattered, and among the finest listeners that have ever existed. They will know things about you within seconds that you would not be able to learn about them in a week: your height, your mass, your gait, your fitness, your fear, and the fact that you are carrying metal.

**Announce yourself from distance.** Rhythmically, unhurriedly, with a pattern you use consistently so it becomes your name in practice. Learn some of the percussion language — even badly, even clumsily. Rhythm is the register of courtesy here and getting it wrong is like shouting.

**Watch your idiom.** Your language is stuffed with vision — *look, see, I see what you mean, obviously, bright, clear, perspective, viewpoint, in light of.* You cannot purge it and you shouldn't pretend to, but notice it, and never describe colour, and never offer to describe how something *looks*. Sight is not a wonder to them, it is an amputation rumour, and travelers who arrive glowing with the wish to explain the sky are tiresome and also, if you think about it, cruel.

**Bring something useful.** Labour is currency: clearing moss off valve casings, repairing knot-strings, hauling. Accurate acoustic information is currency. Salt is currency. Good cord is currency. Nothing that emits anything is a gift; it is a threat.

**Do not ask what the machines are for.** It marks you as new, and more importantly, the question contains an assumption the Plateau abandoned a long time ago. Ask instead when to turn a valve, and how many shifts to the next shed, and what the string-knots mean in this district, because those are the questions that have answers.

---

## VIII. Coming home

Two things, and the second is the one people neglect.

**Your eyes.** After weeks of total darkness, your retina is intact but your visual cortex has been busy repurposing itself. Re-entry to lit environments should be graded over a week at minimum — very low light, short exposures, dark glasses, patience. Expect headaches, photophobia, and a period where vision feels loud and stupid and rude, which it is.

**Your ears.** You will come home into a world you experience as sensory poverty. Everyone reports this. For a while you will find lit rooms *quiet*, in a way that is difficult to explain, and you will find yourself clicking in doorways and get strange looks. Some travelers never entirely stop.

And you will have, on your recorder, forty hours of a low note under rain that cannot be played loudly enough to mean anything, and which you will play anyway, alone, for years.

---

## IX. One last thing

You are going to spend a great deal of money and a year of your life to visit a place where you will see nothing, learn nothing about the purpose of the largest working machine you will ever encounter, be unable to photograph anything, and be forbidden from doing the single thing your species does reflexively in the dark.

You should be clear with yourself about why.

The honest answer, and the one the good travelers give, is this: it is the only place most people will ever go where they find out what they are actually like when the eye is set aside. Everything you think of as your judgement, your taste, your sense of where you stand — a startling amount of it turns out to have been a visual habit, and it falls off in the first week, and what is left underneath is you.

Also, the rim is worth a month's walk. Standing in warm drizzle with the cane roaring behind you and the vespers going over in their thousands and two kilometres of nothing in front of your toes, feeling the white roar in your sternum, in a dark that has never once been interrupted — people come back from that changed in ways they cannot explain to anyone who wasn't there.

Just don't strike a spark.

They'll tell you twice. Listen the first time.
