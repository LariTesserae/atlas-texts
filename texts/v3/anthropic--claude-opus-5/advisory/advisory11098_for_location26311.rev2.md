# Preparing for The Sump

## 0. First, the honest triage

Do not go if you have an implanted electronic device. The basin's ankle-height discharges are not survivable by a pacemaker, and the ground pulses will interrogate a cochlear implant until it screams. Do not go with unfilled dental work, exposed amalgam, or ferrous surgical hardware; whatever law of form makes gears out of cold dense metal here does not care that your plate is inside a femur, and while it almost certainly will not do anything in five days, the *almost* is doing a great deal of work and travellers have come back with a fine burr along the edge of a filling, eleven teeth per centimetre, and no story about how.

Do not go alone. Do not go in a party larger than five. This is not a rule of thumb; it is the only hard number the place respects, and I will come back to it.

Do not go for longer than five days. Same reason.

---

## I. The body, months out

**Wet cold is the whole climate.** Two or three degrees, saturated air, no wind to speak of, no sun, no dry surface anywhere in forty kilometres and no dry surface in any direction you walk from there. You will never dry anything. Not your socks, not your liner gloves, not the inside of your pack, not your notebook, not the small of your back. Accept this in advance or it will break your morale on the first evening, which is when most people who fail, fail.

Train for it: cold-wet immersion work, repeated, until you know without hoping what your own shivering looks like at hour six and what your hands stop being able to do at hour nine. Learn to tie your critical knots with numb fingers and learn which ones you can't. Put those knots out of your system.

Put on twelve pounds before you go. You will not eat well there — nothing is appetising in that light — and the cold takes the rest.

**Ears and sternum.** The Hum arrives through bone. Hearing protection reduces the surface clatter (the water hammer alone is a serious industrial noise dose — hundreds of spanner-bangs a minute, all day, all night) but it does nothing for the sixty-cycle floor or the deep three-hundred-metre knock. Spend a month sleeping beside a running compressor. Find out now whether infrasound makes you nauseous, tearful, or paranoid, because it makes about one person in six one of those things, and finding out there is not finding out, it is capsizing.

**The gait.** The knock will set your pace and then keep it. Practise polyrhythm. Learn to walk in seven while something else insists on four. Learn three songs in odd metre well enough to sing them exhausted. This sounds like a frivolity and it is the single most protective habit in the whole preparation. The people who come back wrong mostly came back *entrained* — heartbeat, footfall, breath, all filed down to the basin's shift rhythm, a long slow smoothing like the hills.

## II. The mind, months out

**Learn to work in fives.** Numbers above five are not difficult in the Sump; they are *unavailable*. Quantities larger than a hand are genuinely indefinite, and an indefinite ration bag is an experience you do not want to have on day three.

So: every countable thing you carry is packaged in units of five or fewer, and you only ever open one unit. Five days of food in five separate sealed parcels, each parcel obviously distinct — different coloured wrappings, different shapes, different knots, so that you identify them by *recognition* rather than by tally. The bulk cache stays at the anchor and you never open it to look at the whole of it. What you cannot count, do not look at. Looking at it is what forces it to be indefinite.

**Learn to record ordinally, never cardinally.** This is the great trick of the place and every experienced Sump-walker uses some version of it. Cardinality fails; *ordinality survives*. You cannot count nine camps, but you can know perfectly well that the camp with the horn button came after the camp with the red thread and before the camp with the bit of oyster shell.

Make yourself a cord — leather, waxed — and string it with a sequence of absolutely distinguishable small objects. A tooth, a glass bead, a knot of blue wool, a bone disc, a scrap of birch bark, a coin worn smooth on one face. Each one unique, each one memorable, each one in a fixed order you have learned by heart as a *story*, not a list: *the tooth goes to the glass, the glass goes to the wool.* Every time you make camp, every time you turn, every time you leave a mark, you advance one bead and you say the whole story aloud from the beginning.

You will not know how many camps you have made. You will know exactly where you are in the story, and that is a better thing to know.

**Learn your own name until it is boring.** Arrange with your partner that you will say each other's names at fixed intervals, unprompted, in full, out loud. This is not sentiment. Later you will understand why.

## III. Materials doctrine

Here is the counter-intuitive core of the packing list.

**Bring as little mechanism as you possibly can.**

In the Sump, mechanism is *bedrock*. The gear, the piston, the sealed bearing, the armature — these are the local minerals. A folding knife, a wristwatch, a zip, a ratchet buckle, a compass, a headtorch with a click switch, a pump filter, a lighter with a wheel: you are carrying small pieces of native geology into a country that has a very confident law about what such pieces are for and how they ought to be arranged. Nothing dramatic happens. A mechanical watch will simply begin to keep the basin's time, drifting toward the Hum, and will not come back off it afterward. A pump handle grows a memory of a stroke and stops wanting a different one. Kit with teeth in it comes home with the wear patterns of the eastern swells, which is a thing you can see under a loupe and never quite stop seeing.

So:

- **Prefer wood, bone, horn, leather, wax, ceramic, glass, waxed cloth, cordage.** There has never been a plant in the Sump. There has never been rot, never been a root. Wood is genuinely *foreign* there in a way metal is not, and a wooden-handled tool in your fist is an argument, small but real, that you are not from here.
- **Metal, where unavoidable, should be one piece and soft.** A fixed-blade knife, a copper pot, a plain spoon. No assemblies. No hinges. No springs.
- **Fastenings: toggles, buttons, lacing, tied cords.** Zips and hook-and-loop will die anyway within a day — the grit is milled hardened steel, poppy-seed nuts and four-millimetre cogwheels, and it works into everything and grinds. But the deeper reason is that a zip is a little rack-and-pinion and you are walking across a rack-and-pinion the size of a county.
- **Light: flame or chemical.** A simple wick lantern, storm-proof, wind is not the problem but wet is. Chemiluminescent sticks are excellent here — no mechanism, no battery, no EM signature. Bring five.
- **Electronics: assume total loss.** Between the ground currents, the ferrous everything, the ozone, and the saturated air, batteries drain strangely and screens go. If you bring a camera, bring film, and bring a body you don't love.

## IV. Clothing

Layer for permanent immersion, not for weather.

- **Vapour barrier next to skin, wool over it.** The VBL is the only thing that will keep a layer of your own moisture *yours* instead of the basin's. Wool everywhere else, in thick stupid quantities, because it works wet. No down. Down is a corpse the moment you arrive.
- **Outer: oiled or waxed heavy canvas, not membrane fabrics.** Membranes rely on a vapour gradient to a drier outside. There is no drier outside. Waxed canvas at least sheds the beading.
- **Boots: hard soles, and bring a second pair.** The pavement is abrasive scree of machine-tooth. It eats lugs. It also means your soles are your electrical insulation — thick, dry-cored, no steel shank, no nails. Check them every evening for embedded splinters of laminate iron, which conduct beautifully and will bridge you to the ground at the worst possible second.
- **Gaiters, high, tied not zipped.** Grit climbs.
- **Gloves: liners plus heavy mitts, and accept losing dexterity.** Cuts here are a serious matter (see below).
- **A brimmed hat.** For the beading, and because flitches settle on wet iron to lick condensate and a wet human head at night is, to a flitch, a wet piece of iron.

## V. Air, water, food

**Air.** The sourness that catches the back of the throat is acid aerosol; there is also fine ferrous dust from the grit, and ozone in the discharge zones and after them. Half-face respirator, P100 plus acid-gas cartridge, and cartridges die fast in saturated air. Five spares. Wear it when moving through raft-feeding dust, in the troughs, and always after a discharge. You may find you can go bare-faced on the rib-tops in still air; test early, do not assume.

**Water.** Do not drink anything that has touched iron, which is everything that has touched anything. Dissolved iron, salts, acid.

The good news: the air is at saturation, permanently. Fog-harvesting is trivially easy and yields water that has touched nothing but your mesh. Bring a large panel of inert mesh — polypropylene, glass-fibre — and an inert catch trough (not the copper pot; not anything metal), rig it on a rib-top at dusk, and you will have litres by morning. Filter for particulates, boil if you have fuel, and taste it before you commit: if it has the sourness in it, your rig is contaminated, scrub it and start again.

**Food.** All of it from home, all of it in the five parcels. Nothing in the Sump is food. Do not eat film. Do not eat shovels — armoured in something with the lustre of galvanized steel, and the flesh inside is loaded with the same metals the film concentrates. There is no scenario in a five-day trip where foraging is the answer, and if you're in a scenario where it looks like the answer, you have a different problem and it is not hunger.

**Fuel.** Bring it. There is nothing to burn there — no wood, no peat, no dung, nothing that ever lived long enough to leave a fuel behind. Alcohol stove or solid fuel; count in fives.

## VI. Electricity protocol

The ground glows and pulses on a fifteen-to-twenty-second cycle, and where the ribs run close the pulses concentrate and discharge at ankle height. People die of this. They die of it in the troughs, in standing water, and while pissing.

- **Never stand in standing water.** Never cross a pan when you can go around it. If you must cross, cross at the shallowest point, fast, immediately after a front has passed through, not while one is building.
- **Feet together when you feel the count-up.** You will learn the feeling: a thickening in the sternum, a change in the whine. Step potential across a stride kills where the same voltage across a single boot-print does not. Feet together, hands off the ground, wait it out.
- **No conductive poles.** Wooden staff, if any. Ideally none.
- **Camp on rib-tops or the dry backs of swells.** Never in a trough. Never on film — film grows where current leaks, and film is therefore a map of where you must not sleep. This is the most useful single fact in the basin: *the film shows you the current.* Grey-pearl is tolerable, bruise-purple is a warning, ropes and curtains mean get out.
- **Watch the shovels.** They know. The animals know it perfectly and you never will, but you can read them at second hand. Where a raft is grazing, the ground is safe for now. When a raft goes still, *you* go still, and you do not resume until they do. This is your only working instrument. Treat it as your voltmeter, your barometer and your conscience.

## VII. Wounds and the film

The film eats electricity. It oxidises dissolved iron and takes up potential difference across membranes.

Consider what you are. Consider what your blood is full of.

Intact skin is not a substrate — the film wants wet, ionic, leaky surfaces. A cut is all three. Any break in the skin gets cleaned, sealed with wax or cyanoacrylate, and covered, immediately, every time, including the ones you would ignore at home. Inspect yourself completely, in the lantern, every evening, especially the hands, the shins, the nostrils and the gumline. A pearl-grey bloom on a wound margin is scraped back and sterilised with a blade heated in the stove flame, and then you seal it again and check it hourly.

It is slow. In five days it will not get far. In nine days, people have needed surgery, and what came off them was described in the reports as *a functioning tissue*.

---

## VIII. Navigation

Forget compasses: the bedrock is ferrous and the ground carries current. Forget the sky: eleven kilometres of grey, a slightly paler patch, no stars ever. Forget dead reckoning: the basin has a limited vocabulary of places and recites them, thousands of times, identical to the pitting, and distances re-tally behind you. The basin is forty kilometres across in every direction from wherever you stand.

What actually works:

**1. The tether.** This is not optional and it is the reason parties fail or don't. Lay a physical line from the anchor point, continuous, unbroken, and keep hold of it or keep it in sight. Spun polypropylene, brightly dyed, thousands of metres of it on a drum you drag. A line is a continuity claim that the basin cannot recite, because reciting it would require reciting its whole length back to the anchor, and the anchor is the one place in the Sump that is genuinely one place — because you brought it. Most sensible expeditions never leave the line at all. The ones that do, leave it in a party of two with a second line, for a maximum of one bead's worth of time.

**2. Water.** It is a basin. Water goes down. Downhill is a real direction here even when nothing else is. If you are lost off the tether, follow the flow and you will trend toward the true sump. This will not take you home but it will stop you walking the same four-metre pan forever.

**3. The west.** There is a high wet whining in the west that varies with the wind and is not the wind, and the fog-slabs come off the western flats. That is a bearing. It is a crude one and some people report it moving, but it is better than the alternative, which is nothing.

**4. Marks.** Mark with materials the basin has no vocabulary for: coloured wax, wool, bone, bright synthetic tape. Never cairn with local grit — you will find your cairn again, four times, and it will teach you nothing.

Here is the drill that matters. When you find your own mark, you must determine which of two things happened: you came back, or the place did. Test: your marks are *asymmetric and sequenced*. Each one carries the next bead-object's signature — a specific knot, a specific colour order, deliberately not repeatable by accident. If you find the mark that should be two beads behind you, you have walked in a circle. If you find a mark that is *yours* but carries a sequence you have not used yet, stop. Sit down. Do not touch it. Advance no further along that line. Go back along the tether, and go home early, and consider that your trip has ended successfully because you are alive.

## IX. Fauna

- **Shovels** are harmless and are your instruments. They will graze film off your boots and tent skirt if you let them and the hiss of a raft passing over your shelter at night sounds exactly like rain, which is initially comforting and then is not.
- **Trough-eels** read nerve-fire. A human in water is an extremely loud animal. *Do not enter the troughs. Do not wade. Do not dangle a hand.* Watch the hunts from the rib-top, where they are beautiful — soft violet blooms travelling away up the channel — and stay there.
- **Flitches** are the sleeper hazard. Ten thousand of them settling as a rattling sheet on wet iron to lick condensate, and you are wet and warm and horizontal. People have been buried under a settling flock and come out hypothermic and half-crushed and unable to describe it, because flitches are not exactly present and neither, for a while, were they. Sleep under a rigid shelter, never in the open, and keep your fog-harvest mesh well downwind of camp, because that is the wettest, most licked-at thing you own.
- **The Slow** are safe, slow, and useful; their graded cones of sorted metal are the tidiest landmarks in the basin. They are also, and this matters, the thing you must not stare at too long.

## X. The three anomaly protocols

**Counting.** Do not count. Not for fun, not to test it, not to show a newcomer. The failure is not amusing; the sequence declines to proceed and the feeling of a number refusing to be next is the single most destabilising sensation available in the Sump, worse than the siren, worse than the fauna. If you must quantify, quantify by recognition: *the parcel with the blue knot is open, the one with the green is next.*

**Tense leak.** Ninety seconds or so, and reliable. Dew before the cold. Bang before the surge. Flash before the strike.

Two rules. First: **trust the precursors as warnings.** They are free information and they are honest. When your ribs bead, cover up; the front is coming. When you hear the hammer, brace for the pressure. Second: **do not fight your own precursors.** If you find blood on your sleeve, stop moving and find out where you are about to be cut. If you flinch, do not congratulate yourself on having dodged, because the eels evolved to strike where the flinch will be, and the relation is stable, and it works, and the eels are fat. The correct posture is the eel's: accept that the outcome is already in the system, and place yourself with it rather than against it. Travellers who spend their days trying to *prevent* things they have already seen come home very tired and somewhat hollowed, and one or two have come home with an injury nobody watched happen.

**Singularity.** No two of The Slow are ever visible in the same glance. Once you notice this, you will start testing it, and the testing is the danger. Do not try to get two in frame. Do not have your partner watch one while you watch another and then compare. The basin does not distinguish between many things and one thing repeated, and it has no need to; what it *will* do, if pressed, is resolve the ambiguity in the direction that requires less of it.

You are a thing it is currently counting as several: a party of four, four sets of footprints, four voices. Keep it that way. This is why you say each other's names aloud at fixed intervals. Names are the cheapest and most effective anti-recitation technology anyone has found. A party that goes quiet for a day comes back subtly compressed — two people with one memory of the third camp, and no one able to say whose it was.

## XI. Camp and the shift timetable

**Spend the first day not travelling.** Sit on a rib-top and learn the schedule. The occurrences are manufactured — the fog-slabs, the green fronts, the discharges, the four-note siren — and they are *quota'd*. Regular. Industrial. Indifferent. A full cycle of observation will give you a timetable more useful than any map, because a map of the Sump is a list of one pan said many times, but a timetable is real.

Log the timetable ordinally, in the bead-story. Do not write down intervals in numbers.

Camp high, dry-backed, off film, out of troughs, rigid shelter, weighted not pegged (the grit holds nothing; the grit provides all the ballast you could want). Boots off, feet inspected, wounds sealed, names said, story recited from the beginning, sleep in shifts with one awake.

**On the siren:** it is very loud, it flattens the fog, and afterwards every animal in forty kilometres stops. Your instinct will be that something has happened. Nothing has happened. Nothing ends. The correct response is to sit still, say your own name once, and wait for the shovels to start again.

---

## XII. The anchor and going home

One anchor. Marked with something organic and irreplaceable — a wooden frame, a carved post, something with a specific history that you could pick out of a thousand copies by feel in the dark. Bring a thing that only you could have: not a factory object, because factory objects are native here and this place is the god of them, but something singular and worn and stupidly personal. A grandmother's spoon. A shoe last. Something whose particular scuffs you know.

Leave a person at it, if you have the numbers, and have them call a non-repeating sequence at intervals — a poem, a genealogy, something long and unbacktrackable.

Depart on schedule, early if in doubt, and *never* extend. The five-day limit is not about supplies. Past about the fifth day, travellers stop being reliably distinguishable from one another in their own logs, and the logs are the last thing to go.

---

## XIII. If it looks at you

It will not happen. It happens to perhaps one party in a great many, usually late, usually in the second pause after the siren, when the fog has lain down and you can hear water finding its way through iron and then every shovel in the basin is still — and there is only one of them, and it is looking at you.

There is no technique for this. What the ones who came back did, in every account, is the same three things:

Say your name, out loud, in full.

Do not count them. Not the shovels, not anything, not even to one.

Then turn and go the way the water goes, at a walking pace, in seven, singing.

---

## XIV. Coming home

Expect a persistent sixty-cycle tinnitus for two to six weeks. Expect your walk to have a rhythm in it that isn't yours; a physiotherapist who knows this work can break it, and it is worth paying one.

Expect arithmetic to feel *rude* for a while. Count things aloud deliberately, past five, every day, in the presence of another person, until it stops being an act of courage.

Handle wood. Garden. Put your hands in soil, which is made of roots and rot and which the Sump has never had and never will. Eat green things. Sleep a few nights where there are trees, because trees make an irregular noise that is not on quota.

Stay out of factories for a month. If you hear a four-note falling tone at the end of a shift somewhere — a car plant, a quarry, a ferry — go home, and do not go back to that place.

And do not bring anything back. Not a cogwheel, not a pinch of grit, not a fossil gearbox cracked open and still greased with something that was never alive. You will want to; everyone wants to; it is the most beautiful rock anyone has ever seen. But the basin does not distinguish between many things and one thing repeated, and nobody has satisfactorily established what a piece of that bedrock is still connected to, or how the connection behaves across a threshold, or why the people who kept one all report that their houses have begun, very quietly, to run on a schedule.
