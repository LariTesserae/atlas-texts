# Field Preparation: The Bowl at the Top of the Weeping Face

## The single most important reframe

Do not prepare for this as a trek. Prepare for it as a **dive**.

Everything about the bowl behaves like a dive. You descend across a hard boundary into a medium your body cannot indefinitely tolerate. You have a limited bottom time, governed by a consumable — not air, but *cold*. You must ascend on a schedule or the pressure change will hurt you, and you cannot simply surface in an emergency. There is no rescue, no chamber, and no one coming.

People die here for two reasons, and neither of them is the Slow Door or the pins or anything interesting. They die of **wet-bulb heat** on the way in, and of **the Lip** on the way out.

---

## The thermodynamic problem, stated plainly

The air inside is roughly blood temperature and effectively saturated. That means your two normal cooling routes are both closed: you cannot radiate or conduct heat into air that is hotter than your skin, and you cannot evaporate sweat into air that will not accept water. Your body will still produce 90–120 watts at complete rest. That heat has nowhere to go.

The practical consequence: **the bowl is habitable but not survivable.** Those are different words. Four hundred people live there, and they live there by being small, lean, slow, and extremely knowledgeable about where the margin is. They move at browser-pace because thermodynamics told them to, and after two generations it became manners.

You will not have their knowledge. You must therefore carry your margin in with you.

### Where the margin actually is

Learn these before you go in; they are your equivalent of knowing where the spare regulator is.

- **High is cooler than low.** The pocket has a calm layer with wind above it. The upper shoulders, near the Lip, are the most forgiving ground. The deep gullies, where the growth becomes near-solid and airflow stops, are the death zone. Never enter a gully in the second half of the day, and never alone.
- **The eastern gate is an air conditioner.** Where the gathered flood drops through the Lip and flashes to cloud, it is dumping enormous latent heat and driving a draft. Anywhere in that outflow, the air is measurably kinder. Locals will know the exact stones. Ask.
- **Large falls entrain and cool.** The margin of a big fall's spray column, where outside-cool air is being dragged down, is a real cool station. The still, veiled sheeting of the Weeping Face itself is not — that water is body-warm, straight off the filaments.
- **The Lip is a hospital.** Ten paces above it is a thin cold wind that will kill you slowly. Slowly is the operative word. Hyperthermia kills fast. If someone is cooking, you take them up and over, accept the pressure penalty, and argue about it afterwards.

### Carrying cold

This is the part most visitors get wrong, and it is not optional. **The only cold in the bowl is the cold you bring down from above it, and there is snow ten paces from the entrance.**

Bring:
- Two large vacuum-insulated transport vessels (30–40 litre, hard-shell, rated for a week's hold). Fill with compacted snow and rime at the col before crossing.
- A phase-change cooling vest with a **28–30 °C** transition point. Not the 15 °C athletic kind; it will be liquid in five minutes and useless. The 28 °C plates recharge in your snow vessels overnight.
- A hammock-and-plate arrangement for sleeping: a cooling plate under the shoulders and neck buys you actual sleep, which is otherwise almost unobtainable.
- A plan for **resupply runs to the col.** Someone in your party goes up every second or third day and comes back with snow. This is the real logistical spine of your visit, and it means your camp must be within a few hours of the Lip. Anyone proposing a base camp on the bowl floor "to be near the interesting parts" has not done the arithmetic and should be overruled.

Do not, under any circumstances, use the cold pins as a heat sink. I know. They are exactly what you want: thumb-thick, flush in bedrock, permanently cold in a place with no cold, and there is one every eight hundred metres like a taunt. The locals' sixth rule exists, one assumes, for reasons. It is also simply bad manners to lie on someone's machinery.

## Body preparation: twelve weeks out

**Altitude.** The approach is four days to over five thousand metres, and it is unforgiving in the ordinary way. Standard staged acclimatisation; carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, and nifedipine and know when to use them. Note the irony you are walking into: you spend a week earning your altitude adaptation and then throw it away in one step at the Lip, where you are suddenly breathing sea-level air. You will feel magnificent for about ninety minutes.

**Heat.** Do the standard humid heat-acclimation block — ten to fourteen days of progressive heat exposure, ideally in a steam environment rather than dry sauna — but understand its limits. Acclimation mostly teaches you to sweat earlier and more copiously, and in the bowl sweat is a wasted resource. What you actually gain is plasma volume, cardiovascular stability, and salt conservation. Take it; just don't mistake it for permission.

**Mass and output.** Go lean. Every kilogram of you is a small furnace. More importantly, **train slowness.** Practise walking at one kilometre per hour with a load. Practise stopping when your heart rate crosses a set number rather than when you feel tired — feeling is unreliable in there, because the sensation of overheating is masked by the fact that you are always overheating. Set a hard heart-rate ceiling and obey it like a gas limit.

**Skin, feet, teeth, hair.** Nothing dries. Ever. Your skin will macerate, your nails will lift, and any fungal passenger you own will bloom into a colony. Clear up every existing problem beforehand, get your dentistry done (there is no dentistry there and abscesses in that warmth are a genuine emergency), and shave your head and body hair. Trim nails short and square. Treat your feet for six weeks in advance as if preparing for prison or a monsoon campaign.

**Immunity, both directions.** Four hundred people in a sealed pocket with no cold season and no dry season are simultaneously a naive population and a perfect incubator. Do a proper isolation quarantine before the approach — the full period, not a gesture — and mask on first contact regardless. You are far more likely to depopulate a hamlet than to be killed by one. Conversely, assume anything you catch in there will not respond to your assumptions, and get out early rather than gambling.

## Kit: the two-wardrobe rule and the drum at the col

**Cache a sealed drum at the col before you cross.** This is the single procedure that most reliably prevents a death.
