# Preparing for the Basin Under the One Leaf

## First, correct your category

You will want to file this under *cave*, and that error will hurt you. Caves are cold, dark, and honest about their entrances. This is a **sealed room eleven kilometres wide, held at blood heat, with no door**. Nothing about it announces itself. You will walk downhill in ordinary daylight and the only warning you get is a change in the colour of the light.

Everything below follows from four facts: **the air is too hot and too wet for a human body to cool itself; the basin is a closed bowl with no wind; the entire structure is a dry, sealed lattice of preserved fuel; and you are the only source of decay that has ever entered it.**

---

## 1. The heat, which is the whole problem

Thirty-eight to forty-one degrees, humidity just short of saturation, and *no air movement at all*. Run the arithmetic honestly, because it is worse than it sounds.

A body dumps heat four ways. In the Basin:

- **Evaporation** — your primary mechanism — is nearly dead. Sweat forms and sits. You will produce litres of it to no purpose whatsoever. (This is the meaning of "your skin never quite dries and never quite runs.")
- **Convection** fails twice over: there is no wind, and the air is *hotter than your skin*. Fanning yourself in this place warms you. Bring a personal fan if you like; it will make you feel better and cook you faster.
- **Radiation** fails: everything around you — rock, cane, the glowing ceiling — is at or above your surface temperature and radiates back.
- **Conduction** is all you have left.

So build the whole expedition around one sentence: **in the Basin, the only way to lose heat is to touch something colder than you are.**

**Find the cold, first thing.** Travertine precipitates where groundwater emerges and degasses. Groundwater is usually cooler than the air above it. Carry a cheap probe thermometer and, on your first hours inside, measure every seep head you pass. If the water comes out of the rock at twenty degrees, you have found the only mercy in the world, and your camps, your rest cycles and your emergency plan should all be organised around those points. If it comes out at thirty-nine, revise your ambitions downward immediately — you have entered a place that will kill an unassisted human in a day.

**Carry cold, second.** Phase-change cooling vests (the 15–21 °C plates, not the "evaporative" ones, which are useless here). Insulated bottles of chilled water. Ice if your crossing method permits it — remember that roughly a kilogram of ice, taken in and melted, absorbs about an hour of a *resting* human's metabolic output. Walking costs you two or three times that. This arithmetic is why you should not plan to march across the basin. Nobody can carry their own cooling.

**Acclimatise beforehand.** Ten to fourteen days of daily heat exposure — sauna, or exercise in a hot room — before you cross over. It expands plasma volume, drops your sweat sodium, and lowers the core temperature at which you start sweating. It is the single cheapest intervention available and it is worth more than any gear on your list.

**Learn the local pace.** The climber crosses a chamber in twenty minutes. That is not laziness; it is the only metabolic strategy the Basin permits, and it is the correct one. Practise it. Walk at a speed that feels absurd. Stop every ten minutes whether or not you want to. The fit, fast, load-bearing member of your party is the one most likely to die, because muscle is a furnace and he has more of it and he is used to being the one who does the carrying.

**Turn-around rule:** not half your water. *Half your cooling.* From the deep centre you are five kilometres from moving air in any direction, and at Basin pace that is five hours. Your heat budget must always contain the walk out.

**Medication audit:** no antihistamines, no anticholinergics, no stimulants, no alcohol, no diuretics — anything that blunts sweating or fluid retention. Discuss beta-blockers with a doctor. Shave close; hair is insulation.

## 2. The air at the floor

Nobody has said this out loud, so I will. Consider:

A closed depression. No wind, ever. Limestone. Travertine forming, which means water arriving *saturated with carbon dioxide* and degassing it into a still basin. A "lake" in the lowest part that does not move. And the only animal in the world lives **entirely on the decks and curtains** and never comes down — and there are no insects, no worms, nothing at ground level at all in the deep places.

That is the signature of a carbon dioxide pool. Denser than air, pooling in hollows, invisible, odourless, and absolutely reliable in a basin where nothing has ever stirred it.

**Take an electronic CO₂ and O₂ meter.** Take two. You cannot use the old test — a candle flame — because (see below) you must never bring fire into this place. Do not lie down in the lowest hollows. Do not sleep at floor level in a depression. Do not crawl into a pit to retrieve a dropped item without reading the air first. Treat the still lake shore as a confined space and the deep floor as suspect until measured.

And note the general principle, because it will serve you everywhere in the Basin: **the animals' behaviour is the data.** In a world with no events, a consistent avoidance is a signpost.

---

## 3. Water: in, out, and salt

You will lose four to eight litres a day doing almost nothing, and you will get no cooling in exchange for any of it. Plan on **eight to twelve litres a day** and understand that you cannot carry that; you must drink the Basin's water.

The good news is remarkable. There are no decomposers here, no fungi, no bacterial slick, no faeces from anything but a few hundred arboreal climbers who never come to the floor. This is very likely the cleanest wild water in any world you will ever visit.

Treat it anyway, and treat it with a **UV pen or a mechanical filter**, not halogens — you should not be dumping chlorine residue into pans that have never had a chemical event. The risk is not pathogens; the risk is unknown mineral load (hard, CO₂-rich, possibly sulphidic) and whatever that algae-film skin actually is in a world with only one kingdom in it. Draw from the seep heads where the water comes out of the rock, not from the skinned pools.

**The purest water in the Basin is the condensate.** What hangs from the veins is essentially distilled. At the margins, where the roof-leaf thickens into its rolled rim, you can stand a free frame under a drip-line and collect it. Do not lash anything to the plant.

**Salt.** This matters more than the water. Litres of useless sweat will strip you, and drinking eight litres of pure condensate on top of that is a textbook route to hyponatremia — confusion, headache, seizure, and a corpse in a place that will not let it rot. Carry oral rehydration salts in bulk. Take them on a schedule, not by thirst.

---

## 4. Fire: the absolute prohibition

Look at what the builders of the twenty-two domes did *not* do. No hearth. No smoke-stain anywhere on the ceiling of the world.

The Basin is a sealed volume containing an eleven-kilometre lattice of **bone-dry dead plant matter accumulated over an unknown number of centuries, never once consumed by rot**, roofed by a translucent membrane and stuffed with oxygen and no wind. It is not a forest. It is a fuel-air charge with a lid on it. One ignition and the whole thing goes, roof included, and the only thing in the multiverse that ever built a leaf like that goes with it.

Therefore:

- No stove. No flame. No smoking. No pyrotechnic signals, no flares, no matches "for emergencies."
- **Cold food only**, for the entire trip. Plan the menu around that.
- Lithium cells are a fire source. Use LiFePO₄ or NiMH, hard-cased, individually bagged, inspected before entry, and never left in the heat unattended — remember the ambient is forty degrees and climbing toward the ceiling.
- Nothing that sparks. No steel on flint, no grinders, no angle-cutting of anything, ever.
- Your headlamp should be a sealed low-power LED. Assume any device you take can fail; ask yourself, for each one, *what happens if this vents*.

If you can only obey one rule in this document, obey this one. Everything else on the list risks you. This one risks the place.

---

## 5. Seeing, in a world with no shadows

The light comes from the entire sky, so nothing casts a usable shadow. Your own is a faint fourfold smear. This is not a curiosity; it is a serious fall hazard. Shadow is how the human eye reads shape. Without it you cannot see the lip of a deck, the edge of a travertine rim, the step down in a corridor floor, the gap between two canes.

**So bring shadows.** Carry a strong directional torch and use it *in the brightest hours*, held low and off to the side, to rake across the ground in front of you. You are not lighting your way; you are manufacturing the relief information the Basin refuses to provide. Rehearse this before you go — it feels ridiculous to switch on a lamp at noon, and you will forget.

**Carry a white-light source for medicine.** Everything here is green-gold. Under that light, red disappears: blood looks black, a flushed face looks normal, cyanosis is invisible, a wound cannot be assessed, and the classic dry-flushed-skin presentation of heat stroke is **completely undetectable**. Any casualty check gets done under white light or it does not count.

**Eyes.** Do not look up. The ceiling is the source and your eyes have no defence against a whole sky of it. Neutral grey lenses if you must, but be aware they make the shadowless-depth problem worse; better to control where you point your face. Expect severe green adaptation — by day three the world will look normal and the exit will look magenta and violent.

## 6. Sound, sleep, and the silence

The quiet is absorbent — room-quiet, not wilderness-quiet. It does two things to a person.

First, it eats your voice. You cannot shout across a hundred metres of hanging curtains. **Radios, or stay in line of sight.** A whistle is nearly useless here, which is a strange thing to write.

Second, in the absence of external sound you begin to hear yourself: pulse, joints, breath, tinnitus. Many travellers find this unbearable by the second night. **Take a small white-noise source and earbuds.** Sound leaves no trace; use it freely.

For sleep: you must drop core temperature to fall asleep, and the Basin will not permit it. Sleep on cool wet stone beside a seep — direct conduction, with a thin non-absorbent mat, not an insulating pad. Blackout mask (if the light does dim, it will not cool, and you cannot rely on it as a cue; if it does not dim, you need the mask absolutely). Expect broken, shallow, four-hour nights. Build the itinerary around a tired crew.

Also: once or twice a day a primary vein groans and the sound rolls the length of the basin for nine seconds. Know this is coming. The first time, unprepared, it will drop you into a crouch. Do not camp directly beneath the low point of a great arch, where the hanging condensation merges and eventually lets go.

---

## 7. Navigation: the ceiling is the map

The floor is a maze in four or five storeys, with sightlines that end in green, no landmarks that differ from one another, no sun compass (diffuse source), no wind direction, and quite possibly no reliable magnetic field. Getting lost is the most likely mechanism by which the heat kills you.

But the roof is a fixed reticulation of primary veins as broad as roads, and it never changes, because nothing here changes.

- **Survey from the rim before you go in.** Walk the edge, photograph the leaf's vein pattern, sketch it, learn it. That is your chart, and it is permanent.
- Inside, navigate by sighting the veins through gaps in the decks. Note the branch angles.
- **The universal escape rule: go up.** The basin is a bowl. Any consistent uphill bearing reaches the rolled rim and the white slot of true light in at most five or six kilometres. Downhill leads to the still lake and the heavy air.
- Do not lay thread or tape. Anything you leave is there forever. If you must mark, use your own cairns of loose travertine chips that you dismantle on the way out, and log every one.
- Mark your **entry point from outside** — the rim looks identical everywhere and the threshold is not a threshold.

---

## 8. You are the decay

This is the part most travellers do not think about, and it is the part that matters most.

A biosphere with no fungi, no bacterial slick, no rot, and no immune experience of any of it has *no defences at all*. You are carrying, on your skin and in your gut and in your breath and in the crumbs at the bottom of your pack, the entire decomposer apparatus of a normal world. A single viable spore of bread mould, given an eleven-kilometre lattice of dry cellulose at forty degrees and ninety-five percent humidity, is not a contamination event. It is the end of the Basin.

You cannot be sterile. You can be careful.

- **Sealed food only**, eaten over a caught sheet, crumbs collected. No fresh produce. Nothing with live culture — no cheese, no yoghurt, no sourdough, no fermented anything.
- **Pack out everything**, including urine if you can manage it, and certainly all solids. Dry-bag WAG system, double-sealed. Nothing goes in the pans.
- **Non-shedding clothing.** No cotton, no wool, no fleece — every lint fibre you drop stays in that lattice permanently. Tight-woven synthetics, taped seams, gaiters.
- Shower and launder immediately before crossing over. Consider an antifungal skin regimen for a week prior.
- **Have a death protocol.** If a member of your party dies in the Basin, the body will not decompose — but it *will* be the largest microbial inoculation the place has ever received. The body comes out. Decide this before you go in, in daylight, with everyone present.

And the smaller courtesies, which are not small:

- Broad, soft-soled footwear. **No trekking pole tips** — they punch permanent holes in soft travertine. Rubber feet or nothing.
- Do not step on the soft white lobes. They take thumbprints, and the print stays.
- Cut nothing. Take no cane, no leaf, no sample without a very good reason and a very small scalpel.
- Every footfall on the dry lattice cracks preserved material that has been intact for centuries and will never be replaced, because nothing is growing. Choose your routes on travertine and bare rock where you can.

---

## 9. The climbers, and the domes

**The climbers:** a few hundred individuals, not increasing, not decreasing, in a closed system with no pathogen history. Keep ten metres. Do not touch, do not feed, do not offer water, do not block their path along a vein. A respiratory infection casually donated by a coughing tourist could take the species in a season. Photograph from distance; move away slowly if one approaches.

**The domes:** do not enter, do not sleep in, do not touch the lashings, do not lift the stone with the pecked basin. Record the compass bearing of the doorways — it is the only asymmetry in the whole basin and therefore the only piece of information the place contains. Photograph, measure, leave.

Do not build anything. A shelter you throw together in an afternoon will stand, unaged, un-slackened, for as long as the Basin does, and the next visitor will count twenty-three.

**And prepare, quietly, for the possibility that the builders are still there.** In a world where the only animal moves like syrup and only announces motion by comparison of memories, stillness is the local mode of life. Before you conclude that a shape at the edge of a chamber is architecture, photograph it and look again an hour later. Compare frames. Do this as a discipline, not as a fear.

---

## 10. Carry your own clock

The Basin has thrown out its clocks and it will try to take yours.

- **Two watches**, mechanical and digital, redundant, and a written log with entries on the hour whether or not anything has happened. Nothing will have happened. Write "nothing" and the time.
- **Eat on the clock, not on appetite.** Appetite dies at forty degrees. If you eat only when hungry you will under-eat, and then hunger — which the Basin itself will tell you is your only remaining measure of time — will stop being reliable too, and you will have nothing left.
- Sleep on the clock. Wake on an alarm.
- Photograph the same scene at fixed intervals. It is the only way to know whether anything moved.

Do not be embarrassed by this. The rhythms you bring in are a private eccentricity, and they are also the only thing keeping you inside your own life.

---

## 11. The body, day by day

- **Feet.** They will never dry. Skin macerates, then tears, then tears deeper. Two pairs of socks minimum, changed and rotated; air the feet at every rest; antifungal powder; do not walk on blisters. This, not heroics, is what ends most long visits.
- **Heat rash** across shoulders, waistband, groin. Inevitable. Loose clothing, powder, patience.
- **Wounds** will not be infected by the environment, only by you. Clean, close, cover, check under white light.
- **Heat stroke drill**, rehearsed before entry: confusion, stumbling, inappropriate behaviour, cessation of sweating. Treatment is immersion in the coldest water you have located, immediately, before evacuation. Yes, that contaminates a pan. Do it anyway. Know beforehand which pan.

---

## 12. Strategy: camp outside

Unless you have real refrigeration and a support chain, **do not camp in the Basin.** Camp at the rim, in the gap between the rolled leaf-edge and the rock, where the air moves and has consequence in it. Make forays: six hours in, six hours out, recover in the wind. Cache water and cooling at a known seep on the first trip and build outward.

The centre is five and a half kilometres away and it will still be exactly the same when you get there, whenever you get there. That is the one promise the Basin makes and it keeps it absolutely. There is no reason to hurry and every reason not to.

---

## 13. Leaving: the transition will hit harder than the entry

Plan it as a procedure.

1. **Drink and salt before you walk out**, not after.
2. Approach the rim gap and **stop at it for twenty minutes.** Do not step through.
3. Have **dry clothing cached outside.** When you pass into moving air at forty percent humidity, several litres of stranded sweat will evaporate off you at once and you will go from heat-stressed to violently shivering inside two minutes. People have gone down at the threshold after surviving three days inside.
4. **Sunglasses on before you look out.** The white light will be unbearable and the colour will be insane — magenta, then a slow decay back toward normal over hours. Do not drive, climb, or make decisions for the first hour.
5. **Expect the smell.** You have been in a world with no putrefaction, no microbial volatiles, nothing but hot sap and wet stone and faintly paper. The outside will smell overwhelmingly of rot. Grass, soil, your own pack, your companions. It is not illness. It is accurate. It passes in a day or two and it is the single strangest souvenir the Basin gives.

---

## 14. Afterwards

Something like grief tends to follow, and it is worth being warned about. You have spent days in the only place any of us will ever stand that is not, at every instant, in the process of coming apart. Everything outside will look provisional for a while: things wearing, things drying out, things going slowly bad in the corners of rooms. Some people find this liberating. Some find they cannot stop noticing it.

Go with someone. Talk about it on the way home.

---

## The hard rules, in order

1. **No fire. Ever. Nothing that sparks, vents, or burns.**
2. Conduction is your only cooling; find the cold water on day one or leave.
3. Move at one-third of your natural pace, and turn around at half your cooling, not half your water.
4. Meter the air before you lie down anywhere low.
5. Salt with every litre.
6. Manufacture shadows with a torch; assess casualties under white light.
7. Always uphill is always out.
8. Pack out everything, shed nothing, cut nothing, build nothing, touch nothing living.
9. Keep your own clock and write in it hourly even when there is nothing to write.
10. Stop at the rim before you step through.

The basin holds exactly as you found it, and will, and does. See that it still can after you have gone.
