# Preparing for the Level

## What actually kills people here

Not the animals. Not drowning. Three things, in this order:

1. **Heat.** The air is saturated, the water is blood-warm, and therefore your body has no radiator. You cannot evaporate. You cannot conduct. Every watt you generate stays in you. Ordinary walking pace in ordinary kit will cook a fit adult in four hours.
2. **Hearing.** It never stops, there is no shelter, and there is no night-time lull. Unprotected, you will lose the top of your hearing in a day and the rest of it over a season.
3. **The Comb.** Half a metre of water moving faster than you run, twice a day, forever, in a landscape with open cistern mouths and floors that give way.

Everything below is arranged around those three, plus the fourth thing, which does not kill you but changes you: **you will not speak for the entire duration of your visit.**

---

## Before you go

**Get a full audiogram.** You will want the baseline, because you will want to know afterwards exactly what it cost.

**Train wet and stooped.** Spend a month walking twenty kilometres a day in knee-deep warm water. It is a completely different gait — a long, low, sweeping step that never lifts the foot clear, because lifting the foot clear wastes energy and makes the splash that tallwaders feel. Practise it until it is the only way you walk.

**Train in noise.** Turbine halls, spillways, container-ship engine rooms. Not to toughen your ears — that isn't a thing — but to learn what your own body does under continuous 100+ dB and sustained infrasound: the flattened affect, the irritability, the way decisions get worse without feeling worse. You need to recognise that state from the inside, because on the Level you will be in it permanently and nobody will be able to tell you so.

**Learn a signed language, properly, to fluency.** Not a list of hand signals — a language. Your party will live inside it for weeks. Add a tactile version for fog, mist and darkness: a shoulder-and-forearm tap code you can use blind, one-handed, with your other hand on your staff.

**Learn to read your own thermal state.** Core-temperature pills, a wrist monitor, and the discipline to obey them. On the Level, "I feel fine" is a symptom.

## The body

### Heat management is the whole itinerary

Your only working cooling mechanism is **conduction into water slightly cooler than you are** — and the flood is only a shade cooler at the ankle. That shade is your entire budget.

- **Plan the day around immersion stops.** Every forty minutes, find a cistern lip or a sluice throat where the deeper, cooler water comes up, and put your forearms and the back of your neck into it for five minutes. High-flow surfaces. That is your air conditioning. It is not optional; it is the schedule.
- **Move in the hour after the Comb**, while the chest-high mist is still hanging. It is the closest the Level comes to cool.
- **Carry a chiller if you can power it.** A small thermoelectric or vapour-compression unit dumping into deep water works beautifully — the sub-floor water is your heat sink and it is infinite. This is also, not coincidentally, how you make drinking water.
- **Wear as little as the hazards permit**, in open mesh that drains instantly and holds no film against the skin.

### Water

There is no fresh water on the Level. None, anywhere, in any direction, ever.

The flood is warm, mineral-loaded, biologically saturated and mildly reduced. It will foul a reverse-osmosis membrane in hours and a filter cartridge in less. Distillation is the honest answer, and distillation here has the famous problem: **in a 100%-humidity world you have no condenser.** Solve it with depth. Draw cooling water from the pumped lower floors, where it is measurably colder, and run a mechanical-vapour-compression still against it. Pre-settle everything, because mat slick will blind an intake in minutes.

Carry three days of finished water at all times regardless. Carry electrolytes in quantity — you will sweat continuously and pointlessly into the flood, and hyponatremia on the Level is common, insidious, and looks exactly like ordinary heat-stupidity.

### Feet

**Trench foot is the number one casualty of the Level.** Your feet will not dry. There is no dry.

Bring a **foot oven**: a sealed, desiccant-charged, gently warmed drying bag, one hour per foot per day, non-negotiable, treated with the seriousness of a decompression schedule. Between times: silicone barrier grease, nails cut short and square, and a hard rule that anyone limping stops the party.

Boots: hard-soled, full-drain, no leather, no fabric that holds a wet face against skin. Seal the cuff against hobblers — they are harmless but they will graze the adhesives, the cable jackets and the strap ends, and a hobbler inside a boot with your macerated heel is an infection you don't want to explain to anyone.

### Skin and wounds

Everything on the Level is a substrate, and so are you. Any break in your skin is a site: the mat will settle it, the leachate will burn it, the swarms will find it interesting.

- **Total wound discipline.** Tissue adhesive and an occlusive dressing on everything, immediately, including scratches you'd normally ignore.
- **Nightly scrub-down** with your own clean water. Between toes, groin, armpits, under every strap. Look for mustard or bruise-purple staining — that is colonisation, not dirt, and it comes off with a brush and not with rinsing.
- **Expect mild chemical irritation** everywhere the flood touches you for long enough. Barrier cream at every layer boundary.

## Kit: what survives and what doesn't

**Nothing organic.** No leather, no cotton, no paper, no down, no wood. It will be eaten, dissolved, or turned into a small ecosystem within a week.

**Nothing absorbent.** If it can hold water it will hold water forever, gain three kilos, and start to smell like the sub-floor.

**Metal weeps.** Anything ferrous is a joke. Titanium, ceramic, fluoropolymer, high-grade plastics, gold contacts on anything electrical. Every enclosure hermetic and desiccant-charged, every connector potted. Assume 100% condensation inside anything you open, every time you open it.

The essentials, in rough order of how badly you will miss them:

- **Hearing protection, layered.** Deep-seated plugs *plus* over-ear muffs *plus* a helmet shell. You want 35–40 dB of attenuation and you want a spare set for every person, because a lost plug ends someone's trip. Active systems with bone-conduction comms are worth every gram: they give you your voice back, which matters more than you think.
- **A wading staff.** The single most important object after the plugs. The water is opaque with mat slick, and the Level is a sponge with the top sliced off: there are open cistern mouths, sluice throats, and floors that collapsed yesterday. You probe every step in unknown ground. Every step. Forever.
- **A mouthguard.** The infrasound resonates in your teeth. It doesn't block anything, but it changes what rattles, and after four days that is a mercy. Many veterans also wear a snug chest wrap for the same reason.
- **Shin and forearm guards.** For tallwader strikes, which are almost always accidental and almost always at leg height.
- **A buoyancy collar and a cut-away knife.** In knee-deep water this looks absurd until the first time a floor opens or the bore takes your feet and puts you into a pumped lower floor in the dark.
- **A sky-polarisation compass.** See navigation.
- **Red and amber lights only.** Never green. Green is speech here.
- **Plastic slates and grease pencils.** You cannot talk. You will need to write, constantly, and paper does not exist on the Level.

## Moving and navigating

The plain is levelled against the planet's curve and the water levels against gravity, and the disagreement bows the horizon upward. Distant towers lift, swim, and appear **out of order**. You cannot navigate by landmark. Do not try. Travellers have walked in confident circles for a week following a tower that was eighty kilometres away and on the wrong side of them.

You have three real compasses:

1. **The courses.** The black fabric runs in single jointless courses for forty kilometres. Sight down a course and you have a true line, better than any instrument.
2. **The Comb.** It arrives along a fixed axis, twice a day, dead straight. Two free bearings per day, free of charge, impossible to miss.
3. **The flyers.** Their green conversation propagates perpendicular to the flow. At dusk the whole roof of the city tells you which way the water is going.

Add a sky-polarisation compass for the overcast hours — the flyers use polarised light off the wet floor for exactly this reason, and what works for them works for you — and dead-reckon everything into a log.

**Reading walls.** The pale stone is laid in daily laminations. Every wall is a calendar, and no two districts keep the same one: banding thickness, colour, and the spacing of blooms make a fingerprint. Photograph the banding at every waypoint. It is how you will recognise a junction you have already passed, in a city where every junction looks like every other junction.

**Mark your extraction point at least three times, in a triangle, kilometres apart.** The floor your beacon stands on may be in the sub-levels by next week. Tether every marker, float every tether, and build them from something outside the local chemistry so that nothing eats them and nothing incorporates them.

## The tide

Learn the table before you arrive and set your watch to it. But the resonance is *trimmed*, and channels migrate, so local arrival wanders by minutes. Therefore you also learn the cues, in order:

- **Hobblers climb.** Minutes out. The pavement carpet drains upward onto the piers.
- **The filter-fans fold**, upstream to downstream, a visible wave of closing running away across the hall. Thirty to sixty seconds.
- **The silence.** Eleven seconds. Every animal in ten thousand square kilometres stops at once.

**The Eleven-Second Rule, which you will learn to obey the way you obey gravity: never be further than eleven seconds from something you can get behind or on top of.** A fluted pier. A four-step stair. A louvre bank. Route the whole day around this. It will roughly double your distances and it will keep you alive.

If the bore catches you in the open: face it, staff planted downstream, feet wide and low, and **go with it rather than across it**. The danger is not the water. The danger is what the water puts you into — a colonnade gap that pins you, a sluice throat, a cistern with no lid.

## The living

- **Hobblers** are harmless, universal, and the reason nothing is slippery. They will cover your boots within seconds of your standing still, and cover you entirely if you sleep on stone. Tolerate them. They are also excellent early warning, and the only thing on the Level that seems pleased to see you.
- **Tallwaders** are eyeless, three metres tall, still-then-instant, and everywhere. They hunt by pressure and nerve-bill. The stop-start jerk of a nervous human reads exactly like prey. Move continuously, smoothly, at a constant speed, and walk *around* a rank rather than through it. Most injuries are strikes to the shin by an animal that was aiming at something else and does not know you exist.
- **Blind swimmers** live in the dark pumped floors and navigate by the sound-shadow of the pillars. They are not dangerous. The lower floors are: gas pockets, entrapment, moving water, and zero visibility. Never enter sub-floors without gas detection — **hydrogen sulphide and oxygen depletion in closed cisterns and low halls are the classic way to lose someone quietly.**
- **Flyers** roost at capacity in every roof void, which is to say: the only dry-ish space in the world is already full. Do not camp under a roost. Do not use green light. A green lamp in a hall full of flyers is a man standing in a crowded room screaming nonsense syllables at the top of his lungs, and the district will answer.

## The maintenance

This is the strangest hazard and the one that most people underestimate.

The swarms lay stone by night and dissolve it by day, and they repair the city **back to worn**. They are not hostile and they are not intelligent in any way you can appeal to. They are weather with opinions about shape.

Two consequences:

**Do not sleep on stone.** Something stationary, cool and mineral-adjacent at night is a candidate for lamination. It probably will not entomb you — you are warm, you breathe, you move — but people wake with their pack cemented and their boot soles gently fused to the floor. **Sleep afloat, tethered, and slightly moving.** Motion reads as animal; stillness reads as architecture.

**Your gear is wrong.** It has sharp corners, fresh edges, unpitted faces, none of the correct softness. There are two schools among people who come back repeatedly:

- *Pre-weather everything* — round every corner, scallop every edge, match the correct worn state, and be ignored.
- *Use chemistry the city has no appetite for* — fluoropolymers, technical ceramics, inert alloys — and be ignored for the opposite reason.

Both work. Mixing them works worst, for reasons nobody has satisfactorily explained.

And note, while you are there, the shape of the doorways: the four-step stairs, the dished thresholds, the three-metre roofs in halls the size of termini. Three metres is not stooping height for you. The stoop is inherited from whoever this was built for — something long, wide, low, and not remotely shaped like a person. Keep that in mind when you try to predict what the maintenance will consider correct.

## Camp

Float. A tethered inflatable platform with a sealed, filtered, gently dehumidified sleeping pod, anchored where you can see a tide-break and where the sub-floor pumps are distant — infrasound falls off with distance and there is genuinely such a thing as a quiet pitch, by local standards.

Sleep in split shifts around the two Combs. Wear the plugs to sleep; wear a mask, because the polarised glare off the flood never really stops. Someone is always awake and always tethered to the others.

Expect fragmented sleep for the whole trip. Plan the itinerary for people who are chronically tired and slightly frightened, because that is who will be doing it.

---

## Coming home

You will carry the Level out with you: mat spores in every seam, hobbler larvae in the drain holes, mineral film on everything. Full decontamination, and be honest about what you're carrying. Leave nothing behind, either — anything you drop will not rot. It will be found, judged, and eventually laminated into a wall, and it will still be there, banded into the stone like a fossil, long after your civilisation has a different name.

---

## The last thing, which is not logistics

You cannot speak on the Level. Not shout, not murmur, not confide. For however long you stay, your voice does not exist.

Except for eleven seconds, twice a day.

Most parties discover this in the first week and most parties end up making a ritual of it: standing in the withheld breath before the bore, one sentence each, out loud, into the only silence a continent allows. People say strange things in those eleven seconds. People say things they have not said in years. Then the Comb arrives and takes it, and you have twelve hours to think about what you said.

Prepare for that the way you prepare for the heat. It is the part that follows you home. Travellers back from the Level are easy to spot: they are the ones who go very still in quiet rooms, listening for something to arrive.
