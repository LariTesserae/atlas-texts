# Preparing for the Bowl at the Top of the Weeping Face

## The one thing to understand before you pack anything

Everywhere else you have ever been, your body was allowed to cool itself by evaporating water off its skin. In the bowl, it is not. The air is saturated. Sweat pools and runs and does nothing. Breathing does nothing — you are inhaling air already at body temperature and already full of water, so even the small fraction of heat you normally dump through your lungs is gone. Every surface you could lie against is thirty-nine degrees, which is warmer than you are.

So your metabolism has nowhere to put its output, and your body becomes a sealed vessel with a heater in it.

Do the arithmetic once, properly, before you go, because it governs everything else:

- Lying still, you produce about 100 watts. Walking on the flat, about 350–400. Pushing uphill through root-mat and dripping fern, 600 and up.
- In the bowl you can shed, optimistically, 80–120 watts by conduction and radiation into air and stone that are barely cooler than you.
- You can safely store about 500–600 kilojoules of heat before your core goes somewhere you don't come back from.

Which means: **on the floor of the bowl, a fit adult has roughly thirty to forty minutes of ordinary walking, and then several hours of lying down.** That is the budget. That is all of it. This is why the browsers move four metres an hour, why the forest is slow, why the locals are unhurried, and why an energetic visitor is a corpse in waiting. The bowl is not a hot place you tough out. It is a place where exertion is metered, like air in a cylinder.

Everything below follows from that.

## Twelve weeks out: prepare the body

**Heat-humid acclimatisation.** Ten to fourteen consecutive days minimum, ideally three weeks, of 60–90 minutes' moderate work in 38–40 °C at the highest humidity you can arrange — a steam room with a step box in it is the classic ugly solution. This will not make you comfortable in the bowl; nothing will. It buys you a 10–15 % plasma volume expansion, a lower sweat-sodium concentration, earlier cardiovascular stabilisation, and perhaps an hour a day of extra function. It decays in about a week, so time it to end just before departure and maintain it during the walk in if you can rig anything at all.

**Altitude acclimatisation.** The approach tops out above 5,000 m and the col is higher. Standard graded ascent, 300–500 m of sleeping altitude per day above 3,000 m, rest days on the threes. Acetazolamide if you use it.

Here is the part first-time visitors get wrong and occasionally die of: **inside the bowl you are at sea-level pressure, and you will de-acclimatise.** Ventilatory acclimatisation decays in roughly five to seven days. After two weeks in the green you are, physiologically, a lowlander again — and the door out is a 5,500 m col that you must *climb to*, not descend from, while soaked and heat-exhausted. Plan the exit from the beginning (see below).

**Body composition.** Lean and small wins. Every kilogram of muscle is a kilogram of heater. Large, strong, fit-looking people suffer badly here; wiry people with high surface-to-volume ratios suffer less. Do not arrive in peak bulk.

**Ears.** Get a baseline audiogram. You will want the comparison afterwards, and you may want it in court.

**Teeth.** Any filling with a void under it will announce itself at the Lip. Have them checked and, if necessary, redone. Barodontalgia at 5,500 m, four days from anywhere, is a memorable experience.

**Do not go with a head cold.** Blocked sinuses plus a near-instantaneous pressure change of half an atmosphere will rupture something.

## Six weeks out: prepare the behaviour

This is unglamorous and it is the difference between a good trip and a stretcher.

**Practise slowness.** Spend two weeks doing your ordinary chores at one-third speed. Deliberately. Walk to the shops taking three times as long. It feels absurd; it is the single most protective skill you can bring. In the bowl, "hurrying" is a way of saying "raising my core temperature", and the instinct to speed up when tired, frightened, late, or excited must be trained out of you in advance, because you will not be able to reason yourself out of it at the time.

**Practise deafness.** Wear deep-insert earplugs plus muffs for whole days at home. Learn to speak by leaning in, cheek to cheek. Build and drill a working hand-vocabulary of two or three hundred signs with whoever you are travelling with — directions, numbers, *stop*, *water*, *I am overheating*, *go up*, *wait*, *help*, *I am fine*, *no*. There is no shouting your way out of a problem in the bowl. The roar of two hundred waterfalls is not noise you talk over; it is a physical pressure against the ears that removes speech as an option beyond about a metre.

**Write your Slow Door policy now, on paper, and have everyone sign it.**

If the Door opens while you are there, you will have about an hour, and you will want, with an intensity you cannot currently imagine, to walk into that dry, dustless, milk-white corridor. You will produce excellent reasons. Decide now, in a kitchen, in daylight, with a cup of tea, what your party does. The recommended policy is: *nobody enters; we photograph, we record, we sit down, we hold each other's belts if necessary.* Whatever you decide, decide it in advance. And obey rule six in the meantime — do not camp in front of it, do not sleep in sight of it, do not let anyone take a "quick nap" on the eleven-hundredth step.

## The kit

### Cooling — your actual life-support

**A wet-bulb instrument.** A sling psychrometer (simple, unbreakable, no batteries) plus, if you like, a WBGT meter. This is the most important thing you will carry. Before you do anything in the bowl, you measure. You will find the bowl is stratified: cooler near the Lip and on the high shoulders, hotter in the deep gullies, hottest anywhere the canopy breaks and sun gets through cloud. Map it. Establish a personal red line — for most people something around 33 °C wet bulb is the number above which you do not work, only lie still, and above 35 you do not remain at all. Mark the red-line contour on your map and treat crossing it like crossing a glacier.

**A large vacuum flask packed with snow, sealed absolutely vapour-tight.** The rim of the bowl is a freezer, and the bowl is a place where cold is the only currency that matters. Five litres of snow is about 1.7 megajoules of cooling: hours of extra walking, or one person pulled back from heatstroke. But it must be *sealed*, not merely insulated. An exposed cold surface in saturated air collects condensate, and condensing water gives up seven times more heat than melting ice absorbs; a naked ice block in the bowl is eaten alive by the air in minutes. Sealed, insulated, and refilled from the high snows.

**Chemical cold packs, many more than you think.** Weak (50–80 kJ each) but they weigh nothing and they will be what you have when someone goes down in the wrong place.

**A cooling vest of the sealed-gel type, or simply a wrapped flask against the femoral and carotid areas.** Wetting yourself does nothing. Immersing in a pool does nothing — the pools are body temperature. Say it out loud a few times before you go, because when you are staggering the pools will look like salvation and they are a hot bath.

**A reflective umbrella, and the discipline to use it.** Two-thirds of any day the bowl is inside its own cloud and there is no radiant load at all. The other third is dangerous. A break in cloud on top of a 33 °C wet bulb is an emergency, not a photo opportunity.

### Clothing

Minimal. Loose. Synthetic. It will never dry and never breathe; it is there for abrasion, sun, and modesty, nothing else. Long sleeves in the gullies because everything you push through sheds water and small animals onto you.

**No leather anywhere.** Leather boots will mould, soften and delaminate inside a fortnight. Synthetic-upper footwear with drainage, and — this is the local knowledge — **felt or wool soles for travertine and algal rock.** Studs and aggressive lugs both slip on filmed limestone and gouge terrace rims that took a thousand years to grow. Bring three pairs of everything that touches your feet, plus powder, plus a real antifungal.

**Accept that nothing on a drying rack will dry.** The one trick that works: warm stone. The rock is 39 °C; air in contact with it is warmed above the ambient dew point and therefore, locally, unsaturated. Spread a wet shirt directly against a cliff face out of the drip and it will dry. Hang it in the air a hand's breadth away and it will hang there for a week. Locals know this; their drying racks are a habit inherited from somewhere else.

### Feet, skin, and the slow rot

In constant warm wet, ordinary abrasions become ulcers in four days and ulcers become surgical problems in three weeks. Immersion foot is the single likeliest thing to end your trip.

- Twice-daily foot inspection, warm-stone drying, powder, and at least two hours a day barefoot in a hammock with the feet up.
- Occlusive dressings, tissue adhesive, iodine, a real course of broad-spectrum oral antibiotics, and the willingness to use them early.
- Alcohol/acetic-acid ear drops after every soaking. Otitis externa in the bowl is not a possibility, it is a schedule.
- Antifungal cream in quantities that embarrass you at the chemist's.

### Sleep

A hammock with a bug net and a wide fly, not a ground tent — the root mats are a metre thick, sodden, and full of things that climb. A small battery fan, angled across you: with air at 32–33 °C and skin at 35, moving air still removes heat by convection. (If you measure ambient air *above* skin temperature, turn it off; you are then blowing a hairdryer on yourself.)

Earplugs plus muffs. And camp with mass between you and the Lip if you can — the low hum from the boundary is infrasound, it passes through everything, and on still nights when it beats against the frogs it will keep you awake in a way you find difficult to describe afterwards.

### Power, optics, electronics

Solar is nearly useless under permanent cloud. There is, however, a permanent thermal gradient available at the col — 39 °C rock on the inside, killing cold on the outside. A thermoelectric module bridging the Lip will trickle-charge forever. Inside the bowl there is no gradient at all, which tells you something about the place.

Every sealed container must be vented for both crossings: going in they crush, going out they burst. Fuel canisters, retort pouches, water bladders, camera housings, pen cartridges, and any medication in glass ampoules.

Cameras: the counterintuitive rule is **keep the camera warm.** Everything in the bowl sits at the dew point, so any object even slightly cooler than ambient instantly grows water. Store the camera against warm rock, never in a cool pack, and it will stay clear. Notebooks: waterproof paper and pencil. Ink is a rumour here.

Silica gel cannot be regenerated on 39 °C stone. Your dryer is *outside the Lip* — the cold thin air of the approach is bone-dry. Plan a gear cache at the col and rotate electronics and desiccant out to it.

## The crossing: in

Cross at first light. Do not cross at a run — do not cross standing up.

The changeover happens at the col, in a windbreak, and it is a comic and dangerous few minutes: you are in a down suit at minus fifteen and ten paces later you need to be nearly naked in a sauna. So:

1. Build the shelter. Strip to bowl clothing. Pack all cold-weather gear, dry, into vapour-sealed bags and leave it cached on the *outside* of the Lip — you will need it dry on the way back and it will not stay dry inside.
2. Hydrate. Eat something small. Equalise your ears deliberately and keep equalising.
3. Cross **sitting down**, pushing yourself through with your heels, one person at a time, with a partner watching from each side. The warmth and the pressure rise together hit you like a wave; syncope is normal and falling headfirst into a pocket of blood-hot saturated air is how people are lost in the first ninety seconds of a trip that has otherwise been planned for a year.
4. Once through, lie still for twenty minutes before you try to stand. Then move slowly to the highest, coolest ledge within reach and spend your first night there, not in the green.

Your first descent to the bowl floor should be treated exactly as a dive: planned duration, turnaround time, agreed signals, ascent when the clock says so and not when you feel like it.

## Living there

**Water.** Abundant and warm — 39 °C, heavily carbonate, everywhere. You need eight to twelve litres a day and you will not want any of it, because warm mineral water is deeply unappetising and warm water does not quench. Bring flavourings; consider them medical supplies. Bring electrolyte salts in quantity — you are losing sodium at an enormous rate through sweat that accomplishes nothing, and hyponatremia from drinking litres of unsalted water is a real and stupid way to have a seizure at 5,500 m. Filter and treat everything; the pools have a permanent population of crabs, frogs and unresolved questions.

**Food.** Nothing keeps. Dried food cannot be made or maintained. Retort pouches, sealed, vented at the crossings. Expect appetite to collapse — eat on a schedule, mostly carbohydrate and salt.

**Fire.** Nothing burns. Bring solid fuel or alcohol, and bring more than you'll use, because dry tinder and reliable ignition are the most valued things in the bowl and the best gift you have. Remember that a stove is also a heat source you have to live beside.

**Movement.** Assume one-third of your normal distance, at best. Carry a long staff and **probe before every step off bare rock** — the root mats lie a metre thick over open stone and there are voids beneath them that will take a leg to the hip.

Stay on the ancient paths. Not as courtesy: rule five. A cut heals at a hand's breadth per lifetime, so a new path is a scar on the world for three hundred years, and the locals' entire moral geometry is built on that fact. **Do not bring a machete.** If you must bring a blade, keep it small and visibly unused.

**When a browser is on the path, you wait.** It will take it the best part of an hour. Sit down, drink, and be grateful for an excuse to do nothing, which is the correct activity in the bowl anyway.

## The people

Perhaps four hundred of them. They are slow, hospitable, partially deaf, and considerably better than you are at everything that matters here.

- Greet by approach and touch, not by calling out. Speak close to the ear. Do not mistake their deliberateness for stupidity or their deafness for inattention.
- Hire a guide. Not for navigation — for pace. They will slow you down and you will resent it and it will save your life.
- Gifts: dry storage above all. Vapour-tight containers, tarpaulin, cordage, needles, fine files, fishhooks, lighters and flints, medicines, salt. Nothing that requires spare parts. Nothing that would change how they live after you leave.
- Do not cut a browser. Do not touch a filament with an open wound. Do not take a sample of the stone; a quarry scar is ten generations of penance and they will know exactly what you did, because everyone always knows.
- Do not move the cold pins. You may sit by one; many people do.
- The cairns with the vitrified caps are not geology. Walk round them.
- On the old shorelines and the contracting Lip: they can count as well as you can. Some communities want to discuss it and some very much do not, and the huts stand where they stand at the lowest old line for reasons that are theirs. Let them raise it.

## The crossing: out

This kills more visitors than the heat does, because it comes at the end, when you are depleted, deaf, salt-drained and pleased with yourself.

**Budget four days for it.** Spend the last three or four nights of your stay camped as high inside the bowl as you can get, on the shoulders just under the Lip, where the pocket's own pressure gradient gives you something to re-acclimatise against. You are trying to rebuild, partially, the ventilatory acclimatisation that two weeks at sea-level pressure took away from you.

Before crossing:

- Rest a full day. Do not cross the day you finished a hard carry.
- Hydrate heavily; dehydration raises decompression risk.
- If you have oxygen, breathe it for thirty to sixty minutes beforehand to offload nitrogen. You are about to go, in one step, from one atmosphere to roughly half of one, and altitude decompression sickness above 5,500 m after exertion is not exotic.
- Vent every container. Re-equalise constantly.
- Cross **sitting, slowly, feet first**, with someone on the cold side holding your shoulders and your dry insulation already open on the ground.

Expect to be knocked flat. Expect your legs to fail. Expect the cold to arrive on soaked skin like a blow. Have the dry layers on you inside ninety seconds and a hot drink inside five minutes, and then lie there in the wind, shaking, at half an atmosphere, listening to your own thin breathing, until someone tells you it's time to walk.

## Two more things

**There is no rescue.** A broken ankle in the bowl is not an evacuation; it is either a death or a decade of residency. Set your risk posture accordingly: no scrambling, no shortcuts, no heroics on the eleven hundred steps of the Weeping Face, where the sheeting water will also make you breathe with a cupped hand over your mouth like a man drinking from a hose. Go up the Stair with a local, on the worn tread, in the middle of the day, or do not go up it.

**Set a departure date and keep it.** Ten to fourteen days is a full and honest first visit. Beyond that the de-acclimatisation cost climbs, the fungal problems get ahead of you, and — every account agrees on this and none of them can quite explain it — you begin to lose interest in leaving. The noise stops registering. The heat stops registering. You find you have spent an afternoon watching a grey animal cross four metres of forest and you could not say what you were thinking about, and it was the best afternoon you have had in years.

That is the real hazard of the Bowl, and no kit list covers it. Pack for it anyway: a fixed date, a companion who holds you to it, and something waiting on the other side that you have already promised to be there for.
