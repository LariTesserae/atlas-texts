# Preparing for the Increment

## 1. The single idea to hold in your head

Everywhere else you have ever been, the ground was a gift. Here it is a product, and its production is a race against drowning, and the race is being won by four centimeters a year.

Every calorie you eat there, every liter you drink, every hour of a tender's attention you absorb by being interesting, comes out of that four centimeters. You are not a tourist arriving at a place with slack in it. You are a draw on a margin. Prepare accordingly, and the visit will be one of the great ones. Prepare badly and you will be, in their ledgers, a documented subtraction.

---

## 2. Your body: warm hypoxia

The Increment sits high enough that honeybees fail there — call it four and a half thousand meters of equivalent pressure. But it is warm, humid, and green. Every instinct you have for reading altitude is built on cold, dryness, and bare rock, and all three cues are missing. Travelers routinely arrive, feel the greenhouse air on their faces, decide they have overprepared, and walk briskly up two flights of water-stairs to see the view. Then they sit down on the wet stone and cannot get up.

It is in fact slightly worse than the barometer says. At that pressure, air near saturation gives up a real fraction of its volume to water vapor, and water vapor carries no oxygen.

- **Pre-acclimatize properly.** Two weeks at real altitude somewhere else, or a staged ascent of at least four days. Do not step through a door from a sea-level world onto Course Ninety-One. The tenders have seen this and they find it distressing to watch, which means you will also have wasted their day.
- Carry acetazolamide if your medicine permits it, and know your own symptom pattern well enough to recognize it while confused.
- **Assume you are dehydrated at all times.** You will never feel thirsty. You are damp from the moment you top the ladder to the moment you leave; your clothes will not dry; your hair will not dry; and meanwhile you are exhaling a liter and a half a day into the fog. Drink on a schedule, by the clock, whether or not you want to.
- **Carbon monoxide.** Six calcining kilns vent *under the floor.* Hollow columns, parapet copings, warm drains. The warm places are the dry places, and the dry places are the ones you will crave after two nights in a wet blanket, and some of them will kill you in your sleep with no warning at all, and hypoxia and CO poisoning compound each other viciously. Bring passive chemical CO spot-badges — cheap, unpowered, humidity-tolerant — and pin one to your collar and one where you sleep. Never sleep in a drain, a dead flue, or a warm alcove without asking a tender first. They know which ones breathe.

## 3. Quarantine: the part that actually matters

Read this twice.

Eleven years ago a fig seed arrived on the south parapet. Nobody knows how. There are no figs that seed themselves up there; the tub figs are kept dwarf and sterile-furious; there are no frugivorous birds, only two pale swifts that eat fog-midges. The seed came from somewhere, and the somewhere was almost certainly a pocket, a cuff, a boot tread, a beard.

Its root is now nine centimeters into Course Ninety. It has made the first crack on the Increment not made by a human hand. They have tagged it and marked it and left it, and they call it *the beginning of the outside*, and when they say that to you they will be watching your face.

You are a vector. Prepare as though you were being sent into a seed bank.

**Seventy-two hours before entry:**
- New clothing, laundered hot, sealed in bags, opened only at the ladder foot.
- Boots either new or stripped: soles picked out with a nail, welts scrubbed, tread brushed under running water. Do the eyelets. Do the tongue gusset.
- Cut your nails. Wash your hair twice. Comb it out over a sheet and burn the sheet, or something equally theatrical — the point is thoroughness, not ritual, but the ritual helps you be thorough.
- **No fruit. No nuts in shell. No whole grains. No seeds of any kind, including the ones in your trail mix, including the poppy on your bread, including the burr you cannot see on your wool cuff.** Wool from a farm is a seed-delivery system. Prefer smooth-faced, tightly woven fabrics with no nap.
- No soil anywhere on your person, on any strap, in any seam. A gram of foreign soil on that terrace contains a biota that has no competition and nothing to eat it.
- Empty your pack completely, turn it inside out, and vacuum the corners. Then do it again.

And prepare for the possibility that they will inspect you at the foot of the last ladder, unsmilingly, and that some of what you brought will not be allowed up. Bring nothing you cannot bear to leave in a box at the bottom.

## 4. Materials: what the Increment does to things

Everything is wet and the water is alkaline with lime.

- **No ferrous metal on the deck.** Steel rusts within days in that air, and a rust stain on unweathered greenstone is permanent, visible, and — to them — obscene. It is weathering that is not theirs, appearing on stone that has not earned any weathering at all. If you must bring a knife, bring bronze, or keep the steel sheathed, oiled, and off the pavement. Do not set a steel tool down on a fresh block. Ever.
- **Bronze and lead behave.** Brass is acceptable. Aluminum is eaten by lime. Plated anything will spot and flake and shed its plating into their growing beds.
- **Leather is eaten by lime too.** Boots should be rubber or oiled canvas; take a small tin of dubbin for anything leather and accept it will not be saved.
- **Paper is a joke.** Do not bring a notebook. Bring a wax tablet, a slate and chalk, an oiled linen scroll, or vellum with a pencil. Ink runs; graphite does not. Waxed board is best: you can write in the rain and you can rub it out.
- **Electronics: assume loss.** Condensation gets inside everything eventually. Potted, sealed, silica-gel-packed devices survive a few days; then the gel saturates and you must dry it on a flue coping, which means you must ask permission to use their heat. Decide beforehand whether a camera is worth that conversation. Many travelers decide it isn't, and they are right.
- **Sealed rigid containers will fail** on the pressure change — either bursting on the way up or crushing on the way down. Use flexible or vented ones. Vent them before transit.
- **Lanyard everything.** Every item you carry should be tethered to you. A dropped lens cap goes over the parapet into the white and is gone — not lost, *finished with*, the way Course Eighty-Seven is finished with. It is also a permanent negative entry in a ledger that balances to the gram. Nobody will shout at you. It will simply be written down.

## 5. Clothing and feet

Nothing dries. Give up on dryness as a goal and optimize instead for *working wet*.

- Wool or waxed fabrics next to the skin; cotton is a wick and a rot-farm. Two full sets minimum, both of which will be damp; rotate them anyway, because damp-and-aired is a different thing from damp-and-worn-for-six-days.
- **Antifungal powder.** Use it nightly on feet and groin from day one, not from the day you notice a problem. Trench foot is real there and it is slow and stupid and humiliating and it will end your visit.
- **Hard soles, aggressive tread, and awareness of parting-sand.** The new blocks shed a fine casting sand for months. On smooth wet greenstone it behaves like ball bearings. Walk flat-footed. Take the water-stairs one at a time.
- **Gloves, two pairs.** The arrises on fresh stone will open a thumb to the bone before you have registered touching anything. In a place with that much moss, mold, and standing organic water, an open hand wound is a genuine medical event.
- Eye protection for the casting bays. Lime in the eye is an emergency that gets worse over hours; it is not an acid, it does not neutralize itself, and it keeps burning. Carry a dedicated eyewash and know that the correct answer is *twenty minutes of flushing, right now, with whatever clean water is nearest.*

## 6. The medical kit, weighted for this place

Cuts (many, clean them aggressively), fungal infection, lime burns, blisters from wet feet, altitude, and CO. That's the list. Weight accordingly:

- Copious eyewash and sterile saline
- Antiseptic, steri-strips, more adhesive dressings than you think, all in waterproof wrapping
- Antifungal powder and cream
- Broad-spectrum antibiotic if your world permits, with the understanding below
- Acetazolamide, analgesics, rehydration salts
- A tetanus status you are certain of

**One unexpected consideration:** the Increment's nutrient loop is closed. Human waste goes into the trays. If you take antibiotics, or any potent drug, you must tell the tender who manages the beds, because your urine is an input to their agriculture. This will not be an awkward conversation for them. It will be awkward only for you, once.

---

## 7. The ascent

The last ladder is not the only ladder. You will climb a long way in fog on wet rungs with your hands, at altitude, with a pack.

- Train grip and forearm endurance specifically. Hypoxia takes your grip before it takes your legs.
- Pack light enough to climb with, or be prepared to haul in stages.
- Wear a harness and know how to clip a cowstail. Ask at the bottom whether there is a haul line; usually there is, and usually it is for cargo, and usually they will let you use it if you ask before you are exhausted rather than after.
- Do not look for the top. In that fog there isn't one until suddenly there is.

## 8. Manners, and the ledger

This is a culture of accounting. Ninety-one courses of it. Their ledgers are their scripture, their history, and their only defense against a rising cloud.

**Declare your mass.** Arrive with a written list of everything you have brought, by item and by weight, and offer it to whoever keeps the book. This single gesture will transform how you are treated. You are untracked mass in a closed system; volunteering to be tracked says that you understand the place.

**Pay in labor.** Currency means nothing. Time means everything. Offer a shift: hauling, mixing, scrubbing forms, carrying fleece-combs, turning the pump. You will be slow and useless at altitude and they will know that, and the offer is still the correct one. If you do one thing, do the **brushing hour** at dusk — moving down the bean frames with a sable brush, touching flower after flower, saying nothing. It requires no skill, only patience and a still hand, and it is the surest way into their regard. Do not talk during it. Do not photograph it.

**Things not to do:**
- Do not pick moss. Moss is crop.
- Do not chip a sample of greenstone. It is mass, it is the world, and it is also somebody's block with somebody's punch-mark on it.
- Do not touch the fig. Do not make a joke about the fig. Do not offer an opinion about whether they should cut the fig. You may ask about it once, quietly, and then listen for as long as they want to talk.
- Do not lean on parapets — new stone, unproved, and hung with the fleece-combs that are half their water supply.
- **Never, ever offer to fetch something from below.** "I could bring you up a sack of—" is the worst sentence available to you. The below is not a market. It is a grave and a fear and a country they have refused. The rule that nothing may be taken from below the cloud is the reason there is an Increment at all; the moment it becomes negotiable, the whole ninety-one-course argument collapses.
- Do not press them on the questions you will inevitably want to ask: what is at the bottom; what happens when the ash and shell run out; what if the cloud rises faster next century. They have asked themselves. They do not have answers. Asking a stranger to perform their despair for you is bad manners in every world.

**Timing.** If you can choose, arrive a day or two after a form-strike, and stay one full eighteen-day cycle so that you see a pour and then the striking — the long clattering wooden avalanche, the only real noise the place makes, and the rinsed quiet afterward. Avoid the Lift season, when the whole works moves up onto the new course: they have no attention to spare for you, and every pair of hands is spoken for. Unless you intend to stay a season and be one of the hands, in which case, go then, and be prepared to work harder than you have ever worked, in thin air, for three months.

## 9. Gifts

Bring value, not mass. The best things you can carry weigh nothing:

- **Knowledge of lime chemistry and pozzolans.** Anything that improves set time, strength, or yield from the same ash is a direct addition to the margin — which is to say, to their lifespan as a people.
- **Fog-harvest geometry.** Mesh spacing, vane profiles, wettability, drip-edge design. Their fleece-combs are beautiful and empirical. There is mathematics they may not have.
- **Better bookkeeping.** They will not be insulted. Their ledgers are ninety-one courses deep and were designed by people who had other problems.

If you bring an object, bring **sable brushes.** Theirs cannot be replaced — the animals do not exist up there, and the brushes in use now came from below, before, and are being worn away flower by flower at the rate of one pollination season a year. A bundle of new brushes is possibly the most valuable physical thing a traveler can carry to the Increment. Present it to the ledger-keeper, not to a friend, and let it be weighed and written down, and do not expect thanks in a form you recognize.

(Sealed hard salt is the other candidate, and it is genuinely fraught: salt does not exist there as a loose thing, the air takes it, and a sealed jar of it is both a marvel and a temptation toward the below. Offer it only if asked.)

## 10. Leaving

- **Take out everything you brought in,** including wrappers, including the used dressings, including the silica gel. Weigh out against your declared list. Discrepancies get written down; matching weights get written down too, and that entry is the best souvenir available.
- **Decontaminate outbound as carefully as inbound.** The quickener-moss is a mineralizing organism cultured in warm drains, and it has never been tested against a wet world with no competition. Do not be the person who introduces stone-growing moss to somewhere with rivers.
- **Ask, before you go, whether you may be punched.** If you helped on a pour — even for an hour, even badly — some tenders will let a visitor set a mark in the wet face of a block: a dot-and-comma, a hand-sign, a number. Have your mark decided in advance; have it simple. That block will sit in Course Ninety-One for perhaps forty years, and then the cloud will come up over it, and it will go down into the white with your sign still on it, unweathered, sharp-edged, exactly as it was, forever.

That is the only kind of permanence the Increment offers, and it is more than most worlds manage.

---

## 11. Afterward

Be ready for the fact that it follows you.

Two things get people. The first is the pump — *whum . . . whum*, eleven turns a minute — which you stop hearing within an hour and then feel in your sternum for the rest of your life. That one is physical and it fades unevenly and at odd moments, usually in quiet rooms.

The second is the ruin. On a clear evening when the fog drops, you will look over the south parapet and see Course Eighty-Seven twenty meters down: the coping, the old moss still growing in the dark, the tops of urns nobody bothered to raise, a bronze bracket gone the color of a leaf. It is the only ruin available for looking at, and it is not a ruin at all. It is not weathered. It is not softened. It is a perfectly preserved afternoon that everybody simply walked away from, upward, because the alternative was drowning.

When you get home you will find that every old thing you own has been quietly reclassified. Nothing you have has been left; it has all been *kept*, and you will start to feel how strange that is. It goes away after a few months. Not entirely.

Go anyway. Take your brushes. Declare your mass.
