# Preparing to Visit the Warm Stratum

## First, decide how you are leaving

Everything else in your preparation follows from this, so settle it before you buy a single strap.

The town sits somewhere between ninety and two hundred metres below the Shine. That is thirteen to twenty atmospheres. You cannot simply walk back out of that. After twelve to twenty-four hours at depth your tissues will be saturated with whatever inert gas you have been breathing, and from that moment onward your body carries a *debt* that is paid only in slow hours of ascent. A week in the warm stratum and a week on the surface are the same thing: a week at the Shine's underside with nothing to do, going up a few metres a day, watching the drift-beach thicken above you.

So there are three honest itineraries, and you must choose one:

**The short visit (under six hours at depth).** You arrive, you are dazed and half-blind, you understand almost nothing, you go up on a schedule. This is tourism in the worst sense and I would not bother.

**The saturation visit (weeks to months).** You accept that you live there now, for a season. You come up once, slowly, at the end. This is the only way to actually *see* the place, and it is the option this guide assumes.

**The locked gate.** If your means of travel can be sited inside a pressurised chamber on the far side — if you can step out of the Warm Stratum at fifteen bar and into a hyperbaric room somewhere sensible — then you may come and go freely, and decompress at leisure on your own world's clock. Arrange this if you possibly can. It is worth an enormous amount of trouble. Note that it also means your arrival must be *compression*, not a step: budget an hour of going down, clearing your ears every few seconds, and expect the cold, giddy, faintly electric feeling of pressure coming on.

Whatever you choose, tell someone. The town has no way to send word to your people, and no concept of a world that could receive it.

## Screening: the body you must bring

An ordinary human body can do this. An ordinary human body with one small unexamined flaw cannot.

- **Teeth.** Have every filling checked and, if there is any doubt, replaced. A void under a filling becomes a vacuum at depth and the tooth implodes inward into your jaw. It is a small, absurd, blinding agony and there is no dentist within a universe.
- **Ears and sinuses.** You must be able to equalise reliably, on demand, hands-free if possible — you will be doing it constantly, and often with occupied hands. Do not go with a cold. Do not go with a cold *developing*. A blocked sinus on ascent is a reverse squeeze: blood in the mask, a headache like a spike, and no way to stop going up.
- **The hole in your heart.** Get screened for a patent foramen ovale. A quarter of people have one and never know; under decompression it is the difference between a stiff shoulder and a stroke.
- **Lungs.** Any asthma, any history of collapsed lung, any scarring — do not go. Air trapped behind a spasmed bronchiole on ascent will tear the lung open.
- **Hernias, bowel, anything that holds gas.** Same principle.
- **Fitness for slow work in warm water.** Not sprinting fitness. Endurance at a crawl, in heat, half-hydrated, for weeks.

And get your vaccinations for nothing in particular, because nothing in particular is what you will meet. The pathogens there have never seen you and you have never seen them. This cuts both ways and the ethical weight of it falls on you, not on four thousand people with no immunology and no word for *quarantine*. Take a fortnight of isolation before you go. Do not go with a cough, a cold sore, a gut bug, or an interesting rash.

## Gas: the thing that will kill you first, and politely

The lung-fruit is a miracle and a trap.

It is a bladder that fills with gas made by a plant. Plants, in your experience, make oxygen. At fifteen atmospheres, oxygen is a convulsant poison; a partial pressure above about 1.6 bar will, sooner or later, take you in a seizure with no warning and no aura, and a seizure underwater is a drowning. If the fruit is even a quarter oxygen you have perhaps ten minutes.

That the townspeople breathe it all day proves only that *they* can. It proves nothing about you.

**Therefore: bring an oxygen analyser rated to pressure, and use it on the first fruit, and on fruit from each new terrace, and on fruit in each season.** This is not optional and it is not paranoia. Bring two analysers; bring spare sensors in oil-filled housings; bring a calibration gas.

Consider also the diluent. If what is padding out the mix is nitrogen, then at fifteen bar you are as narcotised as a man who has drunk a bottle of spirits, permanently, for the whole visit. You will find the townspeople charming and your own decisions excellent. You will also be unable to learn the tap-language, count a forty, or notice that you are in trouble. If you can carry a helium-based mix and a closed-circuit rebreather, do — but read the section on *gifts* below before you do, because arriving with a cylinder of breathable gas is arriving with a bank.

Two rules to write on the inside of your wrist:

1. **Never rebreathe a soft fruit.** When the bladder goes limp it is a bag of your own carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide narcosis is fast, stupid, and feels like nothing but a slight urge to rest.
2. **Never take fruit from a terrace that is not yours.** In a place where breath grows on a vine and is inherited by name, this is not theft. It is attempted murder, and it will be understood as such.

Learn, as well, the gaps. Fruit does not come on a hose. You will spend a portion of every hour not breathing anything, between one bladder and the next, walking calmly with your mouth shut. Train breath-hold before you go — not for depth or heroics, but for *placidity*: a comfortable, unpanicked ninety seconds of doing ordinary work with empty lungs. Train CO₂ tolerance tables. Train the ability to notice the urge to breathe and simply decline it for a while longer.

## Heat: the false comfort of bathwater

"Warm as bathwater" is the most dangerous sentence in the description.

Water pulls heat from you roughly twenty-five times faster than air. If the warm stratum sits at 30–33 °C, you will feel pleasant for two hours and hypothermic by dawn — a slow, unnoticed, cumulative chilling that shows up as clumsiness, then bad judgement, then shivering you cannot stop. If it sits at 36–38 °C you have the opposite problem: you cannot shed the heat of your own work, and you will cook gently from the inside, dehydrating, until you faint.

Bring a thin layer — two or three millimetres of neoprene or a fibrepile undersuit — and bring a thermometer, and spend the first day doing nothing but finding out which of the two problems you have. Bring the means to add insulation (a second layer, a hood; most heat leaves through the scalp) and the means to remove it.

The cold pane is not a metaphor. It comes down over the abandoned upper shelves like a sheet of glass and the temperature drop across it is sharp enough to make you gasp. Do not go up to look at the empty houses "just for a moment." A moment is how long it takes to lose the use of your fingers.

## Water on skin: the injury nobody plans for

Nobody who has not lived immersed understands what weeks of it do.

Your skin macerates. It goes white, then soft, then it comes off in sheets at the pressure points — under straps, between toes, in the groin. Fungal and bacterial colonies bloom in every fold. Small cuts do not scab, because scabs require air; they stay open, weeping, and they get infected, and they stay infected.

And the only sharp thing in that world is *everywhere*. The glassy rind of the pillow basalt flakes into splinters that will lay your hand open to the bone, and you will not much feel it, because warm water numbs, and the first you will know is the cloud — because blood in still water does not wash away. It hangs. A red smoke around your hand, drifting slowly upward, visible to everyone on the shelf.

So:

- **Boots with rigid soles and high ankles.** Not sandals, not neoprene socks. Something you could kick a bottle to pieces in.
- **Gloves, always, and a spare pair.** You will be putting your hands on that rock a thousand times a day.
- **Shin and forearm protection.** Everything in that world is at the height of your shins.
- **A wound kit built for wet work:** cyanoacrylate tissue adhesive, hydrocolloid dressings that bond to wet skin, systemic antibiotics, antifungals, and a great deal of tape.
- **Dry time.** This is the real medicine. Arrange, with whoever will house you, an hour a day in an air-bell with your suit off, drying. Consider it a prescription. Consider it the price of the visit.

And drink. Immersion causes a profound diuresis — the pressure on your body's periphery convinces your kidneys you are drowning in your own blood, and they dump it. You will urinate constantly and you will be dehydrated the entire time and you will not feel thirsty. Chronic dehydration is also the single largest risk factor for decompression sickness on your way out. Drink on a clock, not on thirst.

## Eyes

Without an air gap in front of your cornea you are about forty dioptres long-sighted; the world is a warm brown blur with lamps in it. The locals' eyes have presumably made their peace with this. Yours have not.

A low-volume mask is the obvious answer, and it works, and it costs you something. Faces in that town carry a large part of the language — the hands do the grammar and the face does the tone — and you will be wearing a pane of glass over yours, with your eyes magnified and your cheeks compressed, looking like a specimen. Expect children to stare. Expect adults to address your chest.

If you can obtain hyper-corrective contact lenses ground for immersion, do. Being face-naked is worth a great deal socially, and it removes the single most common emergency in the whole business, which is a flooded mask fifteen metres up a vine ladder.

Either way: **prepare for darkness.** The light is dim as an hour after sunset and the colour of weak tea; red has been eaten by the water, and so has orange, and so — almost — has yellow. Your blood will look black. Your skin will look grey-green. Nothing you brought will be the colour you packed it in. Label your kit by *shape* and by *texture*, not by colour, and practise finding things in the dark with gloves on.

Bring a lamp, small, dim, green-biased, on a lanyard. Do not bring anything brilliant. Beyond the courtesy — you would destroy the dark-adaptation of everyone on the shelf, and glow-moss lanterns are a real and careful technology there — a searchlight in a black basalt town makes you a small god, and you did not come to be one.

## Sound and the loss of your voice

You will not speak again until you leave. Not "you should not" — you cannot. Your voice will emerge as a smeared animal groan that carries a kilometre and means nothing.

Most travellers underestimate this completely. Practise: take a full week at home, before you go, in total silence. Not "quiet" — silence, with everyone in your household enforcing it. Learn what it does. The common report is something close to mild bereavement around day three, and a strange tranquillity after day six.

Then do the actual work, which is months long:

- **Learn the hand vocabulary.** As much as you can get. There is no faster way to be treated as a person.
- **Learn the tapstone code**, at minimum the emergency subset. Two taps and a scrape: *come up*. The long roll: *the vent field is breathing*. Learn what you are supposed to *do* about the long roll. Learn it from three different people, because the answer may not be the same on every shelf.
- **Understand that you cannot localise sound underwater.** Your ears determine direction by comparing arrival times between them, and in water sound travels so fast that the difference collapses. A tapstone struck anywhere in the basin will seem to come from inside your own skull. The locals have compensated all their lives with a thousand small cues you do not have. So: never plan to *run toward* or *away from* a sound. Plan to look at what everyone else is looking at.
- **Bring a slate.** A diver's writing slate and a graphite stick, on a lanyard. It will save your life at some point. Think carefully before you teach anyone to use it.

Underneath all of this is the ticking — the granular, continuous, everywhere tinkling of cooling glass, like a room full of people quietly unwrapping something. For the first two days it is enchanting. Somewhere around the second week many visitors discover they cannot stop hearing it, and that it is the sound that comes home with them.

## Buoyancy, weight, and the religion of the lanyard

You will be issued, or should commission, a stone belt. Have it fitted by someone local; getting it wrong by a kilogram in either direction turns every step into a fight. Trim it so you are very slightly heavy — never, ever positively buoyant. Positive buoyancy in a place with no ceiling is an escalator to death.

Then learn the one physical habit that separates visitors from residents: **nothing falls**.

Everything you let go of leaves. Your knife, your pencil, your glove, your analyser, your last fruit — it rises, gently, out of reach in three seconds and out of the world in a minute, and it will end up on the drift-beach under the Shine with the leaf-parings and the dead.

So: every object you own is tethered. Every one. Tie it before you use it, not after. Build the reflex at home, for weeks, with your ordinary possessions on strings, until it is stupid and automatic. Nothing marks you as a fool faster, or costs you more, than watching your own compass ascend.

The same law governs you. **The dead rise.** If you panic and swim upward, your lungs will expand faster than you can vent them and burst before you have gone thirty metres, and you will arrive at the Shine in the condition of the departed. Everything about that town's moral universe is arranged around the fact that up is where you go when you stop holding on. Do not go up. Not fast, not "just a bit," not to see something.

And hurry, in general, is the whole catalogue of local deaths. Warmth and buoyancy conspire against hurry; so should you.

## What to pack, and what will implode

Pressure decides your kit list more than utility does.

**Will fail:** anything sealed with air in it. Vacuum flasks (they crush). Rigid camera housings not rated to twenty bar. Sealed food packets (they shrink to bricks, then burst on ascent). Ballpoint pens. Watches not rated for depth. Anything pneumatic. Anything with a gas bladder you did not intend.

**Will survive:** solids, oil-filled electronics, pressure-balanced housings, silicone, titanium, polyethylene, braid, bone, stone.

**Bring:**
- Two oxygen analysers, sensors, calibration gas.
- Thermometer, depth gauge, and a decompression computer you trust, plus a printed set of tables and the ability to use them when it dies.
- Dive knife with a blunt tip, on a lanyard. Line-cutter. You will be in a thicket, and thickets entangle.
- Spare lanyards. Spare straps. Ten metres of good cord.
- Wound kit as above. Broad-spectrum antibiotics. Antifungals. Rehydration salts in enormous quantity.
- Calorie-dense food in flexible packaging, eaten in air-bells. Expect to lose weight regardless.
- A soft, small, dim lamp.
- Ear drops, because your ear canals will be wet for a month.
- Something absorbent and entirely dry, sealed in double bags, for the air-bell hour. A towel is a luxury you will come to think about the way other people think about money.

**Do not bring:** fire, or the means of fire, or the idea of fire outside a bell. Not matches, not a lighter, not thermite, not a lesson in lime-burning. You may not think you are carrying an industrial revolution in your pocket, but you are, and the hearth-bells are the single point of failure in four thousand lives, and you have no idea what happens to that town if a stranger makes the impossible look easy.

## Manners, hazards, and things you must not do

**Do not eat the fish.** The superstition is not defended and does not need to be.

**Do not swat the flies.** They are lucky. Their presence in a room of trapped breath is, I suspect, a genuine and ancient CO₂ assay — a bell whose flies have died is a bell that will kill you — but whether anyone knows that or not, you do not swat them.

**Do not touch the tongue-lilies.** It costs them a day to reopen and it costs you nothing to keep your hands to yourself.

**Do not break pillows for souvenirs.** The glass shards you scatter will find someone's foot, and the interiors are still warm, and the rock is the town's stone, timber, tool-steel and coinage all at once.

**Do not go into the scalding band.** The shrimp-gatherers go down for a count of forty and come back boiled pink and they have done it since childhood and some of them do not come back. You would be dead at fifteen.

**Do not cut a belt.** Not your own, not anyone's, not a joke, not a gesture. The weighting of the dead is the central moral argument of that culture, and a stone belt severed is an act with exactly one meaning.

**Do not take a side in that argument.** Cairn or Shine: they have not decided, they do not have to, and they won't. A visitor with opinions about where other people's grandmothers go is a visitor who will be quietly stopped being told things.

**Do not exhale carelessly into someone's air-bell**, and do not let your rebreather vent into one. A bell is filled a bladderful at a time over years. It is a family's savings hanging in the ceiling of their house. Contaminating it with your carbon dioxide is not a faux pas; it is arson.

**Which brings up gifts.** Think very hard. You could, with a single spare cylinder, hand a family more breath than their grandparents accumulated in a lifetime. That is not generosity; it is an intervention in an economy, and someone will be poorer afterward and someone will be dangerous. The best gift you can give is labour: two weeks of tending someone's lung-fruit terrace, pruning under instruction, hauling stone for a wall, sitting the rotation at a hearth-bell. It costs them nothing to accept and it is the only currency you have that does not distort anything.

**And know the long roll.** A stratified, still, kilometres-deep body of warm water sitting on live vents is a thing that holds dissolved gas the way a sealed bottle does, and the panes that make the town possible are also the lid. If the basin ever turns over — if a vent belches hard enough to start a column rising, or the mountain shrugs — the whole lake will de-gas at once from the bottom up and everything in it will suffocate in a matter of minutes. There is nothing you can do about this and nowhere to go. I mention it only so that you understand what the town is listening for, all the time, under the ticking; and so that when everyone stops braiding to listen, you stop too.

## Going home

Start your ascent a day earlier than you think you need to, and slower than the tables allow. You are coming out of saturation; the numbers are not conservative by nature and you are not a professional saturation diver.

In the last two days at depth: no alcohol equivalent, no strenuous work, no new injuries, maximum hydration, no beans and nothing fermentable, and no hot immersion. Sleep. Be boring.

On the way up you will pass through the cold pane, and then through the abandoned upper shelves — the emptied houses with their roofs opened and their air let out, standing in the pale light as a monument to a direction. You will hang there for days in that dim cold with nothing to do but look at them. Bring something to occupy your mind that does not require fine vision or a dry hand. Most people find that this part of the trip is the part they remember.

Then you will come up under the drift-beach: a soft ceiling of pale sediment, a beach hanging over the world, made of dead leaves and spent lung-fruit and, somewhere in it, people. Go through it gently. It is not yours.

## Afterwards

Three things are commonly reported.

The first is that air is too thin. For a week or two after coming back, the ordinary world feels unsupported — you will put a cup down too hard, misjudge stairs, find that your own body has become suddenly, unpleasantly heavy, and catch yourself reaching to tether your keys.

The second is that you will not want to talk, and when you do, your voice will sound to you like an intrusion.

The third is the ticking. Most people stop hearing it within a month. Some do not. You should know that before you go, and decide whether you mind.

Go slowly. Tether everything. Test every fruit. Drink more than you want to. Learn the long roll before you learn anything else.

And decide, before you step through, what you want done with your body if you die there — and write it on something they can keep. They will ask. It is the first serious question they ask anyone, and it would be a discourtesy to arrive without an answer.
