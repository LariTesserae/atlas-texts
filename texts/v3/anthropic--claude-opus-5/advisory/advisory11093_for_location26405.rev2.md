# Preparing for the Shelf

## First, understand what kind of danger this is

The Shelf will not kill you with weather or predators or disease. It is a cool, clean, well-drained place with docile fauna, good water, and no violence to speak of. The hazard is arithmetical, and it works slowly, and by the time you feel it you are already inside it.

Everything below follows from that. Pack for a cold damp hike; prepare, mentally, for a bookkeeping error with a human face.

---

## The body

**Altitude.** Thin air, hard light, and no acclimatization available on-site because there is no lower ground to sleep at — the fog begins immediately below the rim and nobody who lives up there will accompany you down. Acclimatize on the approach. Give yourself four to six days of staged ascent through the valley towns. Arriving winded at the gate is a poor first impression in a place where the whole social order is *steadiness*.

**Temperature.** Cellar-cool, always, never freezing, never warm. This is worse than cold. Cold you dress for and then the sun helps; here the sun never helps. You will be at 9–12°C in high humidity for the entire visit, day and night, indoors and out, and your body will slowly lose the argument. Undyed wool, three layers, with a fourth for sitting still. Wool, not synthetics: the town smells of lanolin and woodsmoke, and you want to smell of the same, partly for courtesy and partly because your own imported smell will be the first thing every animal in the city notices and the last thing you stop noticing.

**Light.** Unmediated. The stone is pale grey-green and throws the sun straight back up into your face. Sunburn under the chin and inside the nostrils. Bring dark glasses and expect to be asked what is wrong with your eyes.

**Feet and hands.** The rock fractures conchoidally: every broken edge on that mountain is a blade, and nothing has worn them, and nothing will. The eastern scree is not scree in the ordinary sense — it is a field of unblunted shards lying exactly where they landed, and it rings underfoot like crockery. Take the thickest-soled boots you own and take a second pair. Take gloves for the rim. Do not steady yourself on a broken edge; do not sit down on one; do not, ever, put a hand out casually to break a fall. The Shelf opens palms.

**The tilt.** Two degrees northwest, everywhere, forever. It does not sound like anything. It is, in practice, the most exhausting feature of the place. One leg is longer than the other for the whole visit. Your inner ear will spend a week insisting the horizon is lying to you, and you will develop a low, persistent, seasick headache that resolves about three hours after you descend. Bring small hardwood wedges for your bed. (Carry them out again — see below.)

## The one decision you must make before you leave home

The Shelf keeps totals. Population four thousand one hundred. The gate-keeper's scale is a literal stone bench: the departing sit until the arriving sit.

So you have two ways in, and they are morally different.

**Arrive counted** — up the switchbacks through the fog, on foot, to the gate, and let them enter you on the cord. This is the honest way. It also means that when you step onto that bench, someone who has been sitting there for a year, or nine years, stands up, and goes down into the fog, and the town's grief-and-gladness machinery turns over once. You will have given a stranger their life back. You will also have taken their place in the most complete sense available in that universe, and you will not be permitted to leave until another body comes up out of the fog to take yours. That could be a week. It is more often a season. It has been, for some people, a decade.

**Arrive uncounted** — by whatever means brought you into this universe, stepping onto the plateau somewhere in the empty northern quarter at dusk with nobody watching. This keeps you free. It also makes you a surplus, a four-thousand-one-hundred-and-first, and the Shelf's whole demonstrated behaviour is that surpluses are corrected.

I cannot tell you which the Even actually counts: the tally cord, or the fact. Nobody there can tell you either, because it has never come up. My instinct — and it is only that — is that it counts the fact, and that it corrects within the day, and that the correction is not directed at you.

If you arrive uncounted, go to the gate within the hour and be counted. Yes, this costs you your freedom of exit. That is the price of the ticket, and if you are not willing to pay it, go somewhere else. There are thousands of worlds. There is one town of four thousand one hundred people who did nothing to deserve your experiment.

## What to carry, and the terrible problem of leaving

Nothing accumulates on the Shelf. There is no midden. Think about that for a moment: a city of that size with no rubbish layer, no earlier walls under the walls, nothing discarded anywhere, ever.

You are made of discard. You shed hair, skin, breath, fibre, water. You will want to eat, and then you will want to not-have-eaten.

**Pack an inventory.** Before you climb, make a knotted cord recording every object you carry — one knot per item, grouped by tens. This is exactly how they keep records and they will understand it instantly, where a written list would make them politely uncomfortable (ink implies revision; a record you can alter is not a record). Present the cord at the gate. Present it again when you go. The relief on the gate-keeper's face is worth the two hours it takes to tie.

**Carry nothing divisible.** No sand, salt, grain, beads, loose tobacco, no pills in a bottle that rattles, no notebook with pages that tear. Anything that can lose a part will lose a part, and you will spend an afternoon on your hands and knees in a stone gutter looking for one lentil, and you will be right to.

**Carry no seeds.** Brush out your cuffs and turn-ups at the bottom of the switchbacks. The vegetation up there does not spread — it *concentrates*, one enormous occupant per hundred feet of pavement — and whatever rule enforces that, you do not want to find out how it handles an intruder. Or how it handles you for having introduced one.

**Carry no iron.** There is no iron on the Shelf. Not "little"; none. Zero is a total like any other, and it is the total I would least like to perturb. Leave your knife at the bottom. They have copper, and they will lend you a copper blade, and here is the thing about borrowing it: *the number of sharp knives in the city is constant.* Do not bring a sharp knife up. If you do, somewhere in the lower quarter a woman's knife goes blunt in the middle of a cut, and she will never know why, and you will.

**Eat your own food where possible, and carry out what comes of it.** All of it. This is not squeamishness; it is the local physics. Ask at the gate what arrangement they make for visitors — they have one, it is matter-of-fact, and it will involve a sealed clay vessel and a promise. Keep the promise.

**Expect the night film.** On cold mornings the whole plateau sweats a fine chalky condensation that returns the day's dust to precisely the places it was taken from. Your dust was never taken from anywhere there. Sleep outside once, for the experience, and notice in the morning where the film has settled thickest. It will be on you.

## Health: the part people are least ready for

I suspect, strongly, that injury and illness are conserved on the Shelf like everything else.

I have no proof. But I would not bet against it, and the implication is this: if you break a bone up there, the break may simply *hold* — not worsen, not heal, not infect, just persist, stable and exact — until somewhere in the town another bone breaks and the ledger allows yours to close. The same for a wound, a fever, a tooth.

Prepare accordingly:

- Bring nothing infectious. Do not go with a cold. A contagion entering a closed conserved system either cannot propagate — or propagates by exchange, which is worse.
- Bring analgesia and clean dressings, and accept that you may be managing a static injury rather than a healing one.
- Do not take risks you would take at home on the grounds that you'll mend. You may not mend.
- And if you are injured, and then, two days later, you hear that a boy on the third level has fallen from a terrace wall and broken his arm, and you feel your own arm ease — do not tell anyone. There is nothing useful in that knowledge and there is a great deal of harm.

## Manners

Speak lower than you think you need to. Everyone does; you will find yourself doing it within ten minutes and it is not superstition, it is that the ambient sound floor is water in gutters and a sheep-bell four hundred yards off, and a normal voice in that room is a shout.

**Do not ask how old anything is.** Not the wall, not the city, not the bell-stalk rings. The question has no referent there and it lands like an obscenity — you are asking a person to imagine a time when things were otherwise. They cannot, and being asked to try is distressing to them in a way you will not immediately read as distress.

**Do not use the comparative casually.** Bigger, more, fewer, growing, dwindling — their speech is thin in this register and rich in a register you don't have: a whole vocabulary of *holding*, *matching*, *closing*. Learn six of those words. It will buy you more goodwill than any gift.

**Which brings us to gifts: don't.** Or if you must, give and receive in the same motion, item for item, weight for weight, and let the Count witness it.

**Offer labour, not goods.** You cannot build — nobody builds; there are twelve masons and their entire profession is walking around with knotted cords confirming that the walls are where the walls were. But you can carry, winnow, herd, and above all you can count. Counting is the town's real occupation and they will put a willing visitor to it gladly. Spend a day counting bell-stalks in a ring of forty. It sounds like a tedium and it is the single best way to understand where you are.

**The animals.** Nothing fears you. A sill-hawk will not move off a wall to let you pass, and you must go around it. Do not shove, shoo, or pet. Do not feed anything, not one crumb — feeding is addition. The stone hares sleeping in the doorways of the forty-one empty houses are not strays and the houses are not abandoned; the hearths are cold but the dew-fins are maintained, because the count of houses is fixed. Children play in them. You may sleep in one if offered. Leave it swept.

## The rim

Go. It's why you came. Late afternoon, with gloves, and stay back from the bevel — the edge is clean-fractured stone at an angle that does not announce itself.

Watch the Spill go over: nine feet of white rope, two hundred feet down, and no plunge pool, no hollow, no sound of landing. The fog below lies level as standing water. The far ridge comes up out of it unrounded.

Do not attempt the descent below the rim. There is no bottom that anyone has found, and the people who say that are people who count things accurately for a living.

---

## Coming back

Two warnings, and the second is the real one.

First: your exit. If you were counted in, you must be counted out, and you will sit on the stone bench and wait for a shape to come up out of the fog. Budget for this. Bring a long, slow book — bring several — and understand that if your multiverse egress is time-limited you have a genuine problem, and that the *only* clean solution is to have arranged, in advance, for a second traveller to come up the switchbacks and take the seat. Two of you, staggered. It's the standard protocol among people who do this more than once. Go alone and you are gambling with someone else's stakes.

Second: the gutters.

Within an hour of arriving you will stop hearing the water. It becomes the floor of your hearing, a soft plural trickling under everything, and you will not think about it again the whole time you are there.

And then you will leave, and about three weeks later, in some loud ordinary world, you will remember it.

I cannot prepare you for that. People who have been to the Shelf recognise each other by it. Give yourself a slow re-entry — a quiet town, a week, nothing with engines — and be warned that a minority of travellers come back unable to tolerate accumulation: they cannot abide clutter, they count their possessions, they get distressed when a number changes. It fades. Usually.

Take the trip. Be counted. Carry your dust out with you.
