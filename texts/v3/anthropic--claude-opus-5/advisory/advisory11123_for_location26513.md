# Preparing for the Sill

## First principle

Everything you know how to do in a landscape assumes two things: that light comes from above, and that sound goes somewhere. Both are wrong here. Almost every mistake visitors make on the Sill descends from one of those two assumptions, so build your preparation around unlearning them rather than around fitness or gear. The gear is easy. The unlearning takes about four days, and it is uncomfortable.

---

## The body: light from underneath

Plan for burns in places you have no habit of protecting.

Ordinary sun discipline protects the top of you. Here the cloud deck is a second sun, dimmer but enormous and level, and the pale tuff throws its own share back. You will burn:

- under the chin and along the jaw
- inside the nostrils
- the underside of the nose, which peels badly and comically
- the roof of the mouth and the back of the palate, if you walk breathing through your mouth (bring lozenges and learn to breathe through your nose while working)
- the lower eyelids and the skin under the brows
- the palms, if you gesture much — and you will gesture constantly
- the earlobes, from below; the scalp along the partings, from above
- the insteps and shins through thin fabric

Twenty minutes of exposure is a real burn. Bring an opaque mineral paste, not a lotion: zinc or titanium, thick enough to be visible, and apply it as a mask that includes the *underside* of every feature. Reapply at midday and again after the afternoon fog, which strips it. Bring three times what you think you need; the tenders will not have any to spare and it is the single most welcome gift you can arrive with.

**Eyes.** Standard sunglasses are worse than useless because they leave the lower field open and encourage you to look down. You want full glacier glasses with side shields *and* a lower shield or a wraparound with a deep bottom seal — something that blocks the ground-return. Bring two pairs. Bring a spare pair as a gift. Polymer lenses yellow and haze here in a season, which tells you what they are absorbing on your behalf; glass survives longer but cracks on the scree.

**Lips and hands.** Cracking, then splitting, then slow healing in permanent damp. Zinc stick, tape, and antiseptic. Any wound you take on the rock — and the rock cuts like broken porcelain, which is what it looks like — will stay wet for a fortnight. Treat every graze on the day it happens. This is not fussiness; it is the local standard of care and people will judge you by it.

**Altitude.** Twenty-six hundred metres is modest and will still hurt you if you arrive from sea level and start walking. Give it three nights. The trap is not hypoxia but dehydration: you will be damp twice a day, cold, breathing dry thin air, and losing water the entire time without ever feeling thirsty. Bring electrolyte salts and drink on a clock, not on thirst.

## Water: the local sin

There is no stream you can put a hand in. There is no pond. All the water on the Sill is in the air, on a wire, in a pipe, in a plant, or falling — and everything on a wire or in a pipe is *sold*. It belongs to a country two thousand metres down that has none.

Do not drink from a gutter. Do not drink from a header. Do not cup your hands in the mist-hem at a cliff base and drink, though it will feel like the most natural act in the world. Water taken from the collection is not a trivial theft; it is the one thing that is genuinely wrong here, and the tenders' response will not be anger — it will be a silence that lasts for the remainder of your visit.

Arrange an allocation from a cistern before you need it, and bring your own mesh fog panel, half a metre square, with a bottle beneath it. Collecting your own drinking water is the local equivalent of good manners: it says you understand what the place is.

## The feet, and the ground

The tableland is unworn. Nothing is rounded. Scree is angular, unsorted, and ungraded, and it clicks and shifts as one sound-event on an otherwise soundless day.

- Stiff full-shank boots, high ankle, replaceable laces (they abrade through).
- Leather gloves, and a spare pair. Every handhold is a blade.
- Trousers in heavy cloth. Softshell will be shredded at the knees inside a week.
- Trekking poles with carbide tips, used mainly for probing.

**The splits.** These are the real hazard, and they are the reason to have a partner. Road-wide, cathedral-deep, in parallel ranks like ruled lines. In good light you see them. In the white hours you do not, and there is no echo to warn you at the lip — no acoustic sense of a void at all. Rope up near them. Never step across a moss cushion that bridges a gap; the moss will hold your weight almost long enough.

If you go *into* a split — and you should, once, because it is the strangest garden in the multiverse — go with a line and reel it in behind you. Density down there is total. You do not walk, you insert yourself, and the vegetation closes behind you silently and without a trace of your passage. Nobody will hear you shout. Nobody will hear you shout even from four metres away. The line is not a precaution, it is the whole plan.

**The mist-hems.** At the base of every cliff, a permanent standing veil of returning water. Slick unweathered rock, bad visibility, cold, and loud enough to swallow the little sound you have. Most injuries happen there. Go through, don't linger.

## Sound: the four days

You will find silence charming for about six hours, then interesting for a day, then upsetting.

Speak at normal volume and the person four metres off sees your mouth move and hears a suggestion. Shout and you get a flat dead exertion with no return, which is not frightening so much as *embarrassing* — an unmistakable sensation of having done something ridiculous. Everyone tries it once, on the first afternoon, and everyone stops.

Prepare for this:

- **Learn signs before you come.** At minimum: *look, over there, slowly* (the most-used gesture on the plateau, and if you learn only one, learn this); *yes*; *no*; *wait*; *come*; *stop*; *water*; *hurt*; *I don't understand*; *thank you*. The dialect has drifted beyond any relative, so learn *theirs*, not a substitute. A pocket card is acceptable and will be treated kindly.
- **Practice at home.** Spend one entire day not speaking aloud. Then spend an hour watching something that moves too slowly to see. Both of these are more valuable than any amount of hill-fitness.
- **Signalling.** Auditory distress signals do not work. Air carries badly; rock and steel carry well. Learn to strike stone on stone at ground level — it transmits through the tuff far better than through the air. Do not, ever, use a harp panel as a percussion instrument (see below). Carry a bright cloth for line-of-sight, and a light: at any hour except the white hours, a light is the loudest thing you own.
- **Sound-hunger.** In the anechoic quiet you will begin to hear yourself: heartbeat, joints, the blood in the ears, tinnitus you did not know you had. A minority of visitors find this genuinely intolerable and want to leave on the second night. Bring headphones and a small amount of familiar noise as a medicine, and ration it hard — heavy use makes the readjustment worse each time, and doing it in company is read as rudeness, like smoking indoors.

## Approach and greeting

**People arrive here by being seen.** There is no announcing yourself.

So: arrive at or near the lift-house, in the open, in daylight. Stop. Stand where you can be looked at, in profile, and wait to be noticed. Never approach a tender from behind. Never touch someone who has not first had you in their peripheral vision — a hand from an unheard direction is genuinely shocking here and is the local nightmare. When you want attention, move into someone's field of view and be still until they look up.

Do not materialise inside a panel row, inside a split, or during a white hour. If you are arriving by any means that lets you choose the moment, choose mid-morning on a clear day, above the cloud, on bare rock, at the escarpment lip.

## Machine etiquette

**The harps.** Four thousand-odd static wire panels. They have no moving parts and no guards and look entirely touchable. Do not touch them. A hand laid flat on a panel knocks the beads off the wires before they can run, and a single careless contact costs measurable litres. Vibration is the enemy of the entire industry; this is why the machines crawl and the bearings are waxed and nobody slams anything. Walk *between* ranks, on the service line, and keep your pack from swinging into wire. Do not lean. Do not photograph with a tripod leg inside a panel gap.

**The Combers.** Eleven millimetres a minute. They cannot hurt you by collision; you could nap in front of one and wake before it arrived. The danger runs the other way — they will *service* you.

A Comber judges a string by tension and drip-rate, not by material. It does not know steel from rush-stem, and it does not know rush-stem from your kit. Therefore:

- Do not rig anything taut, dark, and roughly vertical anywhere near a panel row. Not a tent guy, not a clothesline, not a fixed rope.
- Do not camp in the rows. If you do, you may wake to find a guyline stripped of fouling, re-tensioned to spec, trimmed, and *integrated*.
- Long hair: tie it up and keep it inside a hood in the rows. This has happened.

That the machines have quietly annexed a species of rush into the works — the wireweed panels out at the northwest edge, entirely vegetable and yielding well — is the most interesting thing on the plateau and the thing you should say least about. The question of whether to correct it has been open forty years. Forty years is a normal length of time for a question here. You may ask about it once. Listen to the answer, whatever it is, and then let it go. Do not offer an opinion, do not propose a trial, do not observe that it seems to be working fine. Visitors who arrive with solutions are the one type of guest the Sill actively dislikes. The same goes for the seventh pylon, which is bent, and has been bent for as long as there are records, and is not a problem.

## Kit, specifically

- Matte, non-shedding outer layers. No fleece, no lint, nothing that sheds fibre near wire.
- No velcro, no bells, no metal-on-metal. Not for noise — for the habit of gentleness.
- Everything unscented. Smell is the one sense that works at full strength here; the air is nearly odourless, and a scented soap is the equivalent of shouting. Cooking smells travel further than your voice.
- Silence your devices. Turn off every alarm and beep before you arrive.
- Hard cases and desiccant. Your kit will be soaked in fog twice daily and dried under fierce light in between. Electronics here fail by condensation cycling, not by cold. Seal, purge, re-desiccate nightly.
- Lens care: filters over glass, and expect haze. Photographs will come out flat and scale-less because nothing casts a shadow; put a known object in the frame or you will never be able to prove how big anything was.
- **A downstone.** A pebble on thirty centimetres of cord, in a chest pocket. In a white hour, when there is no up, no down, no distance, and no visible outline to your own hands, the plumb line is the only instrument that still works. The tenders carry them. Copy them.
- A whistle is not worth its weight. Bring the light instead.
- A watch, worn, and a written schedule. There are no shadows, so there is no time of day. You will lose the afternoon entirely on your second day if you don't.

## Ground rules to internalise

**Walk on rock.** Your footprint in a metre-deep moss cushion stays for a week; the cushion took a century. There are lines and service ways; use them, even when the moss is faster and softer and obviously fine.

**Leave nothing on the ground.** Not for an afternoon. Things here do not wear out — the rock is too new, the erosion too slow, the machines too gentle. Things here fail by being colonised. A tool set down at breakfast has moss in its handle-grain by autumn, and there is a concrete pad out on the plateau, footings and all, that now reads to the eye as a low green plinth. Count your kit at every stop.

**Watch your feet for the bird.** The mute grouse-thing does not flush. Its entire strategy is to stand still against a lichen crust for up to an hour, and it will let you tread on it. Walk slowly and look down — which the light punishes, which is the small daily bargain of the place.

**Sleep off the moss.** Wolf spiders hunt by touch and a sleeping bag on a cushion is an invitation. Platform, cot, or bare rock.

**Weather arrives as a change of brightness.** Learn to read the ground-light rather than the sky. When the whiteness begins to come from all sides equally, you have perhaps twenty minutes before the deck is over the lip.

**White hour protocol, decided in advance:** stop, sit, tether to something that is not a wire, downstone out, do not walk. It lasts an hour or so. Nothing stops working; the harps run their best yields of the year while you sit there; the Combers go on at eleven millimetres a minute somewhere out in the white. You are the only thing on the plateau that has to wait it out. Sitting still inside the light, with no distance and no outline to your own hands, is what most people come back talking about.

## The one appointment

Ask, on your first day, where the bench is — silvered wood, the colour of the tuff, sited to face down-cloud. On a clear evening the light comes level across the top of the deck and the whole white floor turns to hammered copper for about eleven minutes. Be sitting on it beforehand. Bring nothing to do. This is the only thing on the Sill that has a schedule, and the tenders keep it.

---

## Coming home

Expect to be quiet for a while. Most visitors find that they have stopped talking at normal volume, that they lean in, that they touch a forearm before speaking, that they look for the shadow under objects and are faintly unsettled when they find one. Expect to be startled by birdsong — the absence of it is the thing people notice *last*, having spent the whole visit not knowing what was missing, and its return is the thing that notices *you*.

Expect also to find ordinary machinery unbearable for a week or two: everything at home is so fast, and so loud about it, and none of it is arguing patiently with a mountain that rises thirty-one millimetres a year.
