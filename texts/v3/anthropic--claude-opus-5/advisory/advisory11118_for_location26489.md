# Preparing to Visit the Kiln at the Ninth Turning

## First, understand what you are walking into

You are not visiting a cave. You are visiting an *organ* — a functioning heat engine made of animals, and you are a lump of wet, cold, loud meat proposing to insert yourself into its working parts. Almost everything that will go wrong for you goes wrong because of one of three facts:

1. **You are made of water in a world that has none.**
2. **You are cold in a place where cold means death — theirs, and eventually yours.**
3. **You make noise, and noise is violence here.**

Hold those three in your head as the spine of every preparation decision. Everything below is a consequence of them.

---

## Part One: The Approach — the mountain, before the door

### Timing your arrival

Do not arrive at night. The scree slope at night is a mean, scraping cold on angular unsorted rock in a world with no moon-glow worth the name and no snow to reflect anything. You will turn an ankle in the dark on a slope that takes a step and gives back a half-step, four hundred miles from anything.

But — and this is the trap — you also do not want to *enter* the Kiln during the outside day. The chamber breathes on a half-day cycle. During the day, the doorway warms and the tide comes **upslope**, toward the entrance, toward you. During the night, cold air sinks in through the door and the whole living mass migrates twenty or thirty paces down toward the fissure, leaving the upper chamber comparatively vacated.

So: **climb in daylight, camp on the scree, and enter in the small hours of the night.** You get a slope you can see, and a chamber whose first fifty paces are as empty as they ever get. You are entering along the same cold draft they are fleeing.

Accept the cost of this: you will be walking *down the cold gradient into the crowd*, and the crowd will be denser and hotter than the daytime average by the time you reach the fissure end. Plan your turnaround before you plan your descent.

### The trail is gone

The mountain has taken back the trail. Whoever cut the spiral and the five strokes above the lintel has been gone long enough that there is no path, no cairn line, no worn tread. You are route-finding on unsorted talus.

Bring:
- **Stiff-soled boots with high ankle support.** Not soft approach shoes. The rock is angular and unweathered — it hasn't been rounded by water because there is no water. It cuts.
- **Gaiters.** Scree in the boot on a slope like that is a trip-ender.
- **Two poles.** On a slope that gives back a half-step you want four points of contact.
- **A helmet.** You are ascending under other people's rockfall, including your own.
- **Heavy leather gloves for the approach.** You will be using your hands on rock that is essentially chipped glass.

### Marking your own way back

There is no trail and no snow and no vegetation and no water and nothing to distinguish one grey slope from another grey slope, and near the summit on the north side the scree simply *parts* around a low doorway. It is a hip-height hole in a rockfall. In flat light, from twenty paces upslope, it is invisible.

**Mark it.** Aggressively. Redundantly. Build a cairn taller than yourself downslope of it. Set a GPS pin *and* a physical marker *and* a bearing to a summit feature. Leave a bright, non-organic, high-contrast marker — a flag, a strip of survey tape, something the wind cannot take. If you come out of that door disoriented, dehydrated, and shaking, you must not have to *search* for your camp.

And leave your camp where you can see the door from it.

## Part Two: The Doorway — thresholds and ethics

### Stop at the lintel

The doorway was made. Three courses of dressed basalt each side, a lintel of the same, the whole pitched slightly forward as if bowing. Above it, a shallow spiral, nearly weathered away, and beside it five vertical strokes worn to soft grooves.

Before you go anywhere, **spend an hour here doing nothing but recording.** Photograph the spiral and the strokes in raking light from multiple angles — they are nearly gone, and your visit may be the last one before they finish going. Do not touch them. Do not chalk them. Do not rub them, take a squeeze, or "just clear the dust." Photogrammetry only.

Note the joint packing. It is not cement; it is the glassy foot-residue of the animals inside, harvested and used as mortar. Somebody went in, gathered residue, came out, and built a doorway with it. That is an enormous fact and you should sit with it before you crawl through.

### The ethics you should have settled before you arrive

You are about to enter a two-hundred-pace room that contains the **entire** known instance of this biosphere. There is no other place like this for four hundred miles. This is not a population; it is a *species*, possibly a whole ecology, in a single chamber.

Which means:

- **You are a biohazard.** Every microbe on your skin, every spore in your pack fabric, every fleck of dead skin you shed is an introduction to a closed system that has had no contact with anything for hundreds of thousands of cycles. You cannot fully sterilize yourself. You can radically reduce your load. Do it.
- **Every animal you crush is a real subtraction.** They pile knee-deep when startled. You will step on them. Accept it, minimize it, and count it if you can.
- **You should not take specimens.** If you must — and I would argue you must not — understand that a body removed from that room stops being alive the moment it leaves the thermal envelope. Its blood is a salt eutectic that solidifies at any temperature the outside world can offer. You will not be carrying a specimen out; you will be carrying out a small casting of a specimen, and you will have killed it to make the casting.
- **Do not touch the bowls.** Eight fired-clay bowls in eleven niches, holding domes of white salt polished by generations of hooked feet. Three more on the floor, broken, half-swallowed by pale stone. These are the only artifacts in the world here. Photograph them. Measure the depth of the swallowed ones against the floor's growth rate if you like. Leave them.

Also: leave a written note at the doorway saying who you are, when you went in, and when you expect to be out. If you don't come out, whoever follows deserves to know.

## Part Three: The Body — heat, water, and the fact that you are a wet animal

This is the section that keeps you alive. Read it twice.

### Understand the specific kind of heat

The heat arrives all at once, the way water would if there were water. It is **dry radiant heat off every surface simultaneously** — floor, walls, low ceiling — plus warm air, plus (down at the fissure end) something approaching an oven. The rock is too hot to rest a palm on for a slow count of four.

That "count of four" is your single most useful thermometer. Rock you cannot hold for four seconds is somewhere around 60–65 °C. That's the *cool end.* The floor over the hot fissure is soft and worked and honeycombed with galleries, which means it is well past that.

Radiant heat is not like hot air. It does not care about your fan, your airflow, or your evaporative cooling. It cooks you through line-of-sight. And in this chamber you have line-of-sight to hot rock in **every direction at once**, including a low ceiling hanging drapes that brush the top of your head.

There is no shade in the Kiln. There is no cool wall to lean against. There is no floor to sit on. There is no thermal refuge inside except the entry crawl itself, and the entry crawl is also hot.

**The doorway is your only heat sink. Everything about your time budget is measured in distance from the doorway.**

### Water

You are the only water on this world.

Your body will attempt to solve the radiant heat problem the only way it knows: by evaporating you. In air with essentially zero humidity and a strong radiant load, you can lose **two to three liters an hour** and never feel wet, because it flashes off your skin before you register it as sweat. You will not feel like you are sweating. You will feel like you are drying out, because you are. Nose dry in three breaths. Eyeballs pressed on. Lips splitting.

Plan on:
- **A minimum of 1.5 L per 30 minutes inside**, carried on your body, insulated, drinkable through a bite valve without stopping or removing anything.
- Electrolytes in all of it. Plain water at those loss rates gives you hyponatremia and a seizure in a room you cannot be carried out of.
- A separate, larger cache **immediately outside the doorway**, in shade, buried under scree so the day doesn't cook it.
- More water in camp than you think you could possibly drink. There is no water within four hundred miles. Every drop you use, you carried.

Insulate your water. Radiant heat will bring an unprotected bladder to scalding in minutes. Reflective sleeve, minimum.

### Clothing: the counterintuitive part

Your instinct will be to strip. **Do not strip.** Against pure radiant heat with air temperature above skin temperature, bare skin is a receiving antenna. You want to be fully covered in loose, light-colored, highly reflective, non-flammable layers with an air gap.

- **Outer layer: reflective, aluminized, loose.** A proximity-suit outer or aluminized coverall. Not tight. You want a still-air gap between the reflective shell and your skin — that gap is your insulation, and the shell's job is to bounce radiant load back at the rock.
- **Nothing synthetic against your skin that melts.** No nylon base layers. Fine wool or aramid.
- **Full hood.** The drapes brush the top of your head and the hunters that pass above you are hotter than everything else. Your scalp will register a bar of warmth when one crosses. Your scalp should also be covered.
- **Gloves, always, at all times.** You will instinctively put out a hand to steady yourself. Everything you touch is either 60 °C rock or a living animal whose feet end in a hook. Heat-resistant leather or aramid, with enough dexterity to work a camera.
- **Sealed goggles.** Not glasses. The dry air will strip the film off your eyes; a sealed pocket of your own exhaled humidity in front of your eyes is a genuine mercy. Anti-fog them, but honestly, in that humidity, fog is not your problem.
- **Boots with a genuinely insulating sole and a rated heat resistance.** You will be standing on a floor that is soft and worked over the hot fissure. Standard rubber will soften. Foot burns through the sole are the injury that ends the trip.
- **A respirator.** Two reasons, below.

### Air: what you are breathing

The chamber smells of scorched salt, something faintly sweet like burnt sugar, a mineral sharpness of sulfur and hot iron. The fissure at the low end gives, every so often, a soft cough of gas.

That cough of gas is the thing that kills you without warning. A volcanic fissure venting into a poorly-ventilated chamber can deliver:
- **CO₂**, which is heavier than air, will pool along the floor and in the low end, and will simply switch you off. You will not smell it. You will get a headache, then confusion, then nothing.
- **SO₂ and H₂S**, which you *can* smell — and H₂S deadens your sense of smell at exactly the concentrations that matter, so "I stopped smelling the sulfur" is an emergency, not a relief.
- **Oxygen depletion** generally.

Carry:
- **A four-gas meter** (O₂, CO, H₂S, combustibles) plus a **separate CO₂ meter**, worn low on your body, alarms on, loud enough to hear over nothing. Set conservative thresholds. The chamber's ventilation is the half-day breathing cycle — during the outside night, cold air sinks in and rolls along the floor to the fissure, which means at night the airflow is *inward and downslope*. Good for your entry. Bad in that anything venting from the fissure has nowhere to go but up into the chamber you're standing in.
- **A respirator with acid-gas and particulate cartridges.** The particulate side matters independently: the chamber is full of fine sand of stiffened bodies, crumbled plates and hooks, salt dust. Do not breathe that. You have no idea what an inhaled salt-eutectic organism does in a warm wet lung, and you do not want to be the case study.
- **A genuine escape breathing apparatus** — a short-duration supplied-air unit, ten minutes minimum — that you can deploy blind, in the dark, with gloves on, in a rain of bodies. Ten minutes is enough to cover two hundred paces uphill to the door if you don't panic. Practice deploying it at home until it is muscle memory.

Do not go alone. Someone has to drag you out.

### The time budget

Be brutal about this. In a fully sealed reflective suit with a respirator, in radiant heat like that, with no cooling available, you have on the order of **thirty to forty-five minutes** of useful function before your core temperature starts climbing on a slope you cannot reverse without leaving. Heat stroke's first symptom is bad judgment, which means the person who most needs to leave is the person least able to decide to.

So don't decide. **Set a timer and obey it without discussion.** Two of you, two timers, and a standing agreement — signed in advance, discussed while cool — that when the timer goes, you go, no matter what you have just discovered. The Kiln is not going anywhere. It has found its shape and keeps it. Whatever you found will be there next time.

Consider a phased approach:
- **Trip one: entry crawl and first thirty paces only.** Fifteen minutes. Learn the heat. Learn how your body fails.
- **Trip two: the niches on the west wall.** They're at standing-person height, spaced by a stride — findable by touch alone. Document.
- **Trip three, if at all: toward the fissure.** With gas monitoring, a tether, and someone stationed at the doorway.

Cool down properly between trips. Actively. In the mountain's night, with water, in the shade, for hours. Heat injury is cumulative; a second exposure on the same day at 80% recovered is how people die.

## Part Four: Light, and the problem of the dark that is not empty

The only light in the world here is a line of dull cherry at the fissure, no brighter than the memory of an ember. It does not illuminate. It gives the far wall an edge and takes away every other detail.

### On bringing light

You must bring light. But understand what you are doing.

Every animal in that chamber has **no eyes at all and no pigment.** Your light will not blind them or startle them — they cannot perceive it. That's the good news.

The bad news: **your light is heat.** A powerful lamp is a moving hot spot on a wall that is a living heat map. Animals arrange themselves obsessively by gradient, thick where hot, thin where dull. Point a 3000-lumen beam at a patch of wall and you have just created a false gradient, and they will respond to it — slowly, tidally, but they will respond. You are writing on them with your torch.

So:
- **Use the least light you can work with.** Low, wide, diffuse. Not a spot beam.
- **Red or long-wavelength if your documentation allows** — mostly for your own night vision, since theirs doesn't exist.
- **Thermal imaging is your primary sense here, not vision.** This cannot be overstated. The chamber's entire information content is thermal. The depth of the living coat *is* a temperature map. The hunters are hotter than everything else. The soft worked floor over the fissure is hotter. A thermal camera turns the Kiln from an incomprehensible dark crowd into a legible, beautiful, readable document. Bring a good one, bring a spare, and bring batteries — and know that a thermal camera in a uniformly hot chamber needs careful range setting or everything whites out. Practice.
- **Redundancy:** three independent light sources, each carried on a different part of your body, each tested cold and tested hot. Lithium cells behave badly at 65 °C. Check the temperature ratings of everything electronic you own, then assume the ratings are optimistic.

### The dark above

The ceiling is lost above in a darkness that is not empty. The hunters come along the drapes from above, from the unmapped dark of the vault, arm's-length long, ten legs, and they are the only things in the chamber that hurry.

You will not see them coming. You will feel a bar of warmth cross your scalp.

They eat the chain — running along a hanging drape and taking every third or fourth body. They are adapted to harvest small plated animals. They are almost certainly not adapted to attack something your size and they have no way to perceive you as anything but a very large, very *cold*, very strangely-shaped feature of the room. To a creature whose whole world is thermal gradient, you are a walking cold spot — which is to say, you look like death. Like the entry crawl. Like the place where limbs stiffen and grow eloquent with weight.

They will probably avoid you. *Probably.*

But an arm's-length ten-legged thing with hooked feet, moving fast, in total darkness, above your head, is a thing you should be armored against and not merely philosophical about. Hooks that leave glassy residue on basalt will go through light fabric. Wear the hood. Wear the goggles. Keep the reflective shell intact — a tear is a hot spot on your skin *and* an entry point.

And accept the real risk: it isn't predation. **It's startle.** Something arm's-length and hot lands on your shoulder in absolute dark and silence, and you flinch, and you shout, and then—

## Part Five: Silence — the discipline that matters most

Read the passage again. *Shout — go on — and the drapes let go. All of them, the length of the chamber, in a cascade you can hear travelling away from you, and then you are standing in a rain of bodies to the knee, and the air is briefly clear and blindingly hot, and then the reassembly begins, taking the better part of an hour.*

Understand every clause of that.

**"All of them, the length of the chamber."** One shout depopulates two hundred paces of ceiling. That is millions of animals dropped out of their gradients. Those on the wrong part of the floor — the white, dead, hard stone where the floor rises toward cooler air — will begin to set. Your shout has a body count.

**"The air is briefly clear and blindingly hot."** Here is the part people miss. The living coat is not just decoration; it is a *thermal blanket*. Millions of radiator-backs standing across the gradient are absorbing and processing a colossal heat flux. Strip them off the walls and ceiling all at once and the chamber's radiant load onto you spikes. **A shout in the Kiln raises the temperature of the room you are standing in.** In a marginal thermal situation, your own startled cry can be the thing that kills you.

**"Taking the better part of an hour."** You have not just disturbed the site; you have destroyed the observation. Whatever you came to see the arrangement of, you cannot see for an hour, and the arrangement you see afterward is a recovery, not a baseline.

### How to train for silence

This is not "be quiet." This is a genuine physical discipline you must acquire before you arrive, and it is harder than the fitness.

- **Practice moving in full kit in total darkness with no vocalization**, for forty minutes, while someone drops things on you. Get startled, repeatedly, until your startle response stops routing to your voice. This sounds absurd. Do it anyway. It is the single highest-value preparation you can make.
- **Learn a full tactile signal set with your partner.** Squeeze codes on the forearm. Two squeezes: stop. Three: retreat. Long hold: emergency. No talking, no radio, no clicks, no beeps. Practice it blind.
- **Silence your gear.** This is the tedious part and it is essential:
  - Tape every buckle. Tape every zipper pull.
  - No hardshell fabrics — no rustling nylon. Soft, quiet outer.
  - Nothing loose in a pocket. Nothing that clinks.
  - **Camera: silent electronic shutter only.** No mechanical shutter, no beeps, no focus-confirmation tone. Disable every sound in the menu, then test it in a quiet room, then test it again.
  - **Your gas monitors have alarms.** This is the direct conflict at the heart of the trip. Solution: convert them to vibration-only and put them against your skin, with a slaved vibrating alert on your partner too. If your monitor can't do vibration-only, get one that can. Do not go in with an audible alarm you cannot afford to have go off.
  - Voice-off on everything. Airplane mode. No notifications. No, seriously, check.
- **Manage your own body sounds.** Cough control. Breathing through the respirator is already muffled — good. But a sneeze in that dry, dusty, salt-laden air is very likely. Take an antihistamine before entering. Have a plan for a suppressed cough into your elbow.

Note also: the sift of dry flour is so even that within a quarter hour you stop hearing it and hear your own pulse instead. This is disorienting. You will begin to hallucinate structure in the silence. Expect it; don't chase it.

## Part Six: Navigation and the floor

### The floor is dead bodies

It is a slope of packed and fused corpses, cemented by the glassy residue, compacted into pale nacreous stone that rings faintly underfoot and crumbles at the edges into a gravel of individual plates and hooks. It is nine or ten times the thickness of a body wall.

Practically:
- **It rings.** Every footfall is a sound in a silent room. Roll your foot. Soft, slow, deliberate placement. Toe-first on a downslope.
- **It crumbles at the edges.** Do not walk near the chamber walls, where the crumbly gravel is; also don't walk near the walls because the walls are two hand-spans of living animal and you'll shear it off.
- **Over the hot fissure it is soft, worked, and dug through with galleries.** This is a **collapse hazard over a heat source.** A honeycombed floor above an open fissure of cherry-glowing rock. **Do not approach the fissure end on foot.** If you must document it, do so from a distance with optics, or approach on a belay with a partner anchored uphill, probing every step. Falling into the low end of the Kiln is not a rescue; it is a cremation.
- **The gradient is your compass.** You always know which way is out: *uphill, toward cold.* The floor slopes; the heat increases downslope; the door is at the top. Even in total darkness, blind, panicked, with your lights dead, you can find the door by walking away from the heat and up the slope. **Internalize this. It is the one navigational fact that will save you.**

### Wayfinding aids

- **A tether.** A thin high-temperature-rated line from the doorway, paid out as you go. Aramid or steel-cored — not polypropylene, which will soften and stretch. This is your handrail out. It is also your partner's route to you.
- **Do not lay physical trail markers on the floor.** Anything you leave in there becomes permanent architecture in the world's only such room, and anything with a different thermal signature than the floor will attract or repel animals.
- **The niches are a known landmark.** Eleven of them, west wall, standing height, one stride apart. Countable by hand. Use them for position.
- **Count paces.** Two hundred paces long, forty wide, forty paces of entry crawl. Learn the numbers before you arrive; you may need them without light.

## Part Seven: Cold, on the way out

Everyone plans for the heat. The reverse transition kills people too.

You will exit through a hip-height door into thin mountain air at night, having lost several liters of water, with a core temperature well above normal, wearing a soaked base layer under a reflective shell, into a mean scraping cold with no shelter. That is a textbook recipe for a hard crash: heat-strained cardiovascular system meets sudden cold, plus dehydration, plus altitude.

- **Have insulation waiting immediately outside the door**, in a dry bag under scree. A real belay parka. A hat. Dry base layers.
- **Have your camp genuinely set up before you enter.** Tent up, sleeping bag laid out, stove primed, hot drink ready to make. Do not plan to build camp after coming out. You will not be capable.
