# Preparing for the Sounding Flats
### A practical brief for the ordinary traveler

---

## 1. Understand what you are agreeing to

You are not visiting a loud place. You are visiting a **medium**. Loud places have edges; you can step out of them, and your body treats them as events. The Flats have no edge inside the valley and no beginning in living memory, and after seventy-two hours your nervous system stops filing the sound as *information* and starts filing it as *terrain*. That transition is the single most dangerous hour of the trip, because on the far side of it you will feel calm. You will not be safe. You will be acclimatized, which is a different thing and a worse one, because acclimatization to 150 dB is your body giving up on warning you.

Frame the whole expedition the way you would frame a trip to a radiation site: as a **dose**, spent from a fixed budget, in a place that is beautiful and mostly indifferent to you.

---

## 2. Screening: who must not go

Do not take this personally. The valley does not.

**Absolute exclusions.** Any prior lung disease, bullae or blebs, prior pneumothorax, asthma with air trapping. Any recent thoracic or abdominal surgery — anything inside you with a suture line and a gas space on one side of it. Pregnancy, unequivocally; a fetus is a small cavity in fluid and the field will find it. Untreated hernia. Aneurysm anywhere. Retinal detachment history. Middle-ear surgery, tympanostomy tubes, cochlear implants, any prosthesis with an internal void. Pacemakers and neurostimulators — not because of electromagnetics but because of lead fatigue at 40 Hz for a hundred hours. Uncontrolled epilepsy. Ménière's or any vestibular disorder. Severe anxiety disorder or a history of dissociative episodes, and I will explain why in §13.

**Relative, with a physician who has read this brief.** Migraine with aura. Sinusitis, ever, chronically. Reflux. Anything that makes you nauseated in the back of a car.

**Dental clearance is mandatory and nobody expects this one.** Have every restoration imaged. A void under an old filling is a resonant cavity in a rigid shell mounted directly in your jaw, and your jaw is coupled to your skull, and your skull is being driven continuously. People come home from the Flats with cracked teeth and no idea why. Get crowns re-seated. Get anything loose out.

Bring your own audiogram, at full resolution, taken twice on separate days. It is your baseline and it is the only evidence you will ever have of what the valley cost you.

## 3. What will happen to your body, in order

**Hours 0–1.** Pressure in the sternum, exactly as described, and it is not metaphor: your chest wall is being physically driven and your lungs are moving a little without your permission. Some people cough. Some gag. Both are normal. Breathe through your mouth, unhurried, and do not hold your breath — a held breath is a sealed cavity.

**Hours 0–6.** Your vision smears. The forty-hertz floor sits close to the resonance of an eyeball in its socket, and fine detail will simply not resolve while you are standing on loud stone. You cannot read a map in the belts. Write everything large, in black, in single words, and accept that detailed work happens in nodes only. Everyone underestimates this and everyone gets it wrong once.

**Hours 1–12.** Headache behind the eyes and across the frontal sinuses; sinus ache; a metallic taste; nausea in about a third of visitors, worse if you have eaten badly (see §11). Diarrhea is common on day two. Sleep will be poor even in the town, because your body knows.

**Days 2–5.** The temporary threshold shift arrives and does not leave. Everything sounds as if it comes through cloth, because it does — your protection is doing its job and your cochlea is bruised anyway. Tinnitus onsets and will be with you for weeks after departure, possibly forever.

**Day 5 onward.** The compensation the local biology has perfected in four centuries begins, absurdly, in you: you start reading pressure through your ribs and the soles of your feet. Badly. Enough that you will *know* you have stepped off a node before you can say how you know. Trust this new sense for navigation cues; do not trust it for hazard assessment. It is a five-day-old organ.

## 4. The head problem, and why earplugs are a joke

Understand the arithmetic before you spend money. The best hearing protection ever made gives you thirty-five decibels of insertion loss and then hits the **bone-conduction floor**: sound arrives at your inner ear through your skull, and no plug or muff can stop that. The floor sits around fifty decibels of attenuation, hard, no matter what you buy.

150 dB minus 50 dB is 100 dB. A hundred decibels, continuously, waking and sleeping, for the length of your visit. That is a chainsaw at arm's length, permanently, as your *best case*.

Therefore:

- **Deep-insert custom molded plugs**, taken from impressions of your own ear canals, plus **high-mass muffs over them**. This is the baseline and it is not sufficient.
- A **mass-loaded, viscoelastically damped helmet** that encloses the whole cranium and the mastoids and clamps to the skull through a decoupled suspension. This is what buys you the last few decibels against bone conduction. It is heavy, it is hot, and it is the most important object you own.
- **A mouthguard.** You will clench. Everyone clenches for a hundred hours.
- **Shave your head.** Hair spoils the helmet's coupling, and in the belts the field combs it into stripes and it abrades your scalp raw under the shell.
- **Sealed goggles** with the lenses mass-mounted, not spectacles. Frames on your nose and temples are a direct coupling path to your skull, and they will buzz your vision into mush.

Budget your loud-belt time in **hours**, not days. Ten hours a day in the belts is an aggressive research schedule. Two hours a day is a tourist schedule and will still cost you some permanent hearing. There is no schedule that costs you nothing.

## 5. The chest, the feet, and the resonance audit

**Torso.** A stiff, mass-loaded chest and abdominal shell — not armor, a *damper* — reduces how much of the field couples into your thorax. It will feel like being hugged by furniture. Wear it anyway; it is the difference between "unpleasant" and "coughing blood on day four."

**Feet.** The sound comes up out of the ground more than down out of the air. High-loss elastomer soles, thick, no air cushioning of any kind. Boots with air bladders in the midsole are cavities and will be found. Replacement soles: bring two sets. They fatigue.

**Now the audit, which almost nobody does properly.**

Before you leave home, put every single item of equipment on a shaker table and run it at 40 Hz and at every harmonic through the braided upper register, for six hours, and then look at what broke. Then do it again with everything assembled as you will carry it.

What you will discover:

- Threaded fasteners back themselves out. Every screw gets thread-locker or a lockwire. Every one.
- Anything with a spinning disc, a delicate optical path, or a relay is already dead. Solid state, potted in resin, or leave it.
- Batteries walk out of spring contacts. Tape and strap them in.
- Zips, buckles, and pocket flaps are irrelevant: anything not tethered will migrate out of a pocket and be gone. **Everything on a lanyard.** Nothing loose, ever, including your hat, which a blowhole will otherwise take.
- Aluminium frames grow fatigue cracks. Inspect at every rest, with your fingers, in a node, in good light.
- **Check the lengths of your tubes.** The wavelength of the floor tone in air is around eight and a half meters; the quarter wave is a little over two. Any hose, pole, tent spar, or breathing line cut near those lengths or their simple divisions will sing. Carry a file, deliberately cut everything *off*-length, and re-check after any field repair. A respirator hose that starts resonating on day three will simply take your breathing away from you and you will not be able to explain why.

## 6. The thing that will actually kill you is the heat

Thirty degrees at midday, twenty-six at midnight, ninety-plus percent humidity, no wind at all, and you sealed inside a damped shell and a respirator.

Read that again. You have no evaporative cooling and no convective cooling. You are a heat engine in a bag in a sauna, doing physical work, wearing furniture. The Sounding Flats do not usually deafen visitors to death. They cook them.

- **Active cooling is not optional.** Phase-change vests with a rotating stock, or a circulating-liquid garment with ice packs; the town has cold water and power, and your operational tempo is set by how fast you can recharge cooling packs, not by anything else.
- **Excursions are short and out-and-back.** Two to four hours. Return, strip, cool, rehydrate, measure your core temperature, and stop for the day if it is up.
- **Weigh yourself before and after every excursion.** Weight lost is water. Replace it with electrolyte, not plain water; you will lose enormous salt in a suit.
- Watch your partner for the early signs, because you will not notice them in yourself and you cannot hear them being told to you.
