# Preparation notes for a visit to the Rise

## First, adjust your expectations about what kind of trip this is

This is not a hostile-wilderness trip. Hostile wildernesses have weather, and weather can be waited out. The Rise has no weather. It has geology happening at a rate you can perceive, which is a different category of problem: nothing here is trying to hurt you and nothing here will pause.

Treat it, logistically, as a vacuum EVA on a surface made of scalpels, with a chemistry hazard and an infrasound hazard layered on top. Treat it, psychologically, as a sensory deprivation chamber with one enigma in it.

Plan hours, not days. Six on the surface is a good excursion. Twelve is an expedition. Nobody should be sleeping out there, because the plateau you mapped on the way out will not be the plateau you cross on the way back.

---

## What actually kills people, in order

1. **Falling.** Not the fall — the landing. You are in a pressurized garment and the ground is knapped obsidian. A stumble that would skin an elbow at home opens a bladder here.
2. **Glove and knee failure from contact wear.** Every wall, lip, and talus block at hip height is a fresh conchoidal edge with a hairline flake on it. You will touch things without deciding to.
3. **Heat, from below.** Long, dull, cumulative. Feet first.
4. **Getting lost.** See the section on navigation. It is worse than you think.
5. **Vomiting inside a helmet during the swell.**
6. **The vents,** which are rare and avoidable and therefore mostly kill the confident.

Notice that the volcano is sixth.

## The suit

Full pressure garment, hard upper torso if you can stand the mass, with an **abrasion-and-cut layer that is sacrificial and replaceable in the field**. Aramid or ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene over the bladder, and then a cheap outer skin over that which you expect to shred and replace. Bring three sets of outer arms and knees. Bring six pairs of overgloves.

Do not bring anything with an exposed elastomer seal. Vent gas here is sulfur dioxide and hydrogen chloride, dry as bone until it meets the only water on the planet — which is inside your suit and in your exhalate. Every seal you carry is a little chemistry experiment. Rinse-down at the airlock with an alkaline wipe, then inspect visually, then inspect again with a loupe. HCl attack on a faceplate anti-scratch coating looks like nothing at all until the coating comes off in a sheet.

**Thermal design is the part people get wrong.** The air is thin and cold; the ground is warm and radiates. Convective cooling is nearly useless, so your suit must dump heat by radiating — upward, into that flat black sky, which is an excellent heat sink. Downward, you are receiving. So: high-emissivity upper surfaces, insulated and low-emissivity lower surfaces, and boots that are essentially oven mitts.

Do not use a sublimator. Do not use water for cooling in any open-loop configuration. This is not an engineering preference; see **Water discipline**.

## Feet, hands, knees

**Boots.** Thick rigid soles — a full ceramic or composite midplate, not just a lug pattern. The ground is 100–200 °C in places and every fissure you straddle is radiating up between your legs at the suit's thinnest point. You want the sole thick enough that heat arrives at your foot as a rumour, not news. Also stiff enough that a plate of glass tilting under your heel doesn't fold the sole into your arch.

Expect the tread to be gone in one excursion. Glass is harder than your boot.

**Poles.** Two, with sacrificial tungsten carbide tips, because you will use them constantly to test whether the plate in front of you is bedded or floating. Half the floor is floating. A slab the size of a door will pivot silently under one foot and take you down onto its neighbours.

**Knees, elbows, hips: armoured.** And bring a **stool**. Genuinely: a light folding seat with broad pads, or at minimum a thick ceramic-fibre mat. There is nowhere on this world you can sit down. Not one place. Rest breaks require furniture.

**Practice falling.** Before you go, in the suit, onto a training bed of crushed volcanic glass. Learn to go down onto the armoured surfaces and to keep your palms off the ground. The instinct to catch yourself with a hand is the instinct that ends the trip.

## Seeing

All the light is coming from underneath and it is dim and it is one colour. Three consequences, all of them dangerous:

**Your shading is inverted.** Your visual system has assumed light-from-above since you were an infant. Here, shadows sit on the tops of things. Bumps read as pits and pits read as bumps, reliably, and you cannot argue yourself out of it in the moment. Train for this. Build a room with the light in the floor and walk broken rubble in it until your gut recalibrates. On site, carry a **small helmet-mounted lamp used only to re-establish top-lighting on a specific patch of ground you are about to commit your weight to.** Flick it on, read the terrain properly, flick it off.

**Distance is unavailable.** No haze, no aerial perspective, no atmospheric softening at all. A ridge at forty kilometres is as crisp as your boot. You will look at something and think "twenty minutes" and it will be a day's walk you cannot survive. **Bring a laser rangefinder and use it compulsively.** Range everything before you form an intention about it. Write the numbers down. Say them out loud. Do not let anyone in the party use the phrase "just over there."

**Dark adaptation is your most valuable and most fragile asset.** Thirty to forty minutes to acquire, one careless white light to destroy. Rule: no white light except the terrain-check lamp, hooded, aimed down, and announced on the radio before use. Everything else is deep red at the lowest usable level. Instrument displays taped over and dimmed to near-nothing. Someone will forget; assign a person whose job is to enforce it.

## Moving

Single file. One route. Same footprints.

This matters more here than anywhere you have been, for a reason that isn't safety. **Nothing on the Rise wears.** There is no rain, no wind, no frost, no biology. Your boots crush glass into powder, and that powder is the first sediment this world has ever had, and it will lie exactly where you left it essentially forever. You are, for the duration of your visit, the planet's entire erosive regime.

So walk in a line. Leave one scar, not a smear. Some crews take this further and route the return along the outbound track precisely, so the record is a single line out and back — a sentence, rather than a mess.

Speed: slow. Slower than that. The ground grades and clicks under you, plates shifting against plates, and each shift is a small negotiation you have to conclude before the next step. Twenty minutes to the kilometre on good terrace, an hour on talus. The talus is stable the way a stack of knives is stable: it will hold, and if it doesn't, it doesn't hold *gradually*.

Never cross a fresh lobe, however dull it looks. Never enter a roofed fissure, however architectural — carbon dioxide is heavier than the air and there is no wind to flush it, and even in a suit you do not want to be under a fallen sheet of glass when the eleven-minute swell arrives.

**Rope on the tilting terraces.** Short leashes between team members are a bad idea on knife talus (one person's fall becomes three), but running belays across suspect ground are not.

## Navigation and time

There is no sun. There are no stars. Assume no usable magnetic field until instruments say otherwise, and don't trust them when they do — you are standing on a inflating magma chamber and the local rock is full of iron that took its remanent orientation an hour ago.

You have: inertial navigation, radio beacons, rangefinder-and-bearing fixes on distant ridges, and dead reckoning.

**And your map has a shelf life of hours.** The plateau is growing. Cracks open a hand's width, terraces tilt three degrees, a route you crossed on the way out has a fresh fissure across it on the way back. Plan return routes as *problems to be re-solved*, not as paths. Budget double the time inbound. Set a hard turnaround by clock, not by objective, and appoint someone with the authority to call it who is not the leader.

**Beacons:** low-power radio, self-locating, placed at every decision point. They are litter, and you must retrieve every one. Count them out, count them in.

**The eleven-minute swell will try to become your clock.** Don't let it. Crews entrain to it within an hour or two — they start breathing on it, walking on it, counting it — and then they lose four hours without noticing. Run a talking clock at fixed intervals. Do scheduled radio check-ins that don't align with the period. Have someone maintain a written log with timestamps, because handwriting is the best available proof to your future self that time passed at the rate you think it did.

## The swell, and your stomach

The great motions of the Rise are nearly silent — the thin air carries almost nothing — so the biggest events arrive as pressure through the soles and the chest. Subsonic. It is not experienced as sound. It is experienced as *nausea and dread*, a rising wrongness with a period of about eleven minutes, and the dread is not a metaphor; that band of infrasound provokes it directly, in everybody, regardless of temperament.

Prepare accordingly:

- **Screen your candidates in an infrasound chamber.** A minority of people respond catastrophically — panic, vertigo, incapacitation. Better to find out at home.
- **Anti-emetics as standard prophylaxis**, taken before suiting up, not after symptoms.
- **Low-residue diet for twenty-four hours prior. No solid food for six hours prior.** Vomiting in a closed helmet is not survivable in any reliable way.
- **Brief the team explicitly that the dread is instrumental.** People handle it far better once they've been told, in advance, that at minute nine they will feel that something is behind them and something is wrong and they should not act on it. Name it before it happens and it becomes a wave to ride. Discover it in the field and it becomes a fact about the world.

## The silence, which is a hazard

Between swells, the acoustic environment is: the tick of glass contracting, thousands of tiny reports a second across the whole visible world, too faint to localise. Plus your own body, amplified by the helmet — heartbeat, joints, breath.

This does two things. It produces auditory pareidolia — insects, footsteps, voices, someone saying your name in a room-tone whisper — and it produces the sensation of an additional party member. Both are universal. Both should be briefed, and both should be logged rather than suppressed: "hearing voices at 03:40, third occurrence" in the log is a healthy crew. Silence about it is not.

Some crews run low-level pink noise in the helmet as a masker. It helps with the pareidolia and hurts your ability to notice the click of a slab going under someone else's foot. Judgement call; I'd run it quiet, and switch it off on talus.

## Water discipline (read this part twice)

There is no water on the Rise. Not scarce — **absent as a category**. No shape in that landscape was made by a fluid.

You are bringing the first water this world has ever seen. Several kilograms of it, in a bag, wrapped around your body.

Consequences:

- **Closed loop, everything.** No sublimation cooling, no venting of humid gas, no urine dumps, no "it's only a few grams." Every gram of water you exhale is captured, condensed, and carried home.
- **No open-loop leaks even in emergency.** Design your contingencies so that failure modes are dry.
- **Full sterilisation of the suit exterior** before arrival, and again on the far side of the lock. Not because anything you carry could survive out there — it couldn't — but because of what's growing on those vents.
- **And this: if a member of the party dies, the body comes back.** All of it. A human corpse is sixty percent water and a great deal of complex organic chemistry, deposited at a warm sulfurous vent on a pre-biotic world. It would be, without exaggeration, the largest single event in this planet's history. Have the recovery equipment. Have the plan written down and agreed to by everyone before departure, including the part where the recovery takes precedence over the sample cases.

The crusts around the vents are chemistry finding the cheapest path through a thermal gradient, and they are a few hundred million years short of learning to copy themselves. **Do not give them a head start.**

## The crusts

Cadmium yellow, verdigris, purplish-bronze; rosettes and fronds and lobed sheets that look exactly like lichen and are not. They grow while you watch — a millimetre per swell-cycle. They are among the most beautiful things in any world you will visit.
