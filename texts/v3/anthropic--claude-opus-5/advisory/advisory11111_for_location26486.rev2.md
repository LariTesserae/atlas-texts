# Preparing for the Ninth Throat

## 0. Understand the one fact first

Every other piece of advice descends from this: **the Ninth Throat has no erosion.** Nothing there wears, softens, rounds, forgets, or settles. Things grow until they break. That applies to the walls, to the note, to your gear, and — for the two hours you are down there — to you.

This has two consequences that govern everything.

**Anything that holds still becomes part of the wall.** Not eventually. Within the hour, measurably; within the season, irreversibly. This is not a curse; it is a precipitation rate. Your rope, your bar, your glove, your knee, your patience.

**You will have no working memory.** The roar wipes thought on a two-to-three-second cycle. You cannot plan, deliberate, count in your head, weigh an option, or remember why you reached for something. You will arrive at the same startled realisation of where you are, dozens of times, and each arrival will feel like the first.

So: everything you intend to do down there must already be finished before you go down. You are not bringing a mind into that room. You are bringing a **trained body and a set of objects that remember for you.**

---

## 1. Who should not go

Do not go if you have: any vestibular disorder, Ménière's, a history of perilymph fistula, migraine with aura, epilepsy, a PFO or unrepaired septal defect, asthma, recent dental work or any filling with a void beneath it, chronic sinusitis, untreated tinnitus or hyperacusis, an implanted device of any kind, prior significant head injury, or anticoagulant therapy. Do not go pregnant. Do not go with a head cold — a blocked sinus in that acoustic field is not an inconvenience, it is a haemorrhage.

Do not go if you are the sort of person who, when something goes wrong, tightens their grip.

That is the important one. Read the account of Ienn again. His error is recorded in six words and it is the only cause of death worth preparing for. Everything else that kills people there is downstream of not letting go.

---

## 2. The window

You cannot visit whenever you like. Two conditions have to coincide and they pull against each other.

**Light.** The chamber is lit by a bar of sun coming straight down a forty-metre shaft. That alignment lasts perhaps half an hour a day, and only for a stretch of weeks in the year when the sun climbs high enough over the lip. Outside that window the room is black, and bringing lamps into it is a bad and expensive idea (§6).

**Flow.** The column is meltwater. Too much and it hammers, the bell is thin and violent, and the shaft is a freezing torrent you cannot climb. Too little and the column stops punching the bell open — the chamber floods to the ceiling and there is nowhere to breathe. The bell exists only across a band of discharge.

The tenders know the band. They will not explain it in terms you can convert into numbers; they will simply say *not yet, not yet, tomorrow, now.* Believe them. Budget three weeks of waiting at altitude for a thirty-minute window and consider yourself lucky.

---

## 3. Permission, and the people

You are not visiting a natural wonder. You are attending a maintenance ritual that has run for thirty-one generations and which is the only thing standing between a village and a lake arriving on top of it.

Spend a season below before you go up. Learn enough of the language to be a person rather than a problem. Learn the eleven signs — properly, from the tenders, large and slow, with the exaggerated over-articulation they use; a sign made at conversational size is invisible in there. (Of the eleven: most concern the sill and the cutting; three concern your body; two concern leaving. The two that concern leaving are absolute. If you are shown either one, you go, instantly, without a query-sign, without finishing your stroke.)

Agree everything above ground, in writing, twice: who leads, what the abort conditions are, who tends the rope, what happens if you fail to respond, and explicit written permission for your partner to physically haul you out against your apparent will. You will not be able to negotiate any of this later. Down there, you will not be able to negotiate the letter *a*.

**Bring your own weight to carry.** The bronze pins are consumables. The dark sill stone is imported at enormous cost from somewhere with soil. Arrive with bronze stock, a spare hand chisel of good steel, and dressed sill stone if you can manage it on your back. Arrive with food for the tenders' families. Do not arrive with a proposal.

---

## 4. Conditioning the body (begin six months out)

**Altitude.** You will live and sleep above 3,500 m for at least two weeks before the ascent, and you will do the approach in stages. Learn the signs of HAPE and HACE in yourself and in others, because you will be climbing hard, cold, and wet, and immersion in six-degree water at four thousand metres is a near-perfect recipe for **immersion pulmonary oedema**. Know its signature: breathlessness out of proportion to effort, a cough, pink froth. If it appears, you come out and go *down*, immediately, whatever else is happening.

**Cold shock.** Six degrees on the face triggers an involuntary gasp. If that gasp happens when your mouth is underwater, you are finished in forty seconds. The gasp response habituates with repetition and only with repetition: at minimum a dozen deliberate cold immersions over six weeks, face-first, until the reflex is a flinch instead of a convulsion. Do not substitute willpower. Willpower is one of the things the room deletes.

**Breath-hold — carefully.** The tenders work the bell as a surface: three breaths, duck, work, rise. You will likely do the same. But you will be doing it hypoxic at altitude, where blackout arrives far earlier and with almost no warning, and the ascent from floor to bell is three metres — plenty. **Never hyperventilate.** Never dive without a partner watching. Set a hard, short, rehearsed cycle and live inside it. Your capacity there will be perhaps half what it is at home; plan for a third.

**Teeth, sinuses, ears.** Full dental check with a specific instruction to look for voids beneath restorations. Sinuses clear and clear for a month. No plugs in the ears — you want the canals flooded, not stopped with air. Accept in advance that you will come out with a temporary threshold shift and possibly a permanent one. Many old tenders are deaf. Some of them regard this as a promotion, and there is a reason: the deaf work well down there, because the wipe is weaker in them. If you are deaf and considering this place, you have a real advantage, and you should say so.

**Gut.** No fermented food, no legumes, no carbonation, for forty-eight hours. Gas-filled spaces are the parts of you the sound can grab.

**Hands and feet.** The approach spurs are unweathered and will open a boot sole. Stiff boots, spare soles, gaiters, and the understanding that everything you touch above and below is *sharp in a way rock in your world is not sharp* — flint-edged, brand new, never once knocked about by a river.

---

## 5. Conditioning the mind (this is the real preparation)

You are building a body that can do a two-hour job with no one at home.

**Externalise all state.** Adopt the tenders' logic and go further. Every piece of information you will need must live on an object you can read in under two seconds by touch or by one glance: a belt of ten wooden pegs you flip one at a time; a knotted cord at the wrist; a chisel marked with deep notches you can count with a thumb. Never store a number in your head. You will lose it before you finish generating it.

**Design for re-entry.** Assume that every two seconds you will wake up in a bright roaring box with no idea how you got there. Your entire procedure must therefore be a loop with a fixed recovery step: *touch the cord, read the pegs, resume.* Drill this until it is the thing your hands do when you are frightened. Frightened is your baseline in there.

**Drill under noise.** Practise the whole task — every stroke, every hand-pass, the whole thing — blindfolded, in cold water, with a metronome or a broadband rig hammering you, with a partner randomly interrupting and shoving you. Practise until you can be interrupted mid-stroke and pick up correctly without thinking. Then practise until you can do it having forgotten that you practised.

**Train against stillness.** Set yourself, over months, a habit of never resting a limb: continuous small shifts of contact, weight always transferring, a hand never on a surface for more than a breath. It is your insulation, it is your circulation, and it is the difference between coming home and becoming a feature.

**Train releasing.** Practise dropping things. Deliberately, repeatedly, on command, at the wrong moment, at the expensive moment. Use no wrist loops, no lanyards, no tethered tools, no gloves you cannot rip your hand out of. The floor of that room is razor rubble and anything you drop is gone — accept the loss in advance, in training, so that when a blade grows across the back of your glove your hand comes out empty and alive rather than staying to argue.

## 6. Gear, and why most of it is wrong

Every object you take in changes the resonance. You are adding mass and surface to an instrument that is continuously retuning itself, and it will retune around you. Think of your kit as an intervention, not equipment. Take the least of it that will do.

**Thermal.** A semi-dry suit, hooded, thickest at the core; not a drysuit. A drysuit's gas bubble will thrum and migrate and hammer you from inside. You will be cold. Cold is the cost of not being a resonator. Plan short shifts and accept that you have perhaps twenty to thirty minutes of useful hands.

**Mask.** Low-volume, small, soft-skirted, tinted. Tinted matters — the chamber is blinding, snow-glare from every direction with no shadow anywhere to rest an eye in, and an untinted mask will leave you functionally blind on top of everything else. **No full-face mask.** The flat plate of your forehead is a drumhead and a full-face mask is a way of beating it.

**Breathing.** If you carry a cylinder: a heavily detuned, downstream-biased, conventional regulator, and a spare. A sensitively tuned second stage will free-flow continuously in that field and empty your cylinder in minutes. **No rebreather, ever** — CO₂ narcosis is invisible to a mind that is already being wiped, and you will die contentedly. Better still: work the bell like the tenders, on breath-hold, with a small bailout cylinder staged in the shaft and a partner who knows exactly where it is.

Note also: clenching a mouthpiece couples the roar straight into your skull through the jaw. A soft, loose bite is not fussiness. It is how you keep any fragment of yourself.

**Rope and anchors.** Here is the room's joke. In the antinodes — the loud ribbed places — anything you anchor will be welded in within hours and will never come out. In the nodes — the smooth glassy silent channels — nothing grows, so your anchor is removable; but the node lines are exactly where the black-green algae lives, so they are slick as oiled glass. You must choose between a hold you cannot lose and a hold you cannot trust. Choose the node, and back it up.

A slack rope resting against a rib for two hours comes up with spar in its sheath. Three visits and it is stone. Your tether must be **tended and moving** the whole time, by a partner in the bell, continuously, or it will be a rod.

**Light.** Only if you must, only shockproof, only sealed and solid-filled, and understand that a falling blade will smash it and that you cannot afford the distraction of a failure. The sun-bar is better than anything you own. Go in the sun-bar.

**Recording.** If you want the note: a hydrophone rated well past 200 dB re 1 µPa, mechanically isolated, with everything else in the chain far up the shaft. It will still clip. Ask permission first, and ask it as a serious question, because a recording is not a neutral act in a place whose only archive is thirty-one generations of hummed pitch tied into a cord. The tenders may say yes. They may equally decide that a recording is a way of not listening, and that this is the one sin.

**Leave behind:** anything with a gate, a zip, a hinge, a diaphragm, a membrane, a spring, a battery, a bearing. It will seize, sing, or fill with stone. Everything you take out will come out chalked and gritty and must be scraped and dried the same day or it will be scrap.

**Hair braided and capped.** A loose hair is a nucleation fibre. Shave for the mask seal. Cover every cut you already have — a bleeding hand pressed against a growing rib is how you and the wall get introduced.

## 7. Going down

Rope to the lip. **Never approach the intake unroped.** The slot takes birds every year; it will take you with far less ceremony, and you will drown in the chamber without ever having decided to go in, and the rib will grow over your shoulder, and someone in eleven years will chisel you free or not.

Forty-one footholds, wet, beside the falling column, cut by hands that are dust. The sound doubles every ten metres and somewhere around the twentieth foothold it stops being a sound and becomes weather. Descend deliberately. Count the footholds with your feet, not your head.

Stop at the bell. Take the time to have the shock. You will have it repeatedly anyway; have the first one on purpose, before you are loaded.

---

## 8. Inside

Do the job. It is two hours of chiselling growth off a sill in a place where you cannot think, cannot speak, cannot locate the noise, and cannot rest.

Keep moving. Do not kneel — kneeling is the posture that took Ienn: low, static, in the antinode, at the sill, with both hands committed. Work from a hovering, shifting stance, neutrally weighted, one contact point always breaking as another forms.

Read the pegs. Cut. Read the pegs. Cut.

Breathe in the worst place. The bell is three breaths' worth of directional hammering a hand's breadth from your face, and it is where the air is, and you will hate going up into it as much as you have hated anything. Go up anyway, on the cycle, on the drill, without renegotiating the cycle, because the version of you down there cannot be trusted to renegotiate anything.

When a blade cracks — you will feel it in your sternum, the note will drop, white shards will come down — do not look up to see. Cover your head with a forearm, hold position low against the sill, let the fall finish, then resume. Looking up is how you take a flint edge in the face.

And at some point, several times, you will simply come to — brilliant cyan light on all sides, no shadow, no direction, a roar coming from inside your own skull and from the year 1200 — and you will not know where you are or who you are with. This is not an emergency. It is the normal operating condition of a human being in that room. Touch the cord. Read the pegs. Resume.

---

## 9. Coming up is the crux

Almost everyone who is going to die there has already survived the room and is on the ladder.

You will be hypothermic, arrhythmic with cold, exhausted, wiped, with hands that will not close, and forty metres of wet chisel-cuts above you in a spray of freezing water. Reserve strength for this. Reserve gas for this. Reserve a rope for this, rigged and tended from above, by someone who is warm, who has a hauling system built and tested *before* you went down, and who has explicit permission to use it.

Set a hard turn-around on the pegs, not on how you feel. How you feel is not information down there.

---

## 10. Afterwards

**Immediately:** dry, insulated, sugar, out of the wind. Watch for pulmonary oedema for the next twelve hours. Expect vertigo, nystagmus, nausea, a profound flattened exhaustion, and ringing.

**Within the first hour, while you are still useless:** talk. Out loud. Into whatever archive you keep, or at a companion who has been told to simply receive. Your memory of the chamber is not stored the way normal memory is stored; it is fragmentary and it is already leaving. What you do not say in the first hour you will mostly not have. Do not wait until you feel coherent. You will not be coherent today.

**Give the note.** Hum what you carried out and let the tenders match it and knot it. Then look at the cord, thirty-one generations long, a minor sixth of falling, and understand that you have just added a single knot to the only instrument in this world that is measuring anything at all.

**Weeks after:** expect the visit to come back sideways — in the shower, in bright snow, in any large sustained noise. Expect a sense that there is a gap in you where an afternoon should be. Expect a specific, recurring dream about a kneeling shape under eighteen centimetres of white. Tinnitus may be permanent. Some hearing loss is likely. Decide now whether you are willing to pay that, because you cannot decide it later, and the room will not give anything back.

---

## 11. What not to fix

You will come out of there with ideas. A steel sleeve for the drain. Sacrificial anodes. An inhibitor dosed into the basin. A pump. A survey. A better sill.

Hold all of it for a year before you say any of it aloud.

Every object in that chamber becomes part of the instrument. Add mass and you move the antinodes; move the antinodes and the mineral starts depositing somewhere it has never deposited, and nobody — not the tenders, not you, not thirty-one generations of knots — knows what the new shape does to the drain. The one certainty in the Ninth Throat is that every change to the geometry is a change to the voice and every change to the voice is a change to where the stone goes. The village downstream has survived because people have been removing material and adding almost nothing for eight hundred years. That is not primitivism. That is the correct engineering answer to a system with no erosion and no equilibrium, arrived at by people who paid for it in Ienns.

If you must leave something, leave a chisel and bronze. They are the shapes the room already knows.

---

## 12. One last item for the pack

Bring something worn.

Everything native to that mountain is three days old at the edges — raw, sharp, unbreathed-on, never once tumbled. The only smooth objects in the whole world up there arrived from somewhere that has time: the imported sill, the flattened pins, the footholds re-cut by four hands across the centuries. Smoothness, there, means *someone carried this from a place where things get old.*

So bring a river cobble. An oiled wooden handle with a palm-shine on it. A coin with the face rubbed off. It is not an offering to the mountain, which cannot be offered to. It is a gift to the people at the bottom of it, and it says the only thing worth saying to them, which is: *there are places where things wear down instead of breaking, and I have come from one, and I will take your note back there with me.*
