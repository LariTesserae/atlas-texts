# Preparing for a Visit to the Lower Cast

## First, the correct expectation

You are not going to a cave. You are going to a construction site the size of a valley, in the fourth or fifth year of its work, where the walls are the product and the landscape is a by-product of the walls. Everything that looks like nature there is a mistake somebody has decided not to fix yet: the grass, the moss stripes, the heron, the fog. The crews are courteous about these mistakes and quietly proud of them, and they will not thank you for pointing out that they are mistakes.

Go with the attitude you would take into a working foundry or a ship's engine room, not the attitude you would take into a wilderness. You are a visitor in someone's workplace, and it is a workplace where the consequences of small stupidities are large, delayed, and expensive.

Budget three weeks minimum. The first week you will be useless — headaches, no sleep, no appetite, and a body that has not yet decided what o'clock is. Anyone who tells you they did the Lower Cast in four days saw the shaft-light circles and the nearest mess hut and nothing else.

## The body: five things there that are working on you at once

**Cold with wet.** It is not deep cold. It is a few degrees either side of freezing, at total saturation for six hours out of every twelve, with a three-to-four metre-per-second wind for the other six. That combination takes heat out of a person faster than dry cold at twenty below, and — this is the part that ruins visitors — **nothing dries.** There is no evaporative drying in the fogged hours because the air cannot accept water, and in the windy hours the wind is cold off the surface and will chill you through anything still damp rather than drying it. Your kit does not dry unless you deliberately dry it, with heat, on purpose, as a scheduled task.

Plan your clothing as a rotation, not an outfit. Assume you will get a full soaking every second day.

**Alkali.** Fresh concrete is caustic. Not corrosive-in-the-dramatic-sense, but pH thirteen, and the burns it gives are the worst kind: painless while they are happening, deep by the time you notice. The crews sit against two-week walls to eat because two-week walls are dry-skinned and mostly harmless and they know it. A new pour is not, and the slurry that collects in the floor slots is not, and the grey water that gets into the top of your boot definitely is not.

Rules: never kneel in grey water. Never sit directly on a surface younger than you have been told to sit on. If your sock is wet with something slippery-feeling, stop and change it *now*, not at the end of the shift, because the standard Lower Cast injury among visitors is a heel or ankle burned to raw meat in a boot the man never took off. Carry a litre of clean water and a fistful of citric-acid sachets specifically for rinsing; flush long, flush cool, then a weak acid rinse, then dress it dry. If you get slurry in an eye, that is not a first-aid matter, that is an evacuate-up-the-shaft matter, and you should know before you arrive how long the hoist takes.

**Ammonia.** Twelve machines, running continuously, in a cold enclosed volume with poor mixing during the holding-speed hours. Everyone on the east terrace lives with a small chronic risk of a large event.

Learn this properly before you go, because it is not intuitive: ammonia is intensely water-soluble, which means a wet cloth held over your nose and mouth is genuinely, unusually effective, and it means the fog will take some of a release out of the air and give you a false reprieve. It also means the release will look like ordinary fog, only whiter and moving wrong. Nominal ammonia is lighter than air; a real release is a cold dense cloud that lies down in the trough exactly where you are standing. **You go up.** Up the terrace, up the stairs, toward an adit, across the wind if there is wind. Not along the trough. Never down toward the basins.

Carry a proper cartridge respirator with ammonia cartridges — and, separately, dust filters for pour days, and know that these are not interchangeable and cannot be made so. Carry goggles that seal. Practise donning both in the dark, in gloves, before you go, until it is boring. Learn the smell threshold in a controlled way at home if you can, and learn the more important fact: at concentrations that matter, your nose stops reporting.

**The beat.** Four beats a second from each hall, out of phase between halls, giving an eleven-second swell and fade. You will feel this in the sternum, the sinuses, the fluid of the eye. It causes headache, a mild persistent nausea, an unearned dread at about hour thirty, and disturbed sleep. Earplugs do almost nothing, because most of it is not arriving through your ears; wear them anyway for the fan howl, which is a separate and genuine hearing hazard.

There is no trick to the beat. There is only the week. Everyone adapts, nearly everyone by day six. Do not schedule anything important, decide anything important, or take offence at anything in the first week. Bring whatever headache remedy you trust and more of it than you think.

**Darkness and orange.** Sodium light is effectively monochromatic; you will lose colour vision within the hour and get it back only in the shaft-light circles, where it comes back so hard it feels like a chemical. Everything at more than twelve metres is mass without surface. You will misjudge distances constantly for the first days, and you will misjudge them in the dangerous direction, which is *nearer than it looks* for large things and *further than it looks* for edges.

Get your dark adaptation and then protect it. Twenty to forty minutes to build, one careless white beam to destroy — yours or anyone's. This is the single most common way for a visitor to make himself disliked in an afternoon.

## Kit, annotated

Bring less than you want to bring, but bring these:

**On the body**
- Wool next to skin, top and bottom, two sets. Not cotton anywhere except an outer smock. Cotton wet in that wind is a slow assault.
- Wool middle layer, and a second one, because you will be standing still a great deal — watching pours, waiting on hoists, standing through changeovers.
- Oilskin or rubberised outer with a **collar that closes** and a proper wide brim on the head. The wet hours drip from eighty metres up and the drops arrive with weight; the brim is stated in the local rules as a morale item, and the local rules are correct. A drop down the back of the neck once an hour for six hours will make a reasonable person cry.
- Rubber knee boots. Not leather — cement destroys leather stitching and alkali water rots the uppers. Take **three sets of felt liners** and rotate them, one on the feet, one drying, one dry in reserve. This is the single best-value item on the list.
- Studded or heavily lugged soles, and understand why: the newest bays still carry mould-release oil, water beads on it and will not wet, and a fresh bay under a film of beaded water is the most slippery flat surface most people ever encounter. Crews walk around new bays. Do likewise. If you must cross, cross short and cross square.
- Gloves: thin nitrile under wool, several pairs of each. Cement eats hands.
- Hard hat over the brimmed hat, or a brimmed hard hat if you can find one. Things are dropped from ducting.

**Light**
- A hand lamp with a *dimmer* and a warm filament or a warm-filtered lamp, hooded so it throws down and forward and not into faces. Tape over anything that spills.
- A red or deep-amber secondary for close work and for reading in a bunk.
- Do not bring the brightest thing you own and do not bring anything that flashes, strobes, or is a colour nobody there has a name for. Light is heat and heat is the enemy — this is stated as thermal doctrine, but by now it is also manners, and the manners are enforced harder than the doctrine.
- Learn one small courtesy on your first day: as you walk, sweep the beam across the phosphor on the stair nosings and bollards you pass. You are charging the paint for whoever comes behind you. People notice whether you do this. It costs nothing and it is the cheapest respect you will ever buy.

**Sleeping**
- A closed-cell pad, thick. Never sleep in contact with a poured floor; it will draw the heat out of you all night and you will wake ill. Never sleep on a warm new bay, either — it is a work surface, it is caustic, and it is not yours.
- Something to make a noise. This is for afterwards; see below.

**Recording**
- Grease pencils and a plastic-leaf notebook. Ink runs; graphite will not take on wet paper. Take twice the grease pencils, because they are excellent gifts.
- No camera flash, ever. If you photograph at all, ask first, and expect the answer to be a shrug that means *not while I am standing here.* Sketch instead; sketching is regarded as harmless and slightly touching.

**Water and food**
- Do not drink from the channels. It is glass-clear, it is dead cold, it is moving fast, and it looks like the best water you have ever seen; it is lime-saturated, strongly alkaline, and carries whatever the cement carries. Filters do not fix pH and do not fix dissolved metals. Drink at the mess.
- Carry your rinse water separately from your drinking water and mark them differently by feel, because you will be doing this in the dark with cold hands.

**Not to bring**
- Screens, anything with a voice, anything that talks to a satellite. There is nothing above ground governing this place and the culture reflects that.
- A cold. Do not arrive with a respiratory infection. This is a closed, cold, damp population with lungs already compromised by dust and mould, and you will do real harm. Give yourself a clean week before you go down.
- Untreated teeth, an unresolved hernia, anything that needs a hospital. The hospital is eighty vertical metres and a long ride away.
