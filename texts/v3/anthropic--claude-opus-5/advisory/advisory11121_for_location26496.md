# Preparing to Visit the Pan

## First, correct your instincts

Almost everything an ordinary person knows about visiting hot empty places will be wrong here, and wrong in the same direction: you will treat this as a desert, and it is not a desert. It is a **wet chemical plant the size of a county**, operating at low light, unroofed, unstaffed, and running continuously for ten thousand years.

Desert heuristics: find shade, ration water, watch the horizon, follow the green to the water. None of them work. There is no shade because there is nothing that casts one. There is no green. Following water gets you killed twice over — scalded and burned. The horizon does not exist for navigational purposes; it is a rumour behind a lid.

The correct mental model is a **boiler house with a bird colony in it**. Prepare the way you would to work a double shift in a saponification plant where the floor is sometimes only a crust over the vat. Then add sunburn.

Hold onto one more thing, because it changes how you behave: this place is not ruined and it is not dying. It has *arrived*. The bareness reads as aftermath and it is not aftermath. Almost nowhere you will ever stand is more biologically productive per square metre than the fly-band at your boot. Visitors who don't get this straight in advance spend three days quietly grieving for a landscape that is perfectly fine and simply doesn't want them.

## Season, and choosing your version of the place

There are effectively two locations here.

**The dry eight months.** Hard crust, mineable, walkable in the right places, industrially legible. This is the version to visit if you want to understand the works, the town, the springs, and the fish. Flies at maximum. Flamingos concentrated in the dredged channels and therefore *findable*.

**The flood weeks.** Twice a year the escarpment sends down a few centimetres over a thousand square kilometres and the pan becomes a mirror with no edges. It is the most extraordinary thing in the basin and the most dangerous. The horizon disappears completely; the causeway appears to run out into open sky; the plant stands on top of an inverted copy of itself. People walking a causeway across a perfect mirror get vertigo, nausea, and a genuine loss of balance — you will need to keep your eyes on your boots or on a fixed near object and take it slowly, and if you are prone to motion sickness, medicate in advance. Vehicles that leave the embankment in this season do not come back. The fly-band is gone; the water goes pea-green; flamingos arrive from four countries.

Pick one deliberately. Do not arrive "sometime around the rains" and hope. And note that the rain that floods the pan falls sixty kilometres away in a season the pan may miss entirely — local forecasting is nearly useless, and you should ask the plant's control room what the escarpment has been doing, because they will know and you cannot.

Either way: aim to be here **five to eight days**. Shorter and you will not have adjusted to the light. Longer and your skin begins losing the argument. The people who live here have hands that are cracked white at the knuckles and do not heal; that is what a career here costs, and you are buying a small fraction of it by the day.

## Arrival: where to put your feet down

If you have any control over your arrival point — and as a traveler you should — arrive on **made ground**: the plant apron, the rail yard, the crown of a causeway. Never the open crust. Somewhere out there is a thin skin over 82 °C liquor at pH 10.5, and there is no visual difference between that and sixty metres of solid raft. Arriving blind onto the pan is a coin-flip with a scalding-plus-caustic burn on the losing face, thirty kilometres from a hospital wing that has three of its four wards shut.

Arrive at **dawn**, around five. It is the coolest hour, the crust is at its most honest, everything is beaded with condensate, and the flamingos are coming in low over the causeways so you get the basin's best minute for free. It is also the hour when a stranger walking into the yard is least alarming, because a shift is changing.

**Your return anchor.** Do not put a physical marker anywhere on the pan. The trona grows back; the crust takes a millimetre with every flood and it will grow over, encrust, or dissolve anything you leave. Anchor to the built world and to numbers: a specific pole on a specific causeway, counted from the shore; the corner of the bagging shed; a GPS coordinate on the embankment crown. And check before you commit: the third dredger has not moved in nine years, which makes it a beautiful landmark, but the *working* two chew new channels continuously, and a spot that was crust last decade is now warm lagoon.

## Water: the single logistic that organises everything else

There is water within a hand's depth of you at all times and none of it can enter your body. Drinking water arrives by rail once a week, in a tank car, for two hundred and forty people. **You must not draw on that.** Bringing your own is not merely prudent, it is the basic courtesy of the visit; a stranger dipping into a rationed weekly tank is the one thing that will turn a friendly place cold.

Budget in litres, and budget in two separate columns, because here is the thing nobody expects:

**Your rinse water and your drinking water are the same water, and the rinse column is the bigger one.**

Every time liquor gets on you — and it will get on you at every step where the crust weeps — the correct response is copious fresh water immediately. Not wiping. Not "it's fine, it's only soapy." Alkali does not announce itself. You will spend more clean water washing your hands, forearms, and boot tops than you drink.

Plan on, per person per day:

- **6–9 litres to drink** (41–43 °C air, high humidity, no evaporative relief near the seeps — you will sweat far more than the numbers suggest and it will not dry)
- **4–6 litres for decontamination and washing**
- **1 litre reserve, sealed, for eyes** — non-negotiable, see below

Carry electrolyte powder or salt tablets. You will lose enormous quantities of salt and you will be tempted to drink very large volumes of plain water; that road ends in hyponatremia, confusion, and a stumble on a hot crust. Deliberately salt your food, which means bringing food that can take it.

Bring your own food entirely, for the same reason as the water. And bring **fresh produce as a gift** — anything green is a small marvel in a basin with not one plant in it, and a crate of vegetables, fruit, or cold drinks buys you more goodwill than money does.

## Chemistry: what the place does to a body

The pan works on you by three routes, in ascending order of seriousness.

**Skin.** pH 10.5 water feels *pleasant*. Soapy, slightly slippery, faintly sweet-smelling. Understand what that slipperiness is: it is your own skin's fats being saponified. You are being turned very slowly into soap. Alkali burns are painless at the start and go on working after you stop noticing them, which is why they end up deeper than acid burns. Rules: nothing wet touches bare skin; wash off immediately with fresh water and keep washing for minutes, not seconds; check under your waistband, cuffs, and sock tops at the end of every day, because crystals collect at the edges of fabric and sit there against damp skin all afternoon. A heavy occlusive barrier cream morning and night, on hands especially, and reapply. Do not shave here; a shaved face is an abraded face.

**Eyes.** This is the emergency that will actually happen to someone in your party. An alkaline splash in the eye is a genuine sight-losing event, it progresses, and it does not stop when you blink. You need, on your person, always: **wraparound sealed goggles** (not sunglasses, not open-sided safety specs — the dust arrives from below and sideways off a reflective floor) and **at least 500 ml of sealed sterile irrigation fluid**, with a litre in the vehicle. The protocol is irrigation for a continuous **thirty minutes minimum**, longer if you have it, and evacuate. Rehearse this before you go — actually rehearse it, with a companion, timed, so that neither of you has to think. And note that irrigation is one of the few situations where you burn your drinking water without hesitation.

**Lungs, nose, throat.** The fine alkaline dust lifts off any dry surface and does not settle; the calciner plume joins it; you are inside it the whole time. It gives newcomers nosebleeds, a raw throat, and a dry unproductive cough within a day or two. Bring **P2/N95 minimum, ideally a P3 half-mask** for anything dusty — the tailings platform, the bagging shed, the swept aprons in the afternoon drift — and accept that wearing it at 43 °C is horrible. Bring saline nasal spray and use it morning and night. Bring lozenges. Talk less than you want to.

Two lesser hazards worth a line each: the leathery cyanobacterial windrows on the shore carry **cyanotoxins** and stink of ammonia — do not handle them bare-handed, and there can be pockets of foul, oxygen-poor air right down at the mat surface in still corners. And in the dead calm before ten, an ammonia note off the drying mats can be strong enough to sting.

## The temptation you must decide about now, in advance

Somewhere along the eastern rim there is a spring outflow at exactly bath temperature, clear, gently bubbling, in the middle of the hottest, dustiest, filthiest week of your life.

**You will want to wash in it more than you have ever wanted anything.** You will reason that it is only a rinse, that it feels wonderful, that the water is soapy so surely that's *good*. Decide now, cold, at home, that you will not, and tell your companion to hold you to it. It will take the skin off you over an hour and if you put your face in it you will not see properly again.

Similarly: the water tastes faintly sweet and soapy rather than obviously foul. The body's ordinary "do not drink this" alarm is muted here. Do not sip. Not a mouthful. Not to test it.

## Feet, and the walking of the crust

Your feet are where this place does its most reliable damage.

**No leather. None.** By evening one day's exposure has the leather cracking; three days destroys a boot. Bring **unlined calf-height rubber boots**, and bring a second pair. Buy them a size generous and wear them **pull-on and un-cinched**, because the specific accident you are designing against is putting a foot through thin crust into 60–80 °C caustic liquor, at which point your entire survival depends on getting the boot off your leg in under two seconds. A tightly laced boot in that moment is a cooking vessel with your foot in it.
