# Briefing: The Basin Under the Lid
## Preparation notes for a first-time human visitor

Read this twice. The Basin kills people in three ways, and only one of them is dramatic.

---

## I. The three facts you must internalise before you pack anything

**1. You are inaudible, and inaudibility is the shape of a predator.**

This is the thing nobody expects. You will arrive worrying about being deafened, and the Basin's inhabitants will be worrying about *you* — because a human being, soft-shod and quiet-breathing, is a hole in the noise. A hush with legs. Every scraper rank within forty metres will break and scatter from you. The listeners will orient on you and stop ticking, which means they are hunting you, or trying to work out whether you are hunting them. Things will come to investigate the absence.

The single most important item in your kit is therefore not your ear defence. It is your **voice**: a mechanical rasp-box, worn at the hip, that runs continuously and makes you *legible*. Not a loud thing — loudness is cheap here, everything is loud. A *characteristic* thing. Something with a steady grain to it that reads, to a sonar-eyed animal, as an object rather than a wound.

Learn to think of it as clothing. You would not walk out of your tent naked; do not walk out silent.

**2. Nothing has ever cast a shadow here, including you.**

Even with lamps you will lose your ability to judge distance and slope within an hour of stepping onto the pan. The light through the lid is directionless smear; the ground is off-white; every feature has been rounded into the shape of a shoulder. There is no horizon, no peak, no sun-angle, no shadow to tell you whether that pale swelling is a hand-sized nodule two metres away or a five-metre bulk at fifty. People walk in circles here. People step off ledges they believed were shading in the rock. **Bring a hard, low, directional light source purely to manufacture shadows** — not to see by, but to restore depth to the world. Head-mounted diffuse lamps make it worse.

**3. The floor is made of bodies and it is hollow.**

Every death in the Basin becomes floor. You are walking on ten thousand years of accreted skeleton over voids of unknown depth, and the crust above those voids can be two metres thick or two centimetres. It booms under your boots — that boom is the only free geological survey you get. Learn its tones. Probe. Rope up on unknown pan the way you would rope up on a glacier.

## II. Medical and physiological preparation

Start six weeks out. This is not an optional programme.

**Hearing.** Have a full audiogram taken and carried with you, because you will want the comparison afterwards, and because you will be tempted to lie to yourself about the losses. Understand clearly: **you will not come back with the ears you took.** Even with perfect discipline, a week on the pan costs you permanent high-frequency loss and permanent tinnitus. Most returnees describe the tinnitus as sitting at the pitch of the scrapers, which is to say they never entirely leave.

Get impression-moulded ear plugs made — two sets, plus the standard foam as backup — and over them, active muffs with a *hush-detector* channel: a filter tuned to flag sudden local drops in the noise floor, because the drop is the warning and you will have deafened yourself past the ability to hear it. This is the paradox of the Basin. To survive the noise you must give up the sense that everything here uses to survive. You must give the sense back to a machine.

**The note.** You cannot plug out infrasound. The Basin's fundamental — sixty kilometres of hollow carbonate driven by a hundred billion animals — arrives through your chest wall, your abdomen, the fluid in your skull. First day: unexplained nausea, a pressure behind the sternum you keep attributing to anxiety. Third day: vertigo when you turn your head quickly, disturbed sleep, a persistent low-grade dread with no object. This is not psychological, or not only. Wear a **mass-loaded damping vest** and, more importantly, **sleep off the pan** (see Camp Doctrine). Chronic exposure is genuinely dangerous and cumulative in ways nobody has properly mapped. Total pan-time for a first visit: **five days, and no arguing about it.**

**Lungs.** Two hazards in the same breath. First, the dust: fine alkaline carbonate ground from bone and shell, plus pulverised grain-bodies — an irritant that provokes something like a chemical bronchitis in about a third of visitors. Second, the sour breath of the deep stone: hydrogen sulphide, seeping along the same joints that feed the rinds, pooling in every hollow and pit and gullet. Every hollow in this landscape is a bowl and every bowl can be a gas trap. H₂S deadens your ability to smell it long before it kills you, so **smell is not a detector; carry two independent electronic monitors, one on the chest and one at ankle height**, and set them to speak into your bone-conduction earpiece.

Full-face respirator with **coarse pre-filters you can change blind and by feel**, because the grain will clog anything fine within the hour.

**Eyes.** Sealed goggles, no vents. The grain falls constantly, three-millimetre hard bodies, uncountable — it is the snow of this place — and it collects in the corners of your eyes, where the drip cements it into a paste. Bring squeeze-bottle irrigation and use it on a schedule, not on symptoms.

**Skin and wounds.** The gravel underfoot is hollow bone and shed plate, and it is sharp. Any cut here is instantly inoculated with sulphur-metabolising organisms that have never met a mammal and may make a terrible, enthusiastic, novel mess of you. **Assume every abrasion is infected.** Full-coverage clothing, gauntlets, no exposed wrists or ankles. Carry more broad-spectrum antibiotic than you think you need, plus a debridement kit, and treat a scratch the way you would treat a bite.

**Immunisation and prophylaxis** are largely theatre — there is nothing here adapted to you — but tetanus-equivalents, aggressive wound protocols, and a low threshold for evacuation are not.

**Vaccinate your psyche, too.** Do a week in an industrial noise environment before you go — a stamping plant, a quarry face — with someone you don't much like, in the dark, communicating only by touch. It will not be the same, but it will teach you the thing you actually need, which is that you can continue to make decisions inside a wall of sound.

## III. Kit, with reasons

**Your voice.** Rasp-box, hip-mounted, mechanical rather than electronic if you can get it: a clockwork file-and-comb that runs six hours on a wind and can be repaired with a fingernail. Carry two. If both fail, you become a hush, and a hush is prey and predator at once. Redundancy here is not caution, it is the difference between being an object and being a hole.

**Touch language.** Speech is impossible on the pan. Not difficult — *impossible.* Shouting into a partner's ear defence achieves nothing. Agree a **tap code on the forearm and shoulder blade** before you leave, drill it until it is thoughtless, and keep it small: *stop, back, down, gas, hush, hurt, well, go, wait, I am fine, I am not fine.* Twelve signs will run an expedition. Sixty will be forgotten in the first crisis.

Rope up short — three metres — because touch is your only channel and the rope is how you find the arm.

**Ear-eyes.** A passive acoustic imager is the single most valuable instrument you can carry: it will let you see, dimly and in false colour, the world as the Basin is actually organised — the ranks, the columns, the pillars ringing at their nodes, the bulk of a mother filling a throat-hole like a cork. Use passive first and for a long time. An **active** sonar imager is a novel voice shouted into a tuned chorus; use it sparingly, briefly, and never near a roost or a rank.

**Hard hat with a wide brim and a shed cape.** The clatter live in the underlid, three hundred metres up, hanging on the accreted crust of their own leavings. When a colony feeds, it rains grain-husk, droppings, and small warm corpses. When the crust fails — and it does — a fist of cemented filth arrives from three hundred metres. The cape is so that you can shrug the accumulation off; without it, the drip will cement your outer layer into a shell over the course of a day.

**Anti-cementing discipline.** No Velcro. No exposed zips. No hinges you cannot scrub. The drip plus the grain makes a pale mortar that will glue your pack buckles, your goggle straps, your respirator seals, and your tent zip solidly shut in about eighteen hours. Every hard closure gets a brush and a wipe at every halt. Budget twenty minutes of gear-scraping per four hours of travel and treat that as sacred time.

**Probing pole.** For the floor, for hollows, for cracks. **Never put a bare hand into a crack.** Reach into a crack here and something in it is already reaching out. Density is the fundamental fact of the Basin: there is no cubic metre that is not occupied.

**Water.** You can harvest it — a canopy under the permanent drizzle fills slowly and reliably — but fog-milk collects grain, droppings, and whatever the joints are exhaling. Filter, then distil or boil, then test. Carry three days' reserve independent of harvest. Sulphide-tainted water is nauseating long before it is dangerous, and nausea plus the Basin's note plus five days of no sleep is how good travellers make bad choices.

**Food.** All of it, from home. There is no plant. There has never been a plant. Nothing in the Basin has ever been green and nothing here would know what to do with a leaf. The entire economy of the world is a slick sour biomass with the consistency of tripe, growing in seams ten centimetres tall, and it eats sulphur and iron and concentrates metals in a way that would make you very sick and possibly dead. **Do not taste anything. Do not taste anything.** People do, and it's always the same reasoning — *it's the only food in a whole world, someone has to* — and it is always a mistake.

**Waste containment, absolute.** Your food scraps and your faeces would be the richest, strangest thing that has fallen into this basin in ten thousand years. A dropped ration bar is a novel resource in a system that reached its answers and stopped. Pack out everything, including the water you wash in.

**And write your carry-out clause into the trip plan:** if you die here, your body must leave. Every death in the Basin becomes floor. Do not become floor.

## IV. Camp doctrine

**Sleep on the rim. Always.**

At the far rim the ground lifts a hundred metres, the lid thins to haze, the light strengthens to something like true dusk, the rinds fail, the scrapers thin out, the grain does not drift. The last few kilometres are almost empty — animals reach that edge and turn back, not from fear, but because there is nothing out there to hear.

That emptiness is your basecamp, and it is the reason a visit here is survivable at all. Up there the roar is a murmur, the note is a rumour, you can speak aloud, you can eat with your face uncovered, you can sleep. Take the ascent seriously as infrastructure: fix your route, cache water on it, and be able to climb it exhausted and half-deaf in whatever the Basin calls darkness.

Sorties onto the pan: **out and back, ten to fourteen hours, never a night on the open floor.** If you must overnight low, do it on the rim of a quiet-well (see below) and not on the pan itself. Lie down on the pan and you are lain upon — that is not a metaphor.

**Your clock is broken and you must build a new one.** There is no day, no night, no sun. What you have is the lid's twice-daily breath: it lowers, thickens, the drip becomes a wet grey mist, the grain moves in denser curtains, the temperature drops a degree or two — always cool, always damp, always the sensation of a cellar. Nine or ten degrees, no more. Use the breath as a tide-mark, but do not use it as a circadian anchor: it is too weak and comes twice. Instead run a hard watch schedule off your own timepieces and a **bright light panel in the tent** on a strict twenty-four-hour cycle. Visitors who let the Basin set their rhythm lose the plot in about four days.

Clothing: layers for **cool, wet, and unrelenting**, not for cold. Nothing here is going to freeze you. Everything here is going to get slowly, permanently damp, and stay damp, and grow a crust.

## V. The quiet-wells: doctrine and temptation

Scattered across the pan there are sinkholes, twenty to a hundred metres across, lined with a soft grey porous rot of the carbonate that does not return echoes. It swallows them. Standing at the lip, half your world goes missing — roar on one side, a smooth blank nothing on the other, an amputation.

Understand what these are to the natives: absolute death. Anything that stumbles in goes blind and mute in the same instant, circles, quieter and quieter, dies, and pads the lining, and the well becomes a little quieter still. The animals know them by the absence of an echo where an echo should be, and they walk the rims and never cross, and no animal in the Basin has ever learned to be curious about one.

Now understand what they are to you.

**They are the rims that make them useful.** No animal crosses; therefore the rims are the only uncrowded ground in the Basin, the only clear paths, the only place you can set a tent and not be lain upon. Route your traverses well-rim to well-rim. It is the closest thing this world has to a road, and it exists because everything else is too sensible to use it.

**They are the only place you can talk.** In a quiet-well, the roar drops away and human speech works again. This is enormously seductive and it is where people die.

Rules, and they are hard rules:

- **Never descend without rigid access.** The floor is metres of soft compacted silence — corpses, pressed down over ten thousand years. It will take your weight and then it will not. Rope, rigid ladder, and a partner at the lip.
- **Assume gas.** Metres of anaerobic decay in a windless bowl under a fog lid. Two monitors, on before you go over the edge.
- **Your radios and sounders die in there.** The lining eats them. Physical tether, physical signal, physical retrieval.
- **Never sleep in one.** Not because anything will come — nothing will come. That is the problem. You are the first thing in the history of this world capable of walking into a quiet-well and walking out, and the temptation to stay is *exactly* the temptation to stop existing in the terms the Basin understands. Visitors report the pull. Take it seriously. Twenty minutes at the bottom, then out, then up.
