# Preparing for The Hour

## First principle: arrive finished

Everything else follows from this.

On the plateau, nothing begins and nothing ends. That includes you — not immediately, but soon, and the process starts the moment you cross the Lip, quietly, at the level of tissue. Your body will not acclimatize up there, because acclimatizing is a change and changes do not accumulate. It will not build red cells. It will not heal a blister. It will not finish digesting what you ate on the way up. It will not develop a fever, and it will not fight one off.

So the doctrine is: **arrive in the condition you are willing to hold.**

Spend six to eight weeks staging at altitude in an adjacent world — the flank villages below the cloud lid will do, and they are where your guides live anyway. Come up thin, slow, and thoroughly adapted, with a resting pulse you would be content to keep. Do not come up with a cold. Do not come up with a splinter, a hangover, a cracked tooth, a stitch in your side, a grief you are in the middle of. Anything unresolved will be held open, exactly as it is, for as long as you are there, and it will not hurt, which is worse.

Dental work matters more than seems reasonable. The hum lives in the jaw and the sinuses; people with metal in their molars report it as a pressure that becomes a taste. Have loose fillings seated before you go. Bring a bite guard, and something to chew.

Empty yourself before the ascent. Whatever the plateau puts on hold, it hands back at the Lip, all at once.

## Time: bring your own, and keep it outside your body

There is no evening. The light is a hard, thin, ten-in-the-morning light and it does not shift by a hair's width. You will have no dusk, no shadow creeping, no hunger, no dimming, no tiredness that arrives on schedule. Within two days your internal clock will simply stop reporting, the way a needle stops on a dead gauge.

Therefore:

- **Two wound mechanical watches**, worn by two different people, compared at fixed intervals and written down. They keep time acceptably; the trouble is not the watches, it is you.
- **A knot cord**, and the discipline to tie one knot per sleep, on your body, where you must feel it.
- **An anchor party below the lid** with a hard extraction deadline, a rope relay or fixed line to the Lip, and instructions in writing that they are to come up and fetch you whether you agree or not. Give them written, signed permission to ignore anything you say to them on the plateau. You will say things.

The logbook is the whole game, and the reason is Rule 2. Change does not accumulate up there, and neither, in practice, does memory of change. You will read your own handwriting from two days ago describing a thing you did and you will feel, with a calm and growing certainty, that it did not happen and was never otherwise. Write everything. Date everything. Never rely on recollection for a single decision.

**The six-day rule.** Contagion runs to a known schedule: hunger goes on the third day, wounds go quiet on the fifth, and on the eighth you will find yourself standing, facing along the seams, at peace. Extractions have succeeded on day eight. None has succeeded after. The herders permit three days on the pan and four with a reason. Plan for three. Budget four. Treat five as a rescue.

## Kit

**Sun and sky.** The zenith is indigo; there is more sky above you than there is atmosphere. And the cloud lid below throws light up, so you burn under the chin, under the nose, inside the nostrils, on the underside of the earlobes, on the palate if you walk with your mouth open. Broad hat, face veil, glacier goggles with side shields, and heavy zinc reapplied by the clock, not by feeling — you will not feel it. There is no night to recover in. Your burn will not progress and it will not fade; you will carry whichever version of your face you earn in the first two days.

**Half-cold clothing.** The sun side of you cooks and the shadow side of you chills, permanently, and the boundary is a line down your body. Layer asymmetrically and be prepared to swap sides deliberately. Never make camp with your body across a seam. Frost in the shadows is at a temperature that has not varied in geological time; a hand laid flat in a seam for a minute will come out numb and stay that way for the duration of your visit.

**All your water.** There is none. The frost in the seams is not a resource — it is furniture, and if you wipe it away it is gone forever and the bare patch is your monument. Carry bottles on the sunward side of your pack. Water in shadow does not freeze slowly; it freezes and then simply is frozen, and stays so, and you will not be thawing it.

**Food that must be chewed.** Dried meat, hard cheese, sugar, resin gum. The eating is partly nutritional and mostly ritual: chewing is a change you make in yourself, on purpose, on a schedule. By the fourth day you will have to be reminded to do it by someone else. Nothing perishable — it will not spoil up there, and it will spoil the instant you descend, all of it, in an hour, which has killed people who were counting on it for the walk out.

**A thing that visibly dies.** A wind-up mechanism, a spring toy, a metronome — something that runs down where you can see it. Test it at the Lip and test it again on the pan. It is not a scientific instrument. It is a companion who is mortal, and you will need one.

**Rope, and a tug code.** Speech travels about ten metres and then stops, absorbed, like a voice into a mattress. Whistles do not carry. Shouting is genuinely useless and frightening to attempt. Rope your party at eight-metre intervals with an agreed set of pulls: *stop / look at me / answer me / I am not answering*.

**A small mirror.** For your own eyes.

**Slates or wax tablets** for silent conversation at range.

**No cairn stones, no paint, no flags, no marker tape.** Discussed below.

## The ascent, and the gradient

The cloud lid does not move. You climb through a cumulus that has held its exact shape since before the lichens, and it is the last wet thing you will touch: condensation beads on your sleeves and stays beaded, not running, not drying, until you rub it off. Coming through the lid is the moment most travelers describe as the point where they stopped being confident about what kind of place they were entering.

The flanks are a gradient, and use them. Low down, birds flap once a minute. Higher, once an hour. The flank herders live in that band and their villages are slow — a woman will begin to greet you and finish some while later. Spend a night at each stage. Learn from them how to move and, more importantly, how they behave: what they refuse to do, what they mutter, where they will not sit.

**Hire them.** Two, minimum, from a family that has done it. They know the one crossing, and knowing it is not optional.

## Movement on the pan

You will not stroll across an open plateau. The Standing are packed at the density of a crowd at a gate, and you must pass *between* them: two-metre animals, matted and warm, breathing on a decade's cycle, wool lightly turfed with blue-grey grass. They are pliant. They will move if you push them. **They will then be moved forever,** and the seam their old position cut into the rock will remain behind them, orphaned, and by the next day you will be quietly sure they were always where you left them.

Because of this the herders travel in single file, in the corridor that already exists, and touch nothing they do not have to touch. The corridor is a permanent scar in the arrangement of the world. Widening it is a moral act, not a logistical one.

The same applies underfoot. The felted insect crust yields with the faintest give and no sound, and each footprint is a permanent injury to some thousands of small living things that will neither die nor heal. Single file. One line of ruin. It is the most you can do.

**Navigation is easy and this is the trap.** Every shadow on the plateau is a fossilized fact, cut a millimetre into the stone. The seams are a compass of absolute accuracy, and you can hold a bearing by them with your eyes half shut. What you cannot do is scout, backtrack, or take a different way home — Rule 4. The plateau lends out one crossing. Attempt a second by another route and the second discovers itself to have been the first: the same cairns in the same order, the same three-legged hare in the same crevice, the same stumps against the same white lid. Plan your line before you go up, on paper, with the guides, and treat it as a one-shot arrow. If you want to see the Pan and the middle stump and the Long One, they must all be on one string.

## Vital signs, or how to tell if you are still a visitor

Assign a watch partner and swap every two hours. Their job is not your safety in the ordinary sense. Their job is these five things:

1. **Blink count.** Two per minute is the floor. Below that, you sit down and drink and are spoken to. Dust on your own cornea is the sign the plateau is beginning to file you under fauna.
2. **Pulse.** Not for rate as such — for whether you can still find it without help.
3. **Speech.** You should be talking, more or less constantly, about nothing. It dies at ten metres and no one is listening; that is the point. Silence is not a mood here, it is a current, and it pulls.
4. **Orientation.** If you are found facing along the seams, you are turned. Physically turned, by the shoulders, across them. The herders' habit is to always stand crosswise, so as to throw a wrong shadow — to remain a discrepancy in the pattern.
5. **The mirror.** Once a day, look at your own eyes. People accept a great deal about their own condition until they see it.

Sleep in pairs, wrist held in wrist, heads out of the shadow-seams, lying across the light rather than along it. Someone in camp stays awake with the knot cord. Sleep is the cheapest available form of standing still.

## Prohibitions

**Do not count anything.** Not the herd, not the Nailed, not the tally-marks on the boulder at the head of the Pan. The counts never agree, never by much, never in the same direction, and the discrepancy does not go into your notebook — it goes into you. Travelers who have counted the eleven thousand-odd scratches twice describe a specific and lasting damage: a permanent small doubt about arithmetic, and about their own hands. If you must record, photograph and count later, elsewhere, once.

**Do not build.** No cairns, no wall, no arrow of stones. Whatever you build is there for the remainder of the world, casting a shadow that will in time cut its own seam, and you will not remember building
