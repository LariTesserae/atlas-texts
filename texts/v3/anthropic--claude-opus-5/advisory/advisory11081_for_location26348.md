# Preparing for The Sheet

## First, understand what will actually try to kill you

Travelers read a description like this and prepare for a desert or a swamp. It is neither. Rank the hazards honestly before you pack, because the ranking is unintuitive:

1. **Heat that you cannot sweat your way out of.** The air and the water agree with your skin. That agreement is the danger. In bathwater heat over a continent of standing water, the air is close to saturated; your sweat has nowhere to go. Every cooling trick you know — wetting your shirt, sitting in the shallows, fanning — depends on dry air taking water off you, and there is no dry air here. Your only real levers are *shade you brought*, *reduced exertion*, and *the cool seeps*. Plan your effort budget like a diver plans gas. Two hours of walking in the mid-afternoon can put an ordinary fit adult into heat illness on a plain where there is nothing to walk toward and no one to find you.

2. **Lightning.** You will be, for most of your visit, the tallest object within a kilometre except a fan palm and a two-metre termite mound. You will be standing on an inch of warm water over a conductive iron pavement — a nearly perfect ground plane. Direct strikes are only part of it; ground current spreading across a wet crust kills at distances that would be survivable on dry soil. The good news is that the plain tells you: the cloud stack builds far off, blue-bellied, all afternoon, and the flash-to-thunder gap gives you twenty minutes' notice, and the rain wall crosses at walking speed with a visible edge. Learn to watch, count, and prepare. The bad news is that there is nowhere to go. See "storm posture" below.

3. **Your feet.** Twelve hours a day of immersion in warm, biologically dense water, over a floor that is abrasive when dry and greasy when wet, seamed with quartz veins standing a finger's width proud like buried knife-backs. Warm-water immersion injury is not the dramatic trench foot of cold campaigns; it is a slow softening, whitening, and splitting, and then the water gets in. Every small cut in a place this fertile is a colonized cut. Foot discipline is not hygiene here, it is survival.

4. **Your eyes.** Sun above, sun below, all day, plus the low-slant post-rain glare when a square mile of water lights up at once. This is snow blindness in reverse — you will burn *under* your nose and chin, inside your nostrils, on your lower lip, under your jaw, on the underside of your earlobes.

5. **Sheet fever.** The place has no horizon, only the end of the shine. Nothing marks progress. The urge to walk on — toward the palm that turns out to be six metres tall and two miles off, toward the wader you took for a grazer — has separated more travelers from their extraction point than any animal or storm. Discipline about turnaround times and markers is not optional.

Everything else — snakes, crabs, the grazers, the frog chorus — is manageable and mostly wonderful.

## Eyes, skin, and the glare from below

Bring **wraparound polarized lenses in a very dark tint, with side shields**, and bring two spare pairs. Dropping a pair into an inch of water over patterned grey-pink stone means losing it; the floor eats things. Attach them with a cord you will actually use.

Bring a **wide hat with a dark, non-reflective underside to the brim** — a pale underside will bounce the water-glare straight into your eyes. Add a neck drape.

Zinc oxide paste, not lotion, applied in the reverse pattern: **under the nose, under the chin, the septum, the lips, the underside of the jaw, the ears.** Reapply after every rain wall. Long sleeves, long legs, light woven fabric rather than tight synthetics — you want airflow and physical UV blocking, because the humid air will not let sunscreen sit and will not let sweat leave.

Expect the first day to end with a headache behind your eyes. Some of that is glare and some of it is the frogs.

## Feet: the ritual

- **Footwear:** a draining shoe with a genuinely stiff, puncture-resistant sole and a closed toe. The crust is glassy and rings underfoot in the dry weeks; the quartz veins will open a sandal-clad toe to the bone. Mesh uppers so water leaves as fast as it enters. No boots that hold water in the tongue.
- **Two pairs of socks minimum, rotated,** thin and fast-draining. Never sleep in wet socks. Never.
- **The nightly ritual, non-negotiable:** get out of the water before dark, dry feet completely, inspect every crease and between every toe with a light, powder with an antifungal, elevate for twenty minutes, sleep dry under a bug net. This is the difference between a two-week visit and a six-day evacuation.
- **Toenails cut short and square** before you depart. A stubbed nail on a quartz vein is a wound that stays wet for a fortnight.
- **Immediate coverage of any breach of skin,** with a waterproof dressing and a topical antibiotic. Carry more dressings than you think is reasonable; they will not stick to damp skin, so carry adhesive prep wipes too.
- **A walking staff.** This is your third leg on a floor you cannot see through the glare, your probe for the wagon-rut pans and the soft-topped iron crust, your snake-warning, your lightning rod to lay flat and walk away from, and — held vertical with a rag tied on — your marker.

## Water: how to drink a plain that never drains

There is water everywhere and almost none of it is what you want.

**Do not drink from the sheet** where it is shallow and warm and matted. The bacterial mats fizz oxygen at your calves all day; they also make things you do not want in your gut, and some of the cyanobacterial cushions are old enough to have gotten very good at chemistry. **Do not drink anywhere near the white rings.** Those are the plain's kidneys — salt, soda, and a faint sourness, brine so thick with invertebrates that the mud moves. Wonderful to watch. Not water.

**Do drink from the two good sources, both of which you find with your ankles:**

- **The upwellings.** Kneel and feel for a cool column against your ankle in the middle of nowhere warm. That is water that went into the crust a mile off and has spent a decade underground. It is filtered, it is cool, and it dimples the sheet from below in a way you will learn to spot from thirty paces once you know the pattern.
- **The whaleback seeps.** Water that took a week to cross a metre of rock. Cool all day, black with cyanobacteria at the lip, green with a low velvet of moss. Collect from the joint above the velvet, never by scraping the velvet.

Even so: **filter and disinfect everything.** A serious hollow-fibre filter plus UV or chemical backup. Assume the local equivalent of skin-penetrating flukes exists — take whatever prophylaxis your outfitter offers, accept that you cannot avoid immersion, and check yourself on return.

**Budget eight to twelve litres a day.** You cannot carry that for a week, which means you must be able to make water anywhere. And note: **there is no fuel.** No wood. The fan palms are the tallest things in creation here and there are three of them. The sedges are hard and wet. Dead termite mounds are somebody's house. Bring your own fuel or bring no fire at all — and honestly, bring no fire. A solar panel and a battery are the right answer in a world where the sun returns, blinding, after every quarter-hour of rain.

## Shelter, sleep, and the fact that nothing will ever dry

Nothing here is cold, ever. You do not need a sleeping bag. You need:

- **A raised sleeping platform or a serious inflatable pad** — something that keeps you off an inch of warm water and off the crust, because a footprint in the greasy top of the iron crust holds for a season and a body-shaped depression holds longer.
- **A fine-mesh bug shelter.** Not for comfort: for sleep. The midge hatch is what the dragonflies come for in numbers that darken the low sun.
- **A tarp with a reflective upper side** — your only midday shade. Carry it, set it, sit under it during the worst hours with your feet up and drying. This piece of fabric is the single most useful object in your pack.
- **A dry bag for everything that must stay dry,** and the acceptance that everything not in that bag is now permanently damp. Electronics, spare socks, medications, matches, documents: sealed, always, immediately, every time.

Where to camp: **not on the whalebacks**, tempting as the dry rock is. They are the water source, the animal crossroads, and the lichen there grows at a fingernail per century — a scuffed boot heel erases three hundred years and you will not be able to stop looking at it. If you must be on rock, use bare stone with no lichen plate on it. Otherwise camp on open iron pavement in a dry week, in a shallow place, away from palms and mounds (lightning), away from burrow chimneys, and pack out the ground you disturbed if you can.

## Storm posture

When the cloud tower goes blue-bellied and the gap between flash and thunder closes toward five seconds:

- Lay your staff down and move ten metres from it.
- Get off the whaleback. Get away from the palms. Get away from the mounds. Move to a *low* place — a wagon-rut depression — not a high one.
- Sit on your insulating pad, not on the crust, feet together, knees up, hands off the ground, head down. Feet together matters: the killer here is potential difference between one foot and the other across a wet conductive floor.
- Wait. It lasts a quarter hour. It is warm as spit. Then the sun comes back at a low slant and everything in the world lights up at once, and you will not be able to see, so keep your eyes on your feet and navigate by the ripples until the sun climbs.

## Ears: the loudest quiet place you will ever visit

Bring **proper earplugs, 30 dB or better, and over-ear muffs to stack on top of them for sleeping.**

The insect layer your brain will file as silence within ten minutes. The frogs are the event. There is a knocking one, a comb-dragged-by-a-thumb one, and there is the small pale frog whose pure single tone, multiplied ten thousand times across a flooded flat, becomes coherent — a pressure behind the eyes, a thing you feel in the sinuses rather than hear. It runs six hours and it can be heard from a mile off. There is no walking away from it. Plan two nights of bad sleep while you adapt, and plan your sleep window with the chorus in mind rather than against it.

One warning about hearing protection: **the field of waders is your alarm system.** When something disturbs one end of it, the alarm crosses the plain as a visible wave of sound, faster than the rain wall. That wave is how you learn about the grazers coming, the snake in the shallows, the storm shifting. Learn to read it before you plug your ears, and unplug when you travel.
