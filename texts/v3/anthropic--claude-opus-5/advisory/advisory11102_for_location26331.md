# Preparing to Visit The Level

## A field manual for an ordinary human

---

## First, understand what kind of trip this is

This is not an expedition into wilderness. It is an expedition into an *interior* — a continent-sized occupied machine — and the mistake almost everyone makes is preparing for the wrong hazard. They fixate on the roar, because that is what the reports emphasise, and they arrive with excellent hearing protection and die of something else.

Rank your dangers honestly, in this order:

1. **Heat.** You cannot cool yourself. This is the killer.
2. **Your feet and skin.** Permanent immersion in warm, mineral-rich, biologically busy water. Nothing heals. Everything macerates.
3. **The Comb.** Twice daily, on schedule, and it will drown you or break you against a pier if you are in the wrong geometry.
4. **Getting lost.** A flat plain with no landmarks, no soil, no cairn that survives, and a city that rebuilds itself.
5. **Collapse.** Something falls in earshot every twenty minutes. You will not hear it.
6. **Hearing.** It will not kill you. It will take something from you permanently if you are careless for even an hour.

Plan around the top of that list and the rest becomes manageable. Plan around the bottom and you become one of the things the mat feeds on.

---

## The heat problem, which is the whole problem

The water is blood-warm. The air is saturated. The sky is bright overcast — you get radiant load without a sun to shelter from.

That combination means your sweat does not evaporate. It runs. You will lose water at extraordinary rates and get no cooling in exchange. Wading knee-deep is roughly three times the metabolic cost of walking, and every watt you generate stays in you.

**Consequences you must accept before you go:**

- **This is a short trip.** Days, not weeks. Anyone who tells you they lived on the Level for a season either had non-human physiology, active refrigeration, or is lying.
- **You will move slowly and rest constantly.** Set a hard metabolic ceiling and obey it. Twenty minutes wading, ten minutes sitting. Not "when I feel hot" — you will not feel it correctly, because the air is the same temperature as you.
- **Swallow a core-temperature telemetry pill.** Wear the reader on your wrist where you can glance at it. Treat 38.5 °C as a stop-and-sit order, no discussion, no heroics.
- **Sit *down* in the water to rest.** The layer against the pavement is a shade cooler. It is not much. It is what you have. Lie back in a quarry basin and let thigh-deep water take your trunk; that half-degree gradient is the only cooling system on the planet that works for you.
- **Bring chilled water in vacuum flasks and ration it as a *thermal* resource, not just hydration.** Cold fluid into the gut is real cooling. It is also the single greatest morale item you will carry.
- **Do not bother with an evaporative cooling vest.** It will not evaporate. Phase-change ice vests work until the ice is gone, and you cannot make more. Budget them as consumables tied to specific hard tasks.

Salt: you will lose enormous amounts. Bring electrolyte far beyond what feels sensible, in a form you can take in warm water without gagging.

## Sound, and the discipline of silence

Assume a continuous 95–110 dB ambient with structure — not white noise, chords, which is worse for cognition. Assume the Comb peaks far above that, with a pressure component.

**Protection:** Custom-moulded plugs *plus* over-ear muffs, worn from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave. Sleep in them. The most common permanent injury is the traveller who removed protection for "just a minute" to check something.

**Infrasound cannot be blocked.** The pumps in the sub-floors will be in your sternum and your teeth regardless. Expect nausea, chest flutter, a faint visual tremor, poor sleep, and a low free-floating dread that is *not* psychological insight, it is a physical response to a frequency. Name it out loud (in sign) so it does not colonise your judgement.

**You cannot speak. Accept this fully in advance.**

- Learn a working sign vocabulary *before* you go — a hundred signs, drilled to reflex, including all of your safety calls: *stop, tide, collapse, hurt, water, cold, lost, follow, wait, go now.*
- Practise them in gloves, in glare, half-submerged, and in the dark.
- Add a touch code for zero visibility: taps on the shoulder, squeezes on a shared tether. One squeeze *stop*, two *forward*, three *now, immediately, no questions*.
- Bone-conduction radios work and are worth their weight, but they fail, and the failure will happen at the worst moment. Sign is primary. Radio is luxury.

**The eleven seconds.** Before each Comb, every animal in the district goes silent. This is your best warning system and it is more reliable than any watch. But you are wearing double hearing protection and may not register the drop. Train yourself to notice the *animals*, not the sound: the hobbler carpet stops moving. Tallwaders lift their heads in unison. The filter-fans fold in a visible wave running away from you. Learn that wave. It is the single most useful piece of natural history on this world.

**A rule about light, which is also a rule about manners:** never show green. The flyers speak in green flank-pulses; a green torch, laser, or chemlight is not illumination here, it is shouting gibberish across an entire district and it will disrupt something you do not understand. Team signals in red and amber only. Filter your lamps at home; do not trust yourself to remember.

## Feet, skin, and the sacred dry hour

You will never be dry. Your skin will never dry. Within three days a faint film of mat will be growing on your legs, and the hobblers will come to graze it, and their little chisels will find every micro-abrasion you have.

**Kit:**
- Fully sealed wading legwear with hard integral boots, rated for abrasion, in *two* complete sets. Not waders you have to trust — waders you can inspect at every rest.
- Inside them: synthetic liner socks, at least one dry pair per day, sealed individually.
- A blunt plastic scraper. You will use it every evening to strip film off your legs, arms, and neck. This becomes a ritual. Do not skip it.
- Antiseptic, in quantity. Cover *every* break in the skin the moment it happens. There is no such thing as a minor cut on the Level; there is only an infection that hasn't declared itself.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics, and the training to know when to start them. Assume any wound will go bad and go bad fast in blood-warm water full of chemolithotrophs.

**The dry hour.** Carry a small sealed chamber — a rigid dry-bag or inflatable pod — and a generous mass of desiccant. Once a day, get your feet and hands into it and dry them completely. It is the most important hour of your day, it is more valuable than food, and it is the only reason your feet will still be structurally sound when you leave. Travellers who skip it come home walking differently.

**Wear a nose-and-mouth respirator with particulate and acid-gas cartridges,** especially in the hour after the Comb when the mist hangs at chest height. That mist is aerosolised mat and mineral leachate and reduced gases from the pumped floors. It is not acutely dangerous. It is quietly, cumulatively bad. Sealed goggles too, or you will spend the trip with your eyes weeping in sympathy with the stone.

## Water and food

Bring your food. All of it. There is nothing here you can eat, and the mat concentrates whatever the city is dissolving out of itself — metals, sulfides, things with no name in your pharmacopoeia. The hobblers are made of the mat. The waders are made of the hobblers. The entire food web is downstream of *dissolved building*, and you are not adapted to any part of it. Eat nothing. Not as an experiment, not as a courtesy, not when supplies run low.

Water you must make locally — you need ten to twelve litres a day and you cannot carry that. Bring a pumped reverse-osmosis unit with a coarse mineral pre-filter and a sacrificial sulfide stage, plus a manual backup, plus more filter cartridges than you think, because the load will foul them at rates the manufacturer never imagined. There is no fuel and nothing dry; you cannot boil. Chemical treatment alone will not touch the mineral load.

Then chill what you can. Warm water goes down badly when you are already too warm, and dehydration on the Level usually begins as simple revulsion.

## Carrying, and the trick that makes it possible

Do not backpack. In knee-deep water, tow.

Build or bring a shallow-draft float-sledge: a broad, low, buoyant tray, hard-shelled, with a hip harness and a quick-release. It carries three times what your back will and costs you almost nothing in heat. This single decision is the difference between a viable trip and a heat casualty.

Design constraints the Level imposes:
- **Everything must survive being rolled and submerged.** The Comb will take your sledge sideways at some point. Positive buoyancy, everything lashed, nothing loose.
- **Quick-release at the hip, tested wet, tested one-handed, tested in gloves.** A tethered sledge in a half-metre bore will pull you under a pier.
- **A second short tether from sledge to a person, so a swamped sledge can be recovered** — but never to the same person who is harnessed. Two humans, one load.

## Navigation: the Comb is your compass and your clock

There are no landmarks that mean anything. The plain is machined flat; the horizon is *convex*, bowed upward, and long sightlines loom and shimmer so that towers appear in the wrong order at the wrong sizes. **You cannot estimate distance here. Do not try. Do not trust your eyes at ranges beyond a few hundred metres, ever.**

What you can trust:

- **The tide axis.** The Comb's forty parallel rollers run dead straight and in step. That is a global azimuth reference accurate to a degree or so, refreshed twice a day, visible from anywhere. Learn to read it instantly. It is better than any compass you could bring, because you do not know what the local magnetic field is doing and the answer is probably "something the pumps are causing."
- **The courses.** The black glassy base fabric runs forty kilometres without a joint. Those lines are your roads and your rails. Follow a course; count courses crossed.
- **Flow direction** in the arcades, which is often *against* the flood — a second, independent bearing.
- **Inertial navigation plus a base beacon at your insertion point.** Bring both. Bring spares of both. Log everything.

**Do not attempt to mark your route.** The maintenance repairs the city back to its correct worn state; your paint, your cairn, your scratched arrow will be dissolved, re-laminated, or restored to precisely the degree of erosion the city considers proper. Passive markers are removed as debris or absorbed as building material. Navigation here is dead reckoning and instruments, full stop.

**Your insertion point is the most valuable object in the world.** Beacon it redundantly, with independent power. Then establish an absolute range limit — a distance from which you can return with a broken instrument, a broken partner, and half your water — and hold to it with religious rigidity. Everyone who dies on the Level dies further out than they intended to go.

## The Comb: geometry, not courage

You will get tide tables from whoever sends you. Memorise them. Also learn the animal cues, because the tables will drift and the animals will not.

**Where to be:** on the *upstream* face of something massive and rooted — a fluted pier, a ziggurat flank — braced, low, tethered, sledge secured, both hands committed. Better still, on one of the four-step stairways, up on the little landing, out of the surge.

**Where never to be:**
- In a colonnade throat, where the bore accelerates and gets combed.
- In a sluice hall, ever, at any tide state. The hinged louvres are hectares of moving stone and they do not care.
- Between two piers where the rollers converge.
- Downstream of anything you cannot see over.
- In a lower floor. See below.

Half a metre of water moving faster than you can run will take your legs out and put you against masonry. Do not stand up in it to see. Wait out the hour of chest-high mist. Then move.

---

## The lower floors

Do not go down.

They are lightless, pumped, at unknown pressure, full of reduced gas and blind swimmers that navigate by the shape of the silence. The floors above them give way regularly — that brown upwelling column is what a collapse looks like from the top. Anyone who goes into the sponge with human lungs and a torch is making an offering.

Corollary: **watch what you step on.** Test dished thresholds. Avoid the scalloped bases of piers that look further along than their neighbours. If a stretch of pavement drums differently under your staff — carry a staff, always, both for probing and for balance in the flow — go around. You cannot hear the crack that precedes a failure. You must see it.
