# Preparing to Visit the Addition

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are not going to see a city. Meniscus is incidental. You are going to stand inside a promise that has been kept, without deviation, for forty years, by people who know they will die before it pays out. Everything difficult about the visit follows from that.

The second thing: you will arrive rich. Not in money — in **wear**. Your boots are scuffed, your hands are lined, your bag has a shine on one corner from a decade of the same shoulder. You come from a world that hands out age for free, and you are going to a plain where age is rationed by committee, out of an intentionally relaxed maintenance budget, a fraction at a time, so that the ground can learn to be old without being dropped into it. Nearly every offence a traveler commits on the Addition is a form of queue-jumping: spending age the plain has not yet earned.

Prepare accordingly, and prepare to be watched with great courtesy while you fail to.

## Your body, before you go

**See a dentist. Genuinely.** The underwriting sits at the absolute floor of hearing and you will locate it, wrongly, in your own teeth. Loose amalgam, a cracked crown, a poorly seated implant — these become instruments. Twice a month, for ninety seconds, the pitch changes, and travelers with bad dentistry describe it as having a tuning fork struck in the roof of the mouth. Get it all checked and re-seated. If you have bone-anchored hearing aids, cochlear hardware, or plates in the skull, write ahead to the wardens; they keep a list and they have quiet rooms.

**Train your sleep off the sun.** There is no dawn, no dusk, no dark. Midnight is the blue-grey of a photograph of a room. Two weeks out, start sleeping to a fixed clock in a blacked-out room with no light cues, and learn to fall asleep with a mask on. Bring three masks. The good one, the spare, and the one you will lose. Melatonin, if your body tolerates it, is worth more here than antibiotics.

**Get fit for humidity, not for heat.** Ninety percent, warm, no wind, ever. Your sweat will not evaporate. You will do less work than you think and feel like you have done more. Toenails, ear canals, groin, the webs of your fingers: treat them now, bring powder, bring an antifungal you trust, and accept that you will use it.

**Two weeks of vestibular practice would not be wasted.** The plain rises over forty kilometers so gradually that your eyes call it flat and your inner ear does not. Add a shell that lets no light come from a direction, and a shadow that lags a third of a second behind your heels. Newcomers get a low, chronic, seasick unease for the first five to nine days. It passes completely. Bring the antiemetic anyway, and bring it in a form you can take without water at 3 a.m.

## Quarantine, and what you must not bring

The Addition's biosecurity is not bureaucratic squeamishness. Almost nothing animal has taken. There are ants, one spider, a beetle, three hundred herons and change. A single pregnant rat coming ashore in a duffel would be a civilizational event, and everyone there knows it.

You will be stripped, scrubbed, and your kit will be handled by someone with more patience than you have. Save them the trouble:

- **Nothing alive.** No sourdough starter, no kefir, no cheese, no seeds, no cuttings, no pets, no "just a houseplant." No live-culture supplements.
- **No dirt.** Scrub your boot treads with a brush and a pick until they are boring. Do this before you pack, and then again at the harbor, and expect them to do it a third time.
- **No leather you love.** It will rot, and it is organic, and it invites questions.
- **No feathers.** Down jackets are a nightmare at the checkpoint and useless in the climate. Nothing insulating; everything wicking.

Bring paper if you must, but bring it knowing paper is a wet-country liability here and that mold is patient.

## The pack

**Soft soles.** This is the single most important item. Crepe, gum rubber, felt — something that abrades itself rather than the ground. Hard heels chip arrises. A chipped arris is fixed by the cats overnight, so no harm is done, and everyone will still know, and it will be mentioned to you kindly, once, and never again, and you will think about it for a year.

**Gloves and shin protection.** Every terrace still has its arris: the sharp upper lip where riser meets tread, restored nightly by machines the size of cats. It is not metaphorically sharp. It will open your palm. Everyone does this once. My advice — and locals endorse it, in the way people endorse a joke they have stopped finding funny — is to do it deliberately, on the first terrace, in company, with a dressing already in your pocket. Then it is done, and you have paid, and you can stop being nervous of the ground.

**A closed-cell sleeping mat, and never sleep on bare grain.** The substrate wicks. Standing water is not permitted on the plates and you are, from the plain's point of view, standing water. People have woken from an unplanned nap on a terrace with cracked lips, a headache like a hangover, and a warden squatting nearby waiting for them to be sensible. Also: anything left on the ground overnight is collected. Twice this has included a person. They were fine. You would probably be fine.

**A dry box.** Silica, gaskets, the works. For your camera, your journal, your documents, your electronics. Lens fungus is real and fast.

**Your journal on your body at all times, in pencil.** The recovery cradles do not steal; they clean. There is a famous case of a naturalist's forty-day notebook returned in the morning, immaculate, faintly warm, and blank. Pencil on rag paper survives better than ink. Duplicating your notes each night is not paranoia, it is the local standard of care.

**A wound mechanical watch.** No sun, no shadow, no way to tell time by looking up. And learn to use the local clock: it rains once a day, hard, vertical, windless, for eleven minutes, at an hour that walks forward four minutes daily and so laps the whole clock once a year. Residents do not ask what month it is. They ask **where the rain is**. When you can answer that question by feel, you have stopped being a tourist.

**A hard-shadow lamp — and discretion about it.** A tight directional beam is used in workshops and tunnels and is unremarkable there. Used casually, in company, in the open, it is not. To throw a sharp black shadow of a person onto a wall in a place where nobody's shadow has ever arrived on time is somewhere between showing someone a photograph of their own funeral and undressing in a restaurant. Children in particular should be shown their true shadow only with a parent's permission, and the wardens ask that you ask. Some parents say yes. It is a large moment. Do not be casual with it.

**Sealed headphones.** The grain eats bass. Not damps — eats. Thunder is felt in the sternum and never heard. A dropped tool sounds like a dropped pencil. Your own footsteps are dry clicks. After ten days the absence becomes a hollow you feel behind your ribs, and a pair of good closed cans with something low and warm in them is the most effective medicine for it. Expect to be asked, shyly, to lend them. Say yes. Lend them for an hour, not a minute.

**A treble instrument if you play.** Flute, whistle, music box, anything glass or bright. Drums are pointless here. A bass guitar on the Addition is an absurdist object.

**Sunglasses — for leaving.** See below.

## Manners

The people of Meniscus are patient in a specific way that is hard to be around at first. It is not serenity. It is the posture of ninety thousand people keeping a building of four million clean for nobody. They are slow with strangers and they finish their sentences. Match their pace or be the loudest thing in the room forever.

**Speak quietly.** Voices carry oddly well and sound as if spoken beside the ear regardless of distance. A normal conversational volume across a plaza is a shout in a library. Travelers are identifiable at three hundred meters by volume alone. There is also no such thing as a private conversation outdoors; assume you are audible and assume that everyone has always assumed that.

**Do not put anything in the channels.** Not a leaf, not a coin, not a hand, not a boat, not a fish, not a stick to see which way it goes. The channels were cut straight and in the last decade they have begun to want to bend, and eleven of them are being allowed to think without interference. This is the closest thing the Addition has to a sacrament and it is enforced without humor.

**At the bench by the eleventh channel: sit, look, and shut up.** One millimeter of cut on the left bank, ten years in the making, and it means the water has found a way to keep a decision. Do not bring calipers. Do not bring a laser. Do not say *I could deepen that in an afternoon.* You could. Everyone there could. They have the extruders, the underwriters, the whole apparatus that holds a continent in the air. Restraint is not their limitation; it is their entire civic religion, and offering to shortcut it is the one thing that will get you removed from the plain. Travelers who have said it describe the reaction as no reaction at all — a polite pause, and then no more invitations, ever.

**Learn the heron count before you speak to anyone about herons.** The current number, exactly, never rounded. Everyone in Meniscus knows it and will tell you unprompted. Getting it wrong is not a crime; getting it approximately right is worse. Do not feed them. If you meet a file of them in a service gallery, stand aside against the wall and let them pass. They will pass very close and they will not care about you.

**If the hum changes, sit down.** Immediately, wherever you are, mid-sentence, mid-stair. Ninety seconds. Water in a glass forms six standing rings. Fog, if there is fog, stops moving. Then it ends and everyone stands up and resumes the word they were on. Do not photograph faces during an adjustment.

**"Your shadow is not late. You are early."** You will hear this said to you. It is a rule from the children's list, and used to an adult it is a rebuke, delivered with enormous gentleness. It means: you are hurrying, and hurrying is the local vice, and you have been forgiven in advance.

## Things not to say

- *Why don't more people live here?*
- *What a waste.*
- *Couldn't you just take the shell off?*
- *Doesn't the emptiness get to you?*
- Anything at all about how much this must have cost.

And do not describe your own world's weather in the boastful register. Wind is the pornography of the Addition. There is none, ever, and there hasn't been for forty years. If you bring a small battery fan — and it is a kind thing to bring — offer it privately, to one person, and let them decide whether they want it on their face. Some people cry. Some hate it. Nobody is neutral. **Do not bring a kite.** It is a cruelty dressed as a gift.
