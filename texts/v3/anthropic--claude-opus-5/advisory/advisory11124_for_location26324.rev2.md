# Notes for a Visitor to the Flats Above the Throats

## 0. What the trip costs before you decide anything else

Every other destination asks you for money, time, and risk. This one asks for those and then asks for something you cannot replace.

You will come back with less hearing than you left with. Not "might." Will. Double protection, worn perfectly, all day, every day, buys you a reasonable trip; it does not buy you a free one. The infrasound — the long *woom* under everything — passes through foam and muffs and skull alike, because it isn't really being heard, it's being *absorbed*. There is no plug for your ribcage.

So decide, before you pack, what you are willing to spend. Write the number down. "Two weeks" is a few decibels at 4 kHz and some tinnitus you'll notice at night for the rest of your life. "Three months" — which is what it takes to have a decent chance at a Silence — is a real, audiogram-visible, permanent loss, the kind that eats consonants in restaurants when you're sixty.

People do it anyway. Just do it knowingly. The locals do.

## 1. Medical clearance: the unusual screening

Standard high-altitude fitness, plus four things nobody screens for elsewhere.

**Teeth.** This is not a joke. At the lip of a great throat your fillings vibrate against each other. Loose crowns, cracked molars, recent implants, unhealed extractions — all of these turn into a very bad week. Get a full dental check and any pending work finished at least a month out. Nothing temporary in your mouth.

**Vestibular and ocular.** Low-frequency pressure in the 10–30 Hz range resonates the eyeball; vision blurs and the horizon smears. It also makes a substantial fraction of people profoundly nauseated. If you can find a low-frequency test chamber, sit in one for twenty minutes first. Better to vomit in a lab.

**Photosensitivity.** The light changes four times a minute. Shadow-blotches the size of towns race across the pavement all day. If you have photosensitive epilepsy or a migraine disorder triggered by flicker, this plateau is a machine designed to hurt you.

**Ears, baseline.** Get a full audiogram, including high frequencies and otoacoustic emissions, within two weeks of departure. You need the "before." You will want to know exactly what you spent.

**Disqualifying:** hyperacusis, severe tinnitus, recent barotrauma, unstable cardiac disease, uncontrolled reflux (you will sleep with a plugged head and a pounding chest), and — an odd one — any psychiatric condition that decompensates under sleep fragmentation. You will not sleep well. Nobody does, for the first month.

## 2. Altitude: arrive already finished

Four kilometers is not extreme, but there is no "down" available. The plateau *is* the ceiling, and the only descent is a four-thousand-meter glass shaft you are forbidden to enter. There is no walking a sick companion to lower ground. Treat the whole plateau as a single fixed altitude with no evacuation, and prepare accordingly.

Do your acclimatization in your own world: three weeks of staged sleeping altitude, or six weeks in a hypoxic tent, and cross already adapted. Carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, and — the one that matters — a portable hyperbaric bag, because that bag is the only "descent" that exists here.

**The garden hypoxia trap.** Nobody warns you about this. The rising air is saturated and warm. Warm saturated air at 620 millibars has meaningfully less oxygen per breath than the cold dry air out on the flats — water vapor takes up room. It may also carry CO₂ from a hundred kilometers of breathing canyon. So the lush green place, the place with water and food and shelter, is the *worse* place to sleep. Climbers coming down from a hard day naturally camp in the gardens and then wonder why they wake at 3 a.m. with Cheyne-Stokes breathing and a headache that goes on for days.

Sleep out on the pavement, thirty or forty minutes' walk from the ring. Carry a gas meter. Check it at the lip, in the ring, and at your camp, and check it again after a stall — when a throat chokes and its neighbors surge, the composition of what's coming up changes.

## 3. Hearing protection: the real gear problem

Assume 150+ dB at the lip of a major throat, 130 at sixty meters, 100–105 at two kilometers, and a floor of roughly 85–90 dB *everywhere on the plateau, permanently, including inside your tent, including while you sleep.*

That last number is the one that gets people. The lips are obviously dangerous and everyone respects them. The ambient floor is not obviously dangerous, and it runs twenty-four hours a day for however long you stay, and it is what actually takes your hearing.

**Bring:**

- Custom-molded deep-insert plugs, two sets, taken from impressions made months earlier. Foam is a backup, not a primary — you will be wearing these for eight to twelve weeks and foam macerates the canal in this humidity.
- High-attenuation muffs, the electronic kind is pointless here; get the heaviest passive pair made. Two pairs. The headband tension is the whole ballgame.
- A hard helmet with integrated muff mounts for lip approaches, and a chinstrap that will not come loose in a rotor.
- **A dosimeter.** Wear it. This is the single most important instrument you will carry, more important than the compass, because within thirty-six hours your subjective judgment of loudness will be completely destroyed by adaptation. You will stand somewhere that is quietly removing your inner ear and think *this is fine, this is the calm part.* Only the number knows. Set an alarm at your daily dose and obey it like a decompression ceiling.
- Otic care: alcohol-and-vinegar drops, a drying regimen every evening, a spare week of antibiotic-steroid drops. Otitis externa in a plugged, warm, wet ear is the most common trip-ending injury here after falls.

**Protocol for the lips.** Double protection at all times. Helmet. Full-face goggles. A hard cap on lip time: no more than a few minutes inside sixty meters of a major throat per day, and treat the final twenty meters as a place you visit twice in your life, briefly, and then talk about forever. There are travelers who have stood at the very edge of a stadium-sized throat with the column coming out of it. Some of them can still hear.

## 4. Speech: you will not use it, so replace it first

You have to solve this at home, months in advance, with your actual traveling companions.

Learn a real signed language — properly, to conversational fluency, with the same people you'll be traveling with. A partially-learned pidgin collapses exactly when you need it: in bad light, in rain, at forty meters, in an emergency. And practice it *in the conditions*: outside, at distance, through binoculars, with gloved hands, in failing horizontal light, with both of you in helmets and goggles. Make your signs large. The plateau's own language is made large for exactly this reason, so you will be in good company.

On top of that, agree a hard-coded emergency set — perhaps eight signs — that mean things like *stop*, *come*, *lip*, *stall*, *hurt*, *water*, *go back*, *Silence* — and drill them until they are reflexes.

Bone-conduction radio works, technically, and you should carry it as a safety net. But it is a trap. It lets you postpone the real adaptation, and the real adaptation is the whole point. The people here stopped speaking because speech was useless. Two weeks in, you will understand that in your body rather than your head, and you will find that you have stopped narrating yourself, and that something has gone quiet inside you that the roar has nothing to do with.

Also: get used to the idea that you cannot hear danger. No footsteps, no rockfall, no shout, no scream. Build the habit now of *looking* at your partner every thirty seconds. Rope-of-eyes. It feels neurotic for the first week and then it's just how you walk.

## 5. Feet, and the floor

The pavement is polished stone veined with quartz. Dry, it's grippy enough. Wet — twenty times a year, and every dawn near a garden — it is a skating rink tilted on kilometer-wide swells, sometimes with a hole in it.

Soft sticky rubber, smooth-soled or minimally lugged. No hard edges, no crampon welts, nothing that scratches: you are walking on the oldest stable surface anyone knows, and every mark you make will outlive your species. Bring three pairs of soles' worth of resole rubber and the glue to do it, because sponge-mat and wet quartz together will polish your soles to glass in a month.

Inside the gardens, expect to be wet to mid-calf permanently. Bring genuine vapor-barrier socks and an absolutely rigid dry-camp discipline: one set of dry sleeping socks that never, ever get used for anything else. Macerated feet at altitude do not heal.

## 6. Anchors, shelter, and the problem of nothing having an edge

This is the quiet logistical nightmare. There are no cracks. There are no edges. Nothing here takes a piton, a cam, a nut, or a hook, and drilling the pavement would be — correctly — read as obscene.

So:

- **Ballast, not stakes.** Bring heavy-duty fabric ballast bags with sewn-in loops, eight of them, and fill them with dust and loose rubble from the base of a knuckle. Your tent lives on ballast.
- **A tent that survives rotors.** Not "wind" — rotors. The air near a cloud column is doing something chaotic and vertical. A geodesic four-season shelter, low, with every guy in use, and a second pole set.
- **For any lip work:** a three-point ballast anchor (three big bags, spread, equalized) or a live anchor around a knuckle. Never trust a single point. Never approach a lip unroped, no matter how far back you think you are, no matter how many times you've done it.

And when you break camp: **any stone you moved, you set upright.** Not back where it was. Upright. This is the first rule you will be taught and the only one that costs you nothing, and doing it properly is the fastest way to be regarded as something other than weather.

## 7. The lip protocol, and why the Silence can kill you

Here is the mechanism that kills visitors, and it is almost elegant in its unfairness.

You approach a throat. There is a river of warm air coming out of it at flood speed. It pushes you *back*. This feels reassuring. You learn, without deciding to, to lean into it a little, to brace against it, to let it hold you.

Then the column stalls.

It takes about ninety seconds to draw down, and if you are leaning on it, you have ninety seconds — generous, actually — to notice that your support is being withdrawn. People who have learned the lean in their *bodies* rather than their heads do not always notice.

**Rules, absolute:**

1. Never let the updraft take any of your weight. Ever. Test this consciously every single time: shift back, feel that you are standing on your own.
2. Never be inside a hundred meters of a major lip unroped to a real anchor.
3. **The draw-down is an evacuation signal.** When the registers start descending — and you will learn to feel this in your sternum long before you understand it — you move away from the lip immediately. Not after one more photograph.
4. During and after a stall, the neighbors surge. A throat two kilometers away can double, and its garden's flutewood goes into a register that genuinely disables people — a head-splitting shriek that arrives with no warning. If you are in a garden ring when this happens, get out of the ring, sit down, and wait.
5. If you fall in, nobody is coming. Nobody descends. There is no rope on this plateau four thousand meters long and there is no one to hold it.

## 8. Water, food, and not stealing from a garden

**Water.** Beard-filament gives about half a cup per squeezed handful, warm and mineral. Squeeze into a collapsible bladder, then filter and UV or boil. Assume the updraft is carrying a whole foreign biosphere's worth of microbes up from the canyon and that the gardens are, in effect, permanent aerosol plumes from a world you have never been immunized against. Treat everything.

Out on the flats there is no water. None. The dew-fins yield a hand's width of stain and the cushions are off-limits — a three-meter dome is nine thousand years old, it is the single oldest living thing you will ever touch, and cracking it for a swallow of water is a crime with no name. The dew-combs belong to people. Plan your legs garden-to-garden with full carry and a hard margin, and take the margin seriously, because a dust devil crossing your route does not care about your schedule.

**Food.** Bring all of it. The local diet is mat-shoots, throat-swift eggs, and grubs from flutewood joints, and every item on that list is a bad idea: unknown biochemistry, unknown toxins, and — more to the point — it belongs to forty people living at the absolute edge of what a landscape will support. Eating a garden's mat-shoots is not sampling the cuisine, it is taking food out of a very small number of mouths.

Bring dense, high-calorie, no-cook food. At altitude with constant noise stress you will burn four to five thousand calories a day and your appetite will be suppressed. Weigh yourself weekly. Eat on a schedule, not on hunger.

**Fire.** Difficult and culturally loaded. Everything near a garden is damp and everything on the flats is stone. Bring a pressurized liquid-fuel stove rated for altitude, and fuel for the whole trip, and do not burn flutewood, which is a living instrument in an instrument that people listen to.

## 9. The quiet alarm

You are told: *do not sleep during the Silence.*

The Silence comes five or six times a year, at no predictable interval, lasting four minutes to an hour, and it can happen at three in the morning. The locals wake because ninety seconds of pressure withdrawing from their bones is the loudest thing that ever happens to them. You, foam-plugged and exhausted and hypoxic, may not.

So build, before you leave, an inverted alarm: a microphone and a small processor that watches the broadband noise floor and fires a hard vibrating alert to a wrist unit when the level drops more than about fifteen decibels over thirty seconds. Test it. Carry two. Sleep with it strapped to your wrist every night of the trip. Spare batteries for the whole expedition.

This is the single most useful thing you will bring, and it is not sold anywhere. You have to make it.

---

## 10. The Silence itself: what to do when it comes

You will have roughly ninety seconds of warning as the roar goes down the stairs.

- **Get away from any lip.** First. Before anything.
- **Stand up.** Both because you have been told not to sleep through it and because the loaf, which never raises its head, raises its head, and it seems only right.
- **Take out your earplugs.** This is the only time on the whole plateau you can safely hear, and it is the reason to have come.
- **Do not speak.** Your voice will work. There will be nothing to stop it. Almost every visitor discovers, in the first Silence, an overwhelming impulse to say something out loud, just to prove they still can. Don't. The people around you will have spent their lives without that option, and using it in front of them is a kind of gloating you will be ashamed of for years.
- **Spore load.** The cushions split and release across the entire plateau, and there is no wind, so it hangs. You are standing in a stationary aerosol of alien spores for up to an hour. An N95 handles this. Wearing it means breathing through paper during the strangest twenty minutes of your life. I'd wear it. Some people don't. Decide beforehand, not in the moment.
- **Recording.** If you brought instruments, have them running *permanently* on a rolling buffer, because you will not have time to set anything up, and because you will not want to spend the Silence operating a machine.
- **Expect to be emotionally flattened.** Weeks of continuous 90 dB, then nothing, plus hypoxia, plus thousands of swifts coming down out of the sky to settle on the stone around you without a sound. Grown, experienced, unsentimental people sit down and weep. This is normal. It's not a symptom.

When the first low note returns, the swifts go up. Put your plugs back in *before* the surge, not after.

## 11. The people

Forty. Maybe sixty. No metal, no speech, no writing except stone.

**Quarantine yourself.** This is not optional and it is the most important paragraph here. A population that size has no immunological depth whatsoever. A common cold could remove a tenth of the entire culture. Fourteen days of strict isolation with symptom monitoring before you cross, full vaccination status, and if you develop *anything* — sore throat, a cough, a fever, a rash — you stay away from people until it resolves. Bring the tests. Use them weekly. This is the price of admission, and if you are not willing to pay it, don't go.

**Sterilize your gear.** New boots that have never touched soil. Every seam, every strap, every hook-and-loop patch inspected for seeds. Each garden is an island; the flats between them are an ocean; you are a bird carrying who-knows-what between them. Wash between gardens.

**Metal.** You will be carrying steel. They have chert and bone. Understand what a steel knife *is* in that context: it is not a gift, it is a restructuring of an entire economy delivered to one person at random. Keep your metal minimal, keep it in your pack, and give none of it away. This will feel mean. Do it anyway. Whatever you leave behind will still be there, and still sharper than anything else on the plateau, in three hundred years.

**Names.** You do not have one here. A name exists in exactly one place, carved, and you have not been carved. Expect to be a nameless thing for a long time, possibly the whole trip. If someone walks you out across the pavement to a knuckle and shows you a mark, understand what has just happened and behave accordingly: look for a long time, touch nothing, and do not attempt to reciprocate with your own name, which exists nowhere and is only a noise anyway.

**Carving.** Do not. Not your initials, not a waypoint, not a thank-you. Marks here last longer than languages. There are glyphs on the knuckles so worn that only the low sun finds them and nobody alive can read them, and they are treated with extreme courtesy — meaning that in nine thousand years, somebody will be treating *your* scratch with extreme courtesy, and will be wrong to. If you need to mark a route, set a stone upright. It's their own convention, it is honest, and it can be undone.

**Manners, condensed:** Any stone you move, you set upright. Don't sleep during the Silence. Don't ask about names. Don't go near the lips in a way that looks like wanting to descend. Don't cut flutewood. Don't sit on a cushion. Don't touch the dew-combs. Don't drop anything into a throat, ever, not even something you think you should retrieve.

## 12. Navigation, and finding your way home

The pavement is a polished plain the size of a small country with two thousand holes in it, no edges, no streams, no trees, gentle kilometer-wide swells that hide everything, and a knuckle maybe every few kilometers.

**Your map is made of clouds.** Every throat grows a column. From the top of a knuckle you can see two hundred of them, and their arrangement is the true map of the plateau. Learn it. Sketch it daily. But remember that columns stall and detach and drift, so a missing pillar does not mean you're lost — it means a throat is choking and you should note which one.

**Your other sensor is the roar**, and your earplugs have taken it away from you. The locals navigate partly by a sound-field you have deliberately deafened yourself to. You can borrow it back: stop, crouch, briefly uncover one ear, take a bearing, re-plug. Do this *only* well out on the flats where the ambient is survivable, and do it as a navigational instrument, not as a pleasure.

**Never move in the dark.** In darkness, a stadium-sized hole is indistinguishable from pavement, you have plugged the warning sense, and there is no second chance. Camp early. If you are benighted, sit down where you are, put on everything you have, and wait for light. This is not a failure. It is the procedure.

**Mark your crossing point.** Before you do anything else on arrival — before you look at the view, before you walk toward the roar — find the nearest knuckle, take bearings, and set a stone upright at your exit. Then set another one a hundred meters out on the bearing you'll return along. It is legal, it is expected, it is the only human contribution the plateau accepts, and it is the difference between going home and dying of thirst four kilometers from a door.

## 13. Storms, lightning, and the racing shadows

The overcast breaks, the columns go gold on one side, and it is the most beautiful thing you will ever see. Also: you are standing on a wet conductive plain, four kilometers up, underneath a field of two hundred continuously convecting cumulus towers, and you are the tallest object for a kilometer in every direction except the dew-fins and the upright stones, which are the *other* tallest objects.

If you can see a column going dark and organized: get off the knuckles, get away from dew-fins, get away from the marker stones, crouch on your pack with your feet together, and stay there. Rain turns the whole pavement into a sheet of water with a hiss on top of the roar, and the footing goes from adequate to lethal in about four minutes.

Goggles, always. Not sunglasses — goggles, with a wide spectrum filter. UV at this altitude under intermittent cloud, reflected off wet polished stone, is a snowfield's worth. Bring high-SPF, bring lip protection, and reapply on a timer, because the wind and humidity will strip it and you will not feel it happening.

## 14. The mind, over eight weeks

Things that are normal and that nobody warns you about:

- **Voices in the roar.** Broadband noise at high level, for weeks, and your auditory system starts manufacturing pattern out of it. You will hear music. You will hear conversation just below intelligibility. You will hear your own name, clearly, more than once. This is auditory pareidolia and it is *expected*. Write it down when it happens; it helps.
- **Sleep fragmentation.** For the first three weeks, badly. It improves.
- **The loss of your interior monologue.** Around week two or three, for many people, the constant subvocal narration quiets. Some find this the best thing that has ever happened to them. A few find it frightening and want to leave.
- **Irritability.** Chronic high-level noise raises baseline stress hormones whether you notice or not. You and your partner will have one very stupid fight, probably around day twenty, about nothing. Agree in advance that when the stupid fight arrives, you both walk three hundred meters apart and sit down for an hour. It works.
- **The Silence aftermath.** For a day or two afterward, a flatness. People describe it as being homesick for something that lasted eleven minutes.

## 15. Coming back

Two things.

Your home world will seem dead. A quiet room will feel like a fault, like something has been switched off that shouldn't be. This lasts a few weeks, sometimes a few months. You will find yourself putting a fan on to sleep, and then a bigger one, and then a recording.

And you will have tinnitus, and in the tinnitus you will occasionally hear, or think you hear, the very low note — the *woom*, the one below hearing that you felt in the long bones — and for a second you will be standing on four billion years of stone with two hundred gold-and-lead cloud pillars around you and warm air from another world on your face.

Get the second audiogram. Compare it with the first. That's the receipt.

---

## If you take nothing else

1. Arrive fully acclimatized; there is no down.
2. Dosimeter, worn always, obeyed always. Your judgment of loudness will lie to you by the second day.
3. Never let the updraft hold any of your weight.
4. Learn a real sign language before you go, with the people you're going with.
5. Quarantine fourteen days. Forty people. Do not be the reason there are thirty.
6. Build the inverted alarm that wakes you when the world goes quiet.
7. Any stone you move, you set upright.
8. Set an upright stone at the place you came in.
