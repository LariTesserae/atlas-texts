# Preparing to Visit the Sill

## Begin nine months out, with your lungs and your voice

The Sill will forgive you for being ignorant. It will not forgive you for coughing.

At that altitude, in that unfiltered dryness above the mist line, almost every lowlander develops the standard high-mountain hack — a dry, barking, three-week cough that comes on around day four and cannot be suppressed by willpower. In most places on most worlds this is merely miserable. On the Sill it makes you a hazard to two hundred people, because for the days you are coughing nobody within eighty paces can hear a rim begin to tick. You will not be punished. You will simply be moved — carried politely down to the outer terraces near the Cold Lip, where the water is cold and the wind takes sound away, and left there with food until you stop. People will visit. Nobody will resent you. It will still be the loneliest week of your life.

So: arrive with clean lungs. No smoking for a year. If you have asthma, reflux, post-nasal drip, a dodgy larynx, chronic sinusitis — treat it, aggressively, and then test yourself at altitude before you commit. Carry a silk or fine-felt scarf to breathe through on the climb. There is one mercy waiting for you: the Sill itself is warm and wet, forty vents' worth of steam in a shallow bowl, and the air there is kinder to a throat than anything for forty miles around. Many travelers cough on the way up and stop on arrival.

Train your snore, too, if you have one. You will sleep indoors, where murmuring is allowed, but a lowlander at altitude breathes in cycles — a rising pant, a long pause, a snort — and you will be sharing a stone pod the size of a large drum with a family. Acetazolamide, started two days before you go up, largely fixes this. Take it. It also helps your eyes and your head.

Then train your silence. Not quietness — silence as a *skill*. Have someone follow you for a month with a switch and tap your wrist for every unnecessary sound you make: the sigh, the throat-clear, the knuckle crack, the coin-jingle, the sniff, the sole-scuff, the setting-down of a cup too hard. You will be shocked. An ordinary human makes something like two hundred audible noises an hour without noticing one of them. Get it under forty and you are ready to be a guest.

## Your ears, your eyes, your feet

**Ears.** The one thing you cannot bring is the thing that matters most: the trained ear for the click. It is described as a beetle in a wall — a dry, irregular tick, faint, coming from behind a rim where the water has found a fault. Nobody can teach you this from outside. What you *can* do is arrive knowing that you don't have it, and spend your first three days sitting beside one of the shaded, doing nothing, listening, with your hands in your lap. This is understood behaviour. It is, in fact, exactly what a sensible new person does, and it will do more for your standing than any gift you carry.

**Eyes.** This is the injury you will actually suffer. Light comes from above, from the side, and *from underneath*, off a fan of wet mirrors. Bring proper glacier goggles with side shields — but bring them dark, unreflective, horn-coloured, and leave the mirrored lenses at home; nobody in a world where shadow is currency wants your face throwing light back at them. Better: buy or be given a slit-visor of pressed ibex horn and wear it over the goggles, or instead of them once you understand the trade-off. Blacken the skin around your eyes with charcoal, every morning, like everyone else; it is not decoration, it kills the glare bouncing off your own cheekbones.

And protect the parts of you nobody warns about. On the Sill you burn *upward*: the underside of the chin, the inside of the nostrils, the septum, the lips, the roof of the mouth if you walk with your mouth open, which at that altitude you will. Grease everything. Bring far more zinc or lanolin than you think you need and reapply after every immersion, because the water will strip it off in one dip. Keep your mouth shut and breathe through your nose. Expect to lose the fine ability to judge distance and slope in total shadowlessness — practice beforehand on a snowfield in flat overcast light, where the same thing happens, and learn how badly you stumble.

Assume some permanent cost. A long stay carries a real risk of pterygium and early cataract. This is why the elders are called the shaded. It is not a metaphor.

**Feet.** Felt-soled boots, two pairs, plus felt overshoes for the wet work, plus more socks than seems reasonable. Your feet will be warm and soaking for weeks; without discipline you will get immersion foot and fungal rot, and there is no fire to dry anything. There *is* hot rock. Learn where the warm dry stone is and treat drying your socks as a daily religious duty. Trim your nails short before you go; you cannot cut them cleanly there because everything you own will be blunt.

Bring a probing staff, horn- or bone-tipped, chest high. You will use it constantly to test depth and heat before you step. This is your single most important piece of equipment.

## The crust tax, and what not to bring

Everything you carry onto the Sill will begin to turn to stone. Not metaphorically. A pack strap left lying in a splash-line for a week is glued to the floor. Boot laces cement. Hair cements — braid it tight and cover it, or you will be sawing a helmet off your head with obsidian. A cup left in a pool acquires a rim. Your clothes will stiffen at the hems and go brittle. Budget a fifth extra weight for the walk out, and pack nothing you cannot afford to leave behind or lose the use of.

Bring: horn, bone, obsidian, hide, felt, sinew, willow-work, ceramics without glaze, dried food in leather. Bone toggles instead of buckles. Rope of plant fibre or sinew.

Do not bring metal. This is not squeamishness. An iron nail set down on a white terrace for a week leaves a brown bloom in the deposit — and the deposit is permanent. You will not be scratching their wall; you will be writing on their thousand-year record in rust, in a place where the past is *looked at through windows*. A dropped steel knife is closer to graffiti on a tomb than to litter. Leave the belt buckle, the watch, the zips, the eyelets, the tin of pills. And take off your rings before you climb: a ring in that water will lock to your finger under a film of stone, and the removal is unpleasant.

Do not bring anything that ticks, hums, beeps, whirrs, or clicks. This includes any recording device with a shutter, any device with a fan, any device that chirps to tell you its battery is low. The click of a shutter is, in that soundscape, indistinguishable from the one sound in the world that means *run*. You could start an evacuation. Sketch on hide with charcoal, and remember that ink and paper bloat and seal; the Sill has no writing because writing there does not stay flat.

Do not bring firewood. You will be tempted — it seems the obvious mercy, the thing they lack. Do not. The grove at the Cold Lip is holy and the economy of the Sill is built on the fact that there is no fuel; an outsider arriving with an armful of dry wood is arriving with an armful of something between a sacrament and an obscenity, and you will not be able to tell which until it is too late.

## What to bring instead: shade

The right gift is a shadow.

A tightly woven black awning, four horn pegs, sinew guys, packable, casting a dense opaque shade about the size of a bed. Or three of them. In a world where the lee of a wall is inherited property and lending your shade for an afternoon is a serious kindness, a portable, giveable, *reproducible* patch of darkness is not a trinket. Understand before you offer it that it is a political object. Give it to a household and you have altered a balance. The safest course, and the one that will be remembered well, is to raise it in a public place, keep it in no one's name, and let it be used, and hand the making of the next one to whoever asks how it was done.

Everything else on the trade list is dull and welcome: good obsidian, worked and unworked. Ibex horn. Salt. Fine-toothed horn combs. Dried fruit from below. Sinew. And a few tools of ground quartz and horn for scraping thresholds, because that is the constant labour of the place and no one has ever had enough of them.

## The climb and the first morning

Come up the warm stream in a day and a half, as everyone does. Do not arrive at midday, when the light off the terraces will drop you to your knees with your hands over your face and you will meet your hosts as an animal. Camp short of the gorge mouth and come over the lip in the last hour of light, when the whole white staircase turns peach and every basin holds its own small burning sky, and you can look at the place without your eyes streaming.

Sleep where you are put. Ask nothing that night.

Then get up in the dark and be standing there when the first hour of the day begins, and when someone hands you a horn paddle, take it, and break ice for an hour with everyone else, quietly. That is the whole of your naturalization. It is not a test and nobody will explain it. It is simply that in the Sill you are what you are seen doing, and the frost-breaking is the one thing the entire town does at once.

## Staying alive

**Do not drink the warm water.** This is the quiet killer that no local will warn you about, because they can't see it. Water that has spent centuries in the dark dissolving rock comes up loaded with more than limestone — arsenic, fluoride, whatever else that particular hot root of the mountain contains. The people of the Sill are partly adapted and partly not; look at their teeth, mottled and brown-flecked, and their skin at forty. You are not adapted at all. Drink meltwater from the cirque side, cold, gathered high, and carry a filter that does nothing for dissolved minerals so you remember it does nothing for dissolved minerals. Eggs and algae bread cooked in the graded pools are fine. Broth made of that water is not.

**Do not sleep in a hollow.** The gases that come up with the water — sulfide, carbonic — are heavier than the air and settle at night into low places: old scoops, dry basins, sealed rooms opened by a new channel. People have died in them and been found looking asleep. Before entering any low or enclosed space, lower a lit tallow lamp or a smouldering wick to knee height and watch it. If it dims, back out. And know that your nose will be useless: the sulfide stink will kill your sense of smell within an hour of arrival and you will not get it back until days after you leave. You cannot smell gas, spoiled food, or smoke. Do not rely on it for anything.

**Scalds.** Forty vents at a temperature just short of pain, and a network that reroutes itself without notice. Test everything with the back of the wrist or a dipped horn tip, never a bare foot, and never step onto a pool that is showing you the sky. That is the first taboo and its practical half is exact: in total light a still pool is a perfect mirror, and you cannot read the depth or the heat of water that is reflecting the cirque back at you. Walk around it. Probe. If you must cross, probe every single step. There is a second, older reason for the rule and nobody explains it; you should not go looking for the explanation.

**The dry-strand.** The thing that will actually endanger your life is not heat but its absence. A rim fails, a vent wanders eighty paces, and your quarter goes cold. Cold at that altitude is a three-day clock. So: never let your pack be more than an arm's reach from where you sleep, keep it packed, keep a cold-weather layer and a bivouac sack in it always, and know two routes to the vents at any hour of the night. Learn the two-note whistle on your first day. You will hear it perhaps once if you stay a moon. Do not be the person who has to have it explained.

## Manners you must have before you arrive

Learn the hand-language. Six hundred signs at conversational speed is a language; you will not be fluent. Arrive with a hundred and fifty, and choose them ruthlessly: the warnings first (*hot, thin ice, click heard, give way, going, moving up, hold*), then the courtesies, then the domestic vocabulary. Understand that the register you learn from a phrasebook will be flat and childish, and that the Sill's real signing is dry, fast, joking and full of dismissals; you will be a solemn clown for a month. Accept it and keep signing. Speech is for indoors, at night, close to the ear, murmured. If you must speak outside, it means someone is about to be hurt.

Learn the touch-etiquette. News travels by a hand on the wrist. Expect strangers to take your wrist. Do not flinch from it, and do not initiate it until you can do it correctly, which you will learn by watching.

And carry these five in your body, not your notes:

- **Give shade when asked, and remember who gave it to you.** Both halves. The debt is real, it is tracked, and it is generational. If you leave the Sill owing shade you have stolen something.
- **Do not block a comber's road without leaving it another.** And do not feed one. Heaping shrimp-cake at a channel mouth is not kindness to an animal; it is a vote about where the water goes next year, cast by someone who will not be there to live with the result. Watch, delight in them, keep your hands to yourself.
- **Do not open a sealed room to take what is in it. You may open one to look.** You will be shown a lamp still standing on a shelf under two hands' thickness of clear stone. You will want it very badly. This is the exact moment at which a visitor becomes a thing that has to be dealt with.
- **Do not hammer.** Anything that needs hitting can be sawn, ground, or lashed. If you brought something that requires percussion to assemble, you brought the wrong thing.
- **Build low, build wet, build knowing it will close.** If you are there long enough to be given a house, understand that it is not lodging. It is a floor being made for someone not yet born.

## What it will do to you

You will find the silence unbearable for four days and then you will not be able to remember what noise was for. You will lose track of the time of day, permanently, because there are no shadows to tell you. You will be startled by your own body's sounds. You will develop the habit of standing still with your head tilted, and you will keep it for years afterward, and people at home will ask you what you are listening for.

You will also be, briefly, part of a two-hundred-person organism that works at full speed in near-total quiet, in the most beautiful light in any world, on a shelf of stone that is politely and continuously trying to swallow it. Nothing prepares you for the moment at dusk from the Cold Lip when you look back up the fan and see the whole town moving like an anthill and hear only breath.
