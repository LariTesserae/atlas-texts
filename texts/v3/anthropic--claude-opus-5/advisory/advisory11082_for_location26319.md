# Briefing: Preparing to Visit the Bell

## First, understand what you are visiting

Most anomalous sites are events. You go, something happens, you go home with a story that has a beginning and an end. The Bell is not an event. It is a condition. Nothing there is going to happen *to* you in the dramatic sense — no monster, no collapse, no door slamming shut. Everything that will hurt you is a slow subtraction: water, salt, hearing, hours, and eventually the part of you that believes things ought to go forward.

Plan accordingly. This is a desiccation-and-noise expedition with a psychiatric aftercare component. Treat the metaphysics as scenery and the physiology as the actual mission, and you will probably come back with your knuckles scabbed and your ears ringing and nothing worse.

Recommended party: three. One below, one at the crack, one at camp. Absolute minimum two, and the second person is not optional, because the exit requires assistance and because you will need someone whose job is to be less interested in the Works than you are.

## Six weeks out: the body

**Heat.** Forty degrees at effectively zero humidity is survivable — pleasant, even, for the first twenty minutes — because evaporative cooling works with obscene efficiency there. That efficiency is the trap. Acclimatize anyway: two weeks of hour-long sauna or hot-room sessions with light work, so your plasma volume is up and your sweat is dilute. You want to be a person who sweats early, sweats a lot, and loses as little salt as possible doing it.

**Hearing.** Get a proper audiogram. You want a baseline you can compare against, partly for your own sake and partly because if you go a second time you will want to know exactly what the first trip cost. 105 dB, continuous, with most of its energy low in the spectrum. Foam plugs alone will not do it — low frequencies leak around and through and, past a certain point, straight through the bones of your skull. Plugs *plus* muffs, properly fitted, gets you to roughly conversation level for the mid and high content. The low notes you will feel in your sternum and your sinuses regardless, and there is no gear for that. Practice wearing double protection for two hours at a stretch while doing awkward physical work in the heat, because the combination of muffled hearing, sealed face, and slow deliberate movement is disorienting the first several times and you do not want the first several times to be twelve meters down.

**Fitness, specifically:** hanging from a bar and dropping cleanly; ankle stability; the ability to chimney upward in a slot narrower than your shoulders while wearing gloves and carrying nothing. Measure the depth of your chest. The crack is as wide as a person turned sideways and it has never been any wider and never will be — nothing there erodes. If your chest depth plus your clothing exceeds the slot, you do not get in, or worse, you get in and do not get out. Build a plywood mock-up at your measured clearance and squeeze through it in full kit until it is boring.

**Selenium.** The tallow is a long-chain sulfur-selenium oil, and there has been a fine aerosol of it in that air for as long as the scoop has been pouring — sixty-three splashes a minute, from thirty centimeters, forever. Organoselenium compounds absorb through skin and mucosa and are hepatotoxic and neurotoxic in accumulation. Arrange a baseline urine selenium and a liver panel before you go, and repeat both a week after. Do not plan repeat visits inside the same season.

## Gear, and the reasoning behind it

**Respiration.** Full-face respirator, P100 particulate plus organic-vapor cartridge, with a heat-and-moisture-exchange element in the inlet if your model accepts one. The full face solves your eye problem at the same time: the air there is arithmetically dry and it will strip your corneas and cake your lids with your own salt inside two hours. Sealed foam-gasket goggles are the fallback if you cannot tolerate a full mask, but you will regret the fallback.

Carry a spare cartridge in a sealed bag. Carry a spare mask seal. The tallow film gets on everything and it is a lubricant; a greased face seal leaks.

**Water, and the fact that you will drink wrong.** Do not carry water in anything you have to open. The demonstration everyone does — unstopper a flask, watch the water leave it like smoke — is not a party trick, it is the ambient behavior of the room, and it applies to your mouth. Use closed bladders with bite valves. Prefer a slightly viscous electrolyte gel-slurry over plain water; it resists the air better and it replaces the salt you are dumping.

Critically: **you will not feel thirsty in a useful way, because you will never feel wet.** Sweat leaves the skin the instant it arrives. You will be dry to the touch while losing well over a liter an hour. So drink by clock, not by thirst — 300 to 400 mL every twenty minutes, whether or not you want it — and weigh yourself at camp before and after on a spring scale. Every kilogram lost is a liter you failed to drink. And do not take the absence of urine as good news.

**Occlusives.** Heavy paraffin or lanolin balm, applied thickly before descent and re-applied at the turnaround, on: lips, nostrils, the rims of the eyelids, the corners of the mouth, and any skin the suit does not cover. The fine parallel cracks people describe in their lips are not cosmetic; they bleed, and blood is the single most interesting substance in that chamber.

**Skin, cuts, and the mites.** Every crystal face down there still carries its growth terminations. There is not one rounded edge in the whole cavity, including in the slot and the chimney you will be pressing your body against. Kevlar or HPPE gloves with gauntlet cuffs, cut-resistant sleeves, knee pads, and a smooth tightly-woven coverall — not fleece, not anything napped, nothing the scale-mites can hold onto. Tape cuffs, ankles, and the neck closure. The mites will graze the tallow film off any surface that has one, and if you brush a seep and then stand still you become that surface. It does not hurt. Everyone who has felt it describes it the same way, with the same face.
