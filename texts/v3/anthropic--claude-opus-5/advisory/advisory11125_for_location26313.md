# Preparing for the Lower Volumes
### A traveller's brief, for someone ordinary

---

## First, adjust the category

Most travellers arrive having mentally filed this as *a sea*, or *a cave*. It is neither, and both errors get people hurt.

It is a machine. It is a machine the size of a country, and you are proposing to go and swim about inside its working fluid. Everything that happens to you there will happen because of a decision made in a control room, or a decision made eight years ago in a drawing office, or the failure of one. There is no weather but engineered weather, no tide but pumping schedule, no shore but formwork, no animal that wasn't stocked or that didn't sneak in down a ventilation shaft. Nothing there is indifferent to you in the way a mountain is indifferent. Everything there is *incidental* to you, which is worse, and much harder to feel in your body.

Frame it like this: you are visiting the inside of a working pump station. It happens to have a sea in it, and gulls, and mornings.

---

## Six months out: the body

**Teeth.** Get everything done. Every loose filling, every crown that's been "fine," every wisdom tooth you've been ignoring. You will spend your visit inside a sixty-per-minute bass throb that couples directly into bone, and you will find out very quickly which of your dental work is sound. Also: you will descend eleven hundred metres in under an hour in a cage or an inclined car. Air trapped under a bad filling expands. People have wept in adits over this.

**Sinuses and ears.** If you have a head cold on the day of descent, you do not descend. Not "it'll be uncomfortable" — you will arrive at the bottom with a sinus barotrauma and blood in your mask and a two-and-a-half-day boat ride between you and anyone qualified. Practise equalising. Learn to do it hands-free. If you have a deviated septum or chronic congestion, deal with it before you go, or accept that you will only ever see the Volumes from the top of a shaft.

**Cold-water tolerance.** Eight degrees. Start cold-water swimming a season ahead — not to become hard, but to buy yourself control of the gasp reflex. Cold shock kills more visitors than anything exotic. The first involuntary inhalation in eight-degree water, at the surface, with a wall of lit nothing under you, is where most drownings actually happen.

**Skin.** Do not arrive with fresh tattoos, recent shaving rash, sunburn, eczema flare, or new piercings. Alkali finds broken skin the way water finds a crack. Let everything heal. Grow your beard if you have one, or shave three days before, not the morning of.

**Hearing.** Get an audiogram before you go, and keep the printout. You will want the comparison afterwards. Everyone does.

**Fitness.** Not athletic fitness. Stair fitness, ladder fitness, the capacity to climb a hundred metres of caged ladder in wet gear with a pack, twice, at the end of a bad day. That is the fitness that gets people out.

## Being counted

Understand this before anything else: **the Volumes run on tallies.**

A person unaccounted for at shift end is not a private tragedy there. It is a system event. Pumps get throttled, gates get held, drawdowns get postponed, and a search launch goes out across a province of water at eighteen knots with nothing to look at. It costs. Crews have long, unforgiving memories about people who cost them.

So: get on a manifest, properly, through whoever hosts you. Get a tally tag — a stamped disc with your cell, your gang, your number — and wear it where a body would still have it. Hang your board tag when you go through a bulkhead and pull it when you come back, *every single time*, even if you're stepping out for four minutes. Especially then.

The traveller's version of politeness here is arithmetic. Be a number that always resolves.

## Timing your visit

The rain has a season: three weeks in eleven, keyed to lift-house maintenance. Decide deliberately.

If you want to *see* soffit rain — and you should, once — arrange to see it from a gallery, from inside, through glass or from under a gantry roof, with ear defenders on. Watching a hectare of ceiling let go at once, from two hundred metres up, is one of the sights of the multiverse. Being on open water underneath it in a small boat is an entirely different experience: fat cold drops falling from a great height, thousands per square metre per second, and the mist thrown up is aerosolised cell water, which is to say aerosolised lime. It gets in your eyes. It gets in your lungs.

If you want to swim, want to see the shoals, want the mid-volume silence — go in the dry weeks.

Also ask, before you commit to any particular cell: **when did it last flood up, and when does it draw down?** A cell four years wet is a different world from a cell six months wet. Younger means higher pH, thinner life, fewer barge passes, harsher water. Older means green stripe, mussel crust, eels in the joints, and something that has begun to smell faintly like a place rather than a product.

## Kit

**A drysuit, not a wetsuit.** This is not negotiable and it is the single largest item of your budget. A wetsuit works by holding a film of water against your skin. Here, the water is the hazard. You want a membrane suit with good latex neck and wrist seals, and you want a *neck seal cover* or a taped collar, because the standard failure mode is water wicking down inside the neck and sitting in your armpits for four hours where you can't feel it doing anything until you get out and find the skin gone.

**Gloves. Many, many gloves.** Dry gloves if you can. The crews wear theirs out in a fortnight, and it isn't the water, it's the arrises — every edge down there is a sharp, un-weathered ninety degrees of high-strength concrete, and it takes leather off like a rasp. Take three times as many as you think, and take a decent set of thin liners for fine work.

**Eye protection, two kinds.** Sealed goggles for anywhere near spray or mist — a splash of cell water in an unprotected eye is a genuine emergency, and the nearest ophthalmologist may be two days away. And separately, tinted glasses for the glare. People don't expect this. It is *bright*, the surface is glassy and mirror-flat, the strings are overhead and also reflected, and after six hours on open water you get a slow grinding headache and a photokeratitis-like burn without a photon of UV involved. Polarised, if the local fixtures allow it.

**Ear defenders, and spare ear defenders.** Worn defaults, not emergency gear. The bass throb won't damage you quickly. The valve-gear bangs will.

**Barrier cream, and an acid rinse.** Whatever the local dispensary issues — usually a buffered wash and a weak acetic solution — carry a personal bottle on your body at all times, not in the boat. Also lip balm, heavy, waxy, reapplied hourly. Your lips will go anyway. Everyone's do. New arrivals are identifiable at fifty paces by the raw ring around the mouth.

**pH strips.** Cheap, weightless, and they will change how you behave. Test the water wherever you are. The dosing barges work in mowing patterns four months to the pass, and the water two kilometres behind a barge is a different chemistry from the water two kilometres ahead of it. Knowing which side of a plume you're on is real, actionable information.

**Lanyards on everything.** Every tool, every camera, every bottle, clipped. If you drop something it does not land somewhere. It falls two hundred metres onto a flat grey invert that no one will ever visit, and it stays there for ten thousand years, and it is gone in a way that objects on planets are almost never gone.

**Two watches.** One set to local — the twenty-six-hour lamp cycle — and one set to your own body's twenty-four. You will need both to understand why you feel the way you feel. Bring melatonin. Bring an eye mask, because "night" down there is only the strings dimmed, not off.

**Vitamin D, and whatever else your medical people say about living under lamps.** Long stays cost you something. Short stays don't, much.

**A pencil and a copy of the drawings, dated.** The stencil at the bulkhead is not a slogan. It is an operating instruction. The drawings are wrong in proportion to how new the thing is, and the crews read rust weeps — a hand's breadth a year — to date what they're standing on. Learn to do this. A gallery with tear-stains down to the skirting is old, established, drawn, reliable. A shiny bracket with no stain beneath it was installed since the last flood-up and exists on no plan anywhere. Believe the rust over the paper.

**A stone.**

Bring a stone. A real one, river-rounded, pocket-sized, from a world with bedrock. There is no natural rock in the Lower Volumes and there never has been, and the crews collect them — a shelf of pebbles above a bunk, a chip of granite on a bootlace. It is the single best gift you can carry in, it weighs nothing, and it will get you invited to sit down.

## Water discipline

**Assume the water is a mild caustic and behave accordingly.** Not lethally so; you will not dissolve. But it is a slow, patient, cumulative injury and it does not announce itself. The tell is texture: *if the water feels soapy, that is your own skin being saponified.* That slippery, sweet, silky quality everyone remarks on is not a charming feature of the place. It is the chemistry happening to you.

Rules that experienced people follow without thinking:

- Mouth shut. Nose clip if you're in and out repeatedly. A swallow won't hurt you; an hour of open-mouth swimming will take the surface off your tongue.
- Rinse *immediately*, not at the end of the shift. Fresh water first, then the acid wash, then dry, then cream.
- Get out of wet clothing at once, and check the folds — waistband, armpit, crotch, behind the knee, sock line. The burns happen where fabric holds the water against you.
- Never rub your eyes with a wet glove. This is how most eye injuries down there happen, and they all happen in the same half-second of thoughtlessness.
- Cuts get flushed and sealed the same minute. An alkaline burn inside an open wound is a wound that will still be open a month later.

## Noise discipline

In air, the noise is a nuisance and a slow hearing cost. **In water, it is a hazard with teeth.**

The cells are hard-walled, empty, and enormous, and they ring. Cell 40 sits near a very low A. Divers describe it as being inside a struck bell that never quite stops, and after a few hours it stops being a sound and becomes a condition, like weather, like the feeling of a room. Some people find it the most beautiful thing they have ever been inside. Some people cannot tolerate it for twenty minutes and have to be brought up. There is no way to know which you are until you're in.

Then there is water hammer. The knocks.

A pressure wave running the length of a conduit and slamming a gate is, in the water, a genuine shock front. Three a shift is normal. Six means someone is having a difficult conversation on the surface. What matters for you:

- **Know the test windows and be out of the water for them.** Ask. Write them down. They are the single most important numbers on any given day.
- Learn the whistle codes before you descend, not after. Three long: drawdown. Two and a repeat: knock in the system. There will be others local to your cell. Know them the way you know your own name; the acoustics mangle everything else, but the codes cut through.
- If you are in the water and you hear or feel the beginning of one: **get your chest out of the water.** Roll on your back, get the torso up, exhale as it passes. Blast overpressure hurts gas-filled organs — lungs, sinuses, gut — and a body floating flat and low takes it full-length. This is the whole of the emergency protocol and it takes four seconds to teach and it has saved a number of people.

Also: expect to be shouted at, and to be shouted at rudely. Everyone's deaf. Hand signals are the real language, and the crews will teach you the local set in about ten minutes if you ask on the first day rather than the fifth.

## Navigation, and the string

There is nothing to steer by. Nothing. The strings are the entire coordinate system of the world: red on one axis, amber on the other, and the ability to say *"red nineteen, amber four"* is the difference between a person who can be found and a person who cannot.

Memorise the grid pitch for your cell. Practise reading your position from a moving boat until it is automatic. Practise saying it aloud in a form a radio operator can copy in one pass.

Underwater, the vertigo is real and it is unlike anything in a natural sea. The water is new and unturbid, the light comes from above in a regular grid, and you can see two hundred metres in every direction and there is nothing in any of them. There is no bottom in sight, no surface texture, no dark below and bright above in the usual reassuring gradient — just pale greenish luminous *everywhere*. People lose the vertical. Watch your bubbles. Carry a depth gauge and actually look at it. If you have any tendency to disorientation, stay within sight of a wall, and be honest with yourself about it, because in the mid-volume there is nothing at all to grab.

Above all, the old rule: **keep a hand on the string.** Literally where possible, figuratively always. In fog, in rain, at lamp-dim, the strings are the only thing in the world that is where it says it is.

Also — mundane but important — this is **fresh water**. If you learned to swim in the sea, you will be startled how much less it holds you up, especially cold and especially in gear. Do your buoyancy checks on day one, in the shallows off a lift-house apron, with someone watching.

## Medical isolation

This is the fact that should shape your entire risk appetite, and travellers routinely fail to internalise it:

**A survey launch crosses Cell 40 in two and a half days.**

There is no golden hour in the mid-volume. There is no helicopter, because there is nothing to fly to and four hundred metres of air gap at best and a soffit above that. If you break a femur at red nineteen, amber four, you are going to be alive on a small boat with your leg broken for a very long time.

Consequently:
- Carry a real trauma kit and know how to use it in the dark, cold, wet, with one hand. Tourniquet, chest seal, a splint that actually works, strong analgesia if you can legally carry it, and a stove and a shelter and a sleeping bag, because a casualty in eight-degree saturated air dies of cold long before anything else.
- Know where the nearest recompression chamber is. There is usually one at a lift house. "Usually" is doing work in that sentence; verify for your cell.
- If you're diving, dive conservatively — much more conservatively than you would at home. There is no margin for a chamber ride that takes two days to reach.
- Take antibiotics for skin infection. Alkali burns colonise readily and the crews all know someone who lost a finger to a graze.

The corresponding discipline is simply this: in the mid-volume, do nothing you wouldn't do solo on a glacier. Out at the walls, near a lift house, you can be brave. Out in the middle, be boring.
