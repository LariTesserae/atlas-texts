# Briefing for Visitors to The Pocket

### Classification: Unfinished Interior. Low hazard, high consequence.

---

## 1. First, understand what kind of danger this is

The Pocket will almost certainly not kill you. It is nine degrees, it is bright, the air is breathable, and nothing in it is larger than your forearm. Novices read the survey and relax.

Do not relax. The Pocket is a **consequence hazard**, not a survival hazard. The risk is not that it damages you; the risk is that you damage *it*, permanently, in about a second and a half, with a gesture you will not remember making. Every surface here is soft, every process here is undecided, and the place is running its history forward from a point that has not started yet. You are arriving into wet plaster with a very short setting time and about four hundred million years of curing ahead.

Prepare accordingly. Prepare the way you would for handling something newborn, not the way you would for caving.

---

## 2. Conditioning (begin six weeks out)

**Cold-damp tolerance.** Nine degrees is not cold. Nine degrees for five hours, at ninety-eight percent humidity, with condensation wetting your outer layer continuously and no way to generate heat by moving quickly — that is cold. Train in a walk-in cellar or a cold room. Sit still in it. The Pocket does not permit the little unconscious warmth-generating movements: no stamping, no hand-rubbing, no jogging on the spot. All of those make noise, and noise is currency here, and you will be spending someone else's.

**Silence conditioning.** This is the serious one. Spend at least ten days in enforced silence before you go, ideally in a low-reverberation environment. You are trying to break two habits:

- *Subvocalising aloud.* Most people talk to themselves far more than they believe. In the Pocket you will not hear yourself do it — the web takes the sound before it reaches you — so you will have no feedback and no correction. You must be able to trust that your mouth is shut without hearing that it is.
- *Filling silence.* The silence in the Pocket has texture. Veterans describe it as felt pressed to the ears, or as a mild constant pressure behind the sternum. It provokes people. Around the ninety-minute mark there is a documented urge to speak — not to say anything in particular, just to *emit*. Train through this in advance so you recognise it as a symptom rather than an impulse.

**Shadow-lag desensitisation.** Rig a mirror or a camera feed with a 400–600 millisecond delay and an overshoot, and spend an hour a day moving under it. The Pocket's shadows lag, overshoot, then settle. Untrained visitors get vestibular nausea within twenty minutes, and vomiting in the Pocket is a catastrophe of the first order — organic matter, acid, noise, mess, all at once, into a sealed ecosystem with no scavengers rated for it.

**Breath control.** You will want to breathe shallowly and slowly, not for oxygen reasons but because you are a weather system. The Pocket's entire erosive economy is condensation: one bead gathering, one bright track, one hour of dust closing over it. A human adult exhales somewhere near half a litre of water per hour. Over a five-hour visit you will deposit more moisture on those walls than the seep delivers in a week. You are, briefly, the largest climatic event in the room's history. Breathe through the nose, low and slow, and keep your face away from the walls.

## 3. Kit

**Do not bring:**

- Anything that ticks, hums, clicks, whirrs, or has a fan. A mechanical watch is a disaster: the glasswrights will farm the ticks, store them, and release them later in clumps. Reports exist of a visitor's watch being "given back" as a four-second burst of eleven hundred ticks, two hours after he left, to an empty room. Harmless — but you have just fed and disrupted four hundred animals for nothing.
- Artificial light sources, except one sealed emergency unit (see below). There is a lichen here that grows on brightness. A torch is a feeding station. Leave it off.
- Anything black. The velvet moths are the only genuinely black object in the Pocket, and they are black the way a defined term is defined. A visitor in black kit is a second answer to a question the room has already settled. Wear undyed wool, bone, oatmeal, pale ochre. Match the strata. Be boring.
- Plastic packaging, foil, hook-and-loop fastenings, stiff nylon, anything that crackles. All fastenings should be bone, horn, or cloth tape, tied not snapped.
- Chalk, charcoal, markers, paint, knives, anything that makes a mark. Obviously.
- Food that crumbs. Food at all, if you can manage the duration without.

**Do bring:**

- **Undyed wool base layers**, two, with a loose wool over-tunic. Wool because it stays warm wet, and because it is silent. Avoid anything with a shell fabric — shell fabrics rustle, and here a rustle becomes a pearl of tension on a filament that some animal has to deal with.
- **Felt overboots.** Rigid soles gouge the floor; the floor is hard cheese. You want to compress, not cut. Felt, or soft leather, and take them off at the threshold — not the threshold *stone*, see §6 — and carry them out in a bag, because they will have Pocket material on them.
- **A wide, soft-brimmed hat with a face veil of fine cloth.** You will find the webs with your face otherwise. The hat is not for your comfort; it is to make sure that when you inevitably touch a web, you touch it with cloth you control rather than with eyelash, lip, and breath.
- **A silk wand.** A metre of flexible cane with a loose hank of fine silk floss at the tip, held vertically ahead of you at face height, moved slowly. Silk finds filament without breaking it, most of the time. This is the single most important piece of kit you will carry.
- **Gloves**, thin, cloth, not rubber. The floor is faintly greasy and your skin oils are a novel chemistry here.
- **A hair net and a full beard net if applicable.** You shed roughly a hundred hairs a day. Each one will be found in four hundred years, and each one is an argument.
- **A hard-shell container for everything that leaves your body.** Everything. Voiding completely beforehand is standard; for visits over four hours, catheterisation is recommended and, in several jurisdictions, mandated. There are no scavengers here rated for you.
- **One sealed emergency light**, red-filtered, taped off, in a pocket, not to be opened unless your transit anchor fails and you are waiting. The Pocket's light is not going out in your lifetime or in the lifetime of your species. But rule 2 exists for a reason and the lichen is thickening.
- **Your transit anchor,** double-redundant, carried on your person, never set down.

## 4. Arrival and exit: the hard constraint

There is no way out of The Pocket.

This is not dramatic phrasing. It is nine metres of pale limestone in every direction, with a single ceiling crack too fine for a finger and a gap that the swallows use, which leads only to more stone. You arrive by transit and you leave by transit. There is no walking out, no digging out, no waiting for rescue from the surface, because in this era the surface is under an unremarkable amount of shallow sea and there is nobody on it.

Therefore:

- **Carry your anchor on your body.** Not in a bag on the floor. Not clipped to the cairn. On your body, and a second one on your body in a different place.
- **Do not trust room geometry for your return.** Rule 6 is genuine. The Pocket has not settled on its shape; count the walls twice and you will get different answers. Anchors that key to local spatial features fail here at a rate of roughly one in nine. Use a personal-frame anchor.
- **Mark your arrival point with a single, deliberate footprint.** This is the exception to leave-no-trace, and it is deliberate. You cannot lay a cairn (you would be adding to the room's artefact inventory), you cannot chalk a mark (see: the tally), you cannot leave an object. But the floor takes a print and keeps it, and a print is honest: it is a record of a foot that was there. Place it once, cleanly, heel and toe, at your arrival point. Look at it before you move away. That print is now four hundred years from being flint-hard and being argued about, and you should understand, standing over it, that you have just made a small permanent contribution to somebody's doctoral thesis and possibly to a schism.

Make it a good print. Make it a print you would be happy to be famous for.

## 5. Sound discipline

Assume that **everything you emit is permanent, harvested, and will be replayed out of context at an unrelated moment, possibly to someone else, possibly after you are dead.**

Rules:

1. **Do not speak.** Not a word. Agree a hand-sign set in advance with your party and drill it until it is fluent, because you will be gesturing in poor peripheral conditions with a veil over your face and a lagging shadow confusing your sense of where your hands are.
2. **Do not say your name, even in your head, if you are the sort of person whose head leaks.** This is not superstition. A name is a compact, high-tension sound and webs hold it well. There is at least one Pocket in this district in which a survey team's names are still being released, at intervals, in the surveyor's own voices, sixty years after the survey. They are not haunting anything. They are simply stored.
3. **Do not break a web.** If your wand snags one, freeze. Let the tension go slack. Back out along your own path. A broken web releases its whole holding at once, late and displaced and out of tune, and some of those webs are holding things much older and much larger than you — the low grinding of a fold that has not happened yet. You do not want to be the one who lets the mountain-building out early.
4. **Keep webs off your chest.** A filament laid against the sternum will farm your heartbeat. This is not dangerous in any measurable way. It is, by universal report, unbearable. People describe realising, an hour later, that they cannot feel their own pulse, and then hearing it, once, from across the room.
5. **Cough into wool.** If you must cough, press your mouth hard into the crook of your woollen sleeve and cough into the fibre. The wool absorbs enough to keep it off the webs. You will still probably get it back forty minutes later from somewhere else. Do not be startled. It is yours; it is just late.

## 6. The artefacts: what not to touch, and why

There are no people in the Pocket. There have never been people. Nevertheless there is a worn doorstep, a cairn, a tally, a lamp, and a flint.

The governing principle is **rule 4: wear precedes use.** These objects carry the wear of use that has not occurred. They are not relics; they are *reservations*. The Pocket has set the table for a history it has not had yet.

Which means that you, standing there, are a candidate.

**The threshold stone.** You will want to stand on it. Everybody wants to stand on it; the saddle is worn exactly to the shape of a foot and it is at the low end where you are already stooping and it *invites*. Standard protocol is: **do not.** Not because something bad happens — nothing observably happens — but because the wear in that stone is the accumulated passage of many thousands of feet, and nobody has established whether those feet are a future population or whether they are visitors, iterated, over and over, with you as the first and the cause. If it is the latter, then every visitor who steps on it deepens a saddle that was already deep before anyone stepped on it, and the whole thing closes into a loop with no opening. Do not be the one who closes it. Step over it or go around.

**The cairn of seven stones.** Count them. Do not restack them, do not straighten them, do not add an eighth. The eighth is a classic contamination — it looks like reverence and it is vandalism.

**The tally marks.** Forty-one strokes, in groups of five, last group incomplete, *getting clearer rather than weathering*. **Count them on arrival and count them again before you leave, and write the number down outside.** This is your single most important instrument reading. If the count is forty-one on arrival and forty-one on departure, your visit was uneventful. If it has changed while you were inside, leave immediately by the shortest route to your anchor, without retrieving equipment, and report it. Nobody knows what the tally is counting. There is a strong professional consensus that you do not want to be a stroke in it.

**The clay lamp.** Unfired, unglazed, dry wick, a thimble of oil that has not evaporated in an unknown span of time. You will have the idea of lighting it. The idea arrives with a sense of rightness and completion, and it is the most dangerous thought available in the Pocket. The room is bright. There is no reason for a lamp. The lamp is waiting for a dark that is millions of years out, and it has been supplied with exactly enough oil for one use. **Do not spend it.** Bring no fire, no striker, no matches, no flame source of any kind, so that the thought, when it comes, has nothing to attach to. This is why your emergency light is sealed and taped: not for the lichen, but for you.

**The flint flake.** Three retouch scars. Do not pick it up. Do not photograph it with a flash. If you must record it, record it edge-on and briefly. The flake is the Pocket's argument that somebody made something here, and it is an argument it has not finished constructing.

## 7. The fauna: handling notes

- **Sifters.** Harmless, slow, essential. They are hardening the floor. Do not step in a herd; do not, under any circumstances, take a handful of the crystal bank. It sparkles savagely and it is the accumulated excreted labour of a hundred thousand animals and it is the Pocket's entire future flooring budget.
- **Chalk shrews.** Five, blind, furious, fast. The only animals here in a hurry. They will not attack you but they will run over your feet and the startle reflex is what kills web discipline. Know where they are.
- **Velvet moths.** They fly at fog-time. Nine thousand silent moths at ankle-to-chest height for the duration of a two-hour fog is, by all accounts, one of the finest things available anywhere in the accessible multiverse. Stand still. Do not raise your hands. Do not touch one; they have no scales to lose but they are matte and absorbent and your finger oil would put a shine on a thing whose entire vocation is not shining.
- **Swallows.** Pale, feathered, real, nesting in mud cups with nowhere to fly. They come and go through a gap that leads only to more stone. **Nobody has followed them, and you will not be the first.** This is not advice, it is the condition of your visa. The standing prohibition exists because three recorded attempts produced three non-returns and one delayed sound — a single note of what the retrieval team described as "a summer evening" — released into the empty Pocket eleven days later.
- **The eel.** It surfaces in a puddle the width of a spread hand, being itself as long as your arm, takes a breath it does not need, and sinks. It is patient with the geometry and you should be too. **Do not put anything in the puddle.** Not a hand, not a probe, not a line, not a bottle. The puddle is deeper than the room is tall and the light goes down with you into a green with no bottom, and the standard advice — which is followed by every experienced traveller and mocked by every new one — is: **look at it for less than a minute, and do not look at it twice.**

## 8. Duration, scheduling, and the plants

Recommended visit: **one fog cycle, three to four hours.** The Pocket has days, though it has never seen a sun; the fog rises twice daily at ankle height, sits two hours, lifts, and smells of wet slate and cut grass. Arrive forty minutes before fog. Leave within thirty minutes of its lifting.

Longer visits are permitted to surveyors and are not recommended to anyone else, for two reasons. The first is thermal: you are the only heat source, and over six hours you measurably warm the room. The second is psychological, and it concerns the oak.

In the exact centre of the Pocket, in a handful of grit, there is an oak seedling with two true leaves. It has had two true leaves for a very long time. There is no room for it to become anything else, and it does not appear to be trying.

Most visitors are fine with the sourceless light. Most are fine with the lagging shadows, the silence, the eel. The oak is what gets people. Something about the two leaves, the patience, the absence of striving, the complete lack of any grievance about it — it opens people up in a way that is not predictable from their personality profiles, and it opens them up in a room where crying is a sound event and tears are a hydrological one.

Prepare for this. Know that it is coming. Have a plan: veil down, breathe through the nose, look at the strata instead, count the tally again. Do not kneel by it. Do not water it. There is exactly the right amount of water here, arriving every eleven seconds, and it has been arriving for a long time.

---

## 9. Departure checklist

1. Recount the tally. Write nothing inside; hold the number.
2. Confirm all equipment on person. Sweep your path visually for dropped items — a dropped item here does not lie around waiting to be found, it becomes *early*, and in four hundred years it will be an object with wear it never earned.
3. Check gloves, veil, and hat for passengers. Sifters travel. A sifter removed from the Pocket dies within an hour and takes several thousand years of floor with it.
4. Return to your arrival print. Stand beside it, not in it.
5. Look once at the red seam. It will have moved a hand's breadth upward since you arrived, or it will not have. Either is normal. It has not settled its order and neither, it turns out, have you.
6. Go.

---

## 10. Afterwards

Expect a forty-eight-hour auditory hangover: ordinary rooms will sound cluttered, abrasive, badly farmed. This passes.

Expect, for about a week, to hear things arrive slightly late.

And expect, at some point in the following months, the delayed recognition that a print of your thumb or your heel is now nine metres down in pale limestone that has not yet agreed to be a cliff, curing, and that in four hundred years someone who does not exist yet will stand on a floor that a herd of thumb-sized animals is currently chewing into hardness, and will look at your mark, and will be wrong about it, and will argue.

You cannot fix this. You should not want to. The Pocket has the wear of use and the innocence of never having been used, and it holds both without difficulty. You will be, in the end, one small clear noise arriving to no one.

Make it a good one.
