# Preparing for the Flat Above the Weather
### A briefing for ordinary travelers, assembled from the accounts of those who came back and the notebooks of some who didn't

---

## 1. Understand what you are actually going to fight

You are not going to fight the cold, though the cold will be the thing that kills you if something does. You are not going to fight the altitude, the boilers, the bog, or the thinning.

You are going to fight the fact that **nothing there is separate from anything else**, including you, and that this is not a metaphor but a slow local process with a rate.

Everything below follows from that. Every rule that seems fussy or superstitious is a rule about staying discrete.

---

## 2. The body: an altitude worse than its number

Three thousand meters is not heroic. Millions of people live there. But this three thousand meters comes with air at ninety-six percent water, which crowds oxygen out of every breath, and it comes without a day — meaning without the circadian signal that governs the ventilatory response to altitude. Travelers who would sail through Cusco go down hard on the plateau.

**Acclimatize elsewhere first.** Two weeks minimum at comparable altitude in a world with a functioning sky. Arrive already adapted; do not plan to adapt on site, because "on site" gives you no nights to adapt through. Carry acetazolamide and know its dosing. Carry dexamethasone and know the symptoms that justify it. Understand that there is no descent available — the plateau is two hundred kilometers of the same elevation, and the standard treatment for altitude illness, *go down*, simply does not exist as an action there. Your only descent is your exit portal. This means your egress must be reachable at all times by a sick person on a stretcher, and that constraint should shape your entire route.

Bring more electrolytes than you think is reasonable. You will drink enormously — breathing that air is dehydrating in a way that feels absurd, and the water you drink will be peat water, tannic and mineral-empty. People have hyponatremic seizures on a plateau made of standing water. Salt everything. Salt your drinking water.

## 3. Clothing: you will not be dry again until you leave

Delete "staying dry" from your planning vocabulary. It is not an achievable state. The air condenses on you. Ten minutes out and your eyebrows bead; an hour and your sleeves are heavy; a day and every layer you own is at equilibrium with a cloud. There is no sun to dry anything and nothing on the plateau you may burn. Any system that depends on drying out will fail on day one and you will spend the rest of the trip pretending it hasn't.

So: **choose insulation that works wet, and plan to be wet, warm, and disgusting.**

- Wool and pile. Thick fleece, the old fuzzy-rubber system: wool base, heavy pile mid, hardshell that you treat as a windbreak rather than a raincoat, because the rain is coming out of the air from all directions at once and also, sometimes, upward.
- No down. Not one gram. Not in your bag, not in your parka.
- Neoprene socks, and *many* pairs of wool socks — not because you can dry them but because you can wring them, and a wrung sock is a different animal from a worn one.
- A vapor-barrier layer for sleeping only, to keep your own body's water out of your bag, since the bag will never dry either. Synthetic bag, overstuffed, inside a bivy.
- Gloves you can operate in while soaked. Three pairs. You will lose one to the peat.

**Foot care is the discipline that decides your trip.** Immersion injury is the plateau's most reliable casualty. Not frostbite — it never quite freezes — but macerated, whitening, splitting feet that stop being able to carry you. Institute a ritual: twice daily, boots off, feet wrung dry with a towel from a sealed bag, inspected by *someone else* (you will not report your own feet honestly), powdered, new socks. Non-negotiable, even when you are exhausted, especially when you are exhausted.

And seal your wounds. All of them, immediately, even nicks. This is partly ordinary tropical-wet-wound medicine: nothing heals in that air, everything goes septic and stays septic. And it is partly the other thing, the thing about incorporation. An open wound is an unfinished edge between you and a landscape that makes tissue of whatever it can reach. Travelers disagree about whether this matters. The ones who came back all sealed their cuts.

## 4. The ground: travel it like a glacier

There is no bedrock. There is no bottom. The boring never stops being vegetable, and this means that a soft spot in the surface is not a hole you can fall into and stand up in. It is a hole you fall into.

**Rope up.** Genuinely. Adopt glacier protocol wholesale: three or four on a line, spaced, probe poles in both hands, the lead probing every step in doubtful ground, a rescue kit that works — pulleys, prusiks, a haul system you have practiced in a cold pool at home until it is boring. Rehearse extraction from suction, which is not the same as extraction from a crevasse. Peat does not let go. The pull required to free a submerged leg is measured in hundreds of kilos, and the standard error is to haul upward on a person who is being held by a column of water; you must break the seal first, by working water down the leg, which takes patience nobody has when their friend is chest-deep and cooling.

**Bring flotation for your feet.** Wide bog boards, snowshoe-like, lashed rather than strapped so you can shed them fast. They halve your speed and quadruple your safety.

Expect four kilometers to cost you what eight would elsewhere. Plan two to three kilometers an hour on good moss, less than one in the sheeted water, and build your food and fuel budget on the pessimistic number.

## 5. Water, food, fuel: carry the whole trip in

Water is everywhere and is the least of your problems, but treat it anyway — filter for the particulate load of a living substrate, then chemical or boil. Bog water is acidic and tannic; it will loosen your bowels and stain your teeth and taste like a cellar. Fine.

**Fuel must all be carried.** There is nothing to burn. Do not entertain the thought of burning peat; it is the body of the place and it will keep burning for years after you leave, underground, hollowing out the crust for whoever comes next. Bring a stove and every gram of gas for it, and know that gas performs poorly in wet cold — bring liquid fuel with a pump. You may cook against a boiler wall for free heat, and it is glorious, and see section 8 before you do.

**Eat nothing native.** Not the moss, not the amphibians, not the grazers, not the fruiting bodies on the boiler flanks that look for all the world like something a starving person could eat. The photosynthetic amphibians are almost certainly alkaloid-loaded — anything that lies still in the open all its life is. But that is not the real reason. The real reason is that the plateau incorporates what it takes in, and there is no principled account of which direction that runs. Do not open that account. Carry your calories, all of them, high-fat, and carry twenty percent more than your worst-case schedule.

Because caches don't work. Anything you set down and leave is taken into the substrate — a season is enough, and less near a boiler. There is no depot logistics on the plateau, no relay, no resupply. **Your trip length is bounded by what your party can carry in one lift.** Ten to fourteen days is the practical ceiling. Anyone planning a month is planning to eat the plateau, and then to become it.

## 6. Navigation: never close a loop

This is the single most important operational rule, and it is the one that people nod at during the briefing and then violate on day three because it is inconvenient.

Distances do not sum. Out-and-back is stable: four kilometers there, four back, reliable, dull, safe. **Triangles are not stable.** Walk A to B to C to A and you have covered eleven kilometers of the four-and-four-and-three you plotted, and the surplus came out of your food and your daylight, except there is no daylight, so it came out of your feet.

Therefore:

- **Route as a tree, never as a network.** Every excursion is an out-and-back from a node. You return the way you came, always, even when a shortcut is visibly right there.
- **Maps are decoration.** Any map of the plateau is internally inconsistent and cannot be otherwise. What works is an *itinerary*: a written sequence of instructions with headings, counts, times, and above all recognized features, valid only in the order recorded and in reverse. Keep it in pencil on waterproof paper. Two copies, two people.
- **Use the boilers as beacons, with reservations.** They are sixty meters tall and visible at eight kilometers and they are the only individuated objects in the world. Learn your home boiler's whistle — each has a voice, and from any point you will hear four at once, which means you can navigate by ear through the cloud. Record the whistles. Practice picking yours out of a mix. But accept that the arrangement of boilers has no globally consistent solution, and that a boiler seen from two directions may not agree with itself.
- **Trail a line.** A kilometers-long spool of light cord, paid out from camp, is crude and heavy and it works. It is also instructive: reeling in a four-kilometer walk and recovering eleven kilometers of cord is the moment the plateau stops being a curiosity and becomes a place you are act
