# Going Down Into a Vault: A Traveller's Preparation

## 0. The decision, first, because it is the only irreversible part

Everything else on this list is fabric and batteries. This one isn't.

People who spend real time in a vault come out with their hearing intact and their solitude ruined. That phrase gets repeated so often it has gone soft, so let me harden it again: for the rest of your life, in empty rooms, at three in the morning, in the middle of a salt flat with no one within forty kilometres, you will have the sense that the quiet is *occupied*. Not haunted. Occupied — the way a held breath is occupied. Some travellers find this a gift. A large minority find it a slow, low-grade, incurable homesickness for a place they were only ever a contaminant in.

Decide in advance whether you are willing to trade your silence for two hours of noise. Do not decide at the seam mouth with the hum already coming up through the moss, because by then you are not deciding, you are being drawn.

If you are going anyway: the rest follows.

---

## 1. Arrival, season, and the walk in

Arrive at least four days early, and arrive *cold*. Gate in somewhere with a roof, sleep in an unheated shelter, and let your body find the local baseline. You want your hands to have re-learned their winter blood habits before you ask anything of them.

**Season.** Late summer to early autumn is the only sane window. In deep winter the polygons are under snow and the seams are plugged with a drift you would have to dig, and digging is forbidden (§9). In high spring the melt runs and the seep-pools rise and the vault floor can be ankle-deep and *working*, and you will do damage just by standing. Late August: the frost-heave pattern is legible, the ground is walkable if unpleasant, the water table has dropped, and the vaults are at their loudest.

**Surface conditions.** Peat bog. Tussock. Water under everything. You will be wet to the knee within an hour of leaving camp and you should plan clothing around that as a certainty rather than a risk. Expect insects in biblical quantity — and here is your first hard prohibition, so learn it now: **no repellent of any kind.** No DEET, no picaridin, no citronella, no treated fabric. You will be carrying those compounds into a chamber whose entire inner skin is a two-millimetre liverwort running on a bespoke antifreeze chemistry, and nobody knows what a smear of solvent does to it, and nobody wants to be the one who finds out. Head net, fine-mesh hood, long cuffs, and patience.

**Finding a vault.** By eye: ten paces where the polygons fail to close, where the peat sags into a shoulder-wide seam. By ear: lie down, ear to moss. By instrument: a geophone or a contact microphone and headphones, walking a slow grid. The watershed holds at least sixty of them and none of them are in tune with each other, which is your best tool — on a still night, standing between two, you can hear the beat frequency rise and fall as you walk, and the beats are slowest where you are equidistant. Triangulate on the *slowness*.

**Choose a known vault.** There is a survey. There is a register. There are locals — herders, botanists, people with a longer claim to this ground than your curiosity — and there is a permitting etiquette, and you should go and sit in someone's kitchen and ask, badly, in their language, before you go anywhere near a seam. The right vault to enter is one that has been entered before, is monitored, has a logged baseline, and has an established route down. An unentered seam is a sealed cast at year 600 of a 400,000-year job. Leave it sealed. You are not important enough to be the first thing that ever went in there.

## 2. Your body

**Contraindications, taken seriously:** any cardiac arrhythmia; any history of syncope; middle-ear or sinus disease; active respiratory infection of any kind; recent concussion; uncontrolled anxiety or panic disorder; pregnancy; anything that makes you cough. Forty hertz at chest-thumping amplitude, in fog, in the dark, at exactly zero, is a combination that has put steady people on the floor.

**Do not go in with an open wound.** Not a graze, not a cracked cuticle, not a cold sore. Tape and seal everything. You are a warm wet bag of nutrient chemistry walking into a mould, and a bleeding knuckle is a donation nobody asked for.

**Fitness that matters:** not endurance. Grip strength, and the ability to do a 3.5-metre hand-over-hand ascent on flexing, slick, organic ribs when your fingers have been at zero and saturated for two hours and have gone to wood. Practise this. Actually practise it — hang from cold wet rungs in a cold shed until you know the number of minutes you have before your hands stop being yours. Whatever that number is, your vault clock is half of it.

**Fast lightly, hydrate heavily, and empty yourself completely before descending.** You will not be urinating down there. If you do, you will be doing it into a bottle and carrying it out, and you will feel appropriately obscene about it.

## 3. The clothing problem, which is the whole problem

Exactly zero, one hundred percent saturated, fog you can hold. This is the single most efficient environment for killing a human being that does not involve moving water. It is worse than minus thirty in dry air, and it is worse in a way that people who know cold routinely underestimate, because nothing in it *feels* dangerous. Nothing bites. Your face doesn't hurt. You just quietly lose the argument.

The rule: **you cannot dry anything in there, so nothing may get wet.**

- **Next to skin:** a vapour-barrier layer. Non-breathable, taped, worn against bare skin or over the thinnest liner. It feels revolting and it is non-negotiable. Its job is to keep *your own sweat* out of your insulation, because your insulation has no other chance of staying dry.
- **Insulation:** wool, or a high-loft synthetic that tolerates wetting. **No down.** Down in that chamber is a sponge with delusions.
- **Shell:** non-breathable. Membranes do not work at 100% relative humidity; they have nowhere to send anything. Take a hard shell with pit zips you open deliberately to dump heat, and close deliberately when you stop moving.
- **Outermost, if you can get it:** a smooth, low-emissivity overlayer. This is partly for you and mostly for the vault. You are a hundred-watt radiant heater walking into a chamber that an entire organism is spending its metabolism to hold at a knife-edge. A reflective outer cuts your radiative coupling to the tissue and reduces the thaw halo you drag around behind you.
- **Fabric discipline:** no fleece, no pile, no anything that sheds. Tightly woven, taped, lint-free. Before you descend, roll your entire suit with a cleanroom tack roller. Yes, really. You are entering a casting.
- **Boots:** flat, wide, soft-soled. Felt, or hide, or smooth rubber overboots. **No lugs.** A lugged sole in the root-mat gouges a decade of calcite per step and leaves a scar in the lattice that will still be legible when the vault is solid stone.
- **Gloves:** three pairs, all wool, all carried dry in sealed bags, and change them. Dexterity is the resource you will run out of first.

**Nothing scented. Nothing salted. Nothing glycol.** This is the deepest chemical prohibition of the place. Freezing-point depressants are the one true poison here — a spilled electrolyte drink, a de-icer, an alcohol hand gel, a leaking battery, a splash of stove fuel. Any of them, on that floor, punches a hole in the triple point and makes a permanent dead spot in a lattice that is being assembled one cell at a time. Leave every such item on the surface, in a sealed box, and check your pockets twice.

**And no chemical hand warmers.** Ever. An exothermic packet in a vault is an act of vandalism with a heat signature.

## 4. Gear

- **Rope and ladder, rigged from the surface.** You anchor *outside*, on the tundra, with pickets or a spread-load deadman. You do not put anything into the columns. You do not put anything into the wall sheets. You do not cut, widen, trim or "clear" the seam with a blade of any kind — bring no blade you could be tempted with.
- **A shore line.** A light, knotted guideline from the seam foot into the chamber, with directional knots you can read with a glove on: one knot, out; two knots, in. In that fog, at 0.9 lumen, with no directional sound and no horizon, the exit is *invisible from four metres away*. People have circled for twenty minutes in a room the size of a chapel. Run the line, clip to the line, come back on the line.
- **Light: dim, red, hooded, downward.** A bright lamp does three things, all bad — it makes a solid white cone of backscatter and blinds you, it burns the twenty minutes of dark adaptation you paid for, and it washes out the floor entirely. Take a lamp with a genuine sub-lumen bottom end. Sit at the seam foot for twenty minutes with it off. Then the floor comes up at you like a city.
- **Hearing protection with flat attenuation.** Musician's filtered plugs, not foam. You want the level down and the *spectrum intact*, because the only warning system in the vault is your ability to hear the difference between the bass note and the bass note with a burr in it. And understand the limit: plugs do nothing about forty hertz. That comes in through your sternum, your spine, the soles of your feet, the fluid in your eyes. You will feel it long after you stop hearing it.
- **A vibrating timer, worn on the wrist under the cuff.** Audible alarms are useless in there. Set three: half-time, call-time, hard-out. Obey the third one even mid-sentence, even mid-thought, especially mid-awe.
- **A gas meter.** O₂, CO₂, CH₄, H₂S. It is a sealed-ish void under a peat bog, with a vast respiring biomass and anoxic seep water, and its ventilation is a shoulder-wide slot. I have never heard of a vault going bad. That is not the same as knowing one can't. Read it at the seam foot, read it at the floor, read it again before you sit down.
- **Thermometers, at least two, from different makers.** You will believe both of them are broken. That is the correct first reaction and it is part of the experience: floor zero, roof zero, mid-air zero, to more decimal places than instrument noise should allow. Bring the second thermometer so that you can stop arguing with the first one and get on with looking.
- **Optics and electronics, sealed and tempered.** Here is the trap: the surface is honestly cold and the vault is warm by comparison and wet beyond comparison. Anything you carry down at minus fifteen will armour itself in rime in ninety seconds and stay useless. Hang your gear in the seam mouth for ten to fifteen minutes and let it come up to near zero before it goes in. Then, on the way out, bag everything airtight *before* you ascend, and don't open the bags until they've re-equilibrated on the surface.
- **Recording kit, if you're serious:** a geophone or contact mic for the bass, and a decent condenser under a windshield for the chimes. Accept in advance that the recording will be worthless as a souvenir. It will reproduce the sound and none of the load. Everyone who plays it back to friends at home watches their face fail to change and feels something close to grief.
- **Food:** eat on the surface. If you must eat down there, something you can take with a gloved hand and no packaging that can escape you.
- **A slate and grease pencil,** and a set of hand signals agreed on the surface. See below.

## 5. Down

Feet first, on the rope, facing the tissue. The ribs flex and hold — they will take your weight and they will *give* first, which is deeply unpleasant the first time and fine afterwards. Don't grab a rib and haul; press and step. Three and a half metres, slick, cold, fibrous, with the sound rising up around you "like water filling a boot." That image is accurate and you should have it in mind beforehand, because the moment when the chord closes over your head is the moment people panic. Know it's coming. Breathe through it. It passes in about fifteen seconds.

Then: sit down at the foot of the rope, on your pad, and do nothing for twenty minutes.

This is the single most important instruction in this document. Everything worth seeing in that room is below the threshold of an unadapted eye and above the threshold of an unadapted nerve. Twenty minutes of sitting converts a deafening white void into a chapel with a hundred thousand blue sparks in the floor and a fog you can read depth in. Travellers who skip this see a wet dark room, take photographs of nothing, and leave.

## 6. Conduct inside

**You will not be able to talk.** Not "it'll be hard to talk" — you will shout and your partner will hear a tone. Every plan must be made on the surface and every deviation communicated by touch, light, or slate. Agree beforehand: an abort signal (I use two hard squeezes on the upper arm, repeated), a "come here" signal, a "stop moving" signal, and a "I'm fine, leave me" signal, because there will be a moment when someone stands still for a long time and their partner needs to know whether it's rapture or hypothermia.

**Assume hypothermia is invisible.** Your usual cues are gone: you will be shivering from the start, and your judgement is already being degraded by the cold, and further degraded by the noise, and catastrophically degraded by how beautiful it is. Do not trust your assessment of how you're doing. Trust the wrist timer. Two hours is a long visit. Ninety minutes is a sensible one. Anything past three hours should be a planned, provisioned, partnered expedition with a surface watch, not a decision made in the fog.

**Where to put your feet.** The floor is the artifact. It is the thing being made. Every step is a millimetre-per-decade seam crushed. Stay on the route the survey has marked, or on a load-spreading mat you carry in, and if you must cross open floor, cross once, on the same line, going and coming.

**The masons.** Glass-bodied, ten centimetres, appallingly slow, stopping for an hour at a time with their undersides pressed to a spot. They are placing seed crystals. They have been told where to go. **Do not touch one. Do not move one, even out of your way. Do not stand between one and wherever it is headed,** and do not, under any circumstances, pick one up "for a photograph." You would be removing a tool from a jig in the middle of an operation that has been running for six hundred years, and you would never know what you broke.

**Touching the column.** You will want to. Do it once. Bare hand, clean hand — washed with nothing, rinsed in stream water, no soap residue, no sunscreen, no anything. Press it flat for three seconds. Feel the surface dissolving and recrystallising against your palm, the *busy*-ness of it, which is not like cold and not like anything else. Then take it away, watch the negative print fill in over the next minute, and then put your glove back on and don't do it again. One print per visitor. That's the convention and it's a good one.

**Take nothing.** Not a chip of calcite, not a crustacean, not a pinch of the felt, not a mason's shed shell. The lattice is aligned to a standing field; a fragment removed from the field is just a rock and a hole. And the hole stays.

**Leave nothing.** Not a fibre, not a wrapper, not a drop, not a cough. You cannot help this place. The only variable you control is how little you cost it.

## 7. Up

Start your ascent with hands you can still close. This is why the hard-out alarm exists. The climb is 3.5 metres of flexing organic rib in a shoulder-wide slot, and it is not difficult, and it kills people who left it fifteen minutes too long. Have a partner on the surface with the rope, and have the mechanical advantage already rigged, so that "I can't grip any more" is a recoverable situation and not a fatal one.

At the top: the honest cold, the dry air, and your breath standing in front of you in a plume. That plume is the first vapour you have made in hours that nobody used, and the feeling that accompanies it is one of the strangest of the whole trip — a mixture of relief and bereavement, like being put down.

---

## 8. Afterwards

- **Get an audiogram before you travel and another a week after.** Impulsive ceramic cracks at close range do real damage and you will not notice it happening.
- **Expect a residual hum.** Most people carry a 40 Hz ghost in the chest for two or three days. If it persists past a fortnight, see someone.
- **Do not sleep alone the first night.** This is not superstition; it is the standard advice from everyone who runs vault work, and it is given because the first night alone after a vault is when the solitude damage announces itself, and it is much gentler with a person breathing in the room.
- **Do not visit two vaults in one day**, and preferably not in one week. The out-of-tune thing does something to people. There are travellers in that watershed who came for an afternoon four years ago and are still walking the grid with a geophone, listening for beats, and cannot really tell you why.
- **Decontaminate between vaults.** Boots, mats, ropes, gloves, everything. Sixty chambers, sixty different microfaunas, none of them in tune. You are the only vector that can cross between them.

---

## 9. The short list, to be memorised, in this order

1. Enter only a registered, previously opened seam. Widen nothing. Carry no blade.
2. No repellent, no scent, no salt, no glycol, no alcohol, no fuel, no heat packs.
3. Anchor on the surface. Never on the columns.
4. Run the shore line. Clip to it. Return on it.
5. Dim light, red, hooded, and twenty minutes of sitting still before you look at anything.
6. Flat soles, spread load, one line across open floor.
7. Do not touch the masons.
8. One palm print per visitor.
9. Take nothing. Leave nothing. Not even breath you could have held.
10. Go up while your hands still close.

---

## 10. The last thing

Bring one more item that isn't on any list: a fixed, pre-decided, written-down reason for why you are going in.

You will need it at about the eighty-minute mark, standing in the fog with your ears ringing, watching the floor invent stone ten thousand times a second, hearing an instruction you can almost understand issued to something that is almost a tool — when it occurs to you that you are the only object in the chamber that has no job.

That thought is the actual hazard of the place. It is not cold or noise or the climb. It is standing inside a four-hundred-thousand-year act of patience, as a warm loud temporary thing with a lamp, and having to find your own answer to the question of what you are for. Have something written on the slate. Read it. Then clip to the line and go up.
