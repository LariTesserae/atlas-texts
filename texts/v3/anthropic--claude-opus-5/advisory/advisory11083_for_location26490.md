# Preparing for the Basin

## First, adjust what you think you are visiting

You are not visiting a village. You are visiting a metabolism that has people in it, and you will be metabolized at the same rate as everything else — a third of you per year, more or less, like the rot tax on baskets.

Every preparation error travelers make in the Basin comes from one wrong assumption: that they are going to a *place*, which will be there when they arrive and there when they leave, and that they can bring objects into it. In the Basin there are no places, only maintained openings, and there are no objects, only things partway through becoming peat. Get this into your body before you go and half the rest is easy.

The practical translation: **plan for a visit that must be continuously re-earned, hour by hour, with your hands.** If you can't work, you aren't a guest. You're weather.

---

## Timing

Three calendars run here and you need all three.

**The browser calendar.** Ask, at the lower settlement where the trail begins, how long since the last band came through. If it's recent, go — the walls are thin, the peat is churned, the mood is good, the fiber-cane is coming up, and the town is in its rebuilding sociability, which is the best time to be a stranger, because everyone is already doing something with their hands and will let you help. If the count says a visitation is due, understand what you are volunteering for: a day and a night in the crowns, in cloud, with no fire, holding a platform edge while an ox-shouldered animal eats the room you slept in. People do survive this routinely. Visitors have died of falling, not of animals.

**The cloud clock.** Two turnings a day: the cloud comes up the western slope at roughly the same hour, and draws off in the late afternoon. There is no sun to speak of and the light is shadowless, so you will have no idea what time it is by any instinct you own. Learn the two turnings as your only hinges. Arrive at the rim *before* the morning cloud, never in it — the last stretch of trail is on the lip and people have walked off it.

**Your own body's clock, which will be destroyed.** Expect four to six nights of near-total sleeplessness before the frog-hiss converts, in your hearing, from noise into a kind of silence. It does convert. Everyone reports the same thing: on about the fifth night you stop hearing it and start hearing *through* it, and then you sleep like a stone. Plan your first useful day for day seven. Do not schedule anything requiring judgment in the first week. The Basin's fatal accidents involving strangers cluster in days two through five.

## The body

**Altitude.** You are at permanent cloud level. Come up slowly, sleep two or three nights at intermediate height on the way, and accept that you will be short of breath doing things that look trivial — twisting fiber, climbing eight feet of ladder. Local people are barrel-chested and unimpressed by this.

**Cold-wet, not cold.** The Basin is not freezing (the Eye never freezes) but it is saturated, and saturated air at that temperature takes heat out of you relentlessly and without drama. You will not shiver dramatically; you will simply get stupid. Learn what your own early hypothermia feels like — most people's tell is a small stubbornness, an unwillingness to change plan. Bring a companion who is authorized to overrule you.

**Immersion.** This is the real enemy. Your feet will be wet for the entire visit. Not damp — wet, continuously, for weeks. Macerated skin, split heels, softened splitting nails, fungus in every fold, and then a small cut becomes a large infection in three days. Preparation begins six months out:

- **Toughen your feet.** Barefoot walking on wet ground, daily, until your soles are leather and your nails are short and hard. Do not arrive with office feet.
- **Do not bring boots.** Boots fill, boots hold the water against you, boots suck off in peat and are lost, and a lost boot in the Basin is not replaceable. Bring thin, drainable, soft-soled wraps you can re-tie a hundred times, and spares of the lacing.
- **Grease is medicine.** Bring rendered fat or lanolin in a sealed gourd and oil your feet, groin, armpits, and the backs of your knees every single evening at the hearth. This is also what the locals do; it will be one of the few things you do correctly on arrival.
- **Antibiotics, in wax, and the discipline to use them early.** Also a small chert-sharp blade discipline: any cut gets washed in clean squeezed moss-water and greased *immediately*, not later. There is no later here; later is a fever.

**Hands.** Your hands will be ruined and then remade. Cordage-rolling on the thigh, all day, every day, takes the skin off palms and the pads of the fingers. Start twisting fiber at home, three months out, an hour a day, until your hands are hard and you can do it without watching. This single skill is worth more than anything else you can bring — see below.

**Ears.** Bring soft wax earplugs and a rule: **never both ears, never when you are the only one awake.** If the frogs stop and it isn't a cloud-hour, something large is in the Basin, and that information arrives only by sound. Deafening yourself for a night's sleep is a reasonable trade only if someone else is listening for you.

**Vertigo.** One insect register runs so continuously that people stop perceiving it. On the rare nights it lapses, everyone gets a wave of vertigo. Locals sit down. You will get it worse, and you may get it while standing on a tilted cane platform four metres up. Rehearse the response until it's reflex: *drop, kneel, hold the nearest upright, wait.*

**Frogs, bats, guano.** Do not handle the small frogs and then touch your eyes or mouth. The roof gardens are fertilized by thousands of bat tenants; the dust of dried guano, disturbed during roof work, is a lung problem. If you help with roofing, wet the surface first and breathe through wet cloth. Everyone will think this is funny. Do it anyway.

**Teeth.** Fix everything beforehand. There is no dentistry, the diet is fibrous and abrasive, and toothache in the Basin is three days' descent on one steep trail.

## The kit: run a rot audit

Lay out everything you intend to bring and sort it into four piles.

**1. Things that will rot.** Cloth, cord, leather, paper, food, wood, bandage, anything organic. Assume total loss on a timescale of weeks. Bring the minimum, and bring it in the local storage grammar: **resin-sealed gourds and smoke-blackened baskets.** Nothing else works. A sealed gourd of dry tinder is the single most valuable thing in your pack; it is your emergency, and it is also the most beautiful gift you can hand to a household, which brings us to —

**2. Things that will not rot, which is worse.** Steel, glass, hard plastic, synthetic fabric. Understand what you are carrying: in a world where a house is a thing being continuously kept and a person's status is written in the smoke-color of their rafters, an object that requires no keeping is not impressive. It's *outside the order*. A synthetic coat that never rots, never needs mending, never asks anything of anyone is, in the Basin's terms, faintly obscene — a thing that refuses to participate. A steel knife that stays sharp will become a legend, and legends distort. And it will corrode anyway, just slowly enough to be uncanny.

Recommendation: bring what you truly need, keep it in the bottom of a basket, use it privately, and let the visible layer of your equipment be organic, mendable, and constantly in need of your attention. Be seen repairing things. Repair *is* the local form of good manners.

**3. Things that will simply not work.** Firearms and any powder. Anything electronic, which in that humidity is a one-week item no matter how it's sealed — silica gel is a finite resource and there is nowhere to dry it out. Notebooks: pulp. If you must record, use a wax tablet in a gourd, or a smoked slat and a charcoal point, or — best — train your memory before you go, because you will want to write at night and at night your hands will be occupied with fiber.

**4. Things to bring specifically as gifts.** Salt. Fat. Copper needles, an awl, a small rasp — the goods the trail already carries, in the quantities the trail already carries them, so you are not detonating an economy. Hard resin. Dry tinder. Do *not* bring alcohol; do not bring sugar; do not bring stone tools of exotic material — there is almost no stone in the Basin and a beautiful stone blade will make somebody permanent in a town organized around nobody being permanent.

**Weight.** Pack light for a reason that has nothing to do with your back. The peat transmits mass. When you step onto the floor of the Basin, the flex travels outward and a hundred paces away someone feels you arrive. A heavy stranger arriving all at once, hard, is *rude before he is visible*. Split your load. Make two trips from the rim. Come in light and slow, and let the ground announce you gently.

## Skills to acquire before you go

Six months, honestly, is not too much.

**Cordage.** Sixty hours minimum, until you can roll fiber on your thigh while walking, talking, and eating, without looking. This is not a quaint craft. It is the currency of participation. Forty thousand paces of string hold that town together, all of it rotting, all of it being replaced, and a guest with busy hands is a guest, full stop. You can be a foreigner, a fool, and half-mute, and if you produce good cord all day you will be fed and housed and eventually argued with, which is the sign of acceptance.

**The quaking-bog gait.** Practice on a floating dock, a trampoline, a sprung gymnasium floor: learn to move without bouncing, to place the whole foot, to read the flex ahead of you, to test with a staff, and to fall *forward and flat* rather than punching a leg down to the knee. Learn how to extract a trapped leg slowly (fast pulls lose footwear and tear knees).

**Ladder and platform work with wet hands, in cloud, carrying something.** All of it. Repeatedly. This is where visitors die.

**Pruning.** Learn to use a bone chisel and a split-cane knife that is sharp for one afternoon and then discarded. Learn to crop an inner hedge-face without opening it to the light. Get this wrong and you don't damage a wall; you damage *a room*, and rooms take eighty years.

**Sitting.** Genuinely. There are three polished boulder-crowns to sit on and generations have sat there. Learn to sit still for two hours in wet cold with your hands working and your mouth mostly shut. It is the local high skill.

## Etiquette, law, and the things that will get you thrown out

**Never ask who owns something.** It's a nonsense question and marks you instantly. Ask: *who keeps this open?* Ownership here is maintenance. If you want the use of a platform, a path, a corner of a room, you ask to be given a keeping — a length of path to recut, an inner wall-face to crop daily. Accept it. Do it every day without being reminded. On the day you forget, the Basin will have taken it back, and no one will be angry, because that is simply how the place works, and they will note that you are not a keeper.

**Never use the word "finished."** There isn't one. If your interpreter offers a translation, distrust it. The closest term means *currently being kept*, and it is not a modest phrasing of "done" — it is the actual ontology. Speak of the house as being kept. Speak of yourself the same way.

**The hearth.** Clay bowl on posts at head height, fed with slow smouldering root-blocks and resin chips, never allowed out, tended constantly. It is the hardest thing in the Basin and it is a household's continuity. Do not touch another family's hearth uninvited. Do not add anything to it "helpfully." Do not — and travelers have done this — try to build a bigger, better, hotter fire to dry your gear. You will fail, waste irreplaceable fuel, and you will have insulted the rafters.

**Water.** Drink what they drink: squeezed moss-water, bromeliad water, tea-colored peat water. Boiling is a luxury because fuel is the scarce thing; asking for boiled water is asking for a week of someone's firewood. Bring chemical purification or a small unobtrusive filter, and use it quietly.

**The Eye.** The cold tannic pool at the center is where the dead go, wrapped and weighted. It is not sacred exactly; it is simply *where people go*. Do not swim in it, wash in it, drink from it, sample it, or float anything on it. Understand also, before you set out, the hard fact: it gives nothing back. If you die in the Basin you go into the Eye and no one from your side of the multiverse will ever retrieve you. Settle that with whoever needs to know. Say it out loud to yourself at the rim.

**Silence means look up.** Memorize it as a body reflex, not a piece of trivia.

**The rim is the boundary.** You may take a room from the plants; you may not take a hill from the water. Don't survey, don't dig drainage, don't propose improvements involving gradient. The one trail goes down the western lip to a warmer country; take it only with a guide, only in clear air, and only downhill in the morning.

**Latrines are wrong.** The peat won't hold a ditch. Ask where. Do not innovate.

## What your first week will actually feel like

Pressed. The sound is a hand on the sternum and it does not lift. The light is generous, even, and shadowless, so you can work anywhere and you cannot judge a step, and you will misjudge steps. Everything you own will be wet by the second hour and will stay wet. You will be cold in a way that has no edge to it. You will not be able to tell what time it is and will feel a low continuous embarrassment about this. You will be woken all night by frogs and unable to sleep, and then you will notice that the *town's* noise has stopped — the snick-snick-snick of pruning ends at dark and starts at first light, and it is that rhythm, not the light, that will eventually reset you.

Around day seven you sleep. Around day ten your hands stop hurting. Around day fourteen you will catch yourself cropping an inner wall-face without having decided to, and someone will hand you a bowl without looking at you, and that is the whole of your admission.
