# Going up to the Shoulder: a traveller's preparation

## First, understand what kind of visit this is

You are not going to a mountain. You are going to a document that is being written, and you will be standing on the page.

This matters for preparation because almost every instinct you bring from ordinary mountaineering will be half-right in a way that gets you hurt. It is at altitude, but it is hot. It is volcanic in shape, but nothing there is molten. It is wet, but there is no potable water. It is silent, but not peaceful. There is nothing on the Shoulder that will attack you. There is a great deal on the Shoulder that will *keep* you, and the difference is the whole of your training.

The single fact to carry in your chest for the entire visit: there is a boot up there, rendered in grey stone with the stitching legible, and nobody talks much about the man.

---

## The approach, and the body

**Acclimatize below.** Twenty-eight hundred metres will not kill a healthy adult, but 2,800 m *plus* saturated air at blood heat plus no evaporative cooling plus a floor that radiates warmth into your boot soles will absolutely put you on your knees. Spend four nights in the forest below, minimum, climbing high and sleeping low on the last two. If you carry acetazolamide, start it before you need it. Understand that on the Shoulder you will not be able to distinguish altitude headache from heat headache from the stoup-tone headache, so treat all three at once: drink, get in the fig's shade, lie in a hammock, and stop working.

**Train for heat, not cold.** The mistake most travellers make is packing for a mountain. You want the fitness of someone who works in a laundry. Do your conditioning in the hottest, wettest room you can find, wearing what you intend to wear, carrying what you intend to carry. Your sweat will not evaporate between two and five in the afternoon. Your body's only cooling channel is closed for two hours a day, every day, on schedule. If you have never overheated while soaking wet, you do not yet know how fast it happens.

**Get your teeth and feet seen to before you go.** There is no dentist and there is no drying out. A hot spot on your heel on day one is a macerated open wound on day four and a systemic infection on day six.

## Feet, hands, knees: the stone

Every edge on the Shoulder is an edge. Nineteen years has given weather nothing to work with, so you are walking on a floor of freshly-broken plates with corners that will open a boot sole like a letter.

- **Two pairs of boots**, stiff-soled, and neither of them boots you love. Vibram-type hard rubber, not soft approach-shoe compound — soft rubber goes gummy on warm stone and shreds. Bring a resoling kit or at minimum shoe-goo and duct tape. Expect to lose two millimetres of sole a day.
- **Gloves.** Leather palms for handling stone, thin nitrile underneath because you will be handling stone that is wet with dissolved silica for hours. Bring six pairs of the nitriles.
- **Knee pads.** You will spend a lot of time kneeling at plate edges looking at laminations arriving in the wrong order, and your kneecaps are not made for young rock.
- **Bandage kit weighted heavily toward closure.** A cut from new Shoulder stone is clean, deep, and does not hurt for the first ten seconds. It bleeds into a joint that drains without a sound, and then it is inside the mountain and you cannot get it back. Superglue, steri-strips, chlorhexidine, a broad-spectrum oral antibiotic, and the discipline to irrigate with water you carried up rather than water that came up warm from below.

Blood into the joints is, so far as anyone knows, harmless. It is also the one contribution you cannot pack out, and you should be clear with yourself, in advance, about how you feel about that.

## Water: abundant, and none of it yours

The terrace weeps everywhere and you cannot drink any of it comfortably. The pools are 39–44 °C and milky with dissolved silica. Silica does not boil out, does not filter out with anything you can carry, scales the inside of a hollow-fibre filter dead in about a day and a half, and leaves a fine gritty film on your teeth and inside your bladder hose that no amount of rinsing shifts.

Practical answer, in order of preference:

1. **Carry water up.** Six litres a day per person for a two-day visit; it is unglamorous and it is correct.
2. **Harvest dew and cloud.** This is what the leaves in the vent-gardens are shaped for and it is a small courtesy to copy them rather than compete with them. String a length of shade-cloth or a clean tarp between the fig and a stoup at dusk, angled to a collector. You will get one to three litres overnight in the dry season, and it is the cleanest water on the mountain: no silica, faintly warm, tasting of nothing.
3. **Boil pool water only for washing and cooking**, and accept the scale.

Electrolytes are not optional. You will lose salt at a rate that has no relationship to your thirst, because you never *feel* dry — you feel drowned. Weigh yourself before and after if you can. Drink to a schedule, not to appetite. Two litres before the cloud comes up; sip through it; a litre in the steaming hour after.

**Do not get into the Sump.** It is thigh-deep, it is 44 °C at dawn when you most want it, it is floored in a soft white gel, and it is the most tempting object in the place. Three reasons: it is hot enough to raise your core temperature dangerously in ten minutes at that altitude; there are two or three eyeless crabs in it and they are the entire known population of their species; and the gel takes a footprint and keeps it. You have come to a place that demonstrably keeps impressions, and there is a boot four paces away. Wash in a bucket, on the shelter's stacked plates, and pour out downslope of everything living.

## The rule, and its corollaries

The rule is: **do not sleep on the ground.**

The corollaries are where the real work is, because the rule as stated is too narrow and lulls people. What the place seems to do is take up things that hold still against it. The tarp on the shelter was not asleep; it was merely *there*, along one edge, for eleven years, and now it continues into the stone, fibres and mineral interleaved for a centimetre and a half.

Therefore, contact discipline:

- **Nothing rests.** Rucksack, camera bag, stove, stool, water cans: everything either hangs or gets moved. Adopt a habit and make it mechanical — every time you pick something up, put it down somewhere else. Not a ritual. A habit, so you do it while thinking about other things.
- **Hang what you can.** The fig and the two tolerated pegs are the anchor points. Use wide webbing straps on the fig, not cord, and not many: it is three metres tall, sixty to eighty years old, being maintained at fixed altitude for reasons nobody understands, and it is holding up your bed. Treat it the way you'd treat the only tree in the world.
- **Do not add a third peg.** People have wanted to. Do not be the traveller who drove an anchor into the throat-side plate to see what would happen. The mountain has *so far* tolerated two.
- **Sleep in a hammock, off the plates entirely, with a bug net you don't need** — you want it because it stops you rolling a hand out to trail on the ground at four in the morning. That is the actual failure mode. Not camping on the floor; falling asleep with a forearm dangling.
- **Boots come into the hammock with you**, or hang from the ridgeline. This is not superstition. This is the size 44 left boot.

## The clock

The Shoulder runs on a timetable and you should surrender to it rather than fight it.

**Dawn to about 1000.** The best hours. Cool by local standards, air clear, light raking across the plates so that new laminations show. UV at this altitude in this latitude is savage and there is exactly one piece of shade on the mountain, three metres tall, and in the dry season the swifts have opinions about it. Cover with cloth, not chemicals — long light woven sleeves, a hood, a brimmed hat. Sunscreen washes off you into a place that keeps records; wear as little of it as you can manage.

**1000 to 1330.** Work. Heat building. Move slowly; there is nothing on a terrace ninety seconds across that requires hurrying.

**1400 to 1700. The cloud.** It comes up from below, which no amount of forewarning prepares you for, and then you are inside a hot pearly room twenty metres wide with water running off every surface including your eyebrows. Plan to do nothing. Genuinely: schedule this as rest. Electronics into dry bags before it arrives, not during. Lenses fog and stay fogged — put a camera in a sealed bag with desiccant at 1345 and do not open it until 1730 or you will grow a fungus on the inside of the glass. Sit in the hammock, drink, and listen to the place drip. This is also the most dangerous time to walk: visibility twenty metres, no wind noise, no echo to orient by, and the seaward lip is the newest stone on the terrace, unsupported, still cantilevering outward at seven metres a year. **Do not go near the growing edge in cloud.** Do not go near it much at all. Nobody has established what holds it up.

**1700 to dark.** The famous hour. The cloud goes over the summit, the terrace exhales in visible ribbons, the stone under you is at blood heat, and the light is long and low. Everyone who has stood on the eastern point in this hour reports the same thing, which is that it is very hard to keep hold of the idea that nothing is watching. Bring your notebook. You will want to write things down and you will not, later, be able to reconstruct them.

**Night.** Warm, clammy, silent, heavy dew. You will not need down. You will need one absolutely dry set of sleeping clothes in a sealed bag, treated as sacred, worn only in the hammock, and the discipline never to put them on while wet. This one item will do more for your morale than any other thing you carry.

## Sound, and what it does to you

Shout and it does not come back. New stone is dead; it neither rings nor echoes. There is no wind. The total acoustic budget of the place is dripping, one hissing vent, the tick of a plate contracting, the wet slap of a fern letting go of its dew, and underneath everything the stoup-tone, at the very bottom edge of hearing.

Three things to know in advance:

1. **The dread is infrasound.** Low-frequency pressure at the threshold of perception produces, in ordinary human beings, nausea, chest pressure, a persistent sense of presence, and the feeling that someone has just left the room. Knowing this does not stop it. Knowing it does stop you from making decisions based on it. Write "this is the stoups" on the inside of your wrist if you have to.
2. **Earplugs do not help and make it worse**, because they remove the drips and leave the tone.
3. **Do not shout for the novelty of it.** Not because anything will answer. Because the place has, in nineteen years, been almost perfectly quiet, and you would be adding to a record that keeps things. For the same reason: leave the drone below. A drone would be the loudest event in the mountain's history, and it would be your name on it.

The frog is heard twice a night, at most, never twice from the same direction. Do not go looking for it. Everyone goes looking for it. Nobody has ever found it, and the searching is done at night, on bare wet plates, near an unsupported seaward edge.

## Manners

The Shoulder has no authority and no shrine, and a set of conventions has grown up anyway, which you should observe because the people below observe them and because they are, on inspection, all sensible.

- **Do not take stone.** Not a chip, not a flake, and above all not a fossil.
