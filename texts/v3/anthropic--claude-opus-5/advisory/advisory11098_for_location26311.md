# Preparing for the Sump

## 0. Before you decide

Most difficult worlds punish you for what you fail to bring. The Sump punishes you for what you fail to be. Its physical hazards are severe but ordinary — cold, wet, electricity, noise. Its real hazards are ontological, and they operate on the traveller rather than the equipment: your ability to count, your certainty that you are one person who came from somewhere, your habit of having thoughts that begin inside you.

So the preparation has two halves, and the second half is the one people skimp.

**Do not go if:** you have a pacemaker or implanted defibrillator, any conduction-system arrhythmia, or a history of syncope. If you are pregnant — this is not a squeamishness about hardship; the basin has a documented problem distinguishing *two* from *one repeated*, and pregnancy is precisely that ambiguity carried in the body. If you have a history of derealisation, depersonalisation, or dissociative episodes. If you are in the first year of a bereavement. If your professional identity rests on arithmetic — pure mathematicians have a distinct and unpleasant presentation there, and it does not always resolve on return.

**Go with:** two other people, or three, or four. Never more than five. Never fewer than two.

## I. The body, in permanent wet cold

Two or three degrees, air at saturation, no wind shelter, no sun, no dry ground, no fire. This is the deadliest combination in the catalogue of survivable climates, and it kills slowly and stupidly.

Bring a drysuit-class outer layer, not waterproofs. Waterproofs assume that water arrives from a direction. Here it arrives from all of them and also condenses inside your clothes out of your own breath. Underneath: closed-cell synthetic insulation that keeps its loft wet. No down. No cotton anywhere on your body, including your underwear, including the sweatband of your hat.

Bring an obscene mass of dry socks — and note that you must think of it as *mass*, not as a number, for reasons that will become clear. Weigh them out at home. Immersion foot is the injury that actually ends Sump expeditions; nothing in the basin will dry anything, ever, and you cannot air a boot in air that is already full.

There is no soil, so there is no digging, no anchoring pegs, no windbreak, no latrine. Nothing tall exists except the iron ribs, and their tops are your only good ground. Plan to sleep on a rib-top, on a thick insulating pad, above the knee-fog and out of the pans. Wear a helmet while you sleep: the ribs work thermally and shed rivet-fragments at speed, and the pops are hundreds a minute across the basin, which means a few a night above your head.

Nothing there rots. There are no decomposers, because there has never been anything organic to decompose. Everything you excrete, shed, spit, or bleed will simply stay, indefinitely, exactly as you left it, in the same shape, in a landscape that repeats itself. Consider what it will be like to walk two hours east and find your own waste again. Pack it out. Pack out hair, nail clippings, blood-soaked dressings, all of it. This is not tidiness. It is the only way to keep your route from being haunted by you.

If you die there, you will not decay. Long-term, you will mineralise into mechanism. Discuss with your party, in advance and out loud, what they are to do with your body, and write it down.

## II. Electricity

Treat the whole basin as a live floor with a fluctuating potential gradient, because that is what it is.

- Insulating boots, thick soles, no steel shanks, no metal eyelets.
- No metal pack frame, no metal trekking poles, no metal-cased instruments.
- Learn the step-voltage stance: feet together, small steps, never a stride across a wet gradient, never one boot in a pan and one on a rib.
- The lethal geometry is *hand on iron, feet in water*. Across the chest. Never steady yourself on a rib while standing in a pan. Practise this until it is a reflex, because the moment you will want to do it is the moment you slip.
- Do not enter trough bottoms. That is where the potential concentrates, where the film grows in ropes, and where the eels live.
- Where the ground is warm enough to dry clothing, it is warm enough to discharge. Choose. Most experienced parties choose wet.

Bring a simple analogue field-potential meter with a long lead and a bright needle, and learn to glance at it the way you glance at a watch. Its readings will sometimes precede the thing they measure. That is not a fault.

## III. Bring everything, because nothing is there

Make a list of what the basin lacks and then supply every item on it. It lacks: fuel, food, dryness, shade, shelter, timber, plant fibre, rot, silence, height, hard shadow, and definite quantity.

**Food.** All of it, from home. Do not eat shovels. Their tissue is an iron-oxidising economy loaded with metals your kidneys will refuse, and beyond that there is the singularity problem: it is not clear that killing one animal there is killing *an* animal. Do not test this with your teeth.

**Water.** The standing water is ferrous, sour, and probably 2°C of dilute inorganic soup. It will not kill you quickly and it will not let you work. Bring a distiller, and power it from the ground — see below — but filter for the film first, always, at every stage.

**Fuel.** There is nothing combustible in forty kilometres in every direction from wherever you stand. Whatever you burn, you carried.

## IV. Power: the basin will run your camp, and that is the problem

You can harvest the ground pulses. Two electrode plates, a wet pan, a rectifier, a buffer: it is not difficult and it is enough to boil water and charge lamps, and it is the single greatest improvement to comfort available there.

Understand what you have built. You have built a voltage source in a world where the primary sense of the largest predator is voltage, and where the base of the food chain grows *toward* potential difference. Your generator is bait, and it is also a farm. Within a day your electrodes will be furred with pearl-grey film. Within two nights something two metres long and blind will have come up the seep-channel to see what is fishing.

Site the harvest well away from where you sleep. Run long leads. Post a watch on it, or accept losing it. Scrub the electrodes on a schedule and scrub yourself on the same one — film will colonise your suit seams, your battery terminals, the warm conductive wet of your ear canals, and the metal in your teeth. Bring a stiff brush and a mild acid wash and use them nightly on your gums. This sounds absurd. It is the most commonly regretted omission.

Respirator with hydrophobic filtration, worn whenever a fog slab is on you. The air is saturated and the film is aerosolised in it.

## V. Navigation: the tether doctrine

There is no mapping here. The basin has a small vocabulary of places and recites each one thousands of times, identical down to the pitting, and the arithmetic that would expose the contradiction is the arithmetic that stops at five. Compasses are useless — the whole bedrock is iron and current. Dead reckoning fails because distances re-tally behind you.

So: **you do not navigate the Sump. You stay attached to your arrival point.**

Bring line. Kilometres of it, on drum reels, bright orange, and pay it out from the arrival point, and never, under any circumstances, leave the line. Length of cord is an analogue magnitude, continuous, and continuous magnitudes appear to survive there where cardinality does not. The cord is therefore the only honest instrument you will carry. It tells you how far, and more importantly it tells you *which* — which of the thousand identical pans is the one on your thread.

Corollary: your arrival/departure point will also be recited. There will be other places that are it, indistinguishable, and only the one on your line is yours. Camp on the arrival point if you can. If you must move camp, move the line, never abandon it.

Do not attempt to mark places. Travellers who scratch a sigil into the rib at a pan and then walk two hours east report one of two results, and both of them cost something. The line does not have this problem, because the line is not a mark on a place; it is a continuous body that includes you.

## VI. Logistics without numbers

Numbers work up to about five. Past five the basin substitutes *many* and will not be argued with. Your rations, your fuel cells, your medical doses, and your days all live past five.

Prepare accordingly, at home, before you go:

- **Pre-count everything and never count again.** Divide all consumables into sealed sets of five. Nest the sets in bags of five. You will not be able to verify the outer count in the field, and you must not try; the attempt produces a specific, exhausting, argumentative distress in a tired party.
- **Convert cardinal problems into analogue ones.** Do not ask how many meals are left; weigh the bag, on a spring balance with an unnumbered dial and a painted band for *enough* and a painted band for *turn back now*. Do not ask how many days; use a spring-wound clock with hands, and a wax candle-scale marked in painted bands, and the length of cord on the drum.
- **Medical doses must be pre-drawn.** Single-use, labelled by colour and shape, never by count. A partner administers; never yourself.
- **Never make a decision that requires a number greater than five.** Design the whole expedition so that no such decision exists. This is most of the planning work, and if you do it properly nothing in the field feels strange.

Do a counting drill at home in a room with more than eleven similar objects, so that you know the *feeling* of the sequence declining to proceed. It is not a blank and it is not forgetting. It is a refusal, and it comes from outside you, and you must have met it once already before you meet it while cold.

## VII. Time and cause

Effects there precede their causes with great reliability. Dew forms ninety seconds before the front. Water-hammer bangs arrive before the surge. The eel's strike-flash precedes the strike.

Two consequences.

**Use it.** The precursors are a free forecast and the local fauna have been exploiting them for aeons. Dew on the ribs: get your shell on, a cold slab is coming. Flash in a trough: something is about to happen there, not something happened. Learn to read the basin's tells and you will be warmer and drier than travellers who insist on ordinary causality.

**Do not automate on it.** Any alarm you build will fire inverted, which means it will fire while you are still safe, which means you will learn to ignore it. Set instruments to *record*, and review the record later, on the rib-top, together, out loud. Judgement stays with humans and stays retrospective.

Do not attempt to exploit the inversion cleverly. Every party has someone who wants to make money out of it. The eels have already stabilised the loop; you will only find the place where the loop closes on you.

## VIII. Noise: carry your own rhythm

The Hum arrives through bone, so ear protection helps only with the surface layers — bring it anyway, for the water-hammer, which will otherwise leave you with tinnitus for a year. Nothing will shield your sternum.

The genuine risk is entrainment. The deep three-hundred-metre reciprocation will set your walking pace, then your breathing, then your heart's time, and it does this whether or not you consent. Some travellers find that they cannot stop pacing to it and cannot sleep off-beat and eventually cannot think in any tempo but the basin's.

**Countermeasure: bring your own rhythm, and bring it in a form that does not need batteries.** A wind-up metronome, set deliberately against the Hum. A song you know entirely, with awkward bars. A drill of physical percussion — clap, clap, stamp — that you and your party perform together on a schedule, badly, laughing at it. It sounds childish. It is the single most effective psychological prophylactic there is, and parties that skip it come out flattened and eerily synchronised with one another.

## IX. Rules of plurality

The basin does not distinguish *many things* from *one thing repeated*. You are a thing.

- Party of three to five. Two is fragile; more than five cannot be verified.
- Physical contact chain, or continuous voice contact, at all times when moving. Never let a member out of sight in fog.
- Nobody walks a route alone that another has walked. Do not create the situation of two identical passages through one recited place.
- Each member speaks their own name aloud at every rest halt. Out loud, in turn, in a fixed order. This feels ridiculous on day one.
- **Differentiation ritual on rejoining and on departure.** Each member states, aloud, one thing that only they could know — a private memory, unshared, unrepeatable. Agree in advance that this is not optional and that failure to produce one is an emergency. Parties that come back wrong come back *smooth*: agreeing too readily, sharing memories they should not share, unable to remember which of them was cold.
