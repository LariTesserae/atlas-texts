# Field Preparation: The Chord Room

## 1. Triage: what will actually injure you, in order

Travelers prepare for the wrong thing here. They prepare for awe. Awe is not the problem.

In descending order of likelihood, the Chord Room will harm you by:

1. **Permanent hearing loss.** Not "ringing for a day." Irreversible, immediate, and — this is the cruelty — painless and unnoticed at the time. You will feel fine. You will feel *exalted*. You will come home a third deaf.
2. **Respirable dust.** Talc-fine fired-clay particulate, perpetually mobilized by an upward wind you are standing in. If there is free silica in that body-clay, a long visit unmasked is a slow sentence.
3. **Desiccation.** Cool air at effectively zero humidity, moving, for hours. You will lose water at a rate your thirst reflex will not report, because the cold suppresses it.
4. **Falls and foot-traps.** A shadowless white glare over a field of black holes. You cannot see the geometry of the ground. Some bores will take a heel.
5. **Low-frequency whole-body exposure.** The drone you feel in the sternum passes through every earplug ever made. Nausea, chest tightness, visual blurring, a formless dread that is not a spiritual insight but a resonance.
6. **Temporal dissolution.** No shadow, no change, no speech, one sound. People lose four hours and swear it was twenty minutes. Others panic at forty minutes convinced they have been there for days.
7. **Contamination of the site.** This will not injure you. It is nonetheless the most serious thing on the list, and I will come back to it.

## 2. Weeks before

**Get a baseline audiogram.** A real one, both ears, with high-frequency extension to 16 kHz if your medicine allows it. You cannot know what the room took from you if you did not measure what you had. Repeat at 48 hours after return and again at one month.

**Train in noise.** Spend time in a genuinely loud industrial space — a turbine hall, a stamping plant, a wind tunnel, the engine room of a working ship — wearing exactly the protection you intend to use. You must learn, *before* it matters, that you cannot speak, and you must exhaust your instinct to try. Untrained people in extreme noise scream themselves into vocal-fold hemorrhage without ever hearing themselves do it. Rehearse until the impulse to shout is dead.

**Build and drill a tactile lexicon.** The room will force you to invent one within minutes. Do not let it. Arrive with one, rehearsed blind and gloved:
- Squeeze forearm once: *yes / acknowledged*
- Twice: *no*
- Three times, sharp: *stop, freeze where you are*
- Sustained grip, increasing: *I am in trouble*
- Pull toward: *come with me*
- Flat palm pressed on the sternum: *we are leaving now, no discussion*
- Tap the crown of your own helmet: *check your seals*
- Tap your own wrist: *how much time*
- Then digits counted onto the back of the other's hand.

Drill it in a dark, loud, cold room until it is faster than speech.

**Practice whiteout walking.** A snowfield under overcast is the closest analogue: light with no direction, terrain with no shadow, and complete failure of depth perception. Learn the shuffle-probe gait — lead foot sliding forward, weight held back, sole feeling for the edge before it commits.

**Tether discipline.** Take your full kit and live with it for a day with every single object lanyarded to your harness. Everything. Spare earplugs, lens cloth, pencil, the cap of the water bladder. In the Chord Room there is no floor — there is a lattice of throats going down farther than you can imagine, and a dropped object is not lost, it is *swallowed*, and possibly lodged, and possibly detuning a note that has held since before your species had hands. Nothing loose. No open pockets. No zippers you can leave undone.

## 3. Kit

**Hearing — the entire point of the exercise.**
- Custom-molded deep-insertion earplugs, fitted by someone competent, with a verified fit test. Foam plugs inserted by amateurs deliver a fraction of their rated attenuation.
- Over them, high-attenuation earmuffs with a good seal — no eyewear temples breaking the cushion. (This is why your eye protection must be goggle-style, strapped under the muff cups or over the top with a low-profile band.)
- A rigid helmet with a snug liner over both. At these levels, sound enters through the skull. Covering the head measurably lowers the bone-conduction floor, which is the hard limit no plug can beat — somewhere around 45–50 dB below the ambient field. This is why **your exposure budget is measured in tens of minutes, not hours, regardless of how good your ears' protection is.**
- **Spare plugs on a lanyard.** A lost plug ends the visit immediately. Not "soon." Immediately.

**Sound measurement** (bring it even if you are not a scientist; it is safety equipment):
- A measurement microphone rated to at least 160 dB SPL, with a proper windscreen — the air comes *up* out of the floor and will overload a bare capsule into meaningless noise.
- A dosimeter you can read by feel or glance, set to vibrate at your dose limit.
- Send the mic in on a pole, ahead of your body, toward the geometric center. Every bore in the bowl is aimed at the air above the middle. That is a focus. Do not walk your skull into a focus you have not measured. Map the field as you go; there will be nulls, and in the nulls you can rest.

**Eyes.**
- Glacier-grade goggles, category 4 lenses, full side seal, with an anti-fog treatment you have tested. Bring a second pair in a hard case.
- Assume ultraviolet until proven otherwise. A sourceless white sky tells you nothing about its spectrum.
- A brimmed or visored helmet to cut the sky, and — this sounds absurd until you are there — **a dark cloth or card on a lanyard**. When the ground starts to swim and pulse, you press the card to your goggles and stand still for thirty seconds looking at one flat dark thing. It resets the eye. It prevents the fall.

**Lungs and face.**
- A P100 / FFP3 respirator, tight-fitting, fit-tested. Half-face at minimum.
- Better: a mask incorporating a **heat-and-moisture exchanger**, which recycles the water from your own breath. In air this dry, an HME is not comfort, it is a significant fraction of your total water conservation strategy.
- Test the atmosphere before you trust it: oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide. The bowl is continuously flushed from below — nothing heavy can pool — but "continuously flushed with *what*" is a question to answer with an instrument, not a nose.

**Feet and hands.**
- Boots with a **stiff full-length shank and a sole plate wider and more rigid than the largest bore you expect.** This is the single most-neglected item. A soft flexible sole folds into a fist-sized mouth and takes your ankle with it.
- Knee pads, elbow pads, padded gloves. A fall onto a field of hard clay tube-mouths is not a graze; it is a hundred small punch-wounds.
- Consider carrying a light rigid board or snowshoe-style platform to stand on for long stationary work. It spreads your load across hundreds of worn lips instead of a dozen.

**Body.**
- Insulating layers. Cellar-cool plus constant airflow plus two hours of standing still equals hypothermia in someone who expected "mild."
- **Anti-static outer fabrics with conductive thread.** Bone-dry insulating clay, moving dust, and a walking human is a triboelectric generator. You will accumulate kilovolts and there is nothing to ground to. You will discharge into the first thing you touch, which may be one of the silver filaments.
- Occlusive lip balm and nasal ointment, applied *before* entry, reapplied under cover. Your lips will split within the hour regardless; the goal is to stop them bleeding.

**Water and waste.**
- A hydration bladder with a **sealed, non-drip bite valve**, routed inside your clothing, drunk through the mask port if your mask allows. Never open a container in the bowl.
- Sealed urine containment. Yes, really. See below.
- No food inside. None. Not a lozenge.

**Time.**
- Two vibrating timers on opposite wrists, independently set, one of them a simple mechanical thing that cannot fail interestingly.
- A written schedule on a wrist slate, in grease pencil, tethered. Not in your head. Your head will not keep it.

**Egress.**
- Whatever your return mechanism is: **redundant, tethered, tactile, and tested at the threshold before you commit to the floor.** No voice-activated anything. No device that signals you with a tone. You cannot hear a tone. You cannot hear a bomb. Every alert you rely on must be a vibration or a light inside your goggles.
- If your return anchor is a placed object: it goes on a tether to your body, or in a hard case clipped to two separate points, and it never, ever rests on the mouths.

## 4. Rules for the body inside

- **Hard clock.** Thirty minutes, first visit. Sixty on a second, only if your dosimeter and your audiogram earned it. The room will give you no signal that you are being damaged, and it will feel like one of the most important places you have ever stood. That combination is precisely how people ruin themselves.
- **Do not linger at the focus.** Walk the radius, measure, retreat to a null.
- **Shuffle-probe gait. Always.** Never a normal step.
- **Do not attempt speech.** Not once. Not "just to see."
- **If you feel chest tightness, nausea, or your vision begins to shimmer at the edges**, that is the low end coupling into your body. It does not improve with acclimatization. Leave.
- **If your buddy becomes still and stops responding to the forearm signal**, do not investigate. Flat palm on the sternum and go. Derealization in there is fast, deep, and does not announce itself.
- **Tether to your partner.** A short cord, waist to waist. When the glare closes your eyes you will not find each other by calling.

## 5. Rules for the room

This is the part most travelers skip, and it is the part that matters.

You are about to walk into a system that finished settling before your world had continents. Every grain of dust is in its terminal position. There is no weather to erase you, because the only weather blows the wrong way and always has. **The Chord Room has no capacity to heal.** Whatever you do there, you do forever.

Understand precisely what you are: a warm, wet, shedding, oily, electrically charged, water-carrying animal entering a world that has never once been rained on.

Therefore:

- **Choose your footprints.** You cannot avoid leaving them; they are eternal. So do not scribble. Walk in along a single clean radius. Do your work standing. Walk out along the same line. Leave one line and a station, not a trampled mess. That line will be there when the last star in your home universe goes out. Make it a line you'd sign.
- **No water leaves your body onto that clay.** No spitting. No blowing your nose. No urine, ever, under any pressure of bladder or dignity. If your lip bleeds, catch it in a cloth inside the mask and keep the cloth. A drop of water is the most violent thing that has happened in that world in geological time, and it will still be visible — a clumped, cemented, dark scar in the talc — long after you are a rumor.
- **Do not touch the rosettes.** A pushed leaf stays pushed. You would be editing a living thing's posture permanently.
- **Do not touch the filaments.** They cannot heal and cannot rot. A snapped one is a bright thread on the clay until the end of the world. Also: you are carrying kilovolts. Keep a meter clear of the eastern arc and discharge yourself to a grounded rod in your own kit before you reach anywhere near them.
- **Never obstruct a bore.** A brief palm over one, to feel the push — that is the classical traveler's gesture and it is survivable. Anything more is surgery on an organ. Never plug one to "hear what it contributes." You would be de-tuning a chord that something, somewhere, is holding on purpose.
- **Take nothing.** The dust is a clock. Each crescent is a datum in a completed record. A pinch in your vial is a deletion. The souvenir you are allowed is the recording — and the recording is better than anything you could pocket, because you can bring it home and play it, attenuated, in a small room, and watch other people's faces.

If your ethics permit and your logistics allow, the maximally respectful configuration is a **fully sealed suit on closed-circuit air, with a boot cover changed at the threshold.** It is heavy, it is expensive, it makes the visit harder. It also means that the only thing you leave in a world that will never be cleaned is the print of a smooth sole.

## 6. Coming out

Budget the hour after exit as part of the trip, not as recovery from it.

- Do not drive, navigate, or make a jump decision for at least an hour. Your time sense and your balance are both compromised and you will not believe it.
- Drink slowly; you are more dehydrated than you feel.
- Expect the chord to continue. It will be in your head for a day, sometimes three, and it will be *precise* — not a ringing, the actual chord, with its grainy fifth intact. This is normal. If it is a whistle or a hiss rather than the chord, that is tinnitus, and that is your audiogram appointment.
- Expect the first spoken sentence to feel obscene. Several travelers report a day or two of voluntary silence afterward and describe it as the most useful part of the journey.
- Write your notes within the hour. The place erodes in memory faster than it has any right to, because there are no events in it to hang memory on — no weather, no incident, no change. What survives without notes is one flat impression and a sound.

## 7. The thing to be ready for that isn't on any list

At some point — usually around minute twenty, usually while you're looking up — you will work out what the last paragraph of every account of this place means. That the bores converge. That they are all exhaling and never drawing in. That a chord is not a noise, that a chord is *tuned*, and that tuning implies an intention and a tuner and a purpose. And that you are not standing in an instrument. You are standing inside one note of one, in a small bright dry bell of something whose lungs are somewhere you will never go, holding.

There is no preparation that makes that land gently. But you can arrange not to be *surprised* by it while standing on a lattice of holes with your eyes half-blind and your ears stopped and your partner three meters away and unable to hear you fall.

Know it in advance. Decide in advance what you will do with it. Then set your timer, walk your single clean line, stand in the null, and listen to the held note — and when the wrist buzzes, go, because the note does not need you and will not notice, and it will be exactly this loud tomorrow, and the day after, and the day the last person who remembers your name is forgotten.
