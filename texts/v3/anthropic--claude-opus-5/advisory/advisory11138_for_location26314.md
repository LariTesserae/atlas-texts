## First, the thing that has to be understood before any packing list

You are not going to visit the Hummock. You are going to *loom over* it.

Everything the account describes — forty chambers, five levels, the shaft that took four generations to braid, the drowned archive with everyone in it — is inside an object two metres across and knee-to-hip high. You can put your palm flat on the sixth level. You could pick the whole country up, if you were strong enough and cruel enough, and it would come away in wet handfuls.

So the discipline of this trip is not survival, though survival is nontrivial. The discipline is **restraint at a scale you have never had to practise.** Ordinary courtesy will not do it. Ordinary carefulness will not do it. You need to arrive having already rehearsed being harmless, because on the fen your instincts are all wrong: leaning in to see better, breathing out while you look, putting a hand down to steady yourself, stepping back onto whatever's behind you. Each of those is a disaster with a name in their language.

Practise, for at least a month before departure, sitting still and cold in the dark for six hours doing nothing.

## When to go

Go at **freeze-up** — the tail of autumn, after the sedge has rattled itself out and before the deep dark, when the till has gone hard and the pools have a rind that carries weight.

Reasons: the ground records your footprints permanently in any other season, and a boot print in flat wet till becomes a pool, and a pool becomes a drainage capture, and that changes the water table for a hundred paces around, which either drowns cities or dries them out over the following decade. Frozen ground forgives. Also the mound itself is firm then; its walls are stiff, its crown is not actively growing, and it is not fragile.

Avoid **warm wet spring** absolutely. That is when the low rooms ask their question. A mound in spring is a structure at the edge of failure and your presence is a load and a heat source.

Understand also where the city *is* in the season you choose. The habitable band is thin: frost coming down from the crown, black water coming up from below, and the living rooms squeezed between them, migrating up and down the year like a bubble in a tube. In late winter the top is glazed shut and the city is deep and quiet. In midsummer it is high and busy and softest. You will meet a different nation depending on your month, and none of them will meet you.

## The body, and the cold

This is a wet cold at sea level in fog. It is not the clean cold of altitude. Nothing dries here — not your gloves, not your bag, not the inside of your shell. Salt comes in on the fog and gets into fabric and then into your skin.

- **Vapour-barrier layering.** Non-negotiable. Your sweat has nowhere to go and no fire to be dried at.
- **Redundant handwear**, four pairs minimum, and accept that two will be ruined. You will spend hours with your hands still and low, which is the worst possible thermal posture.
- **Thick closed-cell insulation for kneeling, sitting, and lying.** You will be prone beside a hummock for long stretches. The ground is a heat sink with no bottom.
- **Fuel for everything.** There is no wood in this world. There is *one* piece of wood in this world and it is a holy object under the mound. Bring stove fuel for all melting, cooking, and warming, and bring half again as much as your arithmetic says, because cold-weather stoves lie.
- **Water discipline.** Standing water is full of rock flour, which will kill a pump filter in an afternoon. Boil or treat chemically. Melt ice where you can. Expect the water to taste like an opened stone and, on foggy nights, faintly of the sea.
- **Anchoring.** There is no soil for pegs. Bring rock bags — but *do not gather stones near any mound.* The black flakeable ice-leavings scattered on the till are that civilisation's entire supply of hard substance: their knives, needles, sieve-frames, fire-hearths. Taking a handful of good stone from within reach of a mound is looting a nation's mineral industry. Gather ballast a kilometre out, on ground you have surveyed as empty.

## Getting across the fen without committing a massacre

Mounds occur at intervals of about a hundred paces, and **some of them are the size of a loaf.** In fog, on grey till, at dusk, a loaf-sized hummock is invisible until your boot is on it.

- Survey before you walk. Arrive far off, on a beach ridge or bare bedrock, and map your approach in good light from a distance with optics. Flag a single corridor and use only that corridor, in single file, for the entire visit. Never freelance a new route because it looks shorter.
- **Wide, soft, blunt soles.** Snowshoes if there's snow. No spikes, no crampons, no trekking-pole tips punched into wet ground — a pole hole is a drain.
- Move **slowly within thirty paces of any mound**, and roll your weight rather than dropping it. Your footfall is felt further than you'd guess. Their catastrophe-myth is the *long noise*: a hollow rushing gnawing from a direction with no door in it. You do not want to be a new entry in that genre.
- Approach the study mound from **downwind**, and set camp at least two hundred paces off and downslope of nothing.

## The three ways you will hurt them without touching them

**Your breath.** Warm, wet, and continuous. Exhaled over a frozen crown it glazes the growing tips shut. Rig a breath diverter — a hose, a scarf-and-baffle arrangement, anything that sends your exhalations sideways and away — and get in the habit of holding your breath while your face is over the mound. Every time. Never speak while leaning in.

**Your heat.** Your hand is at thirty-seven degrees. The crown may be at minus ten. A bare palm laid on the wall thaws, then refreezes, and kills braid-work that took years, and opens the wall. Beyond that, your skin carries salt and oils and — worst — *nutrients*. Which brings us to the real weapon.

**Your nitrogen.** This is the one nobody expects. A sphagnum mound is an engine of poverty; it exists because nothing here is fertile. Add nutrient and you do not enrich it, you *end* it: grasses and vascular plants come in, the moss loses, the mound slumps, and a decade later there is no city, only a green patch. A single human urination within reach of a hummock is a slow bomb with a ten-year fuse.

So: **pack out everything.** All solid waste. All urine, in sealed bottles. Food crumbs, hair, nail clippings, fingernail dirt, the water you rinse a pot with, the tea you throw away, tissue, ash, tape, thread. Eat inside a sealed shelter over a tray. Nothing organic leaves your possession. Nothing.

And sterilise your gear before departure — bake it, freeze it, whatever your era permits. You must not introduce a fungus. Somewhere under that mound is the only wood in the world, being eaten by the only lamp in the world; a competing mould carried in on the sole of your boot could put out the lights of a civilisation forever.

## Light

Their entire optical inheritance is a milk-faint glow from fungal chips the size of a fingernail. They know faces by touch. Their word for stranger is *unhandled*.

Your headlamp is not a tool here, it is an atrocity. Use the deepest red you can work by, at the lowest output that functions, hooded, aimed down, and never onto the mound. If you must illuminate the crown, do it in single brief exposures with the light as far off-axis as possible.

Accept the corollary: **you will see almost nothing.** You are going to spend days beside a low dark dome in fog and darkness and it will look, at all times, like a wet lump of moss. There is no version of this trip in which the city becomes visible to you.

## What you can actually perceive — bring these

This is where the trip stops being an exercise in doing nothing.

- **A good hand lens and a long-working-distance macro rig.** Reach with glass, never with your face. On the outer crown you will see harvestmen going over like slow derricks, and springtails in drifts, and if you are patient and lucky, a worked edge: a knotted foothold, a braid too regular to be weather, a doorway.
- **Contact microphones and a very quiet preamp.** Put a contact mic on the crown and you will hear **the chewing** — the continuous grinding of the substrate being eaten by its tenants, everywhere, never stopping. This is the sound of the place being alive. They find its absence unbearable. You will find its presence unbearable in a different way, for the first hour, and then you will not want to take the headphones off.
