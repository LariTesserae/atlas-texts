# Preparing for the Cistern at the Top of the Rise

## First, understand what you are

You are an animal. Where you are going, that is a category with eight members, and they live in a jar on a shelf.

Everything else in the basin either grows without a courier or turns because a shaft turns it. You will arrive carrying, conservatively, four hundred species in your gut, a hundred on your skin, mites in the roots of your eyelashes, and grit in the tread of your boots that is older than the pillar you are standing on. Nothing there abrades anything. Nothing there decays quickly. Whatever you shed will still be identifiable in a thousand years, resting exactly where it settled, on a trowelled surface that will still be sharp.

Prepare accordingly. Most of the work of this trip is done before you leave, and most of it is subtraction.

---

## The body, weeks out

**Altitude.** The column has carried the basin up through the cloud deck. Assume an equivalent pressure altitude of four to five thousand metres and confirm it with your own barometer on arrival before you do anything else. A portal drop is an instantaneous ascent; there is no walk-in, no night at the trailhead. Arriving cold from sea level you are buying a coin-flip on high-altitude pulmonary oedema, and there is no descent available — there is no *down* except a two-kilometre fall into cloud.

- Sleep in a hypoxic tent for three weeks, stepping the setting upward. Or, better, stage the transit: spend four nights in an intermediate world at moderate altitude and come in from there.
- Carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine. Carry oxygen — not for diving, for sleeping.
- Expect Cheyne–Stokes breathing on your first nights. It is normal and it is horrible in a place with no sound.

**Dryness.** The air at the rim is thin, cold and arid; the water is at body temperature and evaporating steadily. You will lose fluid faster than your thirst reports it, and you will get nosebleeds. Start saline nasal gel three days before. Over-hydrate the day before transit. Note that you will not be drinking from the basin, ever, for reasons given below.

**Teeth.** Have any loose filling seen to. In the hushwater the loudest thing in the world is the tick of your own jaw, and a cracked molar becomes the entire acoustic environment.

**Decontamination.** Begin five days out.

- Clip nails to the quick and file them smooth; nail crescents are permanent artefacts.
- Shave or cover all hair. Hair is the single most reliable vector for spores, eggs and mites, and a shed hair on white concrete will be visible to whoever comes next, for as long as there is a next.
- Chlorhexidine washes, ears and navel included, escalating daily.
- Do not bother trying to sterilise your gut. You cannot. Instead, plan never to open your suit inside the basin — which means planning your digestion. Light residue-free diet for forty-eight hours; full containment while you are in. A p-valve routed into a sealed bag, not into the water. This is not squeamishness. Your urine is a nutrient bloom and a genetic sample, and the basin turns over once every nineteen days, which means it would be everywhere and stay everywhere.

## The skills, months out

**Learn to talk by hand.** A voice dies within an arm's length. If there is anyone there, they speak wrist to wrist, in an etiquette that was invented in a month and never written down, which means you will not find a textbook and must arrive fluent in something adjacent and humble about the rest.

Learn a deafblind manual alphabet — the tactile fingerspelling used by Protactile signers is the closest analogue — and learn it to the point of not thinking. Learn a simple squeeze code for the dark: one squeeze *yes*, two *no*, three *come*, long hold *stop*, rapid repeated *I am in trouble*.

Then learn the manners, which matter more than the vocabulary:

- You offer your wrist, palm up. Contact is consent to speak. Taking someone's wrist without offer is roughly the equivalent of grabbing a stranger's jaw.
- Breaking contact ends the exchange. Do not chase it.
- To interrupt, touch the back of the hand, once, and wait.
- Speech is physically intimate here and therefore rationed. Expect to be talked to less than you are used to and not to read it as coldness.

**Learn to move in polymer.** Dose a training pool with high-molecular-weight polyethylene oxide until spray stops happening and practise there for a week. You will discover that the water refuses to be pushed. Turbulent thrust is what swimming *is*, and the polymer eats turbulence: you will kick hard and slide sideways and go nowhere, and the wake will close over you like nothing occurred. This is exhausting in a way that feels like a failure of your body rather than a property of the fluid, which is why you practise until you believe it.

What works: long rigid fins, slow full-amplitude strokes, and — above all — pulling yourself along fixed structure with your hands. Plan every route in the basin as a handrail route. Swimming across open water there is a two-hundred-metre commitment with no way to call for help and no reserve.

**Learn to be quiet.** Not silent — *unheard*. There is a difference and it will get to you. See the section on the mind.

## What to take, and what it must not be made of

The governing rule: nothing you bring may shed, flake, corrode, dissolve, abrade, or drop. There is no sand, no current, no frost, and no animal to scuff a corner. Anything that comes off you stays where it lands, in its original condition, indefinitely.

**Suit.** Smooth-skin bonded elastomer drysuit, welded seams, no fabric outer, no neoprene (open-cell neoprene sheds constantly), no Velcro, no painted hardware. Light colour so you can see what you are carrying. Full-face mask or, better, a sealed helmet with an integral drink line. Rounded fittings everywhere: the concrete edges will cut you, the glass weirs are unchipped and you must not be the chip.

**Strip your anodes.** Every piece of standard dive kit carries sacrificial zinc. In a closed basin of a few hundred thousand cubic metres with no outflow, your anodes are a dosing regime. Remove them and accept the galvanic wear on your own gear.

**Breathing.** Closed-circuit rebreather, mandatory, and not for the depth. Open-circuit exhaust in hushwater does not stream away; it coalesces into slow flat domes that travel under the terrace overhangs and collect, and it carries an aerosol of your oral flora across the entire basin as it rises. Also: a bubble that gets under a leaf-meadow will lift it.

Bring a gas analyser and verify the atmosphere before you unseal anything. Do not assume Earth-normal air.

**Ballast.** Here is where your training will fight you. The water is stratified in three layers by dissolved carbonate, and the boundaries are firm enough that leaves lie on them in drifts. Descending, you become *more* buoyant, not less. You will come down through the upper layer, meet the halocline, and stop — floating on an invisible plane in a meadow of yellow litter, unable to go further without help.

So: variable ballast only, a sealed pump-and-bladder system. **No droppable weight. No quick-release. Ever.** Dumping lead into that basin is an irreversible act of pollution and vandalism, and the standard emergency procedure you have trained for twenty years is, here, forbidden. Rehearse the alternative until it is reflex.

**Lines.** Run a guideline everywhere, cave-diving discipline, because the weed beds close behind you without noise and one column of ribbon weed looks like the next hundred. But you may not anchor to anything: no bolts, no clamps, no tape residue. Use wide silicone loops around existing stanchions, padded; use a free-standing tripod that comes back out with you. Retrieve every metre. A line left behind is a rope that will still be there, unfrayed, when the column is another sixty metres taller.

**Lights.** Low output, hooded, warm or green-filtered. This is not about your night vision. The plate walls carry a flat black alga that grows in annual laminations like a fingernail — a written record, one line per year. A bright lamp left pointing at a wall for an hour will bleach a patch into *this year's line*, and that mark is now permanent, legible, and dated. Light is a form of graffiti here. Keep lamps moving, keep them dim, and never park one.

**Tools.** All lanyarded twice. Non-magnetic, non-conductive handles, no wood, no leather, no paint. A wrench thrown into that water is swallowed with a sound like a door closing in another building, and then it lies on Terrace Six forever with its mill number facing up.

**Do not bring:** sunscreen (it goes into the water — cover up mechanically instead, and the UV above the cloud deck off white sinter and pale concrete is genuinely dangerous), cotton, wool, paper, chalk, adhesive tape, food of any kind past the gauge-house door, or anything alive that you know about.

## Hazards, in the order they will actually kill you

**1. The edge.** There is no ground and never was. The rim is a nine-minute walk around a dish on a tapering pillar above cloud. There is no rescue, no helicopter, no body recovery. Clip in on the rim in wind. Confirm your arrival footprint before transit with a wide margin: a two-metre positional error at the rim is a two-kilometre fall. Set your return mark somewhere you can find in fog, and note that the column grows six centimetres a year — if your return coordinates are fixed in absolute space and you plan to come back in fifty years, you will arrive three metres inside solid stone.

**2. Electricity.** Bus bars run through the lower galleries, and there is ozone in the air at the rim, which means something is arcing somewhere. Warm carbonate water is a perfectly adequate conductor and a step-potential gradient is invisible. Survey with a two-probe gradient meter on a boom before you enter any gallery, work one-handed near any metal, and treat the copper as live. Remember the specific horror of this place: a current that only paralyses your diaphragm will do so in total silence, three metres from a companion who will notice nothing, because the water forgets you at once.

**3. Heat.** The water is thirty-two degrees at the surface and thirty-four below. Sealed in a drysuit, doing physical work, you will cook, and you will not notice, because you are weightless and calm and cool at the face. Bring an active cooling undergarment or a chilled-loop vest, swallow a core-temperature pill, and set hard work/rest cycles by the clock rather than by feel. More visitors are hurt here by hyperthermia than by anything else.

**4. The meadows.** The leaf drifts on the haloclines are lovely and they are also a false floor three metres above a real one. Lie on them; that is one of the reasons to come. But never work or hover *under* a section that is visibly sagging. Over decades they thicken until a span gives way, and a century of yellow litter comes down in a soundless brown avalanche that takes four hours to finish. You would not hear it start. You would not be found.

**5. Entanglement.** Milfoil and ribbon weed so dense you part them with both arms, closing behind you without noise. Carry line cutters for your own line only. Do not cut vegetation; back out the way you came, slowly, and let the plants win the argument.

**6. Decompression, at altitude, before a transit.** The galleries are deep enough to matter and you are starting at 0.6 bar. Use altitude-corrected tables and then add conservatism. And the multiverse-specific rule that people forget: **no diving within twenty-four hours of a portal transit**, because a jump to a sea-level world is a compression you can survive and a jump to a high world is an ascent you may not. Build your itinerary around this. It is the single most common way travellers are injured in places like this.

## The discipline of leaving nothing

The basin has the geology of a thing brand new, and it will keep that geology for a millennium. There is no wear budget. You cannot "leave only footprints," because footprints do not go away.

- Do not touch the epoxy run on Terrace Four. It is still tacky. Your fingerprint will stay, and will stay, and everyone who comes after you will see it and know exactly what kind of person you were. This is the single greatest temptation in the basin and it catches roughly one visitor in three.
- Do not put a hand flat on trowelled concrete to steady yourself. Use the backs of your knuckles, or a padded tool.
- Do not kneel on liverwort.
- Do not touch the chalked pump logs in the gauge house. They are someone's handwriting and there is no dust here to bury them.
- Do not amend the card on the jar. The tally has been amended twice, in two hands, and neither of them is yours.
- Do not interfere with the combs. Ceramic tines, one turn every eleven minutes, touching each panicle in sequence. If you pass through Terrace Two between turns you can carry pollen out of order, which is a small vandalism against a very carefully maintained principle. Move down-terrace, never up, and rinse in the outflow before you change shelves.
- Do not strike the bells. They cannot ring. Whatever the reason — clapperless, or thin air, or nothing to carry over — testing it is not curiosity, it is stepping on somebody's grief. They were specified early, by someone who imagined a place that could be called across. Nobody has taken them down. Leave them bright.

## The mind

The hardest preparation is for the quiet, and no one believes this beforehand.

The basin is quiet the way a photograph is quiet. There is no animal motion, no wear, no weather, no wake. Your voice does not travel an arm's length. Your hammer blow is a knock in the sternum and gone. You will, within about six hours, develop a sensation that you are not present — that you are looking at the place rather than standing in it. This is ordinary and it passes, but it passes faster if you have prepared for it.

What helps:

- **Rhythm.** Take your time from the machines. The combs turn every eleven minutes; the pump cranks travel their arc at four revolutions a minute, slow enough to watch like a minute hand. Anchor yourself to those. Do not rely on a wristwatch; you will stop believing it.
- **Touch.** If you have a companion, schedule contact — not conversation, contact. Wrist to wrist, twice an hour, for no reason. The etiquette exists for a reason and the reason is not efficiency.
- **Expect the urge.** Around day two most visitors feel a strong, specific, embarrassing desire to break something: to throw a wrench, to chip a weir, to scratch a line in the concrete — anything that would make a mark or a noise and prove the place can be affected. Name it in advance. Tell your companion about it in advance, by touch, so that it is already a shared joke before it arrives. It usually goes away on its own. It has not always.
- **Set a short stay.** Three days is plenty. Nineteen is a turnover and far too long for a first visit.

## What to do while you are there

Go down through the upper terrace in the early light, when the tree fern crowns are in the sun and their trunks are in the water. Find a halocline and lie on it, face up, in the litter, and let the false floor hold you three metres above the real one, with the dappled shade moving over the galleries below. Watch a crank complete one arc. Sit at the rim at dusk, above the cloud deck, in the smell of warm wet stone and cedar and cut stems, and try to say something, and find that it does not go anywhere.

Then look in on the jar, and say nothing, and do not touch it.

---

## Leaving

Decontaminate on exit as carefully as you decontaminated on entry, and in the same direction: the basin is what you are protecting, not yourself.

Account for every item on a written manifest, checked twice, by two people. Anything unaccounted for is not lost — it is *placed*, permanently, and you owe the next visitor an honest note about where.

If you find that you have brought something alive in — a mite, a larva in a seam, anything — do not quietly deal with it, and do not add it to the jar. Bring it out, and say so. Eight is the number of animals in the basin, and the record of how it changes is one of the few things there that is not made of concrete.

And accept, before you go, that you will leave something anyway. A skin cell, a breath of aerosol, a smear of silicone on a stanchion, a very faint bleached patch in this year's black lamination. That is the price of an animal visiting a place built to be visited by nothing. Pay it as small as you can make it.

Then step back through, and let the column go on doing what it does — six centimetres a year, patiently, carrying its warm green dish of engineered water up out of the cloud, going nowhere, growing, holding its breath.
