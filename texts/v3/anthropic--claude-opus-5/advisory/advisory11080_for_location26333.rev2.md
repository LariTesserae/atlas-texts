# Going up to the Upper Shelf
### A preparation brief for an ordinary traveller

---

## First, get the three facts straight

Everything you need to prepare for follows from three properties of this world, and if you hold them in your head correctly you will improvise the rest.

**It is young.** The rock has not been worn. That means edges are sharp, slopes are at their steepest possible angle, nothing is consolidated, and no landform has yet acquired the gentle approach ramps that Earth erosion builds into a landscape as a courtesy. On Earth, a cliff edge announces itself — the ground steepens, the soil thins, the vegetation changes, a gully cuts back. Here the rim does none of that. The flat goes flat, flat, flat, and then it is air.

**It is heated from below.** The temperature never changes. This sounds benign, and mostly is, but it means you will never dry, never cool by evaporation, and never get the useful daily signals — dawn chill, evening cool — that your body uses to keep time. It also means there is magma-derived gas and magma-derived chemistry in the water, which is the single most likely thing to actually kill you.

**It is sterile.** No animals at all. This is not a hazard to you. It is a hazard *from* you. You are a walking zoo — ten trillion gut bacteria, mites in your eyelashes, fungal spores in your socks, seeds in your boot treads, possibly a fruit fly asleep in your pack. If you are careless, you are the meteor.

---

## Six to eight weeks out: the body

**Altitude.** Thin air, deep blue sky, and simultaneously a warm damp landscape — your body will receive contradictory instructions. You will not *feel* like you're at altitude because you aren't cold and you aren't on a windy ridge; you'll feel like you're in a greenhouse. You will therefore overexert. Do real acclimatisation: arrive via a staged ascent if the crossing allows it, or budget four to six days of doing almost nothing on the plateau before you attempt any serious walking. Consider acetazolamide, and consider it seriously, because there is no descent option here — the rim is a two-thousand-foot vertical drop into an unvisited country. There is no "go down to 2,000 metres and sleep it off." You are on a shelf. The only treatment for altitude sickness on a shelf is leaving the world.

**Heat-humidity training.** Warm plus saturated plus thin air plus effort equals a body that cannot shed heat. Sweat will not evaporate. Train in a sauna or a steam room until you have an honest feel for how fast you overheat when evaporation is off the table. Then understand *why the locals walk everywhere at the same slow pace and haul sledges in silence at a stroll*. That pace is not serenity. It is physiology. Match it from your first hour and you will be fine; try to hike at your usual rate and you will be sitting on a warm rock with a headache and a pulse of 150 wondering what went wrong.

**Dental and surgical.** There is no medicine there in any sense you would recognise. No metal, no bone-setting tradition worth the name for a body shaped like yours, no analgesics beyond whatever the ferns provide. Get your teeth done. If you have a grumbling appendix or a hernia, deal with it first. A ruptured appendix on the Upper Shelf is simply a death, in a very quiet room, with people who are kind and can do nothing.

**Nails and skin.** Cut your nails short and keep them short. Treat any existing fungal infection, any athlete's foot, any eczema, before you go. The Shelf will amplify all of it beyond recognition.

## Contamination: the part most travellers get wrong

Be honest with yourself about which protocol you are running.

**The strict protocol** is what a responsible expedition does: a full decontamination cycle before crossing — antiparasitic course, close-cropped hair, scrupulous gear sterilisation, all textiles new and heat-treated, boots with the treads cut flat and scrubbed, pack seams vacuumed, every food item processed and sealed, and **every gram of your waste carried out**. Not buried. Not burned. Carried out, in sealed double-walled containers, faeces and urine and nail clippings and hair and used dressings and the lint from your pockets. Your gut flora has no competitors there. Your dead skin is the richest food that peat has ever been offered.

**The honest minimum**, if you are one person on a short visit and not pretending otherwise: no waste left anywhere, ever, under any circumstances; no organic matter of any kind abandoned; nothing living brought in; and a brutal inspection of your kit before crossing, with a headtorch, seam by seam, for eggs, seeds, and sleeping insects. Freeze your pack for forty-eight hours beforehand. It is not perfect. It is the difference between being a visitor and being a founding event in the world's biological history.

And: **do not bring an animal.** Not a service dog, not a pigeon, not a jar of brine shrimp as a gift. There is no word there for what it would be. There is also no immune system anywhere on that plateau prepared for a gut.

## Clothing and the permanent wet

Understand the central fact: **nothing dries.** Not by fire, not by sun, not overnight, not ever. A wet shirt stays wet for the duration of your visit. Plan around that rather than fighting it.

- **Wear synthetics.** Wool will rot and stink; cotton is a fungal culture medium; leather will soften, then bloom green, then delaminate. Fine-gauge polyester or polypropylene, dark colours (everything is going to stain a mineral ochre anyway).
- **Two wardrobes, strictly separated.** A *wet set* you live in and never expect to dry, and a **dry set sealed in a waterproof bag that you only wear inside your sleeping system and never, ever wear outdoors.** This is not comfort. It is how you keep your skin intact for three weeks instead of two.
- **Vapour-barrier liners** for sleeping — your dry set stays dry because your own sweat is contained, rather than soaking it.
- **Rain shell: mostly useless.** It isn't raining. The air is simply saturated and the ground is weeping. A shell traps your heat and makes you wetter. Better to be wet and ventilated. Bring a light poncho for the fern-drip and put it away.
- **Sun.** This is the trap. A shadowless white cloud deck at high altitude transmits savage ultraviolet light, and it comes at you from the entire sky, including upward off the wet rock. There are no shadows to hide in. You will burn the underside of your chin, the inside of your nostrils, and the roof of your mouth if you walk with it open. Bring a wide brim, a high collar, long sleeves, **and sunglasses you can wear in flat white light** — good ones, plus a spare pair, because eye burn in a glaring fogbound world is a genuine expedition-ender. Zinc paste is better than lotion; lotion will run off a wet face in twenty minutes.
- **Footwear: two pairs, minimum.** Boots for talus (which is glass — it will slice sole rubber and any soft upper), and open sandals for camp and town. Wet stone with algal film is one of the most slippery surfaces that exists. Aggressive lugs help on peat and do nothing on filmed rhyolite; nothing helps on filmed rhyolite except walking like an old person.
- **Foot ritual, nightly, non-negotiable.** Off with everything, dry the feet with a dedicated cloth, powder, inspect between every toe, sleep with feet bare and elevated. Trench foot and fungal maceration are the most probable thing that will actually end your trip, and they are entirely preventable by ten minutes of discipline per day.

## Water, air, and the invisible chemistry

**Do not drink from a hot spring because it is hot and looks clean.** High-silica rhyolitic geothermal water is the classic carrier of **arsenic, fluoride, boron, antimony and lithium**, at concentrations that produce no taste and no immediate symptoms and considerable long-term harm. The townspeople have, over an unguessable number of generations, worked out which spring is the good one — that is *why the town sits where it sits*. Drink what they drink, from where they draw it, and nowhere else. If you must use field water, prefer the cold braided sheet-streams on the flats over anything that emerges warm, filter for particulates, and accept that no filter you own removes dissolved arsenic. Bring more water treatment than you think and consider carrying a reverse-osmosis or distillation option for a long stay.

**The blue-green mats.** The locals farm and eat them. Cyanobacterial mats elsewhere produce hepatotoxins and neurotoxins; their strain is evidently benign to them, and probably to you, but "probably" is doing work. If you eat the dried cakes — and you should, once, because it is offered and refusing food is refusing a relationship — eat a little, wait a day, and watch yourself. Never graze a wild mat.

**Gas is the real killer.** A geothermal field at these temperatures exhales **carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulphide**, both heavier than air, both invisible, both pooling silently in exactly the places the description invites you into: hollows, cellars, and above all **those clean straight-sided clefts you could drop a rope into.** There is no wind here to speak of, no seasonal turnover, and the cloud sits in the hollows at dawn — which is also when the gas layer will be deepest and least disturbed.

- Carry a **four-gas meter** with CO₂ and H₂S, and carry a spare sensor, and keep it in a dry case because the humidity will kill electronics.
- If you have no meter: a **candle on a string**, lowered ahead of you. When it dies, you stop. It is crude, it lags, and it is much better than nothing.
- H₂S smells of eggs at low concentration and **smells of nothing at lethal concentration** — your nose is anaesthetised within a minute or two. Never use smell as an all-clear.
- Do not descend into a cleft, a steaming fissure, or a collapsed cavity. Do not sleep in a hollow. Do not camp downhill of a vent. If you find someone unconscious in a low place, **do not go in after them** — that is how rescuers die, on every world.

**Ground crust.** Sinter and the flat overlapping mineral saucers around springs form thin white crusts over boiling water. They look like pavement. They are pastry. Move on bare rhyolite or on established local paths; follow a local's footsteps precisely, in the Yellowstone manner.

## The rim, the fog, and the other ways the land kills

**The rim is unnotched.** No gorge cuts back. No warning gradient. Streams cross the flats, reach the lip, and leave. In morning cloud — which is on the ground until mid-morning, every single day — you can walk to the edge of the world without a single visual cue. The sound of the falls is *everywhere and constant and identical from every direction*, so it gives you nothing. Rules:

- **Do not move in ground-cloud.** Sit down, drink something, wait for lift. You are in a world with no seasons and no deadlines; you have the time.
- **Know your distance to rim at all times.** Rope up near any edge if you must be there, and remember that the rock at the lip is fresh, jointed, unweathered and untested. It has never been load-tested by an animal, because there have never been animals.
- **Never follow a stream downhill to see where it goes.** It goes over.

**Talus** is angular, glassy, fresh, and unsettled — it slides with that coin-poured sound. It has no interlock, no patina, no stability. Cross it high or not at all. It will destroy soft gear, shred a rope sheath in one pull, and open your shin to the bone without you noticing.

**Peat mats** slough. They peel from the rock in whole sodden sheets. Do not camp on a mat lying on a slope; do not traverse a steep mat; be suspicious of that lovely springy clubmoss bed if it sits on an incline above anything.

**Rockfall.** Slabs part and come down. Rarely. Once in years. But this is a landscape actively disassembling itself, and every one of those clean-edged blocks at the cliff base arrived by falling. Do not sleep at the foot of a scarp, however warm and sheltered it is. That is precisely where the warmth and shelter come from.

## The medical kit, weighted correctly

Skip the snakebite kit, the insect repellent, the antimalarials, the tick tool. There are no animals. This is the most benign biological environment you will ever walk into.

Weight instead toward:

1. **Wound care, heavily.** The local blades are obsidian, sharper than steel, and **they part skin before you feel it**. So does the talus. You will acquire deep clean lacerations you did not notice, in an environment that is warm, wet and perfect for bacteria — largely *your own* bacteria. Bring closure strips, tissue adhesive, sutures if you can use them, iodine, and more dressings than seems reasonable, because dressings will not dry and must be changed often.
2. **Broad-spectrum antibiotics**, oral and topical. Soft-tissue infection is your most likely serious illness.
3. **Antifungals** — cream and oral. See "feet," above.
4. **Altitude drugs** — acetazolamide, dexamethasone as an emergency measure.
5. **Eye care** — antibiotic drops, and something for photokeratitis.
6. **Rehydration salts.** You are sweating constantly and losing more than you think in warm saturated air where you never feel thirsty.
7. **A clear self-evacuation plan.** Write down, on paper, the point at which you turn around and cross back. Give it a name and a date.

## Food

Bring all of it. The local diet is grain, tuber, young fern crowns, and dried algae cake: no fat, no oil, no animal protein, no B12, no fruit, **nothing sweet in the entire world**. A week on it is an experience. A month on it is a deficiency. Bring dense fats and oils, B12, and a broad multivitamin.

Cook with a sealed stove and a fuel you carried in. Do not build a fire from local peat or fern — it is their fuel, it is slow to accumulate, and a smoke plume is visible across the entire plateau.

And be aware that your food smells. In a world with no fruit, no fat, no meat, no spice and no flowers, the smell of you frying anything will be the strongest odour anyone there has encountered in their life. Cook downwind and away.

## Sound: the etiquette you will break first

Three sounds exist: seep, fern-rattle, talus-click. That is the whole soundscape. Into it you propose to introduce **Velcro**, ratchet buckles, a crinkling foil wrapper, zip closures, a hard-shell case snapping shut, the tick of a titanium pot, a sneeze, a cough, and a voice pitched for a world full of background noise.

- **Sound-proof your kit before you go.** Replace Velcro with cord ties. Decant everything crinkly into soft pouches. Tape rattling buckles. Put a sock on the pot. Silence your alarms and use vibration.
- **Halve your speaking volume, then halve it again.** A raised voice in that town carries to the rim, and everyone will hear it, and they will all understand that you did it.
- **Do not whistle. Do not sing. Do not play music aloud, ever.** Use one earbud at most and know that it leaks.
- **Do not touch the slit-drum on the upper bench.** It is struck perhaps once in a generation. Whatever it means, it is not for you, and striking it would be the loudest act committed in that world in a hundred years. People would come. It would be dated and remembered forever, and it would be remembered as *the year the outsider hit the drum*.

Conversely: prepare yourself for **their** silence. At night the quiet is total, and total silence is not restful to a nervous system built for a noisy planet. Your own tinnitus will roar. Your heartbeat becomes an event. Many travellers report two or three bad nights before the mind settles. Bring a very quiet white-noise source for sleeping *through an earbud only*, and do not run it in the open air.

## The people, and what you are to them

They have no word for animal. Sit with that a moment. It means there is no available category for *a thing that moves under its own intention, eats other living things, and can decide to come toward you*. You are not "a stranger." You are an unclassifiable.

Practical consequences:

- **Move slowly and predictably.** Approach from where you can be seen. Stop at a distance and wait to be acknowledged. Nod; they nod.
- **Do not display animal products.** Leather boots, a bone handle, a wool layer, a horn button — these are made from a kind of thing that does not exist in their world, and cannot be explained without explaining the whole bloody business of Earth. Go plant-and-plastic. Keep your photographs to yourself unless asked; certainly do not lead with a picture of a horse or a butchered animal.
- **Do not eat in front of them at first.** Watch how they eat, and eat that way.
- **Offer labour, not gifts.** This is a maintenance society; its entire moral architecture is *the same thing done again, correctly*. The way to be accepted is to take the other end of a drag-sledge, to haul at their pace, in their silence, and to re-lay a course of dry-stone the way it was laid before. Be useful in the local idiom. They will not thank you loudly.
- **Be extremely careful what you leave.** A steel knife in that town is not a gift; it is a detonation. It never wears out, it cuts everything, and it makes the entire obsidian economy — and the knowledge of where the black lenses are, and the skill of striking them, and the ritual around that skill — instantly obsolete. If you must give something, give something consumable, or give nothing, or give a song they can choose to forget.
- **Do not take a house in the empty quarter.** Whole terraces stand swept, warm, open-doored and deliberately unoccupied, maintained empty for reasons nobody can articulate. That is the single most sacred-shaped fact in the town. Camp outside, or on stone, or where you are put. If you are offered a house, ask twice.
- **Ask about water, not about history.** Their language will be extravagantly rich in terms for seep, film, flume, joint-plane, mat condition, camber, grade, and the manner in which stone lets go of stone. It will be poor in terms for time, growth, futures, wanting, and elsewhere. Any translation device you carry will fail on abstractions and succeed on plumbing. Learn the plumbing vocabulary first; you will be able to talk for days.
- **Do not raise the question of down.** The country below the falls is unvisited, and the reason given — *there is no reason to go down, and nothing down there wants anything up here* — is a whole cosmology in one sentence. Prodding at it is not curiosity; it is vandalism.

## Navigation and time

- **No sun, no shadows, no seasons, no star-fix under the cloud deck.** You will lose direction faster than you believe. Bring a compass and check it against known landmarks on arrival — welded ash can hold enough remanent magnetism to lie to you locally. Bring a second, non-magnetic method: pace counting, a sketch map, back-bearings off cliff steps.
- **Mark your crossing point obsessively** and in a way that will not be mistaken for a local marker or removed. On a plateau of identical pale benches, in identical white light, with the cloud down half of every day, losing the way home is a very ordinary mistake with a permanent consequence.
- **Carry a clock and keep a written log with dates.** With no seasons, no weather, no darkness at a varying hour and no biological change anywhere in the landscape, human time-sense unravels fast. Travellers routinely come back convinced they spent eleven days somewhere they spent five weeks. The two daily golden flares — the thinning of the deck at dawn and dusk when the whole wet shelf ignites — are your only natural clock, and they are beautiful enough to be a poor one.

## The mind

This is the part no packing list covers.

A world where nothing changes and nothing threatens you is, at first, the most restful place you have ever been. No predators, no insects, no weather, no cold, no night chill, no hunger emergency, nothing that wants anything from you. Your nervous system will unclench in ways you did not know it was clenched.

And then, somewhere in the second or third week, it goes the other way. The changelessness stops being peace and starts being a wall. There is no news. There is no future tense. The crops are cut a little every day, forever; the spring delivers what it delivered to the grandparents; the town is the right size and a thing that is the right size does not need to become another size. Some travellers find this the sanest proposition they have ever heard, and want to stay, and a few do. Others develop a specific, sharp, panicky grief, usually announced by an urge to make a loud noise for no reason.

Prepare for both:

- **Set an exit date before you go, write it down, and tell someone on the other side.** Do not renegotiate it from the inside. The inside is not a good place to renegotiate from.
- **Bring a project.** Mapping, surveying, language work, drawing. The mind does badly with unstructured time in an unchanging landscape.
- **Go with one other person if you can.** Not for safety from the world — the world is harmless — but for the silence.

## Leaving

Decontaminate outward as well as inward. Pollen, spores, cyanobacterial fragments and peat crumbs travel very happily in a pack seam, and they came from a world that has had two or three hundred million years fewer arguments than yours has, and would lose all of them — or, worse, win one somewhere warm and wet and unoccupied. Freeze, scrub, seal, inspect.

Take out every gram of what you brought and every gram of what came out of you.

And then understand what you have done in their terms. In a place where the only events are geological — a slab down in the night, a spring shifting twenty feet along a joint, a peat mat letting go and taking a path with it — **you are an event.** Your visit will be dated. It will be attached to a name in the way a rockfall is. It will be repeated, unchanged, in the memorised song of that town, alongside the year the flume was re-cut, for as long as the town is the right size — which is to say, as far as anyone there can imagine.

Behave in the way you would want to be remembered by people who will remember it exactly, and only, the way it happened, forever.

---

## If you remember only five things

1. **You are the contamination.** Carry everything out. Bring nothing alive.
2. **The rim is unmarked and the cloud sits on the ground every morning.** Do not walk in fog.
3. **Gas pools in hollows and clefts, and it is silent, invisible, and heavier than air.** Meter, or candle, or don't go in.
4. **Drink only from the spring the town drinks from.** Beautiful hot water is arsenic.
5. **Be quiet, be slow, and don't touch the drum.**
