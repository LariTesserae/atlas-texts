# Preparing for The Increment

## First, understand what you are

You are seventy-odd kilograms of below-cloud matter, walking.

The law of the place is not a regulation. It is the reason the place exists at all: nothing may be taken from below, and everything must be made from what is already up, or from what the air gives. You are neither. You are a lump of foreign carbon that will shed hair, skin, breath, grease, and water onto Course Ninety-One for the whole duration of your stay, and there is no clause that covers you.

The tenders have solved this the way they solve most things — with a small, generous fiction. They classify visitors as weather. The swifts are weather. Fog is weather. You are weather: something the air brought, that will pass, that leaves only damp behind. Everything below in this guide is about deserving that classification.

Behave like weather. Arrive, pass through, take nothing, leave no seed.

## Two months out: the body

**Isolate yourself.** There are a few hundred people on the terrace, and their immune histories are a closed ledger going back ninety-one courses. A head cold you would work through is, up there, a fortnight of six kilns tended by too few hands while the cloud rises a hand's width regardless. The cloud does not pause for anyone's chest infection. Give yourself three clear weeks away from crowds, schools, transit, children. If you cough once in the last five days, do not go. Go next pour.

**Acclimatize, and acclimatize to the wrong thing.** The pressure is low — low enough that bees are sullen and the pollination is done by hand. But it is *warm*, and it is saturated. Nothing in your training will have prepared you for thin air at greenhouse temperature and one hundred percent humidity, which is a genuinely strange physiological insult: you will overheat while short of breath and you will not be able to sweat your way out of it, because your sweat has nowhere to go. Spend at least three weeks at altitude beforehand, then a week doing light work in a steam room or a humid glasshouse. Learn what your own hard ceiling feels like at that combination. Learn to work slowly. Everyone there works slowly; you will think you are being polite by hurrying, and you will be wrong twice.

**Review every medication you take, and think about your urine.** This is not squeamishness. The quickener — the mineralizing moss cultured in the warm drains — is the single irreplaceable organism on the terrace, and the sanitary system feeds the drains. Antibiotics, antifungals, biocidal preservatives, certain hormonal compounds and heavy-metal traces will pass through you and into the culture. Nobody has ever run the experiment and nobody wants to. Declare everything. If you cannot stop taking something, offer to carry your own waste out in sealed vessels for the length of your stay, and mean it. This offer is usually accepted with visible relief and afterwards you will be treated well.

**Cut your hair short. Trim your nails. Shave what you can.** Not for tidiness. For seeds.

## One month out: the small trainings

**Eat their food before you eat their food.** Beans, greens, fern crosiers, something fermented and grey, fig when there is fig, and no salt. Move yourself onto a legume-heavy, low-sodium diet for a fortnight so that your gut has finished its argument before you arrive. The terrace is two hundred paces long, densely planted, and acoustically intimate. Voices don't carry but other things do.

Understand what the absence of salt will do to you. You will sweat constantly in that warm wet air and you will crave salt with an animal clarity, and there will be none, and you must not produce any. Salt does not exist there as a loose thing; it exists only bound into the body of the works. A shaker set down on a table would be an obscenity, a small white heap of the forbidden. Bring what you need for your own survival sealed, take it privately, and let no one see it. Better: train your palate down beforehand so you need less.

**Toughen your feet and then prepare to lose that advantage.** Everything is damp. Rope is heavy. Nothing dries. Trench foot and fungal infection are the actual, boring, likeliest thing to end your visit. Expect to be issued footwear — woven fiber over wood, usually, because deep lugs both hold foreign soil and chew the fresh arrises of new stone. Practice in something similar, wet, for weeks. Bring a small kit of your own foot care and use it religiously every single night, and change your socks more often than you think is reasonable.

**Buy new boots for the ascent and bake them.** The welts of your old boots hold soil from every field you have ever crossed. Soil is contraband of the most serious kind: it is both below-matter and a delivery system for a hundred seeds.

**Practice with a sable brush.** Get beans or squash flowering in a pot and learn the stroke — the touch on the anther, the transfer, the pace. If you are invited to the brushing hour at dusk you should be able to join the line without being taught, and you should be silent the whole time. Learn this because it is the one thing you can offer that costs them nothing.

## Packing: the art of arriving with almost nothing

Everything you carry, you carry up ladders, and everything you carry is an import. The correct kit is embarrassingly small.

- One set of working clothes, wool or a wool-like fiber that stays warm damp, because damp is the permanent condition. No second set. You will not be dry either way and the second set is just more foreign mass.
- Gloves that grip when wet, and more gloves. The stone has arrises that will open a thumb, no weather has yet had time to soften anything, and you are about to spend your visit putting your hands on the youngest edges in the world.
- A broad soft hat. The light comes *up* off the cloud floor as much as down. You will burn under your chin, inside your nostrils, on the underside of your jaw and the lobes of your ears. Travelers who prepare for overhead sun come back looking scalded from beneath.
- Foot care. Hand care. A needle and thread. Nothing that beeps, blinks, or hums.
- Nothing with velcro, no fleece pile, no rolled cuffs, no turn-ups, no seams that open upward. Every one of these is a seed pocket. Go over your kit with a lens and a stiff brush before you leave, and expect the tenders to do it again, better.
- No seeds. No fruit. No nuts. No dried flowers, no herbal tea, no unmilled grain, no bird-food in an old coat pocket from a winter you have forgotten. One burr in a cuff is a second fig, which is to say a second future, arriving unasked.
- Consider bringing exactly one worn thing: a spoon, a knife handle, a bead, something small your family has rubbed smooth over decades. Declare it as **shown, not given**. This is a recognized category. There is nothing smooth up there; there is no patina, no wear, no ancestor-object; everything on the terrace is younger than you are. A worn spoon will be passed hand to hand for an evening with an attention that will unsettle you. Then you take it home. Do not leave it. Leaving it would be kind and would be a violation, and they would have to decide what to do with it, and that is not a gift, that is a burden dressed as one.

## The ascent

Most insertion points put you below the cloud, and you climb.

It is twenty to fifty minutes of ladders in whiteout, on wet rungs, with your hands on rope that weighs twice what rope should weigh, and with no usable sense of height at all. You will pass the drowned courses. Eighty-Seven's coping will come out of the white beside your knee with its dark moss still growing, an urn nobody bothered to raise, a bronze bracket gone the color of a leaf, every edge as sharp and unfinished as the day it was left, because nothing down there weathers either — it only drowns.

Look, once, deliberately. Then keep climbing and do not narrate it. Every visitor wants to say something about it and there is nothing to say that the tenders have not already thought for ninety-one courses.

Expect a quarantine at a lower course before you are allowed onto the newest ground: some days in a warm drain corridor while your clothes are boiled, your hair combed, your kit brushed over a white sheet and the sheet inspected. Submit to all of it cheerfully. It is not suspicion of you. It is the same procedure applied to sacks, timber, and their own children returning from an errand.

## On the terrace: how to move

**Walk with attention.** Not for your safety, though the arrises will cut you. For the stone. You are one of the first things ever to touch these blocks; you are, in a small literal sense, the beginning of their erosion.

**Moss is crop.** Every green seam is deliberate and most of it is quickener stock. Do not scuff it, do not sit on it, do not helpfully clean anything. There is no weeding here. There is only harvest and loss.

**Do not lean on the parapets.** Their outer faces are ribbed with hundreds of little fog-vanes and the whole water economy runs through them. Skin oil clogs vanes. Similarly: do not touch the fleece-combs, do not squeeze them to see how wet they are, do not still them to hear the room without their ticking.

**Never set anything down on the pavement.** Rain here has no habits yet. It sheets sideways, ponds where no one predicted, and makes a lake in a hollow for eleven hours before vanishing. Your bedding, your pack, your boots: hung, always, never grounded.

**Drop nothing over the parapet.** Anything that goes into the cloud is finished with, permanently, and becomes part of the only archaeology they have. On a thin evening, twenty meters down, a stranger's dropped pen will be visible for as long as the ledgers last, an item of foreign matter written into their record by a clumsy hand. This is a heavier thing than it sounds. Carry nothing loose. Pocket everything. Tie on what you can.

**Learn to be small in a small place.** Two hundred paces by a hundred and thirty. You cannot walk away from an argument, a smell, a person, or your own mood. Do not begin anything on the Increment that cannot be finished on a village green.
