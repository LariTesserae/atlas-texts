# Going to the Sill: A Traveller's Preparation

## Understand first what kind of place you are entering

Most worlds you will visit are worlds of erosion. Things wear away, rot, burn, get lost; you can drop something and never see it again. The Sill is the opposite. It is a place of **addition**. Everything that happens there is kept. The water lays down a record of every drip, every roof, every standing bird, every boot-print you make in soft young stone at the wrong hour of the morning.

Prepare accordingly. You are not visiting a landscape. You are visiting a slow and impartial archivist, and it will file you too if you stand still long enough.

The second thing: it is a **listening** culture in a nearly silent place, and you are, whether you believe it or not, a very loud animal. Everything in this preparation bends toward that one problem.

---

## The year before: your ears

Do this seriously or do not go.

Spend the months before your journey getting quiet. Avoid engines, crowds, music through the head, anything percussive. If your world has such a thing, have your hearing measured; if you have ringing in your ears, you are functionally deaf on the Sill and should not put other people's families under your inattention. The warning sign there is a *click* — dry, small, like a beetle in plaster — and it comes from behind a rim four days before that rim lets go. Two hundred people depend on the pooled hearing of everyone present. A guest who cannot hear is not merely useless; they are a hole in the net.

Practise silence, too, not as a discipline but as a habit. Go a week without speaking above a murmur. You will discover how much noise you make involuntarily: throat-clearing, sighing, the slap of your feet, the creak of your knees, the way you set a cup down. All of that must go. Learn to cough into a folded felt pad held against the mouth. You will cough, at that altitude; everyone does; the trick is to make it a small warm event inside your own coat.

## The months before: your hands

There is a hand-language of about six hundred signs, spoken at conversational speed, and it is witty. You will not learn it. Learn thirty signs and learn them until they are reflex, because you will be using them in blinding light with your eyes streaming.

The essential thirty, in rough order of how badly you will need them:

*Stop. Hot. Too hot. Thin — don't stand. I don't understand. Again, slower. Forgive me. Where do I put my feet? May I have your shade. Thank you for the shade. Yes. No. I hear a click — there. Wait. Come. Help. Water, drinkable. Sick. I need to piss. Sleep. Hungry. Mine. Not mine. Visitor, from below. My name. Your name. Wait for me. Go without me. I am leaving tomorrow. Good.*

Accept before you go that you will spend your entire visit as a large, slow, half-witted child. The Sill's people are dry and funny with their hands, and much of the humour within your first fortnight will be about you. Take it well. Taking it well is most of your diplomacy.

## Your body

Acclimatise in the valley for a minimum of ten days, ideally three weeks, before you start up. The Sill sits high; the air is thin as pulled glass; there is no way to be carried out if your lungs fill. If you have a bad heart, do not go. If you are pregnant, do not go. If your eyes are already weak, do not go — the light will finish them.

**Your eyes are the thing most likely to be destroyed.** Snowblindness on the Sill can take you in under two hours, because the light arrives from above, from the side, and *from underneath*, off ten thousand wet mirrors. Bring visors — good ones, slit-type, and a spare pair, and a spare for the spare. The local horn slit-visors are better than most engineered lenses because they cut the field down to a letterbox and stop the flood coming up off the pools. Buy or trade for a local pair on your first day and wear those. Blacken the skin around your eyes with charcoal from the moment you leave the gorge; it is not decoration, it is the difference between seeing on day four and not.

**You will sunburn in places you did not know could burn.** Under the chin. Inside the nostrils. The undersides of the ears and the arch of the upper lip. The roof of your mouth, if you walk open-mouthed panting at altitude, which you will. Keep your mouth shut; breathe through the nose; grease the underside of everything.

**Rings.** Take them off before you arrive and put them in a sealed pouch. Rings grow shut. So do bracelets, so do the eyelets of boots, so does the hinge of anything hinged. If you wear a ring on the Sill for six weeks you will come down with a stone ring, and it will not be coming off.

**Hair.** Cut it short. Shave the beard. Mineral-laden mist sets in hair like size in canvas; a long beard on the Sill becomes a heavy, ticking, crackling thing that you will end up cutting off with an obsidian flake in an unlit room while a small girl watches you and does not laugh, which is worse.

**Nails and skin.** The water is alkaline and will strip the grease out of your hands until they split. Bring a good animal fat and use it every night.

**Guts.** You will be eating pale blind shrimp, fly-larvae pressed into cakes, algae bread, and eggs cooked in a scalding pool. It is good food. It will still take your digestion a week to make peace with it. Bring nothing complicated for this; bring salt, and bring patience.

## What to carry

**Felt.** Felt-soled boots, two pairs, and felt sleeves for the handles of any tool. Everything that could knock against anything must be muffled before you reach the lip of the gorge. Do this in the gorge, not at the top; arrive already quiet.

**A probing staff.** Not for walking — for reading. A shaft of antler or bone, obsidian-tipped, shoulder-height. You will use it to break the reflection on a pool before you go near it and to test the temperature and the crust. Learn to use it *silently*: dip and drag, never tap. A tapping staff makes precisely the sound that means a rim is failing, and you do not want to be the visitor who emptied a quarter of the town at dusk for nothing.

**Grease, charcoal, spare visors, a felt cough-pad, a good horn scraper.**

**A waxed, sealed, oiled box** for anything you want to bring home unchanged: notes, images, keepsakes. Anything left in the open air of the Sill for a season comes back glazed. Do not put down, in the wet, anything you want back. That includes a coat. That includes a child's toy. That includes your hand, if you are sitting and dreaming.

## What not to carry

**Nothing that ticks.** No mechanical watch, no clockwork, no instrument with an escapement. The tick is the alarm sound of that world. A ticking object in your pack is an obscenity and a genuine hazard.

**Nothing that whistles.** No kettle, no flute, no signalling whistle, and nothing with a two-note voice. The two-note whistle means *your terrace is going*. It is sounded perhaps four times a year. You do not want to know what it costs you socially to have sounded it for a joke.

**Nothing that hammers, rings, or barks.** No bell, no metal cup, no dog unless it is a dog you have personally proved will not speak.

**No iron.** It will be rotten in a month, it is useless there, and it marks you as someone who has not understood the place. Bone, horn, obsidian, hard young sinter. That is the whole of their toolkit and it is sufficient.

**No firewood, and no interest in the willow grove.** The grove at the Cold Lip is holy, and it is also the entire fuel and building-material reserve of two hundred people in a place where dry means cold and cold means dead in three days. Do not touch it, do not ask about cutting it, do not admire it out loud with your hands.

## Gifts

You should arrive with gifts, and you should think hard about them, because the wrong gift here is a bomb.

**Charcoal** is the best gift in the world. They make theirs from the sacred grove, in tiny quantities, for blackening eyelids. Bring a good sack of clean hardwood charcoal from below and you have relieved the grove for a year. They will understand exactly what you have done.

**Obsidian**, worked or unworked. **Salt. Dried fruit. Good felt. Ibex horn. Sinew.**

**Cloth that makes shade** is the difficult one. Shade on the Sill is the scarce resource — hoarded, owned, inherited, negotiated like water rights elsewhere. If you arrive with a rolled awning of light canvas, you have arrived carrying wealth on a scale that will distort every conversation you have. Bring it if you like, but bring it knowing that: give it away early, to the whole town rather than to a household, or pitch it and let anyone sit under it, and do not be seen deciding who may. To own shade there and portion it out is to make yourself a small king among people who did not ask for one.

## The approach, and how to arrive

A day and a half up from the dark valley, following the warm stream that smells of eggs and iron. Start early, climb slowly, and plan to reach the gorge mouth in the afternoon rather than at midday; coming out of shadow into that light at noon is a physical blow and you will be no use for an hour.

Put on your visors *in the gorge*, before the light hits. Muffle everything. Then walk out onto the lip.

**Then stop, and sit down, and do nothing.**

Do not walk into the town. Do not call out. Sit on the rock at the edge with your hands empty and visible on your knees and wait to be seen, which will take a few minutes, because everyone there is facing their work and the light makes a newcomer on the lip into a black cut-out that the eye slides off. Someone will come. They will look at your boots first — they are checking your soles for felt — and then at your hands.

Sign: *visitor, from below. Forgive me. Where do I put my feet?*

That is the correct arrival. It will be told about for a decade and it will cost you nothing.

## Walking on the Sill

Walk only where you have seen a local walk, and walk behind them, in their line, for the first three days at minimum. The white crust is a process, not a material: some of it is a foot thick and will hold an ox, some of it is a young shell over a cavity of water just short of scalding, and from above, in a light that casts no shadow at all, **they look identical.** You have no depth perception here and no shadow to give you scale. This is the single most dangerous fact about the place, and it is the reason for the first thing children are taught: *you have no shadow here, so watch your feet.*

Obey the taboo. **Do not step into the sky.** When a pool is holding a perfect mirror of the cirque, you cannot see its floor or judge its heat; break the reflection with your staff first, always, every time, even when you are certain, especially when you are in a hurry. There is an older reason for the taboo which nobody will explain to you. Obey it anyway. In my experience the rules that people cannot explain in places like this are the ones that were paid for.

Scrape your boot-soles every night. Stone grows on them. A fortnight of neglect and you are walking on platforms, half an inch of sinter between you and the crust, and you have lost the one sense that was keeping you alive: the feel, through the sole, of whether the ground beneath you is solid or is a drum.

## Heat and water

The vents come up just short of pain. "Just short of pain" is a judgement made by people whose skin has been in that water since birth. Assume every channel will scald you and test everything with the staff, then the back of the wrist, then in.

Drink a great deal, and drink from where you are told to drink. The water is loaded — that is the entire principle of the place — and you are not adapted to its mineral load. Given a choice, take cooled snowmelt. If you are there more than a month, expect stones, expect pain, and expect to come down early.

## Living in a closing house

If you are lucky you will be lent a room. It will be a hard bright pod of translucent stone, warm from beneath, humming very faintly with the flow underneath the floor. It will be too low for you. The floor is rising and the ceiling is coming down to meet a sitting head, and the doorway narrows a fingerbreadth a season.

Some travellers cannot bear this and discover it at two in the morning. If you suspect you are one of them, sleep your first nights under an overhang and find out cheaply.

Learn to scrape the threshold. It is the constant small labour of the Sill, done with horn and ground quartz, and doing your own share of it without being asked is worth more to your hosts than any gift you carried up.

**Be in the open, with a horn paddle, in the first hour of every day**, breaking the inch of feathered ice that the night lays over the whole town. Everyone does this. Everyone. It is as close as the Sill comes to a liturgy, and it is the one ritual a visitor is permitted to join on the first morning without asking. Do it badly. Do it anyway.

## Manners

**Shade.** If you are given shade, remember it, and say so later, in front of others. Shade-debts are the currency of the place. If you are asked for shade, give it. If you have none to give, sign so plainly and without embarrassment.

**The shaded.** The old, whose eyes have gone milky, are led about by dogs and are consulted because they have stopped seeing and started listening. Do not pity them and do not raise your voice at them; they can hear a rim click at sixty paces, which is more than you can do. If one of them tilts their head toward you, stop moving until they stop.

**Sealed rooms.** When a new channel cuts down through an old level and opens a window into an encased past — a loom, a cradle, a lamp still standing on a shelf under two hands' thickness of clear stone — you may go and look. Go quietly, look for a while, and leave. **Do not take anything. Do not reach in. Do not clean the surface to see better.** People come and look at their great-great-grandmothers' furniture and then go back to work; match their pace. A visitor lingering, or worse, measuring, will not be told off. It will simply be noticed, and you will find fewer hands talking to you.

**Combers.** The long-bodied whiskered swimmers with paddle hands are not tame and are not hunted. They dig, they keep the channels open, and they decide — more than the people do — where the water goes next year. They are bribed with heaped shrimp-cake at the mouths of channels the town wants kept clear.

**Never feed a comber on your own initiative.** Feeding is not kindness there; it is policy. Every cake is a vote about which quarter of the town has water in two years' time. A well-meaning visitor scattering shrimp-cake at a channel mouth is a foreigner casting ballots in an election he has not understood. Watch them all you like. Give them room. If one is coming along a channel, get out of it — not because they are dangerous but because they are working and you are not.

**Do not block a road without leaving another.** This applies to combers and, you will find, to people.

## If the whistle sounds

Two notes. It means *your terrace is going*.

Do not look for the water. Look for the nearest adult and go where they go, immediately, without a question, carrying nothing. Anything you stop to pick up is a thing you are choosing over your legs. The failures are rarely fatal by force — the water never falls far — but they scald, and worse, they strand a quarter dry, and dry means cold, and cold at that altitude means three days to move or start burning the holy grove.

Afterwards, help carry baskets. Do not ask what happened yet.

## If you die there

Ask early, in a quiet room at night, what is done. Have an answer ready. It matters, because there are two possibilities and they are not the same: over the Cold Lip into the cloud, or left in the warm shallows for the stone to take, the way the stone takes a standing wader and holds it mid-stride in the wall forever as a thin bright swelling with a beak.

You should decide which of those you would want, and tell someone, in words, at night, close to the ear.

## Leaving

Stay less time than you want to. Two hundred people on a shelf you can walk in the time a pot boils have a very thin margin of shrimp, shade and attention, and a guest eats all three. A fortnight is generous. A season is an imposition unless you have been asked.

Go down in the early morning, and say your goodbyes the night before, indoors, murmured, because the morning belongs to breaking ice.

**Coming down, you will be in trouble in three ways.**

Your eyes: after weeks of unfiltered white you will be night-blind in the gorge and you will fall. Descend slowly, and keep a hand on the rock.

Your ears: the valley will sound like a war. A stream you walked up without noticing will be unbearable. A voice at ordinary volume will feel like violence. This passes in a few weeks; until then, protect yourself, and do not go straight into a town. Build a quiet stop into your itinerary — three or four days somewhere with nothing in it — or you will arrive home raw and snap at someone you love.

Your hands: you will keep signing at people for months, and they will not understand, and you will find this unexpectedly painful.

## One last thing

You will come down carrying a few ounces of the Sill whether you like it or not: a glaze on your boot-welts, a whitening in the seams of your pack, a faint scale in the lid of your box. Do not scrub it all off in the first inn. Let one thing keep it.

Nothing is lost up there. It is the only place I know where that is literally true, and it takes a while to work out whether it is a comfort or the opposite. Most travellers come down having decided, and most of them change their minds later.
