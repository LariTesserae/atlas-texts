# Preparing for the Sill

## First, decide which of the three trips you are taking

There are only three, practically speaking, and they are not variations on one journey but three different undertakings with three different mortality rates.

**The Rim trip.** Up the face, out onto the quilt for a few kilometres, sleep two or three nights in the outer galleries where the light is best, come down. Ten days on top at most. This is the trip that people take and survive, and it is not a lesser experience — the rim is the only place on the Sill where you can see the sky and where the acoustics are broken up by the hiss of the weeping, so it is also the only place where an unprepared mind is not being slowly taken apart. Most of what follows applies.

**The Interior trip.** Anything beyond about forty kilometres in. Three to six weeks, guided, roped, provisioned from below or resupplied by relay. This is the trip where you meet the doorframes. This is the trip that changes people, in ways that are well documented and boring to the people it has happened to, and which they will not usefully describe to you.

**The Flower.** Do not plan this as your first visit. Read the last section before you let anyone talk you into it.

Whichever you choose, book your acclimatisation before you book your guide. The guides are the constraint everyone plans for and the alveoli are the constraint that actually kills.

## The body

**Altitude, and the particular cruelty of this altitude.** The tableland stands four thousand metres up. That alone would demand three weeks of staged acclimatisation. But the Sill is also blood-warm and saturated — the air is at or very near its dew point at something like thirty degrees, which means a large fraction of the little pressure available up there is being spent on water vapour rather than on oxygen. You will be breathing what feels, to the chest, like five thousand metres or worse, in shirtsleeves, sweating, unable to cool by evaporation because there is nowhere for the sweat to go.

Budget six weeks of graded acclimatisation in a warm-humid chamber if your origin world has such a thing, or four weeks camped at the escarpment base where the returning haze keeps the air permanently at ninety-odd percent. Learn what your own altitude illness looks like — everyone's is idiosyncratic — because the Sill removes your usual cues. There is no cold, no exertion in the classic sense, no thin dry gasping. Hypoxia up there arrives disguised as *contentment*. Men have sat down on a warm soft floor in dim green light feeling absolutely marvellous and have not got up.

Carry a pulse oximeter and treat the number, not the feeling. Two of them, actually; see the section on materials.

**Hair.** Shave it. All of it, everywhere, the day before the ascent, and again every third day on top. Anything with a follicle to grip becomes a trellis. This is not vanity or fastidiousness; a colonised scalp is a fortnight of misery and a colonised groin is an evacuation.

**Feet.** This is the real medicine of the Sill. You are going to a place with no dry, ever, at any hour, for any purpose. Trench foot is not a risk, it is a schedule. Prepare as follows:

- Trim nails back hard, and file the corners round, a week beforehand so the cuts have closed.
- Bring three times the barrier grease you think you need. Silicone-based, not petroleum — petroleum-based greases feed things up there.
- Bring a foot-drying rig. A battery blower, a hammock-slung stirrup that holds your soles into the airstream, an hour a night. This will sound absurd when you are packing and it will be the single most important hour of your day by the fifth night.
- Accept that you will lose nails. Plan the trip so that the last week is not the week you need your feet.

**Skin generally.** Within a day of arrival your skin begins to soften; by two weeks there is a faint green cast in the creases; by a month the Sill has begun, in its unhurried and entirely impersonal way, to incorporate you. This is not infection in the sense you are used to. It is annexation. It does not hurt and it does not smell and it does not resolve on its own. The response is mechanical: a stiff dry brush and a hard scrape of every fold, crease, and web every night, plus alcohol on the scalp, ears, and nostrils. The ear canals are the ones people forget. A drop of oil in each ear at night, and clean them out morning and evening, or you will come down with a biofilm in your hearing that takes months to clear and that hisses.

If your visit runs past six weeks, you should understand that some of the marking will be permanent. There is a look that long-interior guides have — a sort of translucency at the wrists — and they do not consider it a disfigurement.

**Immunology and multiverse biosecurity.** Ask your outfitter for the full Sill panel, and be sceptical if they only offer you the gut course. The Sill is one organism. Its sap is chemically continuous with everything you will touch, breathe, and be dripped on. That means the challenge is not a menagerie of pathogens but a single, vast, slow biochemistry that has never in its history encountered anything it could not eventually envelop, and which cannot recognise you as an exception because it has no faculty for exceptions.

Practically: expect an aggressive sensitisation schedule beforehand, expect to carry a steroid course and a full anaphylaxis kit even if you have never reacted to anything, and expect the quarantine on the way out to be longer and more thorough than the medical on the way in. See **Descent**.

## Materials doctrine

Everything you bring must be chosen on the assumption that it will be permanently wet, permanently warm, and gently colonised.

**Forbidden or futile:** leather (eaten within a fortnight), cotton and wool and most bast fibres (eaten, and heavy, and never dry), untreated wood (welcomed into the family), anything with a seam that can hold a millimetre of water and a spore, anything that needs to be dried to work, anything that needs heat to be repaired.

**Good:** silicone, high-density plastics, sealed ceramics, titanium, glass. Smooth surfaces without texture. Fewer seams. Sealed monolithic units rather than serviceable assemblies, because you will not be servicing anything up there.

**Redundancy over repairability.** On a world with fire, a broken thing is a thing to be mended. Bring two of everything critical and a third of anything electronic. Cyanoacrylate is the one adhesive that thrives here — it cures on moisture — so carry a lot of it, and it is the only field repair you will actually manage.

**Clothing.** Seamless, minimal, sheddable, and light-coloured only so that you can see what is growing on it. Long sleeves are for abrasion, not warmth — you will never be cold on the Sill except for the two hours a day on the face during the ascent, which is a different climate entirely and needs its own separate set of gear that you will then have to carry for the rest of the trip. Many parties cache the cold-and-wet ascent kit at the rim. Cache it *hanging*, off the quilt, in a sealed bag, and mark it in a way that is not a shape (see **Shape discipline**).

## Fire: the absolute rule

There has never been a fire on the Sill. Not once, in a duration long enough to have laid down nine hundred metres of peat. Everything about the place — the softness of the animals, the absence of tools, the survival of thresholds made by people who never smelted anything — follows from that.

You will be carrying a stove or a heater or a lamp, because your world's outfitters sell them and because it seems reasonable, and the guides will search your bags for them and throw them off the rim. Comply cheerfully.

The reason is not sentimental, though the sentimental reason is sufficient. The reason is that the peat below you is exhaling, that the gas is not distributed evenly but pools in the low swells and gathers in pockets that release in that slow *whumph* every few days, and that a flame at the wrong moment does not start a bonfire — it starts a hole. A hole that goes down. A hole that smoulders in a substrate the size of a continent, in the one place in the cosmos that has never had to develop a response to that, and it will not stop, and there is nothing up there that could put it out.

So: chemical light only. Sealed electric lamps, batteries you brought and will carry out, and a red or amber filter for night use so that you keep your dark-adaptation, which you will need more than you expect. Bring more batteries than you would for an equivalent trip anywhere else, because the days are dusk and the nights are absolute.

## Water and food

There is water everywhere and almost none of it is yours.

The lakes are the Sill's held water — blood-warm, black, full of the organism's own chemistry. The veins under the quilt are the same. Sap is the same. You can drink all of it in an emergency and you will survive drinking it once. Drinking it habitually is how people end up on the wrong side of annexation, because you are not just taking in metals and alkaloids, you are taking in the signalling molecules by which the Sill coordinates its own body, and it turns out that a human gut is not immune to being addressed.

**Drink rain.** It rains most nights, warm and vertical, and the whole trick is to intercept it before the Sill does. Rig a catch above your hammock, high, on inert sheet — a simple silicone tarp funnelling into a bladder — and take the middle of the fall, not the first minute. Filter for particulates and UV it. You will get four to six litres a night on a normal night, which is roughly what you need in that heat, and on a dry night you will be thirsty, and that is why you also carry a two-day reserve at all times, always, even walking to relieve yourself.

**Food you bring.** All of it. Every calorie, hauled four thousand metres or paid for in guide-portage. This is the single largest line in your budget and the reason short trips are cheap and long trips are for institutions.

Do not eat the fauna. It is soft, abundant, slow, and trivially catchable, which is exactly the trap. It is also made of the same continuous chemistry as everything else, riddled with things that have had a very long time to get good at living inside soft warm bodies, and — the point that matters — it cannot be cooked. Nothing on the Sill has ever been cooked. You would be eating raw meat from an alien phylum in the wettest place there is. Parties that have tried it describe the sequence of events with a consistency that suggests it is not bad luck.

## The ascent

Nobody warns you enough about the last thirty metres.

The face itself is a long, cold, streaming rock climb — four thousand metres of it, done in stages over four to six days on fixed lines maintained by the rim companies, inside the standing cylinder of cloud, in a permanent fine rain that is falling *past* you and also, disconcertingly, *rising* past you. It is loud in the lower third and then it becomes that even hiss, and then near the top it is nearly silent, and you climb up out of the noise into the quiet the way you'd surface from water. It is a rock climb and it wants rock-climb preparation: rope work, ascender work, days of it, and a head for exposure that you should test at home before you spend the money.

But the top of the escarpment is not rock. The quilt overhangs. The living mat grows out past the lip and hangs over it in a dripping curtain, metres of it, tearing away in slabs under its own weight and dropping into the haze. You do not climb onto the Sill. You come up *underneath* it, into a warm green half-light full of hanging roots, and then you cut a way up through two metres of living tissue and are hauled out through the hole, and the guides will already be cutting the next one because the one you came through will be substantially closed by morning.

People find this part harder than the four thousand metres. It is the only moment on the trip where the place does something to you quickly.

## Movement discipline

**Probe and rope.** Travel roped, in a line, with a probe pole, exactly as you would on a crevassed glacier — and with more discipline than that, because a crevasse at least stays open behind you. The quilt thins without warning, and the lakes sit slightly *above* the surrounding country behind a raised lip of living moss, so the ground can rise gently in front of you and be nothing. If you go through, you are in blood-warm black water with a soft living lid over your head that closed as you fell. Nobody self-rescues from that without a knife they can reach one-handed at chest height and a rope on their harness. Both, always, from the moment you leave the rim.

**The stillness rule.** Fifteen minutes standing and the quilt begins to grow over your feet. It is gentle and it comes away easily and it is not a hazard on its own; the hazard is the habit. Keep a cadence. Shift your weight. When you stop, stop *on your sit-plate* — a smooth silicone or heartwood pad, carried on the outside of your pack, that the mat will not bond to — and set your feet on it too. Every long-interior party has one member whose job is to call the halts and count them.

**Silence.** Nothing on the Sill calls. Nothing sings. There is no bird noise
