# Preparing for the Shelf

## First: understand what kind of danger this is

The Shelf will not kill you quickly. There is nothing on it that bites, no cliff you can't see coming, no weather that arrives in an hour. It kills the way a wet sponge kills — slowly, by cold, by never letting you dry, by the accumulation of small unhealed things. And long before it endangers your body it will embarrass you, because almost every reflex an ordinary human has for moving through a built environment is wrong here.

You will want to hammer a peg. You will want to call across a courtyard. You will want to sit on a low white wall. You will want to help. Each of these is a small vandalism in a place where damage takes years to grow back.

Plan your preparation around that, not around survival gear.

---

## Six months out: the body

**Altitude.** The plateau starts above 3,000 m and doesn't come back down. There is no descending to sleep low. Arrive already acclimatised — spend at least three weeks above 2,500 m somewhere else first, and cross in at walking pace if your method allows it. Carry acetazolamide and know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral oedema in yourself and in anyone you travel with, because there is no evacuation. There is no downhill within four days' walk, and the four days' walk is through ruin.

**Teeth.** Get everything done. Crowns, extractions, the molar you've been ignoring. There is no metal on the Shelf, no dentistry, and — this is the part people don't think about — no way to relieve pain with heat. A cracked tooth on the Shelf is three months of misery.

**Kidneys.** You will be drinking water that is at the absolute ceiling of what it can carry in dissolved lime, and you will be drinking a lot of it, because thin dry air strips water out of you through the lungs faster than thirst reports it. If you have ever passed a stone, do not go. If you haven't, arrange a blood panel, and pack potassium citrate. The locals have adapted over thousands of years; you have not.

**Fat.** Arrive four or five kilos heavy. The diet is pressed algae cake, fermented in stone jars; a parsnip-like taproot; inch-long shrimp by the handful; snails. It is adequate, ancient, and almost completely without fat. At altitude, in permanent damp, you will burn through yourself. You will also need two to three weeks of gut transition; bring your own calories for that period and taper, don't switch.

**Quarantine.** Take this seriously. Half a million rooms, one plateau, no contact with anywhere. A common cold you carry in casually could empty districts. Isolate for two full weeks before you cross, and if you develop so much as a sore throat, postpone. This is the single most likely way for a visitor to do catastrophic harm, and it requires no malice at all.

## Three months out: the senses

These are trainable, and untrained they will make you unbearable to be near.

**Practise speaking at half a metre.** Not whispering — quiet, ordinary, pitched low, with your face close enough that you feel the other person's breath. Most humans find this range unbearable and instinctively step back mid-sentence. On the Shelf, stepping back is the insult; it reads roughly as *I am ending this*. Practise with a friend until you can hold a fifteen-minute conversation at arm's length minus an elbow without drifting.

**Practise not striking things.** Spend a week noticing every percussive act you commit: setting a cup down, knocking on a door, tapping a pen, closing a lid, clapping, drumming a finger, stamping mud off a boot, snapping your fingers to make a point. Replace each one. Learn to set things down in two stages — contact, then weight. You want this automatic before you arrive, because the correction is not a shout. Someone will walk over, stand very close, and say something short and quiet with their face near yours. Almost everyone reports this as far worse than being yelled at.

**Practise stillness.** Not meditation — just sitting outdoors, in cool damp, doing nothing visible, for two hours. It is the fundamental competence of the place. Every wall on the Shelf is the residue of somebody being patient in the right spot.

**Glare.** If you can get to a snowfield or a salt flat, go, and spend days in it. The Shelf's light comes from above through luminous cloud and back up off ten thousand square kilometres of wet white stone. There is no shadow at midday. Depth perception fails; a finger-high terrace rim vanishes entirely into flat white, and you will catch your toe on it, and it will break, and it will take four years to grow back.

## What to pack

The governing principles: **soft, quiet, non-metallic, works wet, leaves nothing in the water.**

**Eyes.** Three pairs of very dark glasses — side shields if possible — plus a spare lens-less slit visor of leather or stiff felt. Assume you will lose one pair and sit on another. Day one of eye protection failure is discomfort; day two is snow blindness, which is agonising, and which here has no dark room to recover in. Adopt the local brow-visor as soon as one is offered, and take the soot: blackening under the eyes and on the cheekbones is not decoration, it cuts the up-glare off the terraces by a genuinely useful margin.

**Clothing.** Wool and felt. No synthetics — they rustle, they squeak, they crackle against themselves, and in a city where a shout dies in twenty paces your jacket becomes a public nuisance. No bright colour; in that light a red shell is an assault. Bring: two thick wool layers, an oiled or heavily felted outer, felt over-socks, a felt cap. Fastenings should be ties and bone or horn toggles. No zips, no Velcro, no snaps, no buckles. Nothing that clicks when you turn over in your sleep.

**Feet.** This is your real vulnerability. The locals go barefoot on damp gritty limestone with soles built over a lifetime; you cannot borrow that in a season. Bring soft-soled felt boots or thin flexible sandals that let you feel a rim before you break it — no lugged soles, which shear the terrace lips. Bring six pairs of wool socks, a closed-cell foam sleeping mat, and a large tin of lanolin. Your night ritual, every night, without exception, is: feet out, feet dried against your own body, feet greased. Immersion foot and fungal maceration are what actually end visits here. Pack antifungal powder and know that the local equivalent — a fine dry powder ground from dead quarter stone, the same stuff used to whiten felt — works, and is freely given.

**Water.** You do not need a purifier, and it wouldn't survive anyway: a membrane filter will cement itself shut with calcite inside a week. Drink from seep heads, where the water leaves the rock — it is groundwater, hard as nails and essentially clean. Do not drink from terrace films. The films are a crop and a fishery and other people's washing. Carry a soft flask, not a rigid bottle; a rigid bottle knocks.

**Light.** A single dim red light, used indoors, sparingly, shielded. Understand what you are carrying: in a place where an entire household's heat is one banked peat coal in a stone cup, a torch is not a convenience, it is an event. Do not flash it around. Do not use it to be helpful. Nights are dark, and dark is simply how the nights are.

**Fire.** Don't. No stove, no lighter, no fuel tablets, no striker. Not because it's forbidden — because it isn't forbidden, and because there is no wood, and because handing out fire in a civilisation whose entire inheritance of warmth is a carried ember is not a gift, it's a detonation. Accept that for the length of your stay nothing you eat or drink will ever be warm, and that you will dry your socks with your own abdomen, and plan your insulation accordingly.

**Hygiene.** No soap. No shampoo. No chemical sunscreen. No toothpaste, no insect repellent, no scented anything. Everything you rinse off goes into a seep, and the seep goes into somebody's shrimp runnel and somebody's algae film and somebody's cup. Use grit, wet moss, and pressed felt, as everyone does. For sun, use cloth and soot, not cream. If you take any long-term medication, be aware that its metabolites go the same place, and mention it — the sentence "where should I piss" is one of the first three you should learn in the local tongue, and is asked without embarrassment.

**Tools.** A bone or antler knife if you have one. A bone file instead of nail clippers. Reusable menstrual equipment, disposed into peat. A notebook of rag paper and a soft pencil — no clicking pens, no shutter sound, no device that beeps or chimes or vibrates against stone. If you must bring a camera, bring one you can silence entirely, keep the lens covered against spray, and expect to spend an hour a day scraping a white haze off the glass.

**Leave behind:** anything metal that will be seen, anything that whirs, anything with an alarm, anything that could be construed as a gift of technique. Seeds and living plants above all — a hardy exotic moss loosed on the Shelf is not a souvenir, it is an extinction event in a place where the entire biosphere is knee-high.

## Arrival

If you have any say in where you land, land **up-gradient**, on unbuilt terrace, and walk in.

Up-gradient is the direction of the young, of the unbuilt, of water that hasn't been used yet. Down-gradient is the direction of the dead, of dry quarters and hollow roofs and the four-day band of ruin. Every stair, every arcade, every prayer and every verb tense on the plateau is oriented to that axis. Coming in from below means arriving out of the necropolis, and while nobody will stop you, it will colour everything about how you are understood, possibly permanently.

Do not walk up to a house. Do not knock — nothing is struck, and there is nothing to knock on but a plug of packed peat.

Find the edge of an inhabited terrace, within sight of people, at a spot where you are obviously not standing in any seep's path. **Sit down. Put your hands where they can be seen. Wait.**

Wait a long time. Hours is normal. This is not a test; it is simply how the place works, and it is also the only introduction the Shelf has a grammar for. You are performing, in the only vocabulary available, the sentence *I am someone who can be patient in the right spot.* That sentence is the foundation of their entire civilisation, and it is the single most persuasive thing you will ever say there.

When someone comes, they will come close — closer than you want — and speak quietly. Do not step back. Do not raise your voice to match the hiss of the water; you will be perfectly audible. Lower it instead.

To get anyone's attention afterwards, at any distance, you drop a pebble into a runnel and watch downstream for the head that lifts. Practise this on your first morning. You will feel ridiculous. Everyone does.

## Daily discipline

**Learn the gradient before you learn the language.** Tense is orientational. You cannot correctly say *I did* or *I will* without knowing which way the water is going, and the tilt is too faint to feel. Read the silver threads. Check them every time you turn a corner. A visitor who gets lost on the axis starts speaking to people about their dead in the future tense.

**Read wet and dry.** You will constantly mistake the new quarters for ruins. They look like ruins — open armatures of moss and withe, half-stone, hollow, unfinished. They are buildings that have not finished arriving. The dry quarters look like buildings and are leaving. Teach your hand the difference: growing stone is slick, faintly furred with living moss, and gives nothing to the fingertip. Dead stone is chalky and comes off white on your palm.

**Never dam, only guide.** Not for washing, not for a moment, not to make a pool to soak your feet in. A blocked seep is a district's future. If you need to divert water, ask, and then wait to be shown.

**Don't sit on rims.** Don't lean on walls. Don't put your pack down on a terrace lip. Your weight in the wrong place snaps a four-year-old lip off a growing wall, and you cannot repair it — you can only set a moss plug in the gap and walk away knowing it will finish healing long after you're gone.

**Don't look at the tall grey bird.** You will want to. It walks the wet quarters and is entirely unbothered. Nobody will explain the custom. Learn to hold things in peripheral attention and let it pass.

**The two hours.** At dawn the fog forms as a flat lid at the third tier and the lower city disappears; at evening it sinks and the upper city greys out. Do not travel in either. Visibility drops to nothing on a featureless plateau. Be indoors, be still, drink the chalky water, sit close, talk at half a metre. These four hours a day are where you will learn everything you actually learn.

## What to do with yourself

Ask to be given children's work.

The small weaving — coarse mats of hair-moss and willow withe, set in the path of a seep — is done by children, because it requires no strength and no judgement beyond siting. It is the exactly correct task for a visitor: humble, useful, quiet, and completed by the water rather than by you. Weave badly for a week; you'll be corrected in a murmur from twenty centimetres away, and by the second week you'll be tolerable.

Your mat will stiffen in a year. It will be a slab in four. You will not see it. That is the point, and it is also the only kind of gift the Shelf really has a place for: not an object, but a beginning that somebody else finishes.

Resist, continuously and consciously, the urge to improve anything. You will see a wall that would take twenty years grow in a fortnight with a mould you could rig from your pack. You will see a diversion that is obviously suboptimal. Sit on it. A civilisation whose single organising principle is *nothing built here was made faster than it could be waited for* does not have a slot for your shortcut; it has a slot for the person who breaks things.

## Departure, and after

Anything you leave behind in moving water will become part of the city. Ten days and your forgotten sock has a rime on it; a season and it's a fixture. Check your campsite for everything, twice, and understand that a dropped bottle cap here is not litter, it is a permanent inclusion in the stone, a piece of alien geology set into someone's future wall.

If you have a fixed extraction point and any significant time is passing, remember the drift: the city moves up-gradient about a metre a year, forever. Your landing terrace will be a dry quarter in three centuries and open hummocked ground in three thousand. Mark nothing permanently; take your bearings from water, as everyone does.

Leave up-gradient if you can, or if you leave down-gradient, say something about it first. They will understand it either way. They will probably prefer it.

Two aftereffects, reliably reported:

The **hiss**. Thin water sheeting over finger-high rims makes a continuous held-breath sound at one volume across the entire plateau. Your ear will stop hearing it within an hour of arrival, and then, some days later, it will start hearing it again and never stop. When you leave, the silence of an ordinary world is not restful; it's an absence with an edge on it, and it takes weeks to close.

The **impatience**. You will come back to a place where things are struck, where people shout across rooms, where a building goes up in eighteen months and comes down in an afternoon, and for a while it will all seem not merely loud but somehow dishonest — as though everyone had agreed to skip the part where you find the right spot and stand in it.

That passes. Mostly.
