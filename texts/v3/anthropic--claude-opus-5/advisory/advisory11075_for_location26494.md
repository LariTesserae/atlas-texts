# Preparing for the Culm Works

## First, understand what kind of place it is

It is not a jungle, a ruin, or a factory, though it will read as all three in your first hour. It is a ninety-year argument that both sides are winning. Everything you do there will either add a small weight to one side or, more likely, be absorbed without notice. Go in expecting to be absorbed without notice. Visitors who arrive expecting to be interesting have a bad time.

Also understand that you are arriving into a *closed hydrological and biological system that has been in equilibrium longer than your grandparents have been dead.* You are, biologically, the single most dangerous object that will cross the sloughs this decade. More on that below, and it is the only part of this document I would call moral rather than practical.

---

## Arrival: do not miss the terrace

If you have any control over your insertion point, put yourself **on water or on the terrace**. Do not put yourself in the bamboo.

The stand beyond the sloughs is unmanaged giant clumping bamboo, and unmanaged means no vertical gaps, culms the thickness of your thigh at two-metre spacings with dead culms fallen across at every angle, a litter layer of silica-edged leaf sheath that will open your forearms, and no line of sight past four metres. There is no ground; there is clay under water under litter. You cannot walk it. You cannot cut it — a machete blunts on the silica in a hundred strokes and the fallen culms are springy, not brittle. People have died two hundred metres from the works.

If you land in it, do not walk uphill or downhill or toward light. Find water. Any water. Get in it and go downstream, in the water, walking or floating, and accept the leptospiral risk as strictly less bad than the alternative. The sloughs converge; the works is on the convergence.

Better: arrive in the fourth or the sixth month, in daylight, on the head slough, in a boat if you can arrange one, and be seen coming. Being seen coming matters. There are perhaps two dozen people there and every one of them will know within the hour.

**Do not arrive in the fifth month.** The water table stands above the topsoil. The boards float or don't, the perimeter walk is a swim, the retting tanks skim over into the general water, and the whole terrace becomes twelve metres above a sea forty kilometres away in the only sense that matters, which is none. They are busy. They are also, in the fifth month, slightly out of their minds, in the way that people are when they have spent five weeks wet to the hip.

## The body

### Feet

Foot care is the discipline of the place and it will be the organising fact of your visit. Nothing dries. Your feet will be wet from the hour you arrive to the hour you leave and for a week after.

- Bring **four pairs of wool socks minimum**, thin, and rotate them onto your body to dry — armpits, waistband — because nothing dries in the air.
- Bring **zinc oxide paste and an antifungal**, more than you think, and a hard bristle brush.
- Air your feet twice daily. Actually air them: sit, off the boards, feet up, twenty minutes. The locals do this without discussing it and you will see it as idleness for two days before you understand it as maintenance.
- Immersion foot is the injury that ends visits. Tinea is the injury you take home.

### Footwear

Soft, flexible, grippy, drainable, replaceable. **No heels, no lugs, no rigid shank.** You will be walking on planks with a one-in-two-hundred fall, worn hollow at the sides, algae in the centre runnel of every step. The local gait is a flat-footed outer-edge shuffle that looks arthritic and is in fact correct; copy it immediately and stop feeling silly about it.

Barefoot is what many of the workers do and it is *not* available to you, because of melioidosis — or whatever that world's version is — which lives in warm delta clay, enters through a foot crack, and kills people who did not know they were sick. The locals have a lifetime's relationship with their own soil bacteria. You have none.

### The wet-and-warm problems

Assume: intertrigo wherever skin touches skin; prickly heat under any waistband or strap; every scratch going septic in forty hours. Carry:

- iodine or chlorhexidine, in a bottle you can operate one-handed
- a broad-spectrum antibiotic course you know how to use
- a **tetanus booster before you go** — the works is knee-deep in ninety-year-old iron and splintered plank, and the spalled band at knee height along every wall is exactly the height at which you will bark your shin
- antihistamine, for the fig sap, which some people react to badly
- rehydration salts. You will not notice you are sweating because you will not dry. This is how visitors go down: not heatstroke, quiet salt loss over three days, and then a bad afternoon.

Do not bring waterproofs. There is nothing to keep out; the water is already inside the arrangement. Bring things that *work wet*: wool, linen, canvas that you have already accepted the ruin of. One light poncho for the actual rain, which is a wall of white noise arriving thirty seconds ahead of itself, is reasonable.

## Hearing

The Walk makes a sound like a long throat clearing that does not stop, with the top-cart's rail joints coming through it four seconds apart, and you feel that four-second beat in the piles through your boot soles anywhere on the terrace.

- Bring **flat-attenuation earplugs**, the kind that reduce level without killing intelligibility. Foam plugs are worse than nothing here because you will need to hear a shouted warning and you will take them out at the wrong moment.
- Expect a headache on days one and two, located behind the eyes, that is the beat and not the heat.
- Expect the beat to become your clock. By day three you will be timing your own actions to the rail joints without deciding to.
- **The important thing about the sound is what its absence means.** If the works goes quiet — if the beat stops — everyone moves, and they move before they have thought about it. You should move too, toward the nearest handrail and off the Walk, and then look. A stopped engine is not restful. It is news.

### Your voice

They pitch their voices over the machine, all the time, and have never noticed doing it. You will be shouted at from a metre away in a manner you will read as anger for about half a day. It isn't.

More importantly: **your normal speaking volume will register to them as evasive, sly, or unwell.** Practise projecting from the diaphragm before you go. Say everything once, loudly, plainly, and do not add softening clauses, because the softening clauses will not survive the belt noise and you will appear to have said something abrupt and then mumbled.

## Smell

The retting tanks run at blood heat and have for generations. The ground note of the terrace is sulphurous, sweetish, vegetal, insistent.

There is no preparation. There are only two facts:

1. **You will be anosmic to it in roughly three days.** This happens more completely than you expect and it is the single greatest relief of the trip.
2. **Everything you bring is now a retting object.** Textiles especially. Do not bring anything you intend to have back — no wool you love, no leather, no notebook you want on a shelf later. The smell does not wash out of wool at all. It comes out of synthetics in about six months.

Do **not** apply anything strongly scented, and do not do the camphor-under-the-nose trick where anyone can see you. It is not that they are proud of the smell. It is that the smell is the fibre, and the fibre is the wage, and holding your nose in a man's workplace reads the same in every world I know of.

## What to bring, and the one thing not to bring

### Bring

- **Files, hacksaw blades, needles, awls, waxed thread, good hard pencils.** Consumables for maintenance are the correct gift everywhere, and here doubly, because the terrace has no hinterland and nothing arrives casually.
- **Salt. Coffee or its analogue. Any spice.** Their diet is delta-monotonous.
- **Machine oil** if you can carry it, in a container that will not leak into your food.
- **Paper that survives.** Synthetic or heavily sized. Ordinary notebooks go to mush and then to mould, in that order, in about nine days. Pencil, not ink; ink runs on damp paper and no pen likes ninety-nine percent humidity.
- **Silica gel in a sealed tin for optics.** Fungus grows on lens coatings there in under two weeks and etches them permanently. If you bring a camera, bring one you can lose.
- **Brass or bronze small tools rather than steel.** Steel blooms orange in three days. This is a good conversational opening, incidentally: leave a piece of bare cast iron on a rail and someone will tell you, without much prompting, about the little orchid that grows only on iron and does not care whether the iron is turning.

### Do not bring

**Anything alive, and anything that might be carrying something alive.**

No seeds, no fruit, no dried plant matter, no untreated wood, no soil, no unwashed boot treads, no potted anything, no "small gift from home" that is a cutting. One aggressive vine, one fig-competitor, one bamboo pathogen, one root-rot fungus in the clay on your soles, and the fourteen-year rotation that has outlasted the ledgers is over — not dramatically, over the course of twenty years, in a way that nobody will connect to you and that you will never hear about.

Scrub your boots to bare rubber before you cross. Bag your clothing and freeze or heat-treat it if you have any means to. If they meet you at the slough with a bucket and a brush and make you strip and scrub before you set foot on the terrace: **let them, gratefully, and do not make a joke about it.** If they don't — and they may not, because they have never had to think about elsewhere — do it yourself anyway.

This is the whole ethics of visiting the Culm Works. Everything else is comfort.

## Etiquette, in order of how badly you can get it wrong

**1. You may not kill a root that holds a beam.** You will not be cutting anything, so this seems not to apply to you. It applies to you as follows: **do not touch, lean on, sit on, swing from, or hang anything from a fig root.** Two of the ten buildings are held up by fig and the brick is only a lining. Some of those roots have an entry in the engineer's ledger ninety years old reading *bearing, sound.* You are, in effect, walking around inside a load path that is also a living thing that people have kept promises to for four generations.

**2. Never cross the Walk in front of the travelling top-cart, and never step over the ropes.** Ninety metres of fibre under tension. If it lets go it does so at the speed of a whip. The correct behaviour is to stand at the side, in a bay, and wait for the cart to pass you — which takes a while, and which you should treat as a pleasant fact of the place rather than as a delay.

**3. The engine has a name. Learn it in the first hour. Use it.** Refer to it as *she* or *he* or whatever they do, correctly, and never as *it*. Ask before entering the engine house. Do not touch the flywheel side. Do not put anything down on the bedplate. If the man who keeps her is wiping her, you may watch and you may not talk.

**4. Do not write in the ledger.** Not even your name, not even if invited, until you understand that an entry there is a statement one of their grandchildren will act on.

**5. When the heron comes, stop talking.** It arrives about every ninth or tenth day, stands on the sluice cheek in the full machine noise, takes shrimp for forty minutes, and goes. People stop what they can stop and watch it. Watch it. Do not photograph it on your first visit. Do not point.

**6. The gecko's evening bark.** Three or four notes at dusk from the engine house. They will tell you it is the same one it has always been, which it cannot be. **Do not do the arithmetic out loud.** Say that you're glad it's still there.

**7. Offer your hands, expect to be poor at it.** Coin is close to useless. Labour is the medium, and the real work of the place is the trimming — light-wells opened over the drying racks, closed over the tar shed, roots cut back from the flywheel pit. You are not qualified to cut anything. You are qualified to carry cut material, sweep boards, and turn fibre bricks on the drying racks. Ask for that work. Do it badly and steadily and you will be, by day four, a person rather than an event.

## The thing that will actually unsettle you

Not the smell, not the noise, not the wet. **The animals.**

The air is hot and dense and absolutely stuffed with life, and there is no birdsong. Not less birdsong — none. The tannin and the retting liquor took the sloughs a long time ago; the bamboo flowers rarely and hosts nothing; there are two species of ant, a translucent shrimp in the tail-race in great abundance, one lineage of dark unafraid rats in the tow store, a heron on a nine-day interval, and one gecko.

You will not identify this for a day or two. It will present first as a mood: a flat, low, unplaceable dread, worse at dusk, worse indoors, and a specific difficulty falling asleep in which you keep listening for something and getting the engine instead. This is normal, it is nearly universal in visitors, and naming it helps enormously. Name it on the first evening. Say out loud: *there are no birds here.*

Two coping notes:

- The bamboo fills the gap if you let it. Culms squeal at the nodes when the air moves, like a badly hung gate, and in a gust a whole clump knocks hollowly against itself high up, a wooden chattering. **Learn to hear that as company.** Everyone who leaves the works says that is the thing they miss, and they mean it, and you will understand why by the end of the first week.
- The upside nobody mentions: **you do not have to shake out your boots.** There is nothing in them. In every other wet forest in every world this is a nightly ritual, and here it is unnecessary, and the comfort of that is the eeriest comfort available.

## Sleeping, eating, drinking

- **Sleep off the floor.** Hammock or raised cot, and hang your kit; anything on boards is wicking.
- **Sleep under the smoke layer or above it, not in it.** The stack is nineteen metres and the pale smoke doesn't rise, it spreads and sits at about roof height and drifts toward the tail slough on the terrace's own faint breath. If you are tall, you are breathing it standing up. Stoop. The locals stoop and don't know they do.
- **Drink only what they drink.** Not slough water, ever, either arm. Not tank water, which leaves black with tannin and lignin. Rain catchment, boiled, and it will taste faintly of the stack, and that taste is the taste of the place and you will find, later, that you can't get it.
- Eat what you're given. Refusing food over the smell is the second-worst manners available to you.

---

## Departure, and afterwards

Expect all of the following, and expect none of them to be brief.

**Your voice.** You will be too loud for months. You will be too loud at a dinner table and see people's faces change and not know why. Warn whoever meets you.

**The beat.** You will hear four-second periodicity in every machine for weeks — trains, pumps, fans — and you will feel a phantom vibration in the soles of your feet at rest, usually at night. It fades.

**Birdsong.** This is the one that surprises people. Your first dawn chorus back will be almost unbearable — not nostalgic, *invasive*, too much information, an assault of small voices. Some travellers weep at it. Plan to be somewhere quiet and private for your first morning home.

**Your feet.** Two to four weeks to normal, longer if you were careless. Get looked at if anything is warm, spreading, or hurts more than it looks like it should — think melioidosis, think of it out loud to whoever examines you, because they will not, and it presents late.

**Your kit.** Bag it separately. Assume the textiles are gone. Assume mould in every seam and every lens.

**The grief.** Mild, real, and specific: you will miss a knocking noise high up in a canopy, and a barking at dusk, and a bird on a sluice cheek every ninth or tenth day. There is nothing to be done about that except to have expected it. Record the bamboo if you can, on the best equipment you can carry, and be ready for the recording to fail — because the sound of that place is half in the air and half in the piles, and the piles come up through your boots, and no microphone has ever got that part out.
