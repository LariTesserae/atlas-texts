# Preparing for the Sill

## First, correct your model of the place

You are not going somewhere dark. Darkness implies that light is absent from a world that is otherwise normally lit. On the Sill, light was never part of the arrangement. What you are going to is a place where **vision has been replaced, socially and technically, by sound** — and where the thing you use to see with is a thing you will be constantly producing, sharing, buying, renting, and accidentally destroying.

Three sentences to memorise before you go, because everything in your preparation descends from them:

1. **Sound is sight.** Your voice is your face. Your clicker is your eyes. A sound-absorbing coat is a mask. A damped room is a blindfold.
2. **Light is private, and a lamp is a weapon.** Not metaphorically. Two hundred thousand fully dark-adapted retinas, in a place where the brightest civic illumination is a bacterial smear, will take real injury from your torch.
3. **The ground is new and the ground is hungry.** Everything is an edge; everything grows; anything you put down long enough becomes part of the country.

Travelers who internalise those three come home. Travelers who treat it as "cold cave world, bring headlamp" come home smaller — fewer fingers, worse hearing, and occasionally with a criminal record they don't understand.

## Part One: The body, months out

**Altitude.** Three and a half thousand metres, and worse than the number suggests. There is no photosynthesis on that plateau — the mats *consume* oxygen against sulfide and methane, they do not produce it — and the local air runs thin in both pressure and fraction. Budget **four to six weeks** of genuine acclimatisation, either staged on-world at a lower station or in a hypoxic tent beforehand at progressively simulated 4,000–4,500 m. Get a haemoglobin panel. Do not arrive fresh from sea level; the combination of hypoxia, cold, sensory deprivation and constant 85–95 dB noise produces a specific and well-documented collapse on day two or three that guides call *the drop*, and it has killed people by making them sit down on the terraces.

**Blood.** This is the single most important medical fact about the Sill and the one visitors reliably ignore. The stone is a field of aragonite needles a centimetre long. It cuts painlessly. It cuts through glove leather, through skin, through the walls of small vessels, and the cuts do not close — partly because they are very fine and very deep, partly because you are cold and vasoconstricted, and partly, the locals believe, because something in the brine film interferes with clotting.

Therefore:

- **Stop all anticoagulants, aspirin, fish oil, and anything else that thins you, six weeks out.** Get clearance in writing.
- Get a **coagulation panel**. If you have any clotting disorder at all, do not go. There is no negotiating with this environment.
- Carry **hemostatic gauze, tourniquets, tranexamic acid**, and know how to use all three *by feel, in the dark, wearing gloves, while someone is screaming over the noise*. Practise this blindfolded until it is boring.

**Cold-wet.** Minus fifteen, no wind, constant condensing drip. Windless cold is survivable and pleasant-ish. Brine is not. If you go into a pool, you are soaked in a saturated salt solution at minus fifteen that **will not freeze into a protective shell**, that wicks into every seam, that conducts heat out of you at a rate nothing in your experience prepares you for, and that will chemically burn wet skin over hours. Rehearse the immersion protocol until it's reflex: *out, cut the clothing off, rinse with warm fresh water, not just dry — salt holds water and keeps killing you — then into a heated interior.* Carry a warm-water rinse bladder against your torso. Guides do.

**Hearing.** Your hearing is now a survival organ, and you are about to spend two weeks inside a continuous industrial shimmer. Get a **baseline audiogram**. Get **custom-moulded active filters** — not attenuators, *filters*: they must compress the environment without creating dead zones, and they must pass the city's carrier tone and its counterpoint intact. Bring three sets. A traveler with failed ear protection is merely uncomfortable; a traveler with *noise-cancelling* ear protection has blinded themselves and doesn't know it.

**Echolocation.** Start three months out. Human echolocation is learnable and the learning curve is steep at first. Work blindfolded in a hallway with a tongue-click until you can reliably sense a wall at two metres, a doorway, a step down. You will not achieve anything like local literacy — those people were raised inside the hologram — but the difference between "no echolocation" and "some echolocation" on the Sill is the difference between being cargo and being a person.

**Pitch.** The city is named by its note. The great vents are named by their pitches. Interval literacy is *literacy* there, in the plain sense: it is how you read the street signs. Spend the same three months on ear training. Bring a reference tone generator. If you can learn to hum Hantha's carrier in tune, do — arriving able to say the city's name correctly is worth more than any gift you could carry.

**Blindfold living.** At least ten full days, at home, before departure. Eat, wash, dress, navigate, sleep. This is less about skill than about burning out the panic. You need the panic gone before you arrive, because the Sill will give you plenty of new things to be afraid of and you cannot afford to spend the budget on the dark.

## Part Two: Kit

**The outer shell must be acoustically bright.** This is the counterintuitive one and the one that gets visitors quietly hated. A thick soft parka is an *absorber*. In an acoustic hologram, a person in a soft coat is a moving hole in everyone's vision — a smear, a blank, an absence walking through the street. It is rude in the way that standing in a doorway with the lights off is rude, and it is dangerous in the way that driving without lights is dangerous. Put your insulation **inside a hard, smooth, reflective outer layer**: polished composite plate, rigid panels, a glazed shell. Locals dress like beetles for exactly this reason (and because polished plate is harder for the fauna to colonise). Copy them.

**Cut protection.** UHMWPE or aramid gloves rated for blade contact, with sacrificial hard palm patches, worn over a sealed liner. Kneepads. Forearm guards. Hard shins. **Train yourself never to break a fall with an open hand** — go down onto padded forearms. Every visitor gets a lecture on this and about a third still come back with a flayed palm.

**Face.** Opaque rigid face guard — not clear. You do not need to see through it and clarity gains you nothing but fog and rime. It must, however, be **acoustically open**: perforated or membrane over the ears, never a sealed dome. Add a **heat-and-moisture-exchange mask with fine filtration**, which does four jobs at once: warms the air you're gasping in at altitude, saves the litres of water your lungs would otherwise lose, filters brine aerosol and sulfide, and stops you inhaling flyers when you cross a column.

**The clicker, and the backup clicker.** You'll be issued one at the gate. Bring a second, mechanical, wind-up, that keeps clicking for seventy-two hours whether or not you are conscious. Set yourself a **signature rhythm** and never change it: on the Sill your click pattern is your face, and swapping it mid-visit is like swapping faces. Visitors are typically issued a marked, clumsy-sounding pattern; don't be insulted, it's a courtesy, it tells everyone around you to give you room.

**Lamp.** Yes, bring one. Deep red, heavily shrouded, with a physical interlock — treat it exactly as you would a firearm. Carry it holstered. It exists for two purposes: medical emergencies, and private use in your own sealed quarters. It never comes out in a street.

**Gas monitor** for H₂S, CO₂ and O₂, with **tactile alarm** — a vibrating alarm, not an audible one, because you will never hear an audible one and because adding tones to the hologram without permission is a minor public nuisance. This device is not optional. Hydrogen sulfide is heavier than air and pools in the terrace hollows, and it destroys your sense of smell before it destroys you, which means your nose will tell you the danger has passed at exactly the moment it has become lethal. In a landscape made entirely of rimmed hollows, in the dark, this is the second most common way visitors die.

**Electronics.** Everything conformally coated. Sulfide eats silver and copper contacts; brine creeps into every connector; batteries die in the cold. Keep power cells against your body. Bring lithium primaries as backup. Expect condensation and then rime every single time you move from minus fifteen to the city's four degrees, and build a de-icing routine into your gate ritual.

**Food and water.** Assume you cannot safely eat local. The Sill's biochemistry runs on enzyme suites built for minus fifteen and a food web soaked in sulfur and dissolved metals; visitors who eat the chitin stews get, at best, a spectacular week. Budget **4,500–6,000 kcal/day** for a working traveler in cold and thin air. Bring it. For water: the brine is not water in any sense useful to you — do not even taste it — and the municipal drinking lines have larvae in them as a matter of course and local opinion, which is not squeamish. Carry filtration plus a sterilising stage, and accept that you will drink condensate.

**Recording.** Bring **binaural or ambisonic recorders**, and bring spares. This is the only souvenir the Sill gives. Cameras are physically useless and socially obscene — there is nothing to photograph, and the attempt to photograph requires light, and light is assault. Travelers come home from the Sill with sound. That is the form the memory takes.

**What not to bring:** soft outer layers; anything flammable near the vents; open flame under any circumstances; laser anything; drones; pets; perfume or strong scent (the air is already iron and eggs, but locals track scent more than you'd expect and yours is very loud); and nothing, *nothing*, that would tempt you to take a sample of the stone.

## Part Three: The first forty-eight hours

**At the gate.** You will be sounded — someone will walk around you making noise and listening to what you do to it. This is your intake examination and your customs inspection at once. Stand still, keep clicking, let them work. If your shell is wrong they will tell you and you should change it, immediately and without argument.

**Learn the four sounds before you learn anything else:** the carrier (the city's hum, always there, always behind you, the wall you navigate against); the stairhead pulse; the level-change pulse; and the *absence* that means a damped chamber. That last one will feel like a cliff edge in your inner ear. It is a room, not a hole. You will not believe this for several days.

**Expect visual hallucination.** In total darkness, over hours, your visual cortex begins to invent. You will see lights, grids, drifting shapes, faces. This is normal, it is not oxygen deprivation, and it is the reason experienced travelers are so calm about it — but the first night, unprepared, it can be very frightening indeed. Name it and move past it.

**Expect the drop.** Somewhere in hour thirty to sixty: exhaustion out of proportion to effort, tearfulness, an overwhelming desire to lie down in a corridor. Hypoxia plus cold plus sensory overload plus the sheer *density* of the place. Plan for it. Have a rest day scheduled. Do not push through it on a terrace.

## Part Four: Etiquette, which is also law, which is also physics

**Do not go quiet.** Not for a minute. A person who stops emitting is a hole in the world — someone will walk into you, off a rim, down a stair. If you must rest, rest in a marked alcove and leave the mechanical clicker running. If you pass out in a street, the automatic backup is what will make you findable rather than a hazard.

**Do not absorb.** Don't stand in doorways. Don't block a carrier line. Don't unfurl a large soft object in a public space. Don't wear the coat.

**Never raise a lamp in the street.** If you genuinely must use light in front of another person — a medical emergency, a mechanical repair — you **ask first, loudly, and wait.** People will turn away and cover their eyes. Then you cup it, aim it down, and use the least you can. To shine white light into the face of someone who has been dark-adapted since birth is to do something they have no word for except by analogy with mutilation. The polite formulation is that "you have destroyed nothing, and yet." Do not test the *and yet*.

**The fauna has right of way, always.** If a rank of scrapers is working a rim, you wait. If a flyer column crosses your route, you either wait or you go through it with your mask up, your mouth shut, and your hands down — and you do not swat, kill, or scatter them. These animals are the entire economy. Killing a flyer in public is somewhere between littering and vandalism; disturbing a nesting colony is nearer to arson.

**Treat every surface as an edge.** Including ones you are sitting on, leaning against, or setting a pack down on. Including the inside of things.

**Do not leave objects in a flow.** Anything left in running brine for a few days begins to be cemented in, and the Sill does not give things back — it incorporates them. This is charming right up until it's your pack, your instrument, or your boot with your foot in it.

**Never dig, never carve, never take stone.** Beneath the current street level there are eleven Hanthas, filled solid, and in them are furniture, tools, tone-grooves, and the dead, perfectly preserved in the mineral that made them. That is the archive, the cemetery and the ancestry of the entire civilisation, and it is also, structurally, the ground everyone is standing on. Taking a crystal souvenir off a terrace is bad manners. Taking anything from below street level is grave robbery, and they have a very old word for it.

**The gift.** If you want to do one generous thing and you don't know what: **buy someone an hour of silence.** Damping is metered and sold by volume; mourners rent it; it is expensive; it is the most intimate thing on the Sill. To pay for a stranger's or a host's quiet hour is a gesture they will understand perfectly and you will never have to explain.

**Never promise anything on a human timescale.** Their architects are trained in units of *lifetimes per storey*. A house takes eleven years to deposit. A tower takes four generations. If you say you will send something, or return, or finish something, they will write it down and hand it forward, and someone's great-granddaughter will be waiting.

## Part Five: Outside the city

Do not go onto the open terraces without a guide. Not "it's advisable not to" — do not. There are no landmarks. There are no visual cues of any kind. You cannot retrace your steps. The terraces are a twenty-kilometre maze of rimmed pools with blade edges and unknown depths, seeded with sulfide hollows, and your entire navigational capacity depends on equipment you have been using for three days.

On the rim: rope up. Tether. Stay on the sounded routes and learn the sounding posts. Keep the gas monitor where you can feel it. Check your ears every hour — the shimmer out there, thousands of vents and a hundred thousand drips ringing on thin crystal plate, is the loudest natural sound on the plateau, and it is deceptive because it is high and fine rather than a roar.

And then, when the guide stops and tells you to stop clicking for ten seconds — do it. That's the reason people come.

You will hear the near shimmer, and the middle roar of the vent field, and the deep hum of the city at your back like a warmth you can't feel, and out at twelve or fifteen kilometres the individual voices of the great vents, each one named after its own pitch, each with its own million-strong cloud of flyers whose hiss arrives four full seconds after the water's note. Nobody has ever described this adequately. It is the reason the Sill is on anyone's itinerary at all.

Then start clicking again.

## Part Six: Coming home

**Quarantine both ways.** Your gear will have been colonised. Egg cases cement into any crevice narrower than a fist and your pack is nothing but such crevices. Inspect every seam, scrape (don't crush — the smell is memorable), and submit to a full decontamination on exit. You are also, in the other direction, a walking bloom of mesophilic organisms entering one of the more fragile biospheres in the accessible multiverse; take the arrival protocols seriously even when they are tedious.

**Re-light slowly.** Your retinas have been fully dark-adapted for the entire visit. Come back through graded filters over several days. Do not step out of the transit into daylight. People have done real and lasting damage this way.

**Expect tinnitus** and have your audiogram repeated against the baseline.

**Expect the silence sickness.** This is the part nobody warns you about. You come home to a world that is quiet and bright, and for a few weeks the quiet rooms will feel wrong — not peaceful, *empty*, like standing at the top of an unlit stair. You will catch yourself clicking. You will find that you have started reading rooms with your ears and that your own house is oddly featureless. Some travelers find this passes in a fortnight. Some find it never entirely does, and those are the ones who go back.

---

## If you only get one visit

Hum the note at the gate. Keep your coat hard and your lamp holstered. Buy an hour of silence for someone who needs it. Ask — politely, and be prepared to be refused — whether you may attend an excavation, because to stand in a room that was a street four hundred years ago, quarried out of the stone with heated wire, with the furniture still in place and the doorway still sealed, is the other reason the Sill is on the itinerary.

And record everything. You will come home with no photographs. You will come home with twenty kilometres of ringing plate, a city singing its own name, and, four seconds late, a million wings.
