# Preparing for the Pan

## First, the honest disqualifiers

Do not go if you have a cough. Not a chest infection — a cough. A dry winter tickle is enough. A single hard cough in the working town shuts a hundred node valves, and the Works loses forty minutes of pressure it cannot make up, and the loss is recorded, and it is recorded against your name because everything is recorded against somebody's name. People have been walked back over the rim for a cough.

Do not go if you are a nervous laugher. Do not go if you talk in your sleep, and find out whether you do before you claim you don't. Do not go if you cannot sit still for two hours. Do not go if you require, for your sense of yourself, to be noticed.

Do not go if you are carrying an infection of any kind and cannot prove otherwise to your own satisfaction, and I mean of the lungs, the gut, the skin, and the soles of your boots. More on this below. It is the only part of the preparation that could kill anybody but you.

## Six months out: the voice

You cannot learn this on the way. The threshold is roughly the volume of a raised human voice, and the trouble is that ordinary people have no idea where that sits, because nothing in our lives has ever asked.

Buy a cheap sound meter. Put it on the table. Talk to someone. Watch the numbers. You will be appalled: normal conversational warmth — the bit where you get interested, lean in, say *no but listen* — goes straight through. The laugh is worse. A sneeze is worse than the laugh by a factor you will not believe until you measure your own.

Train for six months at conversational levels that feel, at first, like sulking. The Pan's speech is a low unpressed murmur with the consonants very clear and the vowels almost swallowed, and it works because everyone is listening. Practice getting a whole sentence out on the tail of an exhale. Practice saying something urgent — *stop, that's hot, behind you* — without the automatic climb in pitch and volume. That climb is a reflex, it is thirty million years old, and it will still ambush you in week three of your stay.

Train, separately, the sneeze. There is a technique and the town will teach it, but you can start now: the felt muff in the pocket, the press of the tongue to the roof of the mouth, the pinch, the fold of yourself over your own knees so the noise goes into your abdomen. Carry the muff at home until it is as automatic as your keys. Some visitors wear it on a cord like a whistle, which is a joke the locals do not get.

And train the warning. Before any noise you can predict — a sneeze coming, a fit of coughing, a fall you can feel arriving — you make the sign: flat palm thrust forward at chest height, held. It means *mine, incoming, sorry.* It does not stop the valves. It stops people wondering whose fault it was, which in a town of a hundred and thirty with a ledger matters more.

## Three months out: the hands

There are two hundred and eleven signs. Learn thirty. Any decent primer from a returning traveller will have them; the core set is:

yes / no / wait / come / stop / sorry / again / slower / I don't understand / water / food / cold / sick / hurt / where / who / how long / now / tomorrow / dawn / dusk / the Turn / the edge / the ruins / beetle / rope / careful / don't / help me / I am a visitor / I am leaving.

*I am a visitor* you will use fifty times a day. It is made low, near the waist, and it is a polite self-diminishment: it says *I own no distance.*

Learn the number signs too. They will ask where you are from and mean, quite literally, how far. They will not understand your answer and will nod anyway.

Do not learn the signs for the ruins-deep vocabulary, the dialect ones on the old charts. Asking about them is a specific kind of rude, like asking a stranger about their dead.

## Biosecurity: the part that is not about you

Everything standing in that basin is one plant. One organism, one genome, four hundred years old, no resistance anywhere that is not resistance everywhere. The town is a suburb of a single vegetable and has been for forty generations. A rust, a wilt, a root rot, an insect with a taste for silicified fibre — any of it, once, and the ledger ends.

So: freeze your kit for a week. Wash everything you own in hot water and dry it hard. Carry nothing wooden, nothing leather that has been in a field, no seeds, no dried food with husks, no plant matter of any kind, no soil, no boots you have worn anywhere green. Take your bags apart and check the seams. Empty your pockets and turn them inside out and look. Take no pets, obviously, and no live cultures, and if you are on probiotics say so at the rim.

At the rim they will make you do most of this again in front of them, badly and slowly, with a tannin dip for the feet and a scrub that takes the top layer off your hands. Do not be offended. They have had it happen. There is a blank stretch in the middle of the ledger, about ninety years back, seven years long, where the entries are all in one hand and all very short.

Also: no scent. No perfume, no scented soap, no deodorant, no insect repellent, no sunscreen. Partly because the lens is their only water and everything you rub on yourself ends up in it. Partly because the Pan is a place where smell is information — the iodine-and-cut-grass of fresh lacquer means a wound is being closed somewhere and closed on time — and your shampoo is a shout in a different channel.

## Kit: the acoustic audit

Lay out everything you intend to take on a hard table and handle each item as you would while tired and cold, and listen.

**Velcro is unusable.** It is probably the loudest thing you own. Cut it off or leave the item.

**Zips**: metal toothed zips on hard shells crackle. Replace with toggles, or tape them. A zip pull tapping against a buckle in a breeze will earn you a look from every adult in a fifteen-metre radius.

**Shell fabrics**: modern waterproofs rustle horribly. You want soft, dull, heavy: waxed cotton, oiled wool, a good felt. Yes it is heavier. You are walking nine minutes across the world.

**Hard soles**: they will issue you felts at the rim, over-shoes of pressed sheath fibre that go on over whatever you arrive in, and you wear them from the rim to the rim. Bring boots with a soft low profile that will take the felts without bulging. Bring spare insoles; your feet will be damp the whole time.

**Metal against metal**: tape it, wrap it, or leave it. Carabiners, buckles, a folding knife's detent click, the lid of a tin, the ring of a watch back on a table. The correct test is: drop it on a table from ten centimetres. If you flinch, it stays home.

**Rigid bottles**: use a soft flask. A hard bottle set down too fast on a fibre floor makes a knock that carries the whole basin.

**Electronics**: every alert off, every haptic off, every shutter off, screen brightness low, and check again after each charge because devices lie. An alarm clock going off in a visitor's bunk at four a.m. is an incident that will be told about for a generation and you will not be told it was about you.

**Cameras**: silent shutter only. No flash, ever, and not for the plant's sake — for the night crews' eyes, which are adapted to a sullen orange and take twenty minutes to come back.

**Torches**: bring one, small, red filter, and use it about a tenth as much as you want to.

**Anything you cannot make quiet, leave.** Travel absurdly light. You do not need much. You will be indoors, or under stems, which is the same thing.

## Clothing and climate

Sixty metres above a cold sea, flat, fogbound, grey. Think of the worst late-autumn day you have had in a maritime country and assume it for the duration.

The Pan is damp, not wet, which sounds better than it is: nothing you own will get soaked and nothing will ever quite dry. The drip is constant and it is cold and it goes down the back of your collar. Layer wool. Bring a hat you can wear indoors — the nodes are heated only by bodies and the lacquer coil — and a second one for when the first is wet. Bring twice the socks you think and a way to dry them, which will be your own body.

Daylight is a flat grey-green, plenty to read by, and in November it is gone by four. The lamps come up small and yellow high in the nodes and the basin turns into a room with the ceiling lost. It is one of the most beautiful things anywhere in any world, and you will be too cold to fully appreciate it, and that is normal.

## Water, food, waste

The lens under the marl is no deeper than a cellar and it is the entire town. You do not wash in it, you do not put anything in it, you do not relieve yourself anywhere within the rim except where you are shown, which will be a trench system at the dying end that runs into peat. Follow the arrangement exactly. Ask twice if unsure. This is the one area where a visitor's mistake is not merely expensive but disgusting.

Drink what they give you. Filter it anyway if you must, quietly. The Thread runs black with tannin and tastes of strong cold tea with the leaves left in; the lens water is chalky and better. Nothing there will poison you. Nothing there will feed you well, either: expect fibre-bread, bracket fungus, a little preserved meat from outside the rim, and a great deal of nothing much. Bring high-density food for yourself and eat it out of sight, not from shame but because food in the Pan is a subject people are careful around.

## Money: you arrive owing

There is no ground to buy and nothing to buy it with. The ledger runs on fibre — weight of retted, dried, pressed plant sheath — and on hours, and a visitor is entered at the rim with an opening debit that covers your bunk, your felts, your minder, and an estimate of the noise you are going to make. That last figure is not an insult. It is actuarial. They have data on people like you.

You settle it in trade goods. What they want, in rough order: fine wire, steel needles, thin brass sheet, rubber sheet and gasket cord, good brine felt, salt, lamp filament, small lenses, spectacle frames, and files. What they do not want is anything that cuts well and looks tempting, anything that makes noise, and anything that would require them to change a practice. Offering technology is a delicate business and mostly resented; the Company's stance, unwritten but absolutely consistent, is that reliability beats power, and they have four hundred years of ledger to back it and you have a brochure.

Medicine is the hard case. They will take antiseptic and analgesia gratefully and they will take antibiotics with a long silence first. Think about it before you go. Think about it more than you want to.

## Arrival

You come across the gravel flat through birch scrub in fog, and there is no view, and then there is a low grit ridge no higher than your chest, and you step over it and the world changes without a sound.

Except it does have a sound. That is the first thing: the drip, three hundred hollow chambers of varying volume being tapped at random by a very patient hand, low and unmusical and constant, and under it the squeak of marl. It will take you four days to stop hearing it and about a year, afterwards, to stop missing it.

At the rim there is a hut, a warden, a trough, a rack of felts, and a card. The card lists four things. The wording varies; the current one reads, in translation, near enough:

> **1.** Do not raise your voice. Not for pain, not for joy, not for warning.
> **2.** Cut nothing.
> **3.** Take nothing out.
> **4.** Do not run.

The fourth is the one visitors break. Running is loud, running makes you fall, and falling makes the worst noise a human body can make in a quiet place. Nobody in the Pan runs. If you see everybody moving quickly you should move quickly too, and quickly here is a long stride, and that is all it ever is.

You will be assigned a minder. Usually a child of nine or ten, occasionally someone very old. Both are people whose hours are cheap and whose signing is perfect. The child will be silent, unimpressed, and better at this than you in every respect, and will hold your sleeve at stairs. Let them. They are working. At the end they will be paid in fibre against your debit and it will matter to their household.

## The rhythms you must not fight

**The dawn surge.** Before light, the roots have pushed the pressure up to three or four atmospheres and the stems flex, and a long low groan runs around the Pan like a rope pulled through a ring. It is the one loud sound the plant makes and forgives. The rule is silence through it — total, everybody, no exceptions, ten or fifteen minutes. Be awake for it at least once. Be awake for it lying still, because the floor moves.

**The Turn.** Forty-minute cycle, in and out, and the beam rocking over like something asleep. The whole town's clocks are set by it and so is everything else — meals, shifts, when people expect you. Get on the cycle by the second day. Count it. Everyone does, without noticing.

**Dusk.** The fluter sings its four notes and that is permitted, and it is the only sound above the threshold that anybody makes on purpose. People stop for it, very slightly, the way you'd glance at a clock.

## Manners

**The beetles are staff.** Pale, waxy, thumb-sized, in the valve throats. Look before you set anything down and look before you sit. If you tread on one you will have killed a worker, and that is how it will be handled: a note in the ledger, a replacement drawn from the trays, and a distinct cooling from the crew whose valve it was. They are not sentimental about them. They are absolutely serious about them.

**Rule 5 is not superstition.** What the plant has taken into itself stays there. In the ruins at the trailing end you will walk through forty generations of swallowed architecture — a bicycle frame grown into callus to the hubs, a lamp bracket, hand-sign charts in a dialect nobody reads, and a pair of spectacles in a node wall, in profile, perfectly clear through the amber. Do not touch. Do not scrape the lacquer to see better. Do not photograph the spectacles, or rather, ask, and accept the answer. Every visitor wants the spectacles. Every visitor is told no, and the no is not explained, and it does not need to be.

**Distance-talk.** Nobody owns ground; everybody owns a distance from the leading edge. When you are told where someone lives you are given a number, and it is a number that will be true in a hundred years when the town has walked a hundred and twenty metres southeast over its own bones. As a visitor you own no distance at all. This is the single strangest thing about being there, and people will be kind to you about it in a way that will take you some days to recognise as pity.

**The deer.** If one comes over the rim, everything stops. Do not move, do not lift a camera, do not be the person who is the reason it left early.

**Laughing.** The Company's oldest surviving document is a fine levied against a man for laughing. Everybody knows this. Do not make the joke.

## Pain, accident, and the thing nobody tells you first

Every crew member carries a quiet: a felt-and-leather bit on a cord under the shirt. It goes in the mouth before the setting of a bone, before a burn is dressed, before anything is done that hurts. Children are given theirs at about six and it is not a ceremony.

If you are injured in the Pan, you will be treated competently, carefully, and in total silence, and someone will hold your jaw shut. Bring your own analgesia and have it where you can reach it left-handed. Understand in advance that there is no evacuation that is fast, that the walk out over the gravel takes hours, and that nobody is going to shout for help on your behalf; they will shutter a green lamp and the right people will come at a walk.

This is the point at which most visitors finally understand that the quiet is not an aesthetic.

## If you are going for the flowering

Don't, the first time.

Once in nine summers every node throws a spike, every valve opens to the spikes, and the pressure goes where the plant wants it. The Works shuts. The Turn stops mid-stroke and hangs there. The lamps go down over eleven days as the cells run flat, and the town lives by candle in a basin that smells overpoweringly of rotten honey, and the moths come in thousands — the only week of the year the place has anything you would call abundance. There are songs, sung under the breath, and they are beautiful, and they are the only songs.

It is also eleven days of a town that runs on margins living with no margin at all, in the dark, in a smell that will make you ill for the first two days. If you come for it you are one more mouth in the week when the ledger is tightest, and they know exactly what you are, and they have never once failed to survive it and never once done so comfortably.

Go in an ordinary year. Come back for the flowering when they know your face.

## Leaving, and the ear afterwards

You give the felts back at the hut. You sign the ledger. You step over a chest-high ridge of glacial grit into flat grey country, and behind you three hundred pewter columns go on ticking with drip and will go on ticking without you, and will be one metre twenty further southeast next year.

The thing to prepare for — genuinely prepare for, warn your household about — is the return. It takes two or three weeks for your hearing to reset, and during it the ordinary world is unbearable. Doors, cutlery, traffic, a child's shriek in a supermarket, your own laugh coming out of you like something breaking. You will find yourself signing at people. You will speak too quietly to be heard and be irritated at having to repeat yourself, and this can last years; ask anyone who has done a long stay.

And at night, for a long time, you will miss the drip — a few hundred tuned wooden buckets being tapped at random by a very patient hand — and you will have no way to explain to anyone who has not stood there why it is the most restful sound in any world, and why a place that fines a man for laughing is one of the few you have ever been where you could actually hear yourself think.

## The short list, to memorize

1. Cough at home.
2. Freeze the kit; carry no soil, no seed, no wood, no scent.
3. Cut the velcro off.
4. Six months of voice training, or don't come.
5. Thirty signs, and the palm-forward warning most of all.
6. Look before you set anything down. The beetles are staff.
7. What the plant has taken is left there.
8. Do not run. Not for anything.
9. You own no ground. You do not own a distance either. Behave accordingly.
