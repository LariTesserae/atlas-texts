# Notes for the Visitor
### being what one ought to have done before, and did not

---

## 1. First understand what you are

You are not a person here. You are a weather event.

Everything the Cold Room is, it is because nothing moves. No wind, no sun-cycle, no flood, no frost-thaw, no spring. Its architecture stands not because it is strong but because nothing has ever been asked of it. Its hydrology is condensation. Its calendar is a crop of frost feathers that neither grows nor retreats. Its population is pinned to the yield of a few hundred hair nets.

Into this you propose to introduce a hundred-watt heat source, four per cent carbon dioxide, thirty thousand shed skin cells a minute, a body of unfamiliar spores, and a shadow.

Prepare accordingly. Almost all of the preparation is subtraction.

---

## 2. Who should not go

- Anyone with a cough, a cold, hay fever, or a tickle they are optimistic about. A sneeze into the gap would take the roofs off three terraces and drive a shockwave into the chalk. There is no rebuilding tradition there. There is nothing *to* rebuild with.
- Anyone with a tremor, restless legs, a habit of shifting, or a back that will insist on being attended to after forty minutes.
- Anyone claustrophobic. Your head will be in a hole under three locked slabs for hours, in still air, in silence, with your own pulse very loud.
- Anyone who has been caving, bat-surveying, mucking out, or gardening in the past fortnight without a full decontamination. See §5.
- Anyone who wants to *bring something back*.
- Anyone in a hurry. If you cannot spend an hour moving your face the last three feet, do not set out. You will see nothing but empty streets and will have terrified a town for nothing.

Head measurement matters more than shoulders unless you intend to enter, which you do not. Measure across the ears with a hood on. If you exceed the gap you may still lie above it and look down the axis, which is less, but is not nothing.

---

## 3. When

**Season.** Late October to early December. Overcast, high, white, windless, a day after fog. You want the room at its brightest, the combs freshly worked, the air above already cold so that your presence is a smaller thermal insult, and the wren elsewhere.

**Never in the nesting season.** Not because of danger to you. Because a human lying still on a limestone shelf for four hours is an advertisement visible to every crow, kestrel and fox in a mile of flat country. You will be inspected. The thing you must not do is make the gap *interesting* to the fen's larger fauna. Leave nothing that smells. Leave no flattened place. Arrive and depart as an absence.

**Avoid hot still summer days.** That is when the thermal contrast between you and the cold pool is greatest, and when a careless warm mass at the gap can start a slow overturning that takes the pool's top metre out into the fen. The town would survive it. The town has, perhaps, survived it. There are marks about that too.

**Avoid rain, and avoid the hour after rain.** Your body is a watershed. Whatever lands on you runs somewhere, and the somewhere is downhill, and downhill is the gap.

**Hour.** Arrive in the dark, be in position before it is light, and let the town find you already there rather than watch you arrive. This is the single greatest improvement you can make to your visit and it costs you only a night's sleep and some discomfort.

---

## 4. The ground

Peat is a drumskin. A footfall at ten paces is felt in the Cold Room; a footfall at three is an earthquake without precedent in the record. The limestone shelf itself is better but transmits sharply.

Bring hurdles — two or three light woven or slatted panels — and lay them from the field edge inward, ahead of yourself, lying down to place each one, so that your weight is spread and your final position is on timber, not on ground. Lay the last one the night before if you can, and let the vibration die out overnight.

**The slabs.** Three of them, fallen against each other and locked. They have not moved since before anyone's memory and they are the roof of the world. Do not touch them. Do not lean on them, do not use one as a handhold, do not rest your forearm on the lip, do not put a tripod leg on one. The floor of the room lies below the water table and is dry only because the shelf holds. Everything else you could do wrong is survivable. This is not.

Build a low frame — a simple A-frame or a plank bridge on two blocks — that carries your chest and shoulders and spans the gap without bearing on any slab. Test it with your weight, on another piece of ground, a week beforehand.

---

## 5. Decontamination

Take this more seriously than anything else in these notes.

A permanently damp, permanently cold, windless chamber with eight kinds of beetle and no predator for a cellar mould is the easiest thing in any world to destroy. You will not destroy it with your hands. You will destroy it with a spore on your cuff.

- New or freshly laundered outer clothing, then bagged and frozen forty-eight hours, then sealed until the morning.
- No boots that have been in a barn, a wood, a compost heap, a cave, a bird hide, a duck pond, or another world's soil. Rubber, scrubbed, disinfected, dried.
- Nothing organic in your pockets. No fruit, no bread, no seed, no wool from a sheep you liked.
- Nails cut and scrubbed. No plasters. No ointment.
- No soap, shampoo, deodorant, perfume, aftershave, hand cream, sunscreen, lip balm, or insect repellent for seven days before. Wash in plain water. DEET in a sealed still room is a nerve agent poured into a valley.
- No garlic, no alcohol, no strong spice for two days. You will be exhaling into a basin that does not empty.
- Shave, or net the beard. A dropped hair falls straight and stays forever. It is not lost to them — human hair is a beam, a cable, a year's cordage — but it is not yours to donate by accident.

---

## 6. Kit

**Essential**

- Hurdles or slats (§4).
- Chest frame (§4).
- Foam mat, full length, and a second one. You will be still for hours on cold wet November peat. Hypothermia is the only thing in this account that is likely to kill *you*.
- Heavy torso insulation — wool, down, a bivvy bag drawn up to the armpits. Warm body, cold face: this is the whole thermal trick. Your thermal plume must rise from your feet, a body-length away, and never from your cheek.
- A face mask of open cloth, pale, unscented, laundered, which catches what you shed and nothing else.
- **A breathing line.** Soft mask over nose and mouth; exhaust hose two metres long led back over the shoulder and *down-slope and downwind*, so that your exhaled air — cold, heavy, wet, bad — cannot find its way home into the pit. Practise until you can breathe through it without thinking, because you will be told to think about nothing else.
- A nose-catch. Cold air on the face produces a running nose within twenty minutes, reliably, in everyone. A drop from your nose is a cistern's worth of contaminated water falling from the sky onto a street.
- A CO₂ meter on a thread, lowered the night before and read before you put your face anywhere. Peat respires; cold pools hold what collects in them. If the gap is loaded you will feel clever and calm and then you will fall forward, and you will be the shrew.
- A tell-tale: a single filament on a wire, set at the lip, to show you which way the air is going before you put a body in its path.
- Patience. Bring more than you think.

**Permitted, with care**

- Optics. Not on your first visit. On your first visit, look. If you must, later: a long-working-distance monocular or a close-focusing scope, matte, *pale-coloured*, pre-cooled overnight in a box at ambient so that it neither fogs nor convects. Never a large black circle presented over the gap: that is an eye, and they know what eyes are.
- A camera with a silent electronic shutter, no fan, no autofocus motor, no indicator light, taped over. No flash, ever, under any circumstance, for any reason.

**Absolutely not**

- **Light.** No torch, no headlamp, no phone screen, not for a second, not shielded, not red. Nothing in that room has ever had a lit side and a dark side. The only moving shadow in their history is the wren's. Do not be the second.
- Anything that ticks, hums, whines, clicks, beeps, vibrates, or charges.
- Zips, velcro, nylon, crisp waterproofs. Wool and cotton. Your sleeve at twenty centimetres is a landslide.
- Food, drink, thermos (steam), sweets, chewing gum.
- Any container you intend to put something into.

---

## 7. The body, in the weeks before

**Stillness.** Train it. Lie prone on a hard floor with your head over the edge of a step for ninety minutes, three times, in the month before. You are not training endurance; you are learning which parts of you will demand to be adjusted, so that you can adjust them in advance rather than at minute sixty.

**Cold on the face.** The cold-water reflex will slow your heart and make you stupid and euphoric. Acclimatise: cold face immersions, two minutes, daily, for three weeks. You want the first shock over with before you meet anyone.

**Hands.** If you ever intend contact, your hands must be soft, unscented, unscarred enough to feel, and held at about the temperature of the room — cold enough not to scald, warm enough to sense a pattern of pressure lighter than a fly's footstep. This is a genuinely difficult calibration. Practise reading raised dots or grains through gloved and chilled fingers in cold water. Most visitors never need this. Prepare as though you will.

**Breath-holding**, to a modest standard, so that you can stop breathing entirely for the twenty seconds it takes to withdraw if the line fails.

**The nine strokes.** Learn to draw the town's name. Copy it from the record; practise it ten thousand times on glass with a badger bristle mounted in a chalk handle, until it is fast, because it is written fast. Then understand that you will almost certainly not use it, and that you will want to, and that wanting to is exactly the reason not to. The name is redrawn each dawn by whoever passes first. You did not pass. You arrived.

---

## 8. The last hour

Everything now is about the light.

Their alarm is not sound and not smell. It is **a change in the light through the gap**. The wren announces itself by dimming the sky. A head appearing at the gap at ordinary human speed is a wren, exactly, and the streets will be empty before you have finished arriving, and you will look down into a beautiful and perfectly convincing piece of architecture and conclude that the accounts were exaggerated.

So: move slower than a cloud.

From the moment your silhouette can affect the light at the gap — call it a metre out — allow **sixty minutes** to bring your face into position. Approach from the side, not down the axis. Move in increments you cannot perceive yourself making. If you can feel yourself moving, you are moving too fast. A useful discipline: advance only on the drip. One drop falls into the thumbprint pool roughly every forty seconds; move on the drop and be still between; and when you have run out of ways to measure a small enough movement, wait two drops and move on the third.

When you are in place, **stop for another twenty minutes before you look at anything.** Let the light settle. Let your breath find its route. Let the frost feathers decide about you.

The streets will begin again. They begin the way water begins.

---

## 9. While you are there

- **Do not speak.** Not a word, not a whisper, not a murmur of delight. Nothing there has ever developed a use for voice and you have no idea what an unmediated human syllable does in a chamber roofed with locked stone. Assume the worst. The loudest recurring event in that town is a drop of water.
- **Do not breathe into the room.** This is what the line is for. If the line fails, withdraw. Do not ration it, do not take "just one".
- **Do not warm anything.** Notice where your cheek is. Notice where the north sills are. The frost feathers are not decoration; they are a two-hundred-year unaltered pattern, and there is reason to believe they are consulted. Breathe on them and you have both broken the clock and told a lie with it.
- **Do not let a lens or a metal surface fog and drip.**
- **Do not write on the fog-film.** Every polished flint face in that town is a public slate carrying the day's notices, prices, gossip and prayers. Your fingertip is a district. A wipe of your glove across a wall is a night's civic conversation erased and overwritten with an idiot smear.
- **Do not scratch anything.** Scratched marks are for what must be remembered longest. There are exactly two categories of scratched event on the north wall: totals, and the shrew. Whatever you scratch joins that company.
- **Do not reach in.** Not to touch, not to steady yourself, not to move the pretty shell.
- **Do not take.** Not a shell, not a tile, not a sample, not a "dead" piece of moss. A fist of moss is two hundred years old and is somebody's pasture. There is no such thing as a spare thing in the Cold Room. The whole place is exactly the size of its water.
- **And the rule that supersedes all the others:** if any of them comes and stands in your way, stop, and do not resume. That is how refusal is said there. It is said patiently, at enormous personal risk, by someone the size of a grain of rice standing in front of something the size of weather. It will be said once. Honour it absolutely and immediately, and then go on being still, so that they learn that it works.

---

## 10. Contact, if it comes

It will not come on your first visit, or your fifth. It comes, when it comes, as a shape drawn on a flint face angled so that you can see it — which means someone has thought about your eye and where it is. Assume you are being written to long before you notice.

**Gifts.** The instinct is to give food or water. Do not. The population is pinned, deliberately, by the yield of the fog-combs; feeding or watering them is not generosity but demographic vandalism, and the correction will be made in the only way a fixed-hearth town can make it.

The one sound gift is **replacement**: combed coarse animal hair — badger, hare, pony guard hair, scaled and crimped, oiled — in a quantity no greater than the combs will have lost to rot since your last visit, which you cannot know, which is why you leave a little and take the rest away.

Leave it on the lip of the shelf, outside, dry, in a hollow of chalk. Do not lower it in. Do not look to see if it has been taken. If it is there next year, take it home.

**What they most want** is not hair. It is news of the place where nothing is finished. And you cannot give it without scratching, and you must not scratch. Sit with that. Everyone does.

---

## 11. Leaving

Leave the way you came: an hour to withdraw, on the drip.

Then police the ground. Every fibre, every wrapper that did not exist, every crumb, every bootprint you can lift. Take the hurdles. Take the frame. Take, if you can bear it, the flattened grass, by combing it up.

Leave no track that a fox will follow to the gap out of curiosity.

Then walk out over the field and understand that you have walked over it, as most do, and that this is the correct relationship and you have just spent four hours in the other one.

---

## 12. Afterwards

Two things happen to visitors and you should know about them in advance.

The first is small and passes: for a week or so, ordinary rooms will seem loud, and your own body will seem loud, and you will find yourself setting cups down with absurd care, and closing doors with both hands.

The second does not pass. You will have stood at the edge of the only finished place you are ever likely to see, and then come back up into a world of scaffolding and plans and unfinished things and wind that moves things without being asked to, and you will not be able to decide whether you have seen the end of a civilisation or the point of one. Their scratched accounts of the fen say *a place where nothing is finished* and they do not mean it as praise, and you will be unable to prove them wrong, and you will go on living up here anyway, which is the whole of the problem.

---

## 13. The thing you must decide about beforehand, because you will not be able to decide there

The fen is drained. Drained peat wastes; wasting peat shrinks. The limestone shelf that once stood a hand's width above the ground stands higher every decade, and will stand higher still, and one day it will not be a crack in a flat field but a rock on a hill, with the wind reaching it, and the water table a foot lower than the floor, and the chalk no longer weeping.

The Cold Room's unchangingness is not eternal. It is *contingent*, and the contingency is a pump station four miles off, running on a schedule set by people who have never heard of it.

You will look down into a town whose entire civic achievement is the perfect matching of its numbers to its water, and you will know that the water has a date on it, and they will not, and the only means you have of telling them is a scratch on the north wall beside the mark for the shrew.

Decide before you go. Decide with people who will argue with you. Do not decide lying on your stomach at four in the morning with a cold face and a slowing heart, looking at nine wet strokes drying on a flint face by a pool the size of a thumbprint.

Most who have decided have decided not to.

They may be wrong. Bring the bristle anyway. Leave it in your pocket.
