# Preparing for the Late Plain

## The one fact that organises everything else

Nineteen kilometres. Hold that number, because it is not decorative.

At nineteen kilometres of altitude, in any atmosphere resembling the one you evolved for, the ambient pressure falls to roughly six kilopascals — the pressure at which water boils at the temperature of your own blood. This is the line above which a human being stops being a workable container for liquid. Below it you can suffer; above it you dissolve. Your saliva foams, your eyes fog from within, the water in your soft tissues leaves without asking.

So understand what you are visiting. You are going to a landscape that has spent every drop it ever had, that has no water in any direction forever, that hosts one hour of frost per night and lets it go straight to vapour — and you are going to walk across it as the only liquid water for a thousand kilometres, carried in a bag, under pressure, at a temperature the plain regards as absurd. Everything in the environment is a slow, patient argument that you should not exist in this form. Your entire preparation is the counter-argument, and it must be airtight, in the literal sense.

Do not think of this as a hike at altitude. Think of it as a spacewalk with a floor.

## Where to arrive, and the forty hours before

Arrive at the escarpment rim, at a staging site, in air. Do not translate directly onto the pavement.

This is not superstition. Whatever your transit method, you want your first fifteen minutes in this world to happen somewhere you can be dragged back from, and you want them to happen *after* you have already been breathing pure oxygen for four hours. Suit pressures are low — most rigs run at a third of an atmosphere — and if you step onto the plain with sea-level nitrogen still in solution, you will spend your first afternoon on the flattest surface in the multiverse being crippled by bends in a place with no water, no help, and no way to lie down comfortably.

So: prebreathe upstream, in a world with a pressure you can survive, and cross over already sealed.

The other forty-hour preparations are unglamorous and non-negotiable. Shave everything that would break a seal. Have your teeth checked for voids under fillings, which will hurt in a way you will remember for years. Eat nothing gas-forming for a full day; trapped bowel gas expands by a factor of fifteen at plain pressure and there is no dignified remedy. Trim your nails. Overhydrate in advance until it is unpleasant, because you will not drink enough out there and nobody ever has.

And check the local gravity before you commit to a load. A flat continent standing nineteen kilometres above datum is not a thing a heavy world can hold up; expect it to be light, and expect that lightness to lie to you about how much you can carry, until the wind gets a grip.

## The suit

Rent, don't improvise. The specification for the Late Plain is unusual enough that generic high-altitude gear will kill you slowly by four separate mechanisms.

**Pressure.** Standard. Nothing exotic. A soft suit at low pressure with pure oxygen is fine and lets you walk, which matters more here than anywhere, because you will walk a long way.

**Abrasion.** This is where most kit fails. The wind here has almost no weight — three hundred kilometres an hour at nine per cent density leans on you with maybe twenty-five kilos of steady push, an insistent hand between the shoulder blades, tiring rather than dangerous. But it is *made of dust*, and that dust is angular silicate travelling at eighty metres a second. It is a sandblasting cabinet with a horizon. Your outer layer is a consumable. Budget one sacrificial oversuit per week and a great many peel-off visor films — a dozen a day is not extravagant. The single most common cause of an aborted crossing is not injury or hypoxia. It is a traveller with three good weeks of oxygen and a visor sanded to frosted glass.

**Thermal.** Your suit must handle being in two climates at once. Sunward, the light arrives undiluted and stings; shadeward, there is frost that has never in the history of the world been liquid. With almost no air to convect, you cannot average these out; you will cook on one side and lose heat on the other simultaneously, and the seam between them runs down your body. You want an active fluid loop and a radiator, and — critically — **you must not use an evaporative cooler.** Sublimators throw away water. You cannot afford to throw away water here for reasons that begin as budgetary and end as theological (see below).

Carry a soft brush. Your radiator will silt up, and if you leave it silted long enough, the dust will begin making copies of your fins.

## Charge

Here is the detail nobody warns you about, and it will break your instruments.

Six kilopascals is very nearly the worst pressure in existence for holding a voltage. It sits in the trough of the breakdown curve: too thin to insulate, too thick to be vacuum. Small gaps arc at a few hundred volts. And this is a world whose dust grips itself electrostatically, whose assembly-fields discharge in silent violet sheets all night, and whose ground is a dry lacquer of manganese varnish over dry rock — an insulator continental in scale. There is nothing to earth to. You *are* the capacitor.

Consequences:

- Every piece of electronics goes in a pressurised housing or is fully potted. Exposed connectors will corona and then fail. Cameras with a pinhole in the case will die in an afternoon.
- Expect your radio to be useless when the fields are active, which is most of the time and all of the night. The plain is quiet to your ears and screaming across the whole spectrum.
- Instead, bring a **mirror**. The air has no haze, the ground has no slope, and because the plain is a plane on a curved world its far reaches *rise* toward you rather than sinking away. Sightlines of two hundred kilometres are ordinary. A heliograph is the most reliable communication technology on the Late Plain, and the most beautiful. Learn a code before you go.
- Drag a bleed chain and accept that it does little. Discharge deliberately, on purpose, before you touch anything you care about.

## Dust discipline

Two rules govern all conduct here, and they are the reason experienced crossers move like people in a temple.

**It copies what it touches.** Set down a cup and by dusk there is a colonnade of cups. Leave a bootprint and by morning there is a two-metre print in your own tread, and by the next noon a range of them marching downwind, each generation larger and rougher and further from the truth.

**It stops at skin.** It will copy your glove and not your hand. Nothing that respires has ever been modelled. This is the only comfortable fact in the region and you should not lean on it harder than it deserves.

From these follow the practical rules:

- **Never navigate by your own tracks.** This is the most lethal misunderstanding available to you. Your prints are not erased; they are *republished*, at scale, in the direction the wind is going. Follow your trail back and you will be following an argument that gets louder and less accurate the further you go, and it will lead you downwind of where you started, into an expanding range of footprint-shaped hills that were once yours. Inertial navigation, sun sights, star fixes. Nothing else. Trust the instrument over the ground even when the ground is wearing your shoes.
- **Camp with the enlargement vector pointing away from you.** Whatever you leave sitting on the pavement will be copied bigger, and then bigger, and then it will fall. A twenty-metre reproduction of your own pack collapsing across your tent will not sound like anything. Work out the wind, and sleep upwind of your own leavings.
