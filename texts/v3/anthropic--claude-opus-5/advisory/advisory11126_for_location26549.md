# Preparing to Visit the Bubble in the Nine-Week Rock

## First, the honest sentence

There is no version of this trip in which you are a visitor. You are an event. In nine weeks this world has produced exactly one thumbnail of debris; you will produce more than that in your first ninety seconds. Everything below is written on the assumption that you have decided to go anyway, and that you would like to do it well — meaning briefly, competently, and with a full accounting of what you break.

If you can be talked out of the body and into a 6 mm borescope on a nickel-alloy pushrod, take that. You will see more, for longer, and the place will survive you. The rest of this assumes you can't be talked out of it.

---

## Timing

You are visiting a thirty-year world in its ninth week. The whole arc is: engine, decline, silence, cold black pocket, eleven thousand years, landslide, ninety seconds of sky.

Weeks 9–24 are the peak. Everything is on: the stroke is strong, the whistle is a full nineteen minutes, the cracking is loud, the sublimate gardens are actively growing, and the margin is at maximum population density. Go later and it is all a little quieter, a little cooler, a little less. Go earlier and the dome is even less stable than it is now, and the pocket's lower wall may not have finished collapsing.

Arrive at week nine to eleven. Do not arrive twice. A second entry adds nothing to your knowledge and doubles the damage.

Within the day: the cycle is thirty-eight minutes — nineteen of whistle (the column rising, gas driving out through the eastern slot), three or four of cracking as the stroke reverses, then roughly fifteen of drawback and relative quiet. **All human activity happens in the drawback.** During the pressure stroke, the pocket is a gun pointed at whatever you have connected to it.

## Getting to the mountain

Four thousand one hundred metres, on the northeast flank of a lava dome nine weeks old.

**Altitude.** Two weeks minimum of acclimatisation, sleeping below 3,200 m, working days up to 4,000. You will be doing heavy physical work in a sealed suit at altitude, which is the worst combination available: your maximum oxygen uptake is down about 30% and your heat dumping is compromised by dry thin air that also can't carry heat away from your radiator. Budget for feeling stupid. Altitude makes people make small decisions badly, and every decision on this trip is small.

**The dome.** It is still deciding its shape. That is the description's phrase and it is the correct one — the thing is inflating, shedding, and periodically collapsing. The hazards, in order of how likely they are to kill you before you ever reach the rock:

- *Dome collapse and pyroclastic density currents.* Non-survivable. Your only defence is topography and escape time. Camp on a ridge, never in a drainage, never on the run-out apron. Know the wind.
- *Ballistics.* Bombs from vent explosions. Helmet, and don't linger on open slope.
- *Gas at the surface.* CO₂ pools in hollows and kills quietly; SO₂ and HCl are in the plume. Carry a four-gas monitor with an SO₂ head and a CO₂ NDIR, and treat every depression as flooded until proved otherwise.
- *The talus itself.* Fresh dome blocks are glass. They are sharp in a way ordinary rock is not — the thumbnail flake on the pocket floor is "sharp enough to open skin" and so is everything you will walk across. Boots with a real shank, gaiters, gloves on all the time. Also many of these blocks are internally hot and hollow; a foot goes through a crust into a 300° cavity every few hundred steps somebody takes on new dome.

**Escape.** Fixed, rehearsed, and walkable in the dark by someone with heat exhaustion. Two routes. A person at the trailhead with a radio and a deadline.

## Getting inside: two options

### Option A — the bore

Eleven metres of new dome rock. You will be drilling a body-sized tube through brittle, stressed, hot glass into a pressurised cavity.

- **No water.** Not for cooling the bit, not for anything. It flashes, it fractures the rock ahead of you unpredictably, and it violates the single most characteristic fact of the destination. Dry cutting with inert gas flush — argon or CO₂, cold-injected as little as you can manage.
- **Case as you go.** Steel or Inconel casing, grouted with a ceramic mortar behind you, so the bore doesn't spall its walls onto you and so it doesn't shed eleven metres of fresh rubble into the pocket. Uncased, this hole *is* an erosional event that continues after you leave.
- **Break through on the drawback stroke,** with a blast valve at the collar and everyone behind it. When you hole through during a pressure stroke you get 340°C gas at overpressure jetting down an eleven-metre barrel. It will not kill the person at the far end because there won't be one, but it will strip your casing and it will empty the pocket's gas budget in a way that changes the chemistry inside for days.
- **Airlock.** A collar chamber with two valves, so that entry and exit don't hand the pocket a lungful of cold mountain air each time. You cannot prevent all exchange. You can prevent the worst of it.
- **Accept the permanent consequence.** An eleven-metre cased hole is a new thermal shortcut into the world. The survivable margin — the whole biosphere, that three-to-eight-centimetre shell — is defined by exactly this: interior heat meeting cold air infiltrating through fractures. You have just made the biggest fracture in the dome. The margin will bulge toward your bore and stay bulged. Plug it on the way out with the same ceramic mortar and it will still never be quite what it was.

### Option B — direct translation

If your multiverse craft can put you inside, it can put you inside. This is kinder to the mountain and much worse for you.

You arrive already suited, already breathing, lying down, in the dark, in a two-and-a-half by one metre lens full of 340°C acid gas, on a floor of fracture-sharp rubble with glowing cracks in it, with no exit you can operate yourself. Your extraction must be automatic and timed, because there is a real chance you will be unable to signal. Set the recall for nine minutes and do not build in an override; a heat-stressed person will use the override.

Also: you displace about a tenth of a cubic metre of gas on arrival, instantaneously, which is a small pressure pulse in a world where the only sounds are a piston and its cracking. The ceiling will tick at you. Some of the sublimate fans on the west wall — the "structurally absurd" ones, the chimneys nothing has yet knocked over — will not survive your arrival. Just so you know what the pressure wave costs.

**Recommendation:** Option A if you have a support team and three weeks. Option B if you are alone, and only if you have already accepted that the west wall gardens are partially forfeit.
