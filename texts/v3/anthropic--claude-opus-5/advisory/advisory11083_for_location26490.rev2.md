# Preparing for the Basin

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are not visiting a settlement. You are visiting a maintenance process that has people in it. Everything you will see — walls, floors, paths, the town itself — exists only because someone cut something back this morning and will cut it back again tomorrow. There is no built environment there. There is only the difference between what is being kept open and what has closed.

This single fact should organize your entire preparation, because it means the usual visitor's posture — observe, don't interfere, leave no trace — is not neutral there. It is parasitic. A guest who occupies a room and does not prune it is an ongoing cost paid by someone else's hands. Go prepared to *work*, or don't go.

## Your body

**Altitude.** A permanent cloud belt in a ridge saddle puts you somewhere between 2,400 and 3,400 metres. Give yourself ten to fourteen days of staged acclimatization below the rim, in the warmer country the trail comes up from. Do not ascend the day you arrive in-world. The trail up is a long climb in thin wet air and you will do it carrying everything you own.

**Wet cold.** This is the actual danger and it kills more travelers than anything with teeth. The Eye never freezes, so ambient temperature probably sits between 6°C and 15°C — not dramatic, entirely survivable, and lethal over weeks because you will never once be dry. You will be cold in a low, grinding, cumulative way that erodes judgment before it erodes body heat. Train for this specifically: spend weeks before departure being damp and cool and functional. Learn what your own early hypothermia looks like — the clumsiness, the irritability, the desire to sit down "for a moment." Learn it while someone can still pull you out of it.

**Skin and feet.** In permanent saturation, small wounds do not heal. They go sideways into ulcers. A thorn scratch on day three can be a suppurating hole on day twenty. Arrive with:
- fingernails cut short and hands already calloused
- no existing open skin anywhere, including between toes
- an immersion-foot discipline: every single night, feet out, wiped, warmed against your own belly if necessary, before you sleep. Non-negotiable. This is the ritual that will keep you ambulatory.
- antifungal powder, more than you think, in sealed containers. Everything fungal thrives there. You will thrive less.

**Gut.** You will drink tannin-brown bog water squeezed from moss, and eat tubers, smoked meat, fermented things, and insects. Plan for a week of misery. Pre-loading with the local flora is impossible, so budget the week: arrive with a fortnight's slack in your schedule and a rehydration-salts supply. *Never* drink from the Eye. It is cold, anoxic, and full of the town's dead, and the locals do not need to say this out loud to each other.

**Light and mood.** Shadowless grey light, all day, for however long you stay. Depth perception degrades — you will misjudge edges, holes, and the distance to branches, which matters when you are on a flexing mat above ten arm-lengths of peat. Bring vitamin D. Bring the knowledge that flat light plus sensory monotony plus constant sound produces, in many people, a peculiar low-grade despair around week three. It lifts. Know in advance that it lifts.

**Hearing.** Do not bring good earplugs and do not use them beyond your first two or three nights. The continuous insect register is load-bearing for your inner ear; people there get vertigo on the rare nights it stops. Blocking it deliberately and then unblocking it does the same thing to you, repeatedly. Habituate instead. Accept four bad nights.

## Skills to acquire before you go

**Cordage.** Learn to make two-ply cord by thigh-rolling and reverse-wrap, with a natural bast fiber, until you can do it without looking, while walking, while talking, while eating. This is not a charming local craft. It is the town's currency of competence and its default state of the hands. A person who sits with idle hands there reads as a small child or as ill. Six months of practice, twenty minutes a day, and you arrive as an adult.

**Pruning.** Learn to cut green wood cleanly with a non-metal edge — chert flake, obsidian, split cane. It is a completely different motion from cutting with steel: less force, more angle, a slicing draw. Learn what a correct cut looks like on a living stem and why a crushed one invites rot. Learn the rhythm. Your incompetence will be *audible* — the snick-snick-snick is the town's heartbeat and yours will sound wrong, and everyone will hear it, everywhere, all day.

**Walking on a quaking mat.** Practice on real peatland if your world has any. The technique is: flat foot, slow weight transfer, follow the root lines and the sedge tussocks, never the smooth green, never a heel strike. Understand that your steps *transmit*. A hundred paces away someone feels you coming, and a stranger's gait is unmistakable. Heavy-footed hurrying is, socially, shouting.

Also rehearse the fall: leg in to the knee, cold black grip, the suck. You do not yank. You lean back, spread your weight over the other leg and both arms, and work the trapped foot loose with a slow twisting pump. Panicked yanking is how ankles and footwear are lost.

**Climbing.** Ladders, poles, root buttresses, wet bark, in the dark, with load. When the browsers come, the town goes up and you go up with it. Build grip endurance above all other fitness; forearms are the limiting factor in everything there — cord, climbing, pruning, carrying.

**Their clock.** There is no sun. Time is the cloud coming up the western slope and the cloud drawing off. Learn to read the frog surge and the bird silence. A watch is fine but it makes you the only person in the basin operating on a private schedule, which is exactly as isolating as it sounds.

**Two drum patterns, minimum:** *someone is coming up the trail*, and the browser alarm. Learn them cold. You will not have time to look them up.

## What to bring

The organizing principle: **nothing is dry, and rot is a tax of about a third per year.** Anything you bring will begin dying on arrival.

**Containers first, contents second.** Resin-sealed gourds, smoke-blackened baskets with waxed liners, sealed jars. Pack in nested layers. Assume anything in a cloth bag is already wet.

**No leather.** It will mould, stiffen, stink, and fail, usually at a seam, usually at the worst moment. No paper journals — waxed wooden tablets or nothing, and honestly, prefer memory and write it all down after you descend. There is also a quiet impropriety in a stranger recording things in a place where nothing is recorded and ownership is only ever *what you are currently keeping open*.

**Metal: very little, and choose carefully.** A steel machete would be the most valuable object in the basin, would reshape local labor overnight, would make its owner strange, and would still corrode into uselessness within a decade. Don't. The economy already knows copper, and copper needles are wanted, small, and beautifully appropriate. Bring several. Bring salt — plain, coarse, in sealed gourds — which at that altitude, on that diet, with that sweat load, is worth more than anything shiny.

Keep any personal metal — a knife, your extraction device, a lens — greased with hard animal fat, wrapped, and out of sight. Check and re-grease weekly. Set a repeating reminder in your own head; you will forget, because the air makes you forget that air can be dry.

**Footwear.** Boots are wrong. Rigid soles punch through the moss mat, lugs tear the root layer, laces make a leg-in-peat event into an ankle injury, and the suction will take a boot and keep it. Go with a woven or lashed sandal you can re-make, or go barefoot if you have months to toughen your soles first (you probably don't). Bring three spare pairs of whatever you choose; they will rot. Have something soft, dry, and reserved *only* for sleeping in.

**Clothing.** Layers of wool or an equivalent that insulates wet. No cotton anywhere. Nothing that needs to dry to function. Accept that everything you wear will be permanently damp and smell of ancient leaves, and that you will have exactly one dry sleeping layer, kept in a sealed gourd, taken out only at night, and defended like your life, because it is.

**Do not bring a firestarter and expect it to matter.** Ignition is not the problem there. Fuel is. Nothing burns. The raised clay hearth-bowls with their slow smouldering resinous root-blocks are a century-old technology for the specific problem of a place where wood never dries, and your lighter solves none of it. If you want to contribute to fire, bring resin, and bring your patience.

**Calories.** The cold-wet plus the climbing plus the constant low-grade work will burn far more than you expect. Bring dense smoked fat and hard rendered stuff in sealed containers, and understand you're bringing it partly to *give*, because arriving as a mouth is bad and arriving as a mouth with provisions is fine.

**Medical.** Antiseptic, antifungal, rehydration salts, a real wound kit, and the discipline to treat a scratch the day you get it.

## The quarantine you owe them

Thousands of frogs per pace of moss. Whole populations, in a closed high basin, with no gradient and no outflow.

If your origin world has chytrid fungus — and if you come from anything resembling a modern Earth, it does — you are carrying a plague that could empty that basin of sound within two years, and the sound is the alarm system, and the alarm system is how the town knows something large has come in.

So: every item that touches the ground, and every item that touched water anywhere else, gets sterilized before you ascend. Heat, or bleach, or both, done properly, done twice. New footwear that has never touched another wetland. No mud in seams, no seeds in cuffs, no soil in tread. Do this even if nobody checks. *Especially* if nobody checks.

Bring no animals. Bring nothing alive.

And going the other way: come back through a quarantine too. You'll be carrying spores in your lungs and things in your gut that your home world hasn't met.

## Social preparation

**Ask for something to keep open.** This is the closest thing to a visa. Ownership there is not recorded and does not need to be — what you own is what you are currently maintaining. Request a path, a short stretch of hedge, a roof-corner. Maintain it daily without being reminded. On the day you stop, you stop owning it, and this will be understood by everyone instantly and without comment.

**Contribute labor before you ask questions.** Two weeks of cord, pruning, and fiber-pounding buys more than any gift.

**Learn the word for "currently being kept."** There is no word for finished. There is no completed thing. Someone will eventually ask you what you are for, and the honest answer — *I am passing through, I am keeping nothing* — is the strangest thing about you and worth thinking about before you have to say it.

**Drill the reflex: silence means look up.** If the frogs stop and it is not a cloud-hour, you do not ask what's happening. You climb. Practice this until it is pre-verbal. You want to already be five arm-lengths up when your conscious mind catches on.

**Decide, in advance, about the Eye.** If you die there, you will be wrapped, weighted, and given to the pool, because there is no other option and no one is carrying a body down that trail. Say so plainly and early, to whoever is hosting you, so nobody has to improvise a courtesy around a stranger's corpse. It isn't sacred exactly. It's just where people go.

**Do not go down the western lip on your own.** Below the rim is a different country with different everything, reached by one trail, and the boundary is a rule, not a topography.

## Your way home

This is the part travelers get wrong, and it is the most Basin-shaped problem you will face.

Set your extraction point **on the rim, not in the bowl** — outside the peat, on ground that holds a shape, where the vegetation is thinner and the transition is clean.

Then understand that *the path to it will close*. Two weeks unused and a trail is gone. A month and a cache is inside a plant. The basin does not preserve your exit any more than it preserves anything else; it closes over gaps exactly as it does over everything, including departed people.

So: **walk your exit route and prune it, every week, without fail, from the day you arrive.** Cut it wide. Mark it with something living, because nothing dead lasts. If your stay runs long, cut it twice a week.

This is not a chore appended to your visit. This is the whole lesson of the place, handed to you in the most literal available form: the way out is not a thing you built once. It is a thing you are currently keeping open.

---

## A short list, if you take nothing else

- Ten days' acclimatization below the rim
- Six months of cordage practice
- Sterilized gear, for the frogs
- Copper needles and salt
- No leather, no cotton, no boots, no steel blade
- One dry sleeping layer in a sealed gourd, defended absolutely
- Feet out and dried every night
- Something to keep open, asked for on arrival
- The climb reflex, drilled
- A weekly-pruned path to the rim

Go in the season after the browsers have passed. The peat will be churned, the walls will be knuckles, everyone will be rebuilding, the fiber-cane will be coming up thick out of the black soup, and there will be more work than hands.

That is the best time to be a stranger there: when the town is visibly, cheerfully, collectively beginning again, which is the only thing it ever does.
