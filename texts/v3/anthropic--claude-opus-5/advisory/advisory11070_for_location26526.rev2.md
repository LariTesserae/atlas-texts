# Field Preparation: The Dry Canopy

## Notes for an ordinary human, first visit, two to three weeks on the plateau

---

## 0. The four things that kill people here

Before any packing list, hold these in your head as a single shape, because they interlock and the interlocking is what actually gets travelers:

1. **You are made of water, and nothing else here is.** You are a walking solvent spill in a world whose entire biochemistry was built on the permanent absence of solvent.
2. **You have to bring oxygen, and the plateau is four hundred kilometers of dry tinder that has never in four billion years met an oxidizer.** Your life support is, in the strictest sense, an incendiary device.
3. **You are an excellent conductor with a large capacitance, walking on a semiconducting carpet under a charged sky, among predators that hunt by completing circuits.**
4. **The place is finished and you are not**, and it will tell you so continuously, at the volume of heavy rain, for as long as you stay.

Everything below is elaboration.

---

## 1. Months out: the body

**Medical clearance.** Standard multiverse fitness plus: pulmonary function (you will be in a mask for the entire stay), audiometry (baseline — you will want the comparison afterward), a vestibular workup, and a cardiac check with particular attention to arrhythmia history. Infrasound at these amplitudes has a long, disputed relationship with the heart, and the plateau is not the place to settle the argument.

**Do not go with:** untreated reflux (you cannot vomit safely in a sealed mask), recent eye surgery, a history of seizure disorders with photic or acoustic triggers, or any implanted electronics that cannot be certified to survive a nearby column discharge. Pacemaker holders have gone and come back fine; they went with cages, protocols, and an exit plan measured in minutes.

**Conditioning.** Three things, none of which are what you expect:

- **Walking in dense, cold, dry air.** The atmosphere is around a bar but cold and CO₂-heavy, so it is noticeably *thicker* than Earth air — maybe a third again the density. Wind pushes harder. Your own legs push harder. Ten kilometers on the wick costs you like fifteen on a beach. Train weighted, train in cold, train in a mask.
- **Stairs made by someone else.** Stack risers are consistent and wrong: typically 21–24 cm with a tread depth around 40 cm, which sounds fine and is in fact a gait you do not have. The builders' legs were not your legs. Practice on mismatched stairs until your knees stop arguing. You may climb 150 vertical meters in a day, in cold, in a mask, on unfamiliar geometry, with no handrails and a 200-meter drop on the open side.
- **Load carriage with a mask.** Everything you consume, you carry. Everything you excrete, you carry back out. Budget 1.5–2 kg per person-day of consumables and about 0.8 kg/day of returning waste mass. A three-week solo trip is not a backpacking trip; it is an expedition with caches.

**Sensory training.** Two drills, both worth the trouble:

- **Beat navigation.** The stacks are tuned to each other, and standing between two of them you hear a beat that walks across the space — the beat rate is a function of where you stand. Practice on a pair of tuned infrasonic sources at home until you can put yourself on a line to within fifty meters by ear and by sternum. On the plateau, when the haze closes and your inertial unit drifts, this is the thing that walks you home.
- **Dark adaptation and orange-world color.** Noon here is a single lamp behind drawn curtains, and the lamp is orange. Spend evenings working under 30–80 lux of 2400 K light until you can read a gauge, judge a wound, and tell gray-green from bronze without thinking. Bring one small full-spectrum lamp for medical use only — you will need it once, to look at someone's lips or someone's blood, and you will be grateful.

## 2. The oxygen problem (read this twice)

This is the section that veterans get emphatic about.

The wick is finely divided, bone-dry, carbon-rich cellulose analogue, doped with metals, continuous over a hundred thousand square kilometers, and it is *permanently sputtering corona discharge from every tip*. It has no fire ecology because there has never been an oxidizer in this atmosphere. Nothing on the plateau is adapted to burn, which means nothing on the plateau is adapted to *survive* burning: no fire-resistant bark, no rhizome refugia, no seed banks waiting for the ash.

A sustained oxygen leak on that mat, in a corona field, does not start a brush fire. It starts something that is still going when your grandchildren are dead.

Therefore:

- **Carry the minimum possible oxygen inventory.** Small cylinders, many of them, never a single large one. Each cylinder individually valved, individually armored, burst discs plumbed into a CO₂ flood manifold so that a rupture dumps into inert gas rather than into the air.
- **All oxygen handling — every cylinder change, every regulator swap — happens inside a sealed glovebox flooded with local atmosphere.** No exceptions, no "it's a two-second job," no doing it in the wind because the glovebox is annoying. The glovebox is not annoying. The glovebox is the whole reason you're allowed here.
- **Run a closed or semi-closed loop, never open-circuit venting.** You do not want to be exhaling an oxygen-enriched plume into a static field, and you do not want to be exhaling water either (see §3).
- **Non-sparking everything.** Brass and beryllium-copper tools, no steel-on-rock, no ferrocerium anything, no lithium cells outside a sealed and vented case.
- **Charge discipline and oxygen discipline are the same discipline.** The hazard is not oxygen; the hazard is oxygen *and* a spark. You will be generating sparks with your feet all day (§5). Assume the ignition source is always present and manage the fuel side absolutely.

Fire drill, memorized: **valve, dump, distance, downwind-never.** If a line fails — close the isolation valve, trigger the CO₂ flood on the affected cylinder, move directly away at least 100 m, and never run downwind of the leak, because dense cold CO₂-rich air does not disperse an O₂ plume fast and you will be running inside it.

If it catches anyway: you will not put it out. Get to the nearest trampled apron — the bare licked rock around a stack base — and stand on stone, and call for extraction, and start composing what you are going to say.

## 3. Breathing, water, and the fact that you leak

**The mask is full-face, sealed, and it does not come off.** The ambient air is heavy with CO₂ in percentages that will have you in air-hunger within one breath and unconscious within a few minutes. There is also no oxygen to be had at all. There is no "just for a second." There is no lifting it to scratch your nose. Travelers have died from a two-second reflex while a fly— while something walked across their visor. Train the reflex out.

The seal is also protecting your eyes. CO₂ at these concentrations dissolves in tear film into carbonic acid; unsealed eyes sting within a minute and abrade within ten, and the dust does the rest.

**Water is the whole logistics problem and also the whole ethics problem.**

- Bring all of it. There is none there, in any form, in any phase, anywhere, for any price.
- Budget 3 L/day input in the cold dense air, of which a good condensing loop reclaims 1.2–1.8 L. Reclaim it. Not to save mass — to keep it off the ground.
- **Every gram of your water that reaches the wick is a wound.** Exhaled vapor at 6 °C condenses out fast. A slow leak in your loop, camped in one place overnight, will print a dead patch under your tent the shape of your breathing, and that patch does not come back. It does not regrow like a tiller's discharge-scar. It is a hole in a continuous organism several hundred kilometers across, and you made it by breathing wrong.
- Urine and solid waste: sealed, packed out, no negotiation. Menstrual products: sealed, packed out. **Blood is the worst spill of all** — it is water plus electrolytes plus iron, and on a semiconducting mat it does something ugly and locally permanent. Carry a dedicated fluid-capture kit in an outer pocket you can reach one-handed. Nosebleeds are extremely common here (dry air, infrasound, boundary crossings). Practice capturing one in a mask, fast, without panicking.

**And the prohibition everyone mentions and some people test:** do not pour water on the wick to see what happens. It snaps, the hiss goes silent in a ring around the drop, and the silence spreads about two meters and stops, and it never comes back. People have filmed it. You do not need to.

## 4. Cold, and the way dense air steals heat

Six to eight degrees sounds trivial. It is not, because the air is thick and dry and moving, and thick dry moving air at 6 °C pulls heat off you like water at 12 °C. Plan for an effective ambient in the low negatives whenever the wind is up.

- Insulation layered for **continuous mild cold and heavy exertion**, not for arctic extremes. Your enemy is a slow twelve-hour bleed, not a flash freeze.
- **No vapor barriers that vent.** Your suit should be a closed humidity system feeding the same condenser as your mask. You want your sweat back, both as water and as not-pollution.
- Extremities: the dense air gets into every gap. Overboots, cuff seals, a hood that mates to the mask.
- Batteries: derate everything 30% and keep cells against your torso. In the cold stack interiors, derate 60% and assume the worst.

**The cold rooms.** The interiors of older stacks are colder than the outside and radiate nothing back — they are a one-way heat sink, and not a metaphorical one. Your body heat goes in there and does not come out, and neither does your heater's. Rules:

- Hard timer, audible, non-defeatable. Twenty minutes for a first entry. Extend only with data.
- One person in, one person at the door, line of sight or a physical tether.
- Skin temperature telemetry. You will feel *fine* right up until you are not, because the cooling is even and painless and does not produce the stinging that normally warns you.
- Take a mechanical thermometer as well as an electronic one. Instruments misbehave in there — memory corruption, timebase drift, the occasional camera that comes out with frames it did not take. Bring a pencil and a wax-paper notebook. The sober ones bring a wind-up recorder.

## 5. Charge: how not to be a battery

You will walk all day on a dry semiconducting mat in a CO₂ atmosphere. You will triboelectrically charge to tens of kilovolts within minutes of your first step. This matters for three reasons: your oxygen, your electronics, and the sparrowers.

**Personal charge discipline:**

- **Controlled-resistance footwear.** Not insulating (you'd build up and dump catastrophically), not conducting (you'd be shorting the wick's stored charge to ground with every step, which is both theft and vandalism, and which *tastes like food* to things that hunt by charge). You want dissipative footwear in the 10⁶–10⁸ Ω range, soled broad and soft to spread your load over the filaments.
- **A bleed pigtail.** A fine carbon-fiber brush on a short mast above your pack, bonded to your suit. It sheds your accumulated charge as a quiet corona — the same trick the wick uses. You become, in a small way, a stack. It works, it is silent, and it means the sparrowers read you as ambient rather than as a meal.
- **Never a drag chain.** They work beautifully and they scour a two-centimeter furrow through a living tissue the size of a country. Don't.
- **Bond everything to the suit.** Every tool, every case, every camera. A floating metal object in your hand is a capacitor waiting for a chance.
- **Carry a field mill** and look at it as often as you look at your oxygen gauge. Rising local field is your only real warning.

**The column discharges.** Each stack fires its violet column on a schedule. The schedule is regular and it is *knowable*. Therefore:

> **Spend your first two working days doing nothing but sitting at a safe radius and logging discharge times for every stack you can see.** Everything after that is safer, and people who skip this step are the people who get caught on a crown.

The fetches converge *after* a discharge, riding the ionized wake, so they are confirmation, not warning — though experienced travelers swear the fetches begin drifting inward a minute or two *before*, and I believe them, and I would not bet my hearing on it. The reliable tells are the field mill and the hiss: the wick's white-noise floor dips for several seconds after a column, as the whole field drinks, and with practice you can hear a dip from a stack over the horizon and know its clock.

When one goes off within a kilometer of you: the flash stands for two or three seconds, then the crack arrives and then it rolls. Mouth open, ears protected, electronics in the Faraday box, and expect to lose whatever wasn't. Bring a full spare comms set, boxed, never both sets out at once.

## 6. Dust and lungs

The dust here has never been wet. Not once, not ever. It has not been rounded by water, only licked by wind, and it is fine, angular, feldspathic, and electrostatically clingy in a way that Earth dust simply is not. It will climb onto you against gravity. It will find every seal.

- **Double-seal everything, and carry a dust-sacrifice layer** you brush down at a set distance from camp.
- **Change filters on a schedule, not on a symptom.** Carry triple the filters you think you need.
- Assume any breach of your mask is a silicosis exposure as well as an asphyxiation event. Log every breach. Get imaged when you get home, and again in five years.
- Visors will haze from abrasion. Bring three.

**The one gift the dust gives you:** from the top of a stack, the pale contour lines written across the flats are a live map of the field structure. Photograph them every time you climb. They shift weekly, so they are useless as landmarks and priceless as data.

## 7. Noise, infrasound, and your sternum

The place is *loud*. A continuous white-noise floor at roughly rain-on-a-roof volume, forever, plus 8–60 Hz organ tones from the stacks, plus snaps, plus thunder.

- **Hearing protection from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave.** 25–30 dB attenuation, comfortable enough to sleep in, integrated into the hood so you never take it off to adjust something.
- **Bone-conduction comms.** Speech in air is hopeless at any distance. Learn hand signs for the twenty things that matter: *oxygen, field, stop, tower, go, back, help, time, mask, leak, sparrower, down.*
- **Infrasound cannot be blocked, only dodged.** Expect, in the first three days: chest-wall buzzing, blurred vision in the 18–20 Hz bands, abdominal discomfort, nausea, an unfocused dread that is a *physical symptom and not a feeling*, though it will not feel that way. Most people adapt in 48–72 hours. Some never do; about one in twelve comes home early because of it and there is no shame in it whatsoever.
- **Camp in a null.** Between two tuned stacks there is spatial structure to the standing field. Walk with a meter and a hand flat on your sternum and find the quiet pocket before you pitch. The difference between a good camp and a bad one is the difference between sleeping and not sleeping for two weeks.
- Do not camp where the beat period is near 4–5 seconds, no matter how good the null is. It synchronizes with something in the chest and people wake up certain that someone is standing outside.

## 8. Navigation and the slow ground

No water, no rivers, no drainage, no coast. The shield-stumps are too gradual to perceive — you will top a twenty-meter rise and only know because the horizon changed its mind. GPS analogues do not survive the field environment.

Use, in order of reliability:

1. **The stacks.** Three to nine kilometers apart, forty to two hundred meters tall. Survey them, number them, triangulate.
2. **The beat.** As above. It works in haze, it works in the dark, and it works when your instruments are dead.
3. **Shadow-lean.** Shadows tilt toward the nearest stack by a few degrees, more strongly the closer you are. This is useless for finding the sun and *excellent* for finding a tower you cannot see. Bring a proper gnomon and a table. It is the single most practical piece of local weirdness.
4. Inertial dead reckoning, which drifts.

**The slow ground** is the one that kills schedules, and schedules here kill people.

Within about eighty meters of each stack's foot, everything runs at roughly nine-tenths speed. Inside, you notice nothing: your clock, your heart, your oxygen consumption all slow together. It is only in relation to *outside* that you lose.

- **Carry two clocks. One stays outside the apron, always, with a cached transponder.** Log every entry and exit. Reconcile daily.
- Ten hours of sleep in a slow apron costs you **eleven hours and six minutes** of the outside world. Do that three nights running near the end of a trip and you will arrive at your gate three and a half hours after it closed. This is the classic death on the Dry Canopy, and it is always the same death: someone camps in the calm of an apron because it is quiet and the dust falls slowly and it is beautiful, and they do the arithmetic wrong, and the plateau does not care.
- **Never take a hard rendezvous while inside an apron.** Set all timings against the outside clock and give yourself a 20% pad.

**Crossing the boundary.** It is a sharp edge — animals step over it like a curb, and that is the correct technique. Cross **upright, briskly, perpendicular, one decisive stride.** Expect the lurch: a half-second of vertigo, a pressure sensation in the ears, occasionally a nosebleed. Do not cross bent over. Do not cross carrying something long and rigid across your body. Do not cross while a column is standing. Do not cross repeatedly for fun — repeated crossings produce a documented boundary sickness: nystagmus, epistaxis, and a persistent conviction that you are running late, which lasts for weeks after you get home and which some people never entirely lose.

And never, ever sleep with the line running across you.

## 9. The stacks, and the things that live in them

You will want to go inside. Of course you will: there are lintels, there is proportion, there are eleven identical floor heights, there is a bas-relief on the walls that reads as circuitry from one angle and as liturgy from another. Your whole species is built to respond to this.

Go in. Just go in *properly*.

- **The colonies in the upper galleries maintain the architecture.** They clean ports, patch lattice, repair damage. You are damage. There are credible reports of travelers who stood still on a ledge at dusk and were *begun*: seams sealed, fabric integrated, a glove patched into a wall and left there. Keep moving. Do not set gear down. Do not sleep in a gallery. If they start on you, walk away — they do not pursue, they simply work on what is in front of them.
- Up close their traffic is genuinely unbearable, well past the threshold where hearing protection helps. Ten minutes in the upper galleries, then out.
- The open-sided ledges have no rails and the wind is dense and punchy. Rope up above thirty meters. Anchor to structure, never to the wick.
- **Take nothing.** Not a flake of the wall, not a fragment of lattice. Beyond the ethics, the lattice does something with phase coherence and the samples that have come back have been, universally, disappointing and slightly wrong.

## 10. The animals

None of them are interested in you as food, because you are not made of anything they eat. All of them are hazards anyway.

- **Tillers** are harmless and will ignore you. Do not walk through a grazing scatter — you'll scatter it into the path of whatever is stalking it, and you'll break the applause, and you'll feel bad about it later in a way you won't be able to explain.
- **Fetches** drift, silently, and are the only quiet thing here. They are also three to four meters of brittle charged sheet and if one settles onto you it will discharge through you. Watch the sky after every column. They converge fast — a dozen in under a minute from kilometers out. If they're coming, move out of the wake and let them have it.
- **Sparrowers** hunt by grounding: they plant their feet and complete a circuit and dump the prey's stored charge through themselves. **A human at ground potential with a bleed pigtail reads as boring and will be ignored.** A human in insulating boots carrying twenty kilovolts reads as the largest tiller anyone has ever seen. This is the entire argument for charge discipline in one sentence. If one does plant near you: do not run, do not raise a limb, plant your grounding staff firmly, and walk away in a straight line. The snap is a kilometer-audible event; from two meters, with a suit at potential, it is a cardiac event.

## 11. Manners

These are not rules anyone enforces. Do them anyway, because the travelers who do them come back better.

- **When the chord changes — stop.** Eleven times a day, roughly, and every animal on the plain stops moving for the duration of the transition. Stop with them. It takes twenty seconds. There is no mechanism by which this matters and every person who has done it says it matters.
- **The list.** Some stacks recite. It is not language, but it has the exact prosody of a voice reading coordinates, patiently, ending on a downward inflection, starting again. It is the same list every time. It has been the same list for an unreasonable length of time and it refers to nothing that still exists.

  Record it. Everyone does; the corpus is enormous; nothing has ever come of it.

  **Do not answer it.** I have no argument for this. I can tell you only that the people who sit down in a slow apron under a reciting stack and speak back to it are the people who come home changed in ways their families notice, and that they cannot say what they were hoping for, and that they all describe the same thing: a feeling that it paused. It did not pause. Every recording confirms it did not pause.
- **Take nothing, leave nothing, and pack out your dead.** If someone dies, they come home. A human body is forty liters of solvent and a corpse left on the wick is a spill that kills a patch the size of a house and possibly shorts a section of something that has been computing for four billion years. The dry-bag with desiccant liner is mandatory kit and you should know who is carrying it and how it opens.

## 12. The mind, which is the real trip

Every expedition report ends up here.

The plateau is *finished*. The mountains finished, the chemistry finished, the builders finished, and what's left is a thing that has stopped falling — worn to its floor, cooled to its temperature, holding one enormous chord that it has been holding since before your planet had continents, entirely indifferent to whether you are in it.

You are the only unfinished thing there. You are hot, and wet, and fast, and full of intentions, and you make a noise, and you will be there for two weeks, and the stack you are standing next to will be reciting the same list, unchanged, when the sun goes out.

The documented syndromes, in rough order of frequency:

- **Plateau flatness.** Days 4–9. Anhedonia, loss of task motivation, "why am I bothering." It is partly infrasound and partly the obvious. It lifts. Schedule through it: fixed wake times, fixed meals, a mandatory daily task list written the night before, and a hand-written journal. The journal is not sentimental. The journal is a work instrument that proves to you tomorrow that you were a person yesterday.
- **Reverence spike.** Days 1–3 in the stacks, especially the first time you see the lintels. Harmless, occasionally lethal — it's what puts people on crowns at discharge time and in cold rooms past their timer. Treat awe as an impairment, like altitude: you are not allowed to make new decisions while under it. Decisions get made in camp, in writing, by the flat version of you.
- **Lateness.** Post-boundary, persistent, sometimes permanent. See §8.
- **The dignity thing.** The plain is littered with intact, hollow, weightless corpses in poses of tremendous composure, because nothing rots here. They are genuinely beautiful and everybody photographs them and about a third of people find, three or four months after returning, that they cannot stop thinking about them. Talk about this in your debrief. It comes up in almost every one and people are always surprised to learn it's normal.

**Go with at least one other person.** Not for rescue — for the daily, boring proof that a human-scale conversation is a real thing that can exist on that plain. Solo travelers on the Dry Canopy come back thinner in some way that doesn't show on a scale.

**Set a hard exit date before you go and give someone else the authority to enforce it.**

## 13. Packing list, condensed

**Life support:** full-face sealed mask ×2 · semi-closed rebreather with water-reclaim condenser · O₂ in small armored cylinders, total inventory logged and minimized · CO₂ flood manifold and burst-disc plumbing · glovebox for all O₂ handling · scrubber cartridges ×3 planned · filters ×3 planned · visors ×3

**Electrical:** dissipative footwear (10⁶–10⁸ Ω), broad soft sole · carbon-fiber bleed pigtail and mast · bonding straps for every carried object · grounding staff · field mill · Faraday box with complete spare comms set

**Thermal:** layered insulation for sustained mild cold and heavy work · closed-humidity suit tied to the condenser · cuff and hood seals · skin-temp telemetry · non-defeatable audible cold-room timer

**Acoustic:** 25–30 dB integrated protection, sleepable · bone-conduction comms · SPL and infrasound meter for camp siting

**Navigation/time:** two clocks, one cached outside all aprons · transponder · gnomon and shadow-lean tables · inertial unit · stack survey log · discharge-schedule log (fill in on days one and two, before anything else)

**Backup instruments:** mechanical thermometer · wind-up recorder · pencils and wax-paper notebook · film camera. Everything solid-state may lie to you inside a stack.

**Containment:** sealed waste system, all streams, packed out · one-handed fluid-capture kit in an outer pocket · dust-sacrifice layer · desiccant dry-bag for a body, and everyone knows where it is

**Not permitted:** open flame of any kind, flares, pyrotechnics, ferrocerium, steel tools, drag chains, unsealed lithium cells, free water outside a sealed container, and — please — samples.

## 14. What it's actually like

You'll arrive expecting a dead place and be knocked sideways by how loud it is: the hiss everywhere, like a hundred thousand square kilometers of frying oil, which is the closest thing this world has to rain and which you will, by day six, find comforting. Under it, the towers, tuned to each other, holding a chord for hours in a register you feel in your breastbone, and a slow throb walking across the gap between them every four or five seconds. Then, every few minutes, from somewhere out on the flats, a single dry *snap* as something's charge dumps through something else, like a bored person cracking their knuckles at the far end of an empty hall.

The light will be a disappointment and then, around day three, will stop being one. The ground will give and rasp like a coir doormat and sound, underfoot, like walking on stale bread — you will hate this sound for two days and then not hear it again.

And at some point you will stand between two stacks with the chord about to change, and every tiller within sight will go still, and you will go still with them, and the beat will walk across you, and the nearest tower will begin reading its list again in that patient descending cadence, and your own shadow will be leaning a few degrees the wrong way toward it, and you will understand — not as an idea but as a fact in your chest — that this has been going on without an audience for an extremely long time and will continue without one, and that you are a brief wet noise in the middle of it.

Note the time. You are probably standing in an apron. You are probably ten percent late already.

Then get up, and check your oxygen, and walk out over the curb.
