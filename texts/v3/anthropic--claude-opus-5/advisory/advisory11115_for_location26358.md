# Preparing for the Drowned Cordillera

## First, correct your mental model

Two errors will kill or spoil you, and both are errors of category.

The first is thinking of this as *open water*. It is not. Below the first lid-meadow you are in an overhead environment — a cave, with a ceiling of knitted leaves that closes behind you and looks identical from below for forty kilometers in every direction. Everything the cave-diving tradition knows about continuous guideline, thirds-of-gas, redundant light, and never trusting your memory of a hole applies here with the added insult that the ceiling *heals*.

The second error is thinking of it as *wilderness*, in the sense of something robust that shrugs off visitors. Wilderness on most worlds is a machine for erasing traces: wind, rain, rivers, rot, scavengers. This basin has none of those. It has no weather. It has no current. It has almost no decay. Whatever you do here is permanent on the scale of your own species' history. You will be the first weather the Drowned Cordillera has ever had, and it has no immune response.

Hold both of those. Everything below follows from them.

## Timing

Learn the Turning cycle for your target basin before you commit, and get it from someone who has actually counted, not from a gazetteer. Eleven years is approximate.

If you are ordinary — first or second visit, competent but not a specialist — go in year four, five, or six of the cycle. The stratification is mature, the lids are thick enough to be trustworthy, visibility is at its absolute maximum, and nothing structural is going to move.

Do not make the Turning your first trip. It is the great event and everybody wants it, and it is a six-week period in which every reference you have learned becomes false at the speed of an hour hand: your neutral-buoyancy shelves migrate, your ceilings tear open and sink past you, your marked penetrations end up in a different water, and the whole basin goes to milk with a smell like cut grass and hot iron. Experienced parties do it with acoustic beacons, hard-tethered surface support, and an abort plan for every day of the six weeks. Novices do it and are found, eventually, still tidy, still neutral, at the depth that suited them.

Plan for three weeks minimum on site. This is not a place that can be visited in a weekend. Nothing here moves at your speed, and if you have only four days you will see a very beautiful aquarium and none of the actual world.

## Your body, above the waterline

You arrive on a summit. There is no beach, no shelving shallows, no gentle margin — the ranges are young and they go straight down. Expect to make your camp on moss mattresses a meter deep, on rock whose edges will open your palms if you catch yourself carelessly on them. Tape your hands. Gloves for all scrambling. Anything you sit on, kneel on, or drag rope over needs a pad between it and the stone: the arêtes are unworn and they cut like fresh glass, both ways — they will take your skin and your drysuit, and you will take flakes off them.

The air is barely air. Saturated, warm, mist condensing *upward* off leaf undersides. This has consequences people consistently underestimate:

- **You cannot cool yourself.** Sweat does not evaporate. Any exertion — hauling gear up from the water, climbing between camps — becomes a heat-stress problem, at altitude, with no shade that helps because the light is diffuse everywhere. Work slowly. Work in the coolest hours. Drink far more than thirst suggests.
- **Nothing dries. Ever.** Bring one hard, sealed, desiccated box, and treat it as sacred: electronics, spare scrubber cartridges, medical kit, paper, optics. Everything outside that box will be damp within an hour and will stay damp for the whole expedition. Plan for macerated skin, hot spots turning to open sores in two days, fungal infection in the groin and feet and armpits and in the seals of your suit. Antifungal powder, antifungal cream, chlorhexidine, and a genuine discipline of foot inspection every single evening. More expeditions here are ended by feet than by anything underwater.
- **There is no fire.** Not "fire is difficult" — there is no dry fuel on the world and no dry air to support it, and the local biology has never encountered combustion. Bring sealed chemical heat, or catalytic burners with their own oxidant, or eat cold. Do not experiment with drying and lighting sheath-tree stems. They are stacked chambers of plant-made oxygen and you do not know what they do, and if you find out, so does the basin.
- **The pollen.** The water is visibly thick with pollen, spore dust, shed leaf-scales, glassy diatom-things, and the air above the waterline carries the same load in suspension. This is one of the highest allergen environments in the accessible multiverse. Get sensitised-response testing beforehand if you can. Carry epinephrine — two doses, one on your person, one on your buddy. Wear a filtering mask whenever you are on the surface with your face wet, which is always. And expect your gear to suffer: pollen fouls filters, glues zips, and lays a fine film on every lens; dissolved silica deposits a faint glaze on optics that no amount of wiping removes. Bring spare faceplates.

## Breathing

**Closed-circuit rebreather. Non-negotiable.** Not for gas duration, though the duration is welcome. For the bubbles.

An open-circuit diver under a lid-meadow exhales a stream of gas that rises, cannot escape, and pools against the underside of the leaf-ceiling as a spreading silver blister. That blister kills the meadow it touches — the lid dries against it, unknits, and eventually opens a hole that leaks the water above through and destabilises tens of metres of seam in either direction. Twenty minutes of ordinary breathing leaves a scar that will be visible for a century. There is nothing here to repair it.

So: closed circuit, twin oxygen cells minimum plus a third, full bailout planning, and a scrubber margin sized for the cold-water case even though the water is warm, because you will be in it for hours and you will be moving very slowly, and slow work with high CO₂ loading is exactly how people die on rebreathers.

Then be honest about depth. The basin goes down further than you can go. Daylight reaches the third interface, and the third interface on most flanks sits well past where an ordinary human can operate on anything you can carry. **Accept that your world is the upper two or three floors.** With nitrox and disciplined decompression you have the surface layer, the first lid, and — with commitment — the second. The amber-felted floor fifteen metres thick, the fourth and fifth ceilings, the deep clouds: those need helium you cannot resupply, deco you cannot shorten, and a support structure that is no longer "an ordinary traveler." Going to look at the third lid because it is only twenty metres further is the classic Cordillera fatality, and the bodies do not surface, because nothing here falls fast and nothing here rises fast either.

Correct all your decompression planning for altitude. The basin is high; your surface pressure is not sea-level pressure. Every table and every computer must be set for it, and your post-dive ascent to camp on the summit ridge is *additional decompression* that people forget to count. Do not climb from the waterline to camp within an hour of surfacing.

Fresh, silica-loaded water: you need less lead than you think. Weight yourself in the water, on the day, in the layer you will be working in.

## Buoyancy, and the gift of the shelves

Here is the loveliest piece of local physics and the most dangerous.

The layers differ in density by trivial amounts, but they differ, and as you descend through a boundary your buoyancy changes step-wise. Above a boundary you sink into it; at the seam itself, with your suit compressed just so, you will find yourself simply *parked* — hanging without effort, without a fin stroke, without a breath of adjustment, on the same shelf where a hundred thousand leaves came to rest. It is one of the great sensations available to a body. Divers describe it as being *set down*.

It is dangerous because effortless is not free. You are still burning scrubber. You are still accruing tissue load. The absence of any sense of work removes the signal your gas planning depends on. **Set hard timers, audible and haptic, and obey them the first time.** Do not use "how tired am I" as a clock. There is nothing here to make you tired.

Note also: you are a heat source. Your body plumes upward and locally punches a soft column through the boundary above you. Park too long in one spot under a lid and you will thin it. Move on.

## Working under the lids

Treat every lid-meadow as a cave ceiling with the additional property of self-repair.

- **Penetrate deliberately, in one place, with hardware.** Drive a rigid collar — a hoop of neutral tube, big enough to pass a body with gear, with a lit downline through it — and leave it in place for the duration of the trip. Never rely on the dimple you made with your shoulder. Yes, the older lids will let you through and yes they bloom water and close, and yes that is the pretty part, and yes people have died drifting along the underside of a leaf-ceiling in perfect daylight, one hand up, looking for the way they came.
- **From below, the lid is a sky:** bright, veined, backlit, uniform, gorgeous, and utterly featureless. Silhouette marking works — dark, high-contrast, geometric shapes, not lights. Rig at least three, spaced, and lay continuous line between them.
- **Continuous guideline to open water at all times.** Reels, spools, a line-cutter within reach of both hands.
- **Never establish a penetration under a hanging flake.** Look up at the cliff above your entry point before you commit to it. Door-sized flakes hang everywhere, released but not fallen, held by friction and by the water's patience. When one goes it goes slowly — through drag, absurdly slowly, a minute of soft continuous ripping as it shears through tier after tier of leaves — and it will still crush you flat, and you will have watched it come.
- **The mountain groans.** You will feel it against your sternum before you hear anything. That pressure is your cue to look up and to move out from under overhangs. Sometimes nothing follows for a week. Sometimes something follows in eight seconds.

## Movement discipline, and the ethics of a fin stroke

Every leaf in the forest tears on contact. Every one. Single blades four metres wide, hovering a hand's width apart, forty tiers deep, arranged in strata that have never touched each other. A diver moving through them at ordinary swimming speed cuts a tunnel of shredded green that will be there when your grandchildren are old.

Therefore:

- Move at a speed that feels absurd. A metre every three or four seconds. Slower.
- Use the shafts, gaps, chimneys, cliff faces, and open water above the seams. Route around the forest rather than through it. The best views in the basin are from open rock anyway, looking out.
- No propulsion vehicles. The turbulence is worse than your body ever will be.
- Streamline everything. Nothing dangling, nothing clipped loose. The sheath-trees are guyed to the rock by fine white anchor-threads under tension, hair-thin, effectively invisible in the diffuse light, arranged in lattices. You will not see them until they are across your mask. Entanglement here is quiet, patient, and does not let go, and cutting a guy-thread releases a ninety-metre floating stem that has never in its life had to consider what to do next.
- Do not touch the clock-snails. A palm-sized animal whose entire life's work is a hundred metres of grazing line on a leaf-ceiling; you can undo a century with a thumb.
- If you are lucky enough to meet one of the great fringed gliders, stop and hold still and let it pass. It cannot avoid you. It has never needed to avoid anything.
