# Preparing for a Descent into Sowder's Bottom

## First, correct your expectation

Most travellers arrive braced for a wonder and are unprepared for a *facility*. The Bottom is not a grove, not a shrine, not a mystery to be solved in an afternoon. It is a going concern with a four-century operating record, and it will treat you exactly as a mill treats a man who wanders in off the road: without malice, without notice, and without slowing down.

The single most useful piece of preparation is to stop thinking of yourself as an observer and start thinking of yourself as loose material that has entered a plant under compression. Everything else follows from that.

## Timing

**Know where the cycle stands.** Before you go anywhere near the ridge road, get the local reckoning of the seed. Count the scars on the northeast slope yourself if you can, but buy the local number — whoever keeps the notes will have a date for the last opening, and if that date is fifty-five years back or more, do not descend without deciding, deliberately and in advance, what you will do if the mat parts while you are standing on it. An opening is a once-in-a-generation event and a cavity forming under three metres of living mat is not a spectacle you observe from inside.

Note also that the arithmetic of the place does not close. Twenty-one scars at sixty years apiece wants twelve centuries; the wall-growth count gives four hundred. Bring a policy about this. Mine is: write both numbers down, resist reconciling them in the basin, reconcile them at home, at a table, in the dry.

**Descend in the early morning.** Before the belts dry, the strap-vines hang slack and silent, and the gaps between the drivewood columns are passable without threading a hand's-breadth of leather at singing tension. By noon those forty belts are drawn hard and tuned, and a strap-vine that lets go at noon does what harness does: it comes across the gap faster than you can flinch. Cross the ring inward in the first two hours of light. Do your looking at noon from a position you can hold. Come out in the afternoon by the same gap you used going in — mark it, because forty identical columns in green light are not distinguishable by memory.

**Allow more time at the rim than in the basin.** Budget an hour of sitting down outside before you attempt anything requiring judgement, including the climb back up to the ridge road.

## The body

**Ears, in two layers.** Foam plugs under proper muffs. The chord is not loud in the way a hammer is loud; it is loud in the way weather is loud, and it will find any single-layer protection. Plugs alone leave you the bone conduction. Muffs alone leave a seal that the pressure wave defeats.

**Accept that the part you cannot block is the part that will affect you.** The component of the chord that "isn't a pitch at all" is below hearing and passes through you. Expect: pressure behind the sternum, a slight smearing at the edge of vision, nausea, and — this is the important one — a strong unearned sense of significance, of being attended to, of something behind you or above you in the frond-ceiling. Pre-label it. Say to yourself now, in advance, in these words: *the dread is mechanical.* Travellers who have not done this pre-labelling spend the rest of their lives with a story they half believe.

**Respirator, P100 or equivalent, and wear it in the inner ring.** Warm hundred-percent humidity over a fermentation works is a spore soup. The rain tastes of yeast because it is yeast. You may not want to filter for the whole visit — nobody manages it — but filter near the pod-lungs and reed-stacks, and filter absolutely if you put your face near an opened sac or a bearing socket.

**Do not drink the indoor rain.** Drink the culvert water if you drink anything from the basin; it comes out of the wall, through 1847 brickwork, and the mat has left it working. Even so, carry your own.

**Gas discipline is the thing that kills the naive.** Forty-odd anaerobic digesters venting methane on nine-hour cycles into a still, roofed hollow. Rules, in order of importance:

1. **No flame. No spark. No smoking, no stove, no lighter for a leech, no camera with a magnesium habit, no venting a damaged battery.** If your kit contains anything that arcs, leave it on the ridge.
2. Carry a gas meter if your world runs to one. Heavy fractions and carbon dioxide pool in low pockets and old probe-holes and the lee of the reed-stack bases. The mat is not flat; its dimples hold air the way they hold water.
3. Learn to read a taut sac. A slack pod-lung sweats and gives under a palm. A charged one stops sweating, goes drum-hard, and the woody lips at the crown pale at the seam. Stay two sac-lengths off anything drum-hard and never, ever stand over a reed-stack mouth to look down it.

**Feet.** High-laced, wide-soled, and *nothing that can be pulled off*. The mat closes over a boot in seconds and takes it with the patience of a press. Gaiters for the approach, which is cut-over scrub and clay and will be the most physically miserable part of your day. Change socks at the rim on the way out; you will have spent hours in warm-wet and your skin will be macerated and stupid.

**Warmth for the exit.** You come out of a bathwater downpour into dry scrub in ordinary weather, three metres apart. Soaked, at altitude, after a two-hundred-metre climb: that is a hypothermia set-up in mild conditions. Dry layer sealed in the pack, left at the rim, changed into before the climb.

## Inventory discipline

This is where visitors to the Bottom differ from visitors anywhere else, and it is the discipline you should rehearse before you go.

**Nothing you drop here comes back.** Not "it's hard to find" — it is drawn inward, it becomes a pale strand in the weave, and the weave is under one-way compression. Assume every object you bring either leaves on your body or joins the ledger of things that ended.

Therefore:

- **Weigh and list your kit before descending. Weigh and list it at the rim on the way out.** Do this even for pencil stubs and lens caps. The list is not bureaucracy; it is the only way you will know, that evening, whether you left something in a four-hundred-year process.
- **Remove rings, earrings, anything with a clasp.** Tape over watch pins. Lanyard everything you cannot afford to lose to your torso, not your wrist.
- **Do not anchor your return to a dropped or placed object.** If your method of leaving this world depends on a physical anchor, it goes on your body, sealed, redundant, and never on the mat, never in the ring path, and never — this has happened — on the flywheel, which is turning.
- **Bring nothing living.** Scrub your boot treads before and after. You do not want to be the traveller who introduced something to a closed vegetable economy, and you do not want to carry the pod-lung flora home in your hair. Wash down at the first opportunity.

## Ring-path protocol

The bare band of ground silica is the only clean surface in the basin and it is therefore where everyone wants to walk, sit, eat, and photograph. It is also the one place where the works can, given time, do to you what it does to everything else.

Stand in it. You should — it is the sensation people come for, the whole rim leaning at you at the speed of a fingernail, four hundred years of falling forward. Two minutes. Then step out.

**Do not sit down in the path. Do not put your pack down in the path. Do not put a hand into the silica bed under a column,** where the teeth are, where the grease-beetles are, where the clearance closes at a rate you cannot argue with. Nothing here moves fast enough to hurt you unless something else holds you still: a wedged boot, a slipped ankle, a syncope from the infrasound, or sleep.

## The sleep problem

I think this is the real hazard of Sowder's Bottom, and almost nobody is warned about it.

You will be inside a bright green room at blood temperature, in a warm fine rain, on a surface that gives underfoot like the flank of a large animal, under a continuous forty-note drone punctuated every few minutes by an enormous soft chord. There is no birdsong, no insect noise, nothing that startles. It is the most powerful sedative environment I know of. Every account contains, somewhere, a sentence about how pleasant it was to lie back on the mat, and every account written by someone who lay back on the mat contains a gap.

**Go with a companion and agree to a spoken check every fifteen minutes.** Set a physical timer, vibration not sound. Eat and drink on schedule whether you want to or not. If you feel the warmth get into your judgement — and you will notice it as a reluctance to leave rather than as drowsiness — go out to the rim, into the dry, into the silence, and reassess there. The rim is a hard boundary in every respect; use it as one.

Do not spend a night in the basin. There is no version of this that is a good idea.

## Conduct toward the works

You are entering a laboratory notebook that is also an organism that is also, in the local view, a business under continuous operation.

**The grease-beetles are not specimens.** Two hundred blind lard-coloured animals in the whole basin, secreting the wax that keeps forty bearings from seizing. Take twenty and you have shortened a four-hundred-year run. If your discipline requires a specimen, argue it out with whoever keeps the notes, and expect to be refused.

**Log every mark you make.** If you scratch a column to measure the turn — and this is the standard, honest thing to do — record its position, depth, date, and your name, and leave that record with the local notes. Unlogged scratches corrupt the only long instrument anyone has. The columns are collectively the most patient measuring device in that world and casual graffiti is not vandalism so much as data fraud.

**Do not cut a second probe-hole.** One exists. It is famous. It is enough.

**Do not add a tally mark inside the culvert.** Four hands, four thousand one hundred and eighty marks, groups of five, last group of three, cuts old but not old enough. Whatever that record is, it is not yours, the person keeping it stopped mid-group, and adding a fourth stroke to that group is the single most tasteless thing a visitor could do. Photograph it. Count it yourself, independently, and see whether you get 4,180. Then leave it alone.

**If you are present at an opening, do not go after the seed.** Nobody has ever recovered one. The reason may simply be that the mat parts and the clay accepts and a walnut of glassy tissue at that density goes down out of reach in a second. It may not be. Either way you would be reaching into a closing wound in three metres of muscle-warm weave.

## The locals

Someone on the ridge holds the notes: the scar dates, the flywheel argument, the tuning drift of the reed-stacks, the tracks by the culvert. Find that person first. Pay them, hire them, buy their reckoning, and adopt their vocabulary — the place is *the works*, the cycle is *the run*, and the correct register is that of a man discussing a mill that has changed hands. Enthusiasm about miracles will close doors. Asking who cut the last three marks may also close doors, but ask anyway, late, and listen to how the answer is phrased rather than what it says.

Do not offer the reading that the plant copied the machine unless invited. Locally the settled view is that the works never shut, and it is not really a hypothesis; it is a form of loyalty.

## Coming out

For about an hour after you leave the rim you will hear the drone in ordinary sounds — a passing lorry, a kettle, your own breath. This is normal, it is an auditory afterimage, and it will pass. **Do not drive, climb, or make plans during it.** Sit at the rim. Eat. Change into dry clothes. Do your inventory count out loud, item by item, with your companion answering.

Expect two or three flat days afterward. Expect, more troublingly, the pull to go back — and know that second visits are where the carelessness happens, because on a second visit you have stopped being frightened of a place that has not stopped being a press.

Finally: write your account within forty-eight hours, and date it, and lodge a copy with the notes on the ridge. Nineteen, twenty, twenty-one scars go up that northeast slope, and the whole point of the Bottom is that no single observer sees a run through. You are one lamina. Make yours legible in cross-section.
