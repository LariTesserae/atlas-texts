# Preparing for the Chamber Under the Gutter

## A briefing for ordinary travelers

---

## First, the two questions

Before any packing, answer these honestly.

**Can you be useless for the entire visit?** There is nothing you can improve here, nothing you can trade, no skill you possess that this place lacks and wants. Travelers who cannot tolerate being surplus tend to start *helping*, and help is the single most destructive substance you can carry in. If you need to be needed, go somewhere else.

**Can you keep a secret for the rest of your life?** You will be carrying knowledge — of surface, of season, of abundance, of the concept of *better* — that would function in this chamber the way a lit match functions in a grain silo. You must be able to hold that in your mouth for the entire duration and never let it out, including when someone is kind to you, including when you are lonely, including when you are asked directly. If you are a person who confides under pressure, you are a hazard.

Everything else below is logistics.

## The body

### Cold

The temperature is described as never chilling to shivering. Do not trust this. That description is written from inside a body that has lived here for a lifetime and has recalibrated. Expect something in the range of a wine cellar — call it thirteen to sixteen degrees — and note that you will be *wet in it, continuously, for the entire visit, including while sleeping.* Water at that temperature strips heat from a body roughly twenty times faster than air at the same temperature. You will not shiver dramatically. You will simply get slowly, quietly, cumulatively colder over three days until your judgment goes soft and you cannot remember why you came.

Bring:

- **Vapor-barrier layering.** A thin, sealed, next-to-skin waterproof layer under insulation, not over it. The classic mistake is a breathable shell: there is no dry side of the membrane here. Nothing you own will ever dry. Accept that and seal your own warmth in with you.
- **Closed-cell foam sleep insulation, thin and stiff.** You will be sleeping on stone with running water six inches beneath your ear. That stone is a heat sink connected to a river. An inflatable mat will fail on limestone edges; bring foam, and bring more of it than seems necessary, and bring it in two pieces so that one puncture is not a catastrophe.
- **Chemical or catalytic heat sources — and a plan to conceal them.** More on this under *Gifts*. A heat pack is, in this chamber, an object of nearly religious strangeness. Use them inside your bag, at night, out of sight.

### Feet

**This is the injury that ends most visits.** There is no dry land. Your feet will be submerged or soaked from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave. Immersion foot — maceration, then splitting, then infection in water that carries everyone's waste and everything the Gutter has drowned — can render you unable to walk in seventy-two hours, and there is no rescue infrastructure, no wood for a crutch, no litter, no cart, no dry bed.

The regimen:

- **Thin, hard-soled, fully draining footwear.** Not boots. Boots become buckets and then become poultices. You want something like a rock-shoe or a taped sandal with a puncture-resistant sole, that admits water freely and lets silt out. Limestone splits into plates; those plates have edges like broken pottery, and the whole floor is invisible under moving water.
- **Consider going barefoot in the city proper, once acclimated.** The locals do. Their feet are also *sense organs and speech organs* — they read the chamber through vibration and they talk with their heels on resonant stone. Shoes make you deaf and mute in a way you won't notice. If you have any tolerance for it, learn to spend the quiet hours barefoot and the transit corridors shod.
- **A nightly drying ritual, non-negotiable.** Every sleep period: out of the water onto a raised threshold, feet dried with an absorbent cloth wrung into a bottle (not onto the floor — see *Waste*), inspected by touch and low light, dusted with antifungal powder, insulated and kept out of contact with wet fabric for at least four hours. Two pairs of "sleep socks" in a dry bag, rotated and re-dried against your torso.
- **Toenails cut short and square before departure. Any existing crack, blister, or athlete's foot treated to full resolution first.** Do not bring an open wound into this water. It will not close while you are here. Nothing closes here.

### Hands and head

The ceiling in most places is low enough that a tall adult walks ducked. You will hit your head. You will hit it in the dark, on a stalactite you were forbidden to touch, and you will do it while unable to hear the warning someone tried to give you. **Bring a thin, close-fitting helmet** — climbing style, no brim, no bulk, nothing that occludes peripheral vision, which is the only vision that works well at this light level. Wear it in transit corridors always.

Hands: the film on every untouched surface is *slick as raw egg.* You will grab a wall for balance and the wall will decline. Thin gloves reduce grip. Bare hands rub the film off, which is a small vandalism repeated everywhere by everyone, and is normal, and is how the city's negative-image traffic map got made — you may participate in this. But keep your knuckles unsplit. Every abrasion here goes septic.

## The ears

This is the central problem of the visit and the place where most of the ethical difficulty lives, so read it twice.

### Damage

A flat, sustained, broadband roar loud enough to make speech impossible at arm's length is, conservatively, ninety-five to a hundred decibels, possibly more in the eastern third. At that level, unprotected exposure causes permanent, measurable hearing loss in *hours*, and you are contemplating days. There is no quiet night. There is no break. The acoustic shadows are the only relief and they are other people's houses.

**You must wear hearing protection.** Custom-molded plugs with a flat attenuation curve, deep-seated, plus over-ear muffs for transit through the eastern roar. Bring three sets, in three places, because a dropped plug is unrecoverable in silt.

### The scandal

Now understand what you are carrying.

In this chamber, **quiet is property.** The acoustic pockets are the wealth, the inherited estate, the thing fought over and roofed and floored and mapped with more precision than anything else in the world. The entire social order — where the shrine is, where children are born, where agreements become binding, who is rich, who is poor, who may speak and where — rests on the physical fact that speech is impossible except in a few dozen small volumes of rock.

Your earplugs abolish that fact. Not metaphorically. Two travelers with plugs and any kind of throat-contact or bone-conduction communication can hold a conversation standing in the middle of the loudest corridor in the world. If you demonstrate this — even accidentally, even kindly, even once — you have not given them a gift. You have voided the deed to every valuable room in the city, and you will not be there for the consequences.

So:

- **Passive protection only, in public.** Nothing with a wire. Nothing you speak into. Nothing that visibly makes you comfortable in the roar.
- **Do not let it be seen that the roar does not bother you.** Adopt the posture of the place: cross the loud corridors briskly, head slightly down, no lingering, no conversation attempted. Perform the constraint even though you have escaped it. This is not deception for your benefit; it is refusing to introduce a technology.
- **Never, ever, respond to something said to you in the open floor.** If your protection is good enough that you catch a word, let it fall. The correct response to speech in a street is not to have heard it.
- **Talk with your traveling partner only in pockets, and only where locals speak.** Your private conversations must obey their geography. Sit in the noise and be silent together like everyone else.

### Adaptation

Within an hour the roar becomes silence. The description is exact and you should not be reassured by it: your auditory system will simply stop reporting the signal, and you will *feel* fine, and the damage will continue at the same rate. Adaptation is not protection. Keep the plugs in after it stops bothering you. Set a reminder, because you will forget that you are being injured.

Also: you will lose the ability to hear yourself. Speech in the pockets will come out too loud, and too loud in a quiet room here is an obscenity — burning a fortune to say hello. **Practice speaking at a whisper's volume while wearing plugs, before you go.** Practice with a partner, in a room with a fan on, until you can hit a target volume without auditory feedback. This is a genuine skill and it takes about a week to acquire.

Assume the locals' hearing has a notch in it precisely the shape of the fall. Assume you are the only person in the world who can hear the high end of a clay bell. Say nothing about it.

## Air, water, and the invisible

Ninety fathoms of limestone overhead, a static thermal regime, and one narrow outflow. Some things to check with instruments, not intuition:

- **Carbon dioxide pooling.** The waterfall entrains air downward and probably ventilates the main chamber adequately — that column of falling water is a pump. But the city continues into blind tunnels where the ceiling comes down, and cut-off dead-ends in cave systems accumulate CO₂ in invisible lakes that sit in low spots. **Carry a CO₂ meter with an audible-and-vibrating alarm.** (Vibrating. Audible is decoration here.) If the locals decline to enter a passage and cannot tell you why, that is your data; respect it absolutely.
- **Radon.** Deep limestone, poor ventilation of side passages, high humidity: expect elevated radon and radon daughters. Irrelevant for a two-week visit, statistically significant if you make a career of returning. It is also, almost certainly, one of the things quietly killing people here, and it is not something you can do anything about, and it is not something you should mention.
- **Water.** You will be tempted to drink from the plunge basin, upstream of everything. Don't, untreated. That column has fallen out of a careless world and it arrives carrying whatever the plateau discarded, including agricultural runoff from a civilization that does not know this chamber exists. **Bring filtration and chemical treatment both, sized for the whole trip plus a third.** Treat everything. And note that the locals drink this untreated and have for uncounted generations, which means their gut biology and yours are not interchangeable — see below.
- **Do not treat water in front of them.** A person who makes bad water good is a person with a technology of improvement.

## Calories: bring all of them, take none

**The chamber's entire meat supply is perhaps two potfuls of blind crustaceans.** Forty or fifty per tank, breeding grudgingly, doubling as the only fat and the only lamp oil. Everything else edible is filtered out of the water, sorted by hand, and eaten as paste.

Therefore:

- **You must be entirely self-provisioning.** Every calorie for the entire visit, plus fifty percent margin, carried in. Dense, cold-eatable, no cooking (there is no fuel; the only fuel is lamp oil and the lamp oil is *food*).
- **Refuse hospitality.** This is delicate and you must plan the refusal in advance, because you will be offered something at some point, and in most cultures refusal is an insult. Here the correct frame is not "no thank you" but *"I am not owed"* — an ethics of pure conservation has a very well-developed concept of shares, debts, and taking what is not yours. Learn the sign for *not my portion* / *I am outside the count* before you arrive. Practice it until it is reflex.
- **Eat out of sight.** This is the hardest rule and the most important. You are carrying, in one shoulder bag, more concentrated food energy than this city consumes in a month. Eating it openly is not sharing culture; it is displaying a quantity of wealth that has no meaning here except as a wound. Eat in your sleeping place, in the dark, quietly, and account for every wrapper.
- **Bring no crumbs into the water.** Anything organic you drop is caught in the Screens and enters the food supply. A dropped ration bar is a windfall of unprecedented size and will be found, and will be discussed in every quiet room in the city, and will not be understood.

Weigh your food in and weigh your waste out. Know your own mass balance.

## You are the only invasive species

There is nothing green here. There has never been anything green. There is one microbial film that lights the world and lines the finest Screen; one flat wall-grazing insect; one crustacean; and a rumour.

That is a monoculture ecology of extraordinary fragility, and you are a warm sack of surface-world organisms walking into it.

Before departure:

- **Freeze, autoclave, or chemically sterilize every piece of gear**, with particular attention to seams, Velcro, boot treads, zipper tracks, and the inside of pack bottoms — the four places seeds and spores live. Take nothing with a fabric hook-and-loop closure if you can avoid it. Take nothing that has been in soil, ever.
- **Take no plant matter, no cotton, no wool, no leather, no untreated natural fiber.** Not because these are dangerous in themselves but because they carry passengers, and because a scrap of them left behind is a substance that does not exist in this world and cannot be identified or disposed of.
