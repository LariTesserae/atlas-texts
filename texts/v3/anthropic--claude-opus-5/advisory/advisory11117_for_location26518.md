# Field brief: visiting a Triple-Point Vault

## First, the one idea everything else hangs off

You are a 100-watt heater full of warm wet air, and you are about to climb into a machine whose entire function is holding one temperature to a tolerance no laboratory achieves, using energy it has to scrape out of peat.

Take that seriously and the rest of the preparation writes itself. Most of what follows is either *don't wet yourself*, *don't warm the room*, *don't damp the sound*, or *don't crush the floor* in some new costume.

The second idea, less obvious and more likely to kill you: **a chamber at exactly one temperature has no convection.** Nothing rises, nothing sinks, nothing mixes. That's why the fog stands there like a solid. It also means gases stratify and *stay* stratified, indefinitely, sorted by molecular weight, in a sealed volume full of respiring tissue and a peat bog's worth of methane seep. Nobody has ever been asphyxiated in a vault, as far as the records go, but nobody has ever spent eight hours kneeling with their face fifteen centimetres off the floor either, which is exactly what you will want to do.

## Who shouldn't go

Anyone who can't fit a shoulder through a gap and then climb three and a half metres of slick, flexing ribs while soaked, gloved, cold and half-deaf. The descent is trivial. The ascent, at the end, is the crux, and it is where people are hurt.

Also: significant cardiac or respiratory disease (an hour of 40 Hz whole-body coupling is not neutral, and there is no way to walk it off), untreated claustrophobia (the seam is a squeeze and it is the only door), and anyone with a fresh middle-ear problem. If you are prone to migraine you will probably be fine — the floor is a shimmer, not a strobe — but bring your medication to the surface camp.

Never solo. Never a party larger than three below ground, and two is better.

## Season and finding it

Go in the last three weeks before snow. The polygons are readable, the surface is walkable, the insects have given up, and the seam is at its driest.

In deep winter the vault is the warmest thing for a hundred kilometres and it exhales. Look for a rime bush: a low, absurd, coral-like thicket of hoarfrost standing on open tundra with nothing under it, sometimes a metre high, growing out of nothing at all. That's a vault mouth breathing. It is the easiest way to find one and by far the worst way to enter one, because you will come out saturated into forty below.

In summer, do it the old way: walk the sag, watch for polygons that don't close, then lie down and put your ear to the moss. Don't trust one listening point — triangulate three, because the seam is often two or three metres off the loudest patch.

**Mark it properly before you touch it.** Cairn, GPS, bearing pair, and — this matters in a watershed with sixty of them — record the pitch. Hum against it and write down what you get. They are all slightly out of tune with each other and that is the most reliable fingerprint of which vault you were actually in.

## The surface camp, which is the real safety equipment

Set up before anyone goes down. Twenty metres away, upwind of the seam.

- A tent, pitched, guyed, and *already warm*, or as warm as you can manage.
- A complete dry change of everything for every person going below. Everything. Base layer, socks, gloves, hat. Bagged individually so it can be handed in without unpacking.
- Hot drinks made and in insulated flasks. Not "makeable." Made.
- A surface watch who is dressed for standing still in the cold doing nothing for ninety minutes, which is a colder job than the one below, and who has a watch, a whistle, and the authority to haul.

Here is the sequence that hurts people: they spend an hour at exactly zero in saturated fog, wet through and not much noticing, because zero and windless is oddly comfortable. Then they climb out into honest cold and turn into a suit of armour in ninety seconds. **Plan the ten minutes after the exit more carefully than you plan the descent.** Strip and change inside the tent, not at the seam.

## Clothing, layer by layer, and why

You cannot stay dry. Do not build a system that tries. Build one that fails gracefully.

- **Vapour barrier next to skin.** Thin non-breathable layer over a light wicking base. Your sweat stays in and your insulation stays dry. Unpleasant for the first ten minutes; then you stop noticing; then it saves your life on the walk back.
- **Modest insulation.** Less than instinct says. You are going somewhere warmer than the tundra and you will be working.
- **Hard, smooth, fully waterproof shell.** Not softshell. Not fleece. Not anything fuzzy.

That last one is not fashion advice. Frost grows on every surface at a visible rate, including you, and fuzzy fabric grows a five-centimetre pelt of feather frost in twenty minutes, which then melts against your body heat and wicks. Smooth shells beat it off.

The other reason is acoustic. At 40 Hz the wavelength in that cold air is over eight metres and your body is acoustically invisible — you don't perturb the bass at all, which is a relief. But the glassy middle register, the hundred thousand bells, runs from hundreds of hertz up into the kilohertz, and textile is a superb absorber up there. A party of five in loft-heavy fleece is a giant sponge sitting in the middle of the mid-band. Nobody can tell you whether that matters to the masons. Wear shells, keep the party small, and don't stand in the middle.

Gloves: thin waterproof, plus a spare pair sealed in a bag. Boots: sealed, and *clean* — see quarantine.

**Nothing over your eyes.** Goggles and glasses fog to opacity in under a minute and you cannot wipe them clean in a saturated atmosphere. Go bare-eyed and accept frost in your lashes. If you have a beard, expect a mask of it, and expect it to melt down your neck.

Tape every buckle, sling, and carabiner. Nothing metal may touch anything metal. You will understand why the first time a strap-end taps a column and the chamber answers you for eleven se
