# Preparing for the Stanchion

## First, understand what kind of place it is

The Stanchion is not a ruin, a wonder, or a shrine, although it will be described to you as all three. It is a working machine with a town's worth of people inside it and a plain's worth of people downstream of it. Everything you do there is done in someone else's water supply.

Hold that in your mind as the governing fact and most of the etiquette derives itself.

The second governing fact: **you are going somewhere that will take something from you and not give it back.** Not metaphorically. The sound is 130 dB broadband with a 31 Hz fundamental, and there is no equipment in any world that will return you from a long visit exactly as you went in. The tenders call the hearing loss an entry fee rather than an injury, and they are not being poetic — they are being accountants. Decide, before you commit, what you are willing to pay. If you are a musician, a linguist, a sonar operator, a parent of a small child who still needs to hear them breathe at night — think hard. Many travellers go to the ridge, walk the eleven minutes around the outside, look at the door, and go home. That is a complete and respectable visit.

---

## Timing

The tower does one thing in a year: the valve cycles, in autumn, for about sixty seconds. If you want to see it — the fundamental sagging a whole tone, the light through the crown going strobic as every swift in the tower comes off the wall at once — you must plan the entire journey around it, arrive at least three weeks early for acclimatisation and orientation, and accept that you will be standing exactly where you are told to stand and nowhere else. That minute is the only maintenance window on the calendar. Crews will be moving fast on wet iron with heavy tools and no ability to warn each other by voice. Being underfoot is not a discourtesy; it is a way of killing someone.

Otherwise, go in the dry season, when the cloud sea is low and the ridge approach is walkable. Budget three days for the approach, three for altitude, and then as many days inside as your ears can afford, which is fewer than you think.

---

## The body: altitude first, everything else second

Above 4,000 m you have about 60% of sea-level pressure. Do the ordinary thing: sleep two or three nights at 2,500–3,000 m, then gain no more than 300–500 m of *sleeping* elevation per day. Arrive with a week of slack in your schedule so that you can lose days without losing the trip.

The complication specific to the Stanchion is that **altitude sickness and drum sickness present identically.** Headache, nausea, vertigo, disturbed sleep, poor judgement, a feeling of pressure behind the eyes. You will not be able to tell whether you have mild AMS or whether the 31 Hz has simply got into the loose water of your head. The tenders can't tell either. The only safe rule is the crude one: **if it doesn't resolve within two hours of being outside the drum and downhill of it, treat it as altitude and descend.** Nobody has ever been harmed by going down the mountain unnecessarily.

Two further quirks worth knowing:

- The interior air is warm and saturated. Water vapour takes up partial pressure that oxygen would otherwise occupy; the garden's own breath thins an already thin air by another five or six percent. The inside of the Stanchion is effectively two or three hundred metres higher than the outside of it.
- You cannot evaporate sweat in there. You will step through a pressure door from dry cold into blood-warm fog, and within twenty minutes of climbing stairs you can be genuinely heat-stressed at 4,000 m, which is a combination most bodies have never met. Climb slowly. Climb *insultingly* slowly. Nobody inside moves fast; you will look like you belong.

## The ears: do the arithmetic honestly

Take deep-insert foam plugs (rolled thin, held thirty seconds until they expand — a badly seated plug gives you a third of its rating), plus over-ear muffs with a good seal, plus, if you can get them, custom-moulded plugs made from your own ear casts before you travel. Bring three times as many plugs as you think you need; they wet through, foul with biofilm, and become useless.

Understand the ceiling. Doubled protection buys you perhaps 30–35 dB in the mid and high frequencies — which is exactly where the damage lives, the white shriek off the throat vents and the 440 Hz rasp of the dying bearing, so it is protection worth having. At 31 Hz it buys you almost nothing, and past a certain point sound simply enters through your skull and ribcage regardless of what is in your ears. Call your effective exposure 85–90 dB and budget it like money: **four to six hours inside per day, maximum, with the rest of the day spent outside on the ridge in the dry and the cold.** The tenders live inside. You are not a tender. Your visit should have a tidal rhythm: in, out, in, out, and every transition a shock you plan for.

Expect temporary threshold shift. For several days after you leave, the world will be muffled and wrong, and you will have tinnitus that may or may not go. **Do not schedule anything for the week after** that requires hearing — no negotiations, no piloting, no goodbye conversations that matter.

## Kit, and the single most important rule about it

**Nothing on your body may rattle, and nothing on your body may fall.**

The tenders will go through your pack at the door and they will do it thoroughly, because at 130 dB the one thing you *can* perceive clearly is a loose buckle chattering against a carabiner four inches from your ear, and it will destroy you within a day. Tape every strap end. Fill every void. Pad the inside of your pack with cloth until it is solid. Remove anything with a loose lens cap, a hinged lid, a swinging tag.

And lanyard everything. The hollow is 380 m of nothing. A dropped spanner falls for nine seconds and lands among people who cannot be warned, because there is no shout in the world that gets through the Ox. Dropped-object discipline is the central safety culture of the place, more important than fall arrest, more important than heat, more important than your ears. Anything you take to a gallery rail is either tethered to you or in a closed bag.

Otherwise:

- **Clothing in two complete sets**, one sealed in a waterproof bag at all times. Nothing dries inside. Nothing. Wool over synthetic, because you will be wet for the entire visit and wool is less offensive when wet and less hospitable to the mould, which is aggressive and everywhere.
- **Layers that come off in one motion** at the threshold. You will go from windchill to sauna in the time it takes to close a door.
- **Boots with real grip on wet, furred, biofilmed steel.** The galleries drip continuously. The handrails wear a slick of living matter you will grip a thousand times.
- **Thin gloves with bare or split fingertips** — you need grip and you need your hands for language.
- **A personal harness and two lanyards**, used on every gallery, every time, without exception, including for the ten seconds it takes to look at something.
- **Three light sources.** The crown gives you exactly one day's worth of daylight and no more, arriving at the floor as a soft grey-gold that is entirely sufficient until it isn't. At dusk the lower drum goes dark fast. **Do not move inside at night.** If your lamp dies at the fourth gallery in the dark, you cannot call for help and nobody will hear you fall.
- **Two timepieces, at least one non-mechanical.** Mechanical watches run fast or die in that vibration. You are budgeting exposure by clock and there are no acoustic cues for time of day.
- **Chalk — hard chalk**, and a good supply of wax crayon or grease pencil for wet steel. Ordinary soft chalk is useless on damp rind. Bring more than you need; it is the local currency of thought.
- **Salt.** The tower's water is condensate: soft, mineral-poor, near-distilled. Live on it for three weeks and you will quietly strip your electrolytes and wonder why you feel stupid.
- **A light mask for the fruiting bands**, particularly the second, where the pulp ferments knee-deep. Warm, wet, spore-dense air and lungs unaccustomed to it is how travellers come home with fungal pneumonia six weeks later. Also: ethanol vapour and pooled CO₂ in hollows of the pulp bed. No flames. Ever. Anywhere inside.

Leave at home: any translation device with an audio front end (it will be pinned and useless, and worse, it will encourage you to try); any tripod (the floor itself is the vibration source — there is no stable surface in the building, and the only sharp photograph anyone has ever taken here was taken from the cage); anything with seeds in the seams.

## The threshold

The sign says **THE DRUM IS SPEAKING. DO NOT ANSWER.** The language is dead and the sentence is not a metaphor. It is a rule about your voice.

From the moment you cross, **do not vocalise.** Not because it offends anyone — it offends nobody, they can't hear you — but for two practical reasons. First, deprived of auditory feedback, you will within a day be shouting without knowing it, and you will shred your throat and lose the little voice you have. Second, the tenders will read it. Voice-use marks you instantly as someone who has not accepted the terms of the building, and in a place where every safety signal is visual and trust is established by attention, that is not a good first impression.

Learn hands before you go. Get a primer from whoever brokered your visit and put in the forty hours. You will not be fluent; nobody expects you to be. What is expected is that you arrive knowing the safety corpus, the numbers, the directions, *yes*, *no*, *wait*, *stop*, *I don't understand*, *help*, *I am going up*, *I am going down*, *I am new*, *thank you*. Learn the three terms of art everyone will use in front of you: *the pattern has slipped*; *the bearing is telling a new story*; *go to the cage*.

Learn also the protocol for getting attention, which is not what you are used to. You do not tap a stranger's shoulder from behind, because you may be doing it to someone at a rail 190 m up. You move into their field of vision, you stop, and you wait to be seen. In dimness you flick a lamp across the deck in front of them — never into their eyes; the eyes are all they have. Three flashes is help. One long sweep is *look where I'm pointing*.

You will be given a sign-name within a few days. You do not choose it and you do not object to it.

---

## Chalk law

Chalk on the rail, chalk on the plate, chalk on your own forearm — that last is the normal place for anything you need to say more than once, and writing on your own arm in front of someone is a completely ordinary act of speech.

The critical rule: **do not erase anything you did not write.** A chalk mark may be a joke, or it may be a maintenance record older than you, or a warning about a ledge, or a name, or an apology left by someone who went to the cage. There is no way to tell them apart in a dead alphabet. Write in the clean places. If you are unsure whether a surface is clean, it isn't.

## Water, waste, and the second band

The forearm-thick pipe at the plug is a city's drinking water. Therefore:

- Nothing of your body enters the tower's water. Nothing. There are designated closed vessels and you will be shown them on your first hour and the demonstration will be unusually slow and unusually direct, and you should not mistake that for an insult.
- Do not wash in the drip channels. Do not rinse kit in them. Do not put your hands in them.
- Do not eat the fruit. Not because it is poisoned but because you are an unknown gut arriving in a closed ecology, and because at least one endemic in the fifth band is found nowhere else in any world and you do not want to be the vector that ends it.
- Clean your boots and turn out every cuff and seam before you cross the door. Nine bands have been sealed inside an iron drum for centuries. You are the most dangerous thing on the ridge.

And in the second band, where the pulp ferments, something has arranged the seed husks into a low spiral wall that it repairs and nobody has seen. **You do not touch the wall.** You do not photograph it with a light. You do not stand where you would block its repairs. If you find you have disturbed it, tell a tender at once, in writing, and accept whatever follows. This is the one piece of etiquette at the Stanchion that has no explanation attached and no exceptions.

---

## Sleep

Sleep is your real constraint, more than the ears.

Try to sleep outside, in the plug quarters or on the ridge in the lee, on a mass-loaded pad, decoupled from the structure. Anything rigid that touches the shell is a conduit for 31 Hz, and 31 Hz in the sternum will hold you an inch below sleep for hours without ever quite waking you. Three nights of that plus altitude plus sensory monotony is how travellers start making bad decisions at rails.

Expect strange auditory events: your brain, starved of information and drowned in one note, will manufacture music, voices, fragments of speech in the harmonics. Everyone gets this. It is not the tower and it is not a message. Sleep, eat, go outside, and it passes.

Eat things you don't have to chew loudly, or learn to tolerate the peculiar silence of food — chewing normally reaches you by bone conduction, and here it is swallowed whole by the Ox, and travellers report being unexpectedly upset by mute biscuits. Small thing. It gets people.

## The cage

You will want to go. Almost everyone does.

Preparation, in order of importance:

1. **Not in your first week.** Every tender will tell you this and every tender is right. You go when you have been in the sound long enough that its absence will mean something, and long enough that you know how to move on a line.
2. **Not alone.** There are line tenders. You will agree a time limit and a signal before you leave the gallery, and you will agree in advance that if you do not return the signal, you are coming back anyway. Some people, in the cage, discover they do not want to come out. This is common enough that the protocol exists, and the protocol is not negotiable.
3. **Stay in the plane.** The quiet path out to the centre is a horizontal disc, and you can see it: it is the standing dust, the plate of motionless smoke at your gallery's height. Ride out through it, level. If the dust begins to boil or comes into your eyes, you have drifted out of the node and into the hammer, and you correct immediately. The dust is your only instrument and it is a good one.
4. **Decide in advance what you are going to say.** This is the actual preparation. In the cage you will hear a pin drop. You will hear your own voice for the first time in days, and it will not sound like you — unmasked, wrecked, unfamiliar, in a dead little pocket of air a metre and a half across with nothing around it for forty-five metres. People come back changed and explain it badly. The ones who seem least harmed are the ones who went out with a sentence already chosen.
5. **Write it as well as say it, and leave the chalk.** Most of the chalk in the cage is apologies. Read them or don't — that is genuinely your choice, and the two choices produce different visits. But add yours. The cage is the only place in the Stanchion where writing on the wall is the whole point.

---

## Leaving

Come down slowly and expect an ugly week.

The ridge will seem *wrong* — a silence so complete after days of being inside a material that many travellers find the first night outside frightening rather than restful. You will hear the 31 Hz in your sternum for a while after there is nothing making it. Ordinary sounds will be intolerable: cutlery, doors, a raised voice. That is hyperacusis and it usually fades, but plan for a quiet place to land for ten days. Don't go straight to a city. Don't go straight home to people who will want to be talked to.

Get your hearing tested before you leave your own world and again a month after you return, so that you know exactly what you spent. You are entitled to know the price of the thing you bought.

---

## What to bring for the tenders

They have everything the tower makes and nothing it doesn't. Useful gifts, in rough order of welcome: good hard chalk in quantity; fine files and small precision tools; lamp cells; spectacle lenses and anything for eyes; strong tea; paper and graphite that survive damp; thin gloves. Bearing grease is appreciated with a certain grim humour and you should let them make the joke, not you.

Do not bring music, or anything that plays it. Do not bring seeds, cuttings, live anything. Do not bring pity.

---

## The thing you cannot prepare for

Everything above is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is all doable. What isn't doable in advance is the fourth gallery.

You will come up wet stairs inside a screaming iron cylinder, clip on at the rail, and look across ninety metres of warm lit air at a hanging jungle in perfect focus, and down at a turbine the size of a coin, and there will be nothing — nothing at all — in between. No cable, no strut, no dust except nine level plates of it hanging edge-on in the empty, marking the sound. Ordinary daylight will be falling the length of it from a lattice choked with vine, arriving grey-gold and entirely sufficient, and the swifts will cross in long slow arcs using the dust as their floor.

Your body will not know what to do with that. Humans judge distance partly by ear, and here your ears are full and useless, and the hollow gives no depth cues of its own. Grip the rail. Keep one hand on iron always. Let it take as long as it takes.

People say the Stanchion is a machine, and it is. But from the fourth gallery, looking up at the bands, what you are looking at is a sound, held still for four hundred years, with a garden growing in it.

Go with your hands ready and your sentence chosen.
