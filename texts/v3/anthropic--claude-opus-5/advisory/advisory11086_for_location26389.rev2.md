# Field Preparation: The Sheet

## What kind of trip this is

Read the description again and notice what it doesn't say. It doesn't say *dangerous*. Nothing there wants to eat you; there is nothing there that *could* want anything. There are no predators, no parasites, no venom, no disease reservoir, no crevasses, no cliffs, no weather that will kill you in an afternoon. By the standards of hostile worlds it is a garden.

It will still kill you, and it will do it by attrition and by arithmetic. Everything that goes wrong on the Sheet goes wrong slowly, quietly, and without a gradient sharp enough to alarm you. That is the whole safety brief. **You must not rely on discomfort to tell you when something is wrong**, because in a place where a hot coal and a cold stone can sit together for hours without resolving, your body's alarms are working against a physics that has been ground blunt. You will not notice you are cold. You will not notice you are exhausted. You will not notice that the return route has drifted.

Plan the trip the way the surveyors plan a journey: with drift accounted for, with margin, with a willingness to be somewhere else.

## The body: altitude, and the fact that there is no down

This is the first and most serious item, and it is the one that cannot be fixed on site.

The sky is dark blue at noon and the loud stars are out all day. That is not poetry, that is a barometer. You are looking at a pressure somewhere in the range where unacclimatised humans lose consciousness in minutes to hours. The locals are adapted — generations of it, barrel chests, high haemoglobin, some blood chemistry we don't have. You are not.

And the standard treatment for altitude sickness is **descent**, and on the Sheet there is no descent. There is no down. The lowlands are a rumour in the oldest songs. If you develop pulmonary or cerebral oedema out on the plain, the only lower place in the universe is through your arrival point, which may be forty days' walk away, and which does not hold still.

So:

- **Acclimatise before you go.** Six to ten weeks in a hypoxic tent, stepped, supervised, with genuine haematology done on you and not just a pulse oximeter. If your multiverse access permits a staged arrival — a week at a lower shelf, if any such thing exists — take it and spend the money.
- **Carry supplementary oxygen and treat it as your evacuation, not your comfort.** A portable hyperbaric bag (Gamow/Certec type) is the single most valuable object you can bring, because it is the only "down" that exists in that world. It is also bulky, and with no animals and no wheel worth the name there are no pack beasts and no carts. Somebody has to carry it. Budget a porter, or budget a shorter trip.
- **Acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine**, and a companion trained to recognise ataxia in you before you recognise it in yourself. Rehearse the heel-to-toe test until it is a joke between you. Do it every evening.
- **The mouth-breathing problem.** At that pressure you will breathe through your mouth, constantly, dry cold air, and you will cough until your ribs hurt. Bring a heat-exchange mask, several, and accept that you will still cough. The "khumbu cough" that cracks ribs is real and here it is worse, because the air is also *wet* — you will be inhaling fog and exhaling ice.

## The central discipline: individuate everything you own

This is the part travellers get wrong, and it is the part unique to this world, so read it twice.

Our civilisation's core competence is **making things identical**. Mass production is the manufacture of objects cut to the same dimensions, from the same seam, with the same tool. You are about to carry a bag of it into a place where the rule distinguishing two similar things has worn almost through.

Every duplicate in your kit is a live hazard. Two identical bootlaces. A strip of twelve identical tablets. A sleeve of AA cells — a battery is a *stack of deliberately identical units*. Two identical lens caps, two identical carabiners, a box of matches, a magazine of anything.

We do not know exactly what happens when a pair of your factory-identical objects finishes converging, and neither do the locals, who have never seen machine tolerances. The local phenomenon takes weeks to months for hand-cut stone. Machine-made items are orders of magnitude more alike. My intuition is that for something like a matched pair of injection-moulded buckles, convergence could begin in **days**.

The consequences range from inconvenient (scratch one water bottle, find the scratch on the other) to lethal (twelve identical tablets that are now one tablet in twelve places — do you get a twelfth of a dose, or twelve doses, or does the whole strip vanish leaving a socket of dry air?).

**Therefore:**

1. **Before departure, individuate every duplicate you own.** File a distinct notch pattern into each carabiner. Dye one sock red. Cut each bootlace to a different length and burn the ends differently. Scratch a different numeral into every lens, every buckle, every tent stake, every pot lid. Do this with a hand tool, badly, so the marks are not themselves identical. This job takes a full day and it is the most important day of your preparation.
2. **Dress asymmetrically.** Odd gloves. Two different-coloured boots. One trouser leg cuffed. You will look ridiculous and the locals will read you instantly as a sensible person.
3. **Pack badly.** A neatly packed bag — matched items nested, stacked, aligned, in the same orientation — is precisely the geometry that accelerates identity wear. *Same orientation* appears in the local rule and I would take it literally. Jumble everything. Store duplicates in different pockets, at different angles, never touching, never facing the same way. Repack randomly every night. Packing tidily is the one skill you must unlearn.
4. **Medication: bring single-dose, individually marked, physically separated.** Different manufacturers where possible. Different formulations of the same drug. Write on each ampoule with a pen. Never let two doses share a pocket.
5. **Bring no electronics.** Not because of cold, or damp, or the field, but because a memory chip is an array of ten billion structures manufactured to be as identical as human beings know how to make things. It is the most identity-vulnerable object our species produces. I would expect it to fail in the first hour, and I would not want to be holding it when it did. Mechanical watch, brass compass (which will not work anyway — try it and write down what it does), pencil and paper.
6. **Do not bring matched rings.** Matched bands worn by two people at distance is not jewellery on the Sheet. It is a marriage instrument. You may find you have performed one.

## Feet

You will be standing in three millimetres of water for the entire visit, including while you sleep unless you arrange otherwise.

Immersion foot is the number one medical event on this trip. Not spectacular, not sudden — just tissue quietly giving up over eleven days.

- **Knee-high boots**, not ankle. The Sheet comes over the welt, but you will kneel, you will misjudge a cushion, and a fog-tree stands in a permanent puddle deeper than the film.
- **Vapour-barrier socks**, and a rigid drying discipline: feet out, dried, powdered, inspected by your companion (not by you — you will lie to yourself) every single night, without exception, in a dry place.
- **A dry place is a thing you must manufacture.** The only naturally dry ground in the world is the six or eight metres of a town mound. Everywhere else, you need a raised sleeping platform: a light folding cot or a slatted frame, and a groundsheet that you will never, ever lay directly on the Sheet. Sleeping on the plain in a bivvy bag is how you wake up as part of the landscape.
- **Three pairs of boots is not excessive.** Each one visibly different. Rotate them and let them dry for days at a time.

## Eyes

Two problems, and one beautiful solution.

The first problem: the ground looks like distance. The horizon is a seam. You cannot judge scale, slope, or the position of your own feet. Newcomers stagger, reach for surfaces that are reflections, and step on things they thought were sky. People have broken ankles walking on a perfectly flat plain.

The second problem: extreme altitude UV, doubled by a continent-sized mirror. Reflected burn under the chin, inside the nostrils, on the lips, on the roof of your mouth when you gasp. Photokeratitis in a few hours of carelessness.

**The solution to both is a polarising filter.** A polarised lens cut to the Brewster angle kills the surface reflection off water. Put them on and the mirror simply *stops*, and the plateau becomes what it actually is: wet dark stone, very flat, with your boots on it. Bring polarised glacier glasses with side shields, and bring several pairs (individuated, notched differently), because losing them is not a discomfort here, it is an incapacitation.

Bring zinc paste and use it under your jaw, under your nose, on your eyelids. Reapply after every fog.

Practise, at home, walking on flooded salt flats. Uyuni, the Bonneville sheetwater, a tidal mudflat at slack water. Half a day of it will teach your inner ear more than any amount of reading.

## Things that will not work

- **Shouting.** A shout dies at forty paces. Whistles won't carry either — it's the law that carries sound that has been worn shallow, not the medium. **You have no way to call for help.** Therefore: travel roped, or at minimum corded, with an agreed tug-code, in physical contact range, always. Learn the code cold. One tug: stop. Two: come. Three: I am hurt. Continuous: emergency. Practise it blindfolded before you go.
- **Boiling.** Thermal gradients are lazy and the boiling point at that pressure is around seventy degrees anyway. You cannot sterilise water by heat in any practical time. You cannot cook. Bring chemical or UV-free filtration — a good hollow-fibre filter plus chlorine dioxide — and bring food that requires no cooking. A stove is for morale, and it will take four hours to make tea, and the tea will be tepid, and you will drink it anyway.
- **Rapid rewarming.** If someone gets cold, you cannot warm them quickly. There is no fast transfer. Prevention is the entire strategy. Insulation, dryness, calories, and never letting it start.
- **Steel.** The metals here have been worn towards a general grey obligingness. Your knife, your tent stakes, your crampon points, your cookpot — I would expect them to soften, to lose temper and hardness, over weeks. Test a sacrificial blade daily and log it. When your steel starts behaving like copper, your stay has a deadline whether you like it or not.
- **Arithmetic on distance.** A hundred east, a hundred north, a hundred west, a hundred south does not close. Your GPS is gone with the rest of the electronics; your compass will do something interesting; your pace count will lie. See below.

## Getting home

**This is the item people underprepare and it is the one that strands them.**

Your arrival point is a location on a plain where locations do not add up, in a world with no landmarks, under a horizon that is a seam. You must not treat "I'll remember the way" as a plan.

1. **Build a cairn at the arrival point the hour you arrive.** Big. Then build three more at known bearings from it, so that no single collapse orphans you.
2. **Register it with the surveyors.** This is the actual answer. They are the only priesthood and the reason the towns hold together; they re-measure the plateau continuously, forever, and post the corrections. Your arrival point is a fixed thing that needs to be in the net. Go to the nearest town, find a pair with knotted cord and lamp-blacked grease, and ask. Pay them properly — with labour, with cord, with anything but tools that will destabilise their economy.
3. **Learn the greeting before you go.** A surveyor's greeting is to state aloud the distance to the nearest cairn, to be corrected, and to thank the corrector. You will be wrong. Being wrong is the greeting. Thank them with real warmth; it is a sacrament, and being able to perform it correctly will open every door on the plateau.
4. **Consider commissioning a pair.** If you are staying long enough — months — have a mason cut two blocks to the standard, set one at your arrival point and one in a town you can always find, and let them deepen. A stone that is in two places is a bearing that cannot drift. This is exactly what their civilisation is made of. It is also the single best souvenir of the trip, and you must leave both halves behind.
5. **Hard return date, written down, held by two people.** More on why below.

## Biosecurity: you are the animals

There is nothing with a spine. Nothing with legs. No insects in the cushion, no worms under it.

And then you arrive, carrying about two kilograms of bacteria, a personal population of Demodex mites living in your eyelashes, possibly a gut protozoan, and the complete metabolic apparatus of a large warm animal. You will be the first legged thing on that plateau since the fossil beds were laid down. Every breath you exhale seeds it.

I cannot tell you whether this matters. I can tell you that the air there *learns habits and is slow to forget them*, that the fetches are the residue of animals that stopped existing an unimaginable time ago, and that you are about to spend three weeks teaching the weather what a person is. Take that as seriously as you would take carrying a rat ashore on an island of flightless birds.

- **Pack out everything.** Every gram of waste, in sealed doubled bags. Yes, all of it. There is no decomposer community waiting for you; there are no worms under the cushion. Whatever you leave will sit there, warm and novel, in a world that has had one biochemistry for longer than our sun has been the colour it is.
- **Do not bury anything. Do not bathe in the Sheet.** The Sheet is everything's water supply and it is all one water.
- **Stay off the cushion.** Kneel on one once, because you should feel it, and then stay off. It is one organism from one end of the plateau to the other, everything green is one green thing with many hats, and it fills a handprint back over the course of an hour. Work out how long a bootprint takes.
- **Have a contingency for a body.** If you die there, you are a corpse in a world that has no protocol for animal decay. Agree with your companions, in advance and in writing, that you come home either way.

## Manners

Get these right and the towns will be extraordinarily good to you. Get them wrong and you will be politely and permanently unwelcome, which on a plateau with one telegraph is a total social event.

**Speak quietly and close.** Two people can converse. Three, standing close. A crowd cannot. Do not raise your voice to be understood; it doesn't work and it reads as a small tantrum. Carry a slate. Learn the cord-tugs and the placard conventions.

**Do not look at the fetches.** On cold mornings there will be a four-legged thing pacing a wall, or something low that pours itself sideways when you turn your head. They break and diffuse under sustained attention. The polite thing is to keep walking. Do not photograph them (you have no camera), do not point, do not follow, do not try to make one hold. Children are taught this; you are, socially, a child here. Obey the rule you were given and do your wondering later.

Note also: you are the only actual animal on the plateau. If a fetch behaves toward you in a way nobody has described before, do not investigate. Walk on.

**The Common Room.** Nine walls cut to the standard; it is *the same room* in every settlement. Rules:

- Bring nothing in and leave nothing in it. Clutter breeds confusion about which town's floor you are standing on, and that is not a metaphor.
- **Tether yourself.** Enter with a cord to a companion standing outside in your own town. Travellers have walked in at one town and out at another four hundred kilometres away, and that is not a shortcut, it is a catastrophe — your kit, your companions, and your arrival-point cairn are all on the other side of a plateau whose distances do not add.
- Keep your town-mark in sight. Chalk nothing without one.
- **Assume everything you say in that room is heard in every town on the plateau.** Say nothing you would not say to the entire civilisation, because you are saying it to the entire civilisation. Do not test this. Do not joke in there.

**The slack.** Bread comes out of the oven as the dough goes in. A cord snaps a half-breath before the strain. Someone will answer you before you have spoken, and will have been answering for a moment already. **Do not remark on it.** They find it convenient, not miraculous, and they time their working day around it. Visitors who gasp every time are exhausting.

Practically: learn to let your sentences land early, and do not repeat yourself when someone has already replied. And take the safety implication seriously — if you feel a rope-pain a half-breath before the load, that is a warning, and the locals treat such things as warnings. Train yourself to act on a symptom that hasn't happened yet.

**The Rehearsal.** Four times a year, a wind out of the flat west carrying the sounds of a day that has already happened. The towns stop work and listen, because listening is difficult here and worth doing when the air is briefly generous.

Stop work. Sit down. Listen. Do not talk over it, do not try to write down what you hear while you hear it, do not answer it, and above all do not answer it if it uses your name. Write afterwards. If you can time your visit to include one, do; an hour of generous air after weeks of forty-pace silence is, by every account I can construct, the most moving thing on that world.

## The real risk

It is not the altitude, though the altitude is what will actually kill you. The real risk is what the place does to your sense of being a distinct person moving through a sequence.

Consider what you are walking into. A world where *before* and *after* have been worn nearly flat against each other, where the rule that makes two similar things two things has thinned to transparency, where sound dies at forty paces, where the horizon is a seam, where nothing moves except fog and the eleven-year green waves and the water going down a finger a decade. You will have no birdsong, no insects, no voices at distance, no weather worth the name, and almost nothing to look at but your own reflection standing on the far side of an enormous window.

People come back from the Sheet flattened. They report that their urgency wore off, that decisions began to feel already-made, that the distinction between intending a thing and remembering it stopped being sharp. That is not a mystical hazard; it is what several weeks of sensory deprivation and mild hypoxia do, plus whatever the local physics contributes, and the local physics is *specifically the erosion of distinctions*. I would not assume you are exempt.

Defences, in order of effectiveness:

1. **A companion whose job is you**, and whose job description is written down and agreed before departure, including the sentence *"I will take you home on the agreed date even if you object."*
2. **A hard return date, recorded in three places**, one of them in the hand of someone not on the trip.
3. **Work.** The local answer to this world is to measure it. Surveyors walk in pairs, forever, and their prayer is a recitation of the current known length of the trunk road, different every year, and *the difference is the point*. Copy them. Take up a daily quantitative task — pace counts, cairn recounts, a thermometer left in the same puddle at the same hour, the depth of one well — and do it religiously. Counting the world is how these people stay themselves inside it, and it will work for you too.
4. **Write by hand, every night, with a date on it.** Dates are a claim about sequence. Keep making the claim.
5. **Limit the visit.** Three weeks is a good trip. Three months is a different decision and you should make it deliberately and with your family's knowledge. People who stay a year do not usually come back, and the ones who do come back are described, by everyone who knew them, using the same word: *level*.

## Short packing list

Knee boots, three pairs, all different · vapour-barrier socks · foot powder · polarised glacier glasses with side shields, four pairs, all notched differently · zinc paste · heat-exchange breathing masks · down and a wind shell, the shell in a colour nobody there has ever seen · folding raised cot and slatted platform · hollow-fibre filter and chlorine dioxide · no-cook calorie-dense food for the whole trip plus a third · one stove, for morale · mechanical watch · brass compass, for the entertainment · pencils and hand-numbered notebooks, each cover different · slate and chalk · fifty metres of good cord and a memorised tug-code · knotted measuring cord, greased and lamp-blacked, made by your own hands · hyperbaric bag · oxygen · the altitude drugs, single-dose, individually labelled, stored apart · a full trauma kit on the assumption that no one will hear you · waste-out bags, more than you think · a file, for notching everything · no electronics · no matched pairs of anything, ever.

## And when you go

Do the thing the locals do on the nine days a year it rains: go out and stand in it.

And when you come home, remember what they will tell you if you ask the right question. Visitors see the flatness and the silence and the empty mirror-stone and assume they have arrived at an ending. They have arrived at a final form, which is not the same. The fog comes up every night and is combed down every morning, and the *tk, tk-tk* moves across the plain like something walking, and people are fitting stones that will still be fitted when the sun has changed colour.

You are the transient thing there. Behave like one.
