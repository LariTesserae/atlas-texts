# Briefing: The Bud

## Read this part first, because everything else follows from it

You are not in danger there. Not much, anyway — the heat is survivable, the edges are only sharp, the fumes are only fumes. The danger runs the other way.

Understand what you are, thermodynamically, from the room's point of view. You are a warm wet bag that sheds roughly forty thousand skin flakes an hour and eighty hairs a day. You exhale about forty grams of water per hour of moderate exertion. The single bead under the eastern overhang — the entire hydrological history of that place, the pin on which its whole delayed future turns — weighs about sixty milligrams.

**One unmasked minute of your breathing puts ten times the room's total water into the room.**

And you will arrive with grit in your cuffs. One grain, dislodged from a trouser seam onto that feldspar ledge, does precisely what the drop is waiting patiently to do, except without permission and without the eleven-year kilns and the millimetre-a-day copper having agreed to it. You would be the mouth coming up the mountain, arriving early, and there would be no record, because the place has no past tense and cannot testify against you.

So: this is not a survival briefing. It is a containment briefing. You are the contaminant. Prepare accordingly.

## Medical screening — go/no-go

- **No respiratory infection.** Not a mild one. Not a cleared-up one. A single sneeze is an act of colonization.
- **No open wounds, no cracked cuticles, no eczema, no psoriasis, no sunburn in the peeling phase.** Nails cut to the quick and filed smooth two days prior, so the edges have stopped shedding.
- **Cough suppressant and a non-drowsy antihistamine, dosed to be at peak on entry.** The air dries the nose in sixty seconds and the throat in five minutes; your body's response to that is to cough, and you must have already won that argument chemically.
- **Pre-hydrate for twelve hours, then stop drinking three hours out.** You want to enter with your tissues loaded and your bladder empty. Nothing leaves your body into that room. Not urine, not sweat onto stone, not blood, not tears. If you cannot promise this, do not go.
- **Fast for eighteen hours.** For the same reason, and because heat plus a full stomach plus an unstable causal order is how people vomit.

## The suit

There is a genuine conflict here and you must simply lose part of it: cleanroom discipline says seal everything, and a kiln-shed August says vent everything. Seal everything. Accept that you will be miserable and that you have perhaps forty minutes.

- **Inner layer:** a laundered-to-death, tight-weave base garment, sealed at wrists and ankles with tape, worn to catch what you shed.
- **Outer layer:** a one-piece coverall, taped seams, integral hood, that has been washed, baked, vacuumed and then vacuum-*sealed*, and is opened for the first time inside the cleft. Not before. Not in the tent at the col. In the cleft.
- **No loose fibre anywhere.** No fleece, no felt, no cotton batting, no cord, no webbing, no Velcro. Every strap that frays is a future soil horizon.
- **No oils.** No sunscreen, no lip balm, no hand cream, no hair product, no insect repellent. The smelter trees exhale oxygen; there is glowing ceramic and freshly-drawn hot copper in that room. Oxygen-enriched air plus an oily glove is a flash fire, and you would be burning inside the only place that has never burned by accident.
- **Gloves:** cut-resistant liner inside a smooth sealed outer. Two spare pairs, sealed. The quartz will part your skin like a scalpel because that edge has never once been argued with.
- **Boots:** rigid soles, mineral or metal, ceramic-wafer insulated, wire-brushed and heat-cleaned, sealed in a bag, put on in the cleft. Nothing rubbery — the floor is hot enough to smear a polymer sole and leave a black gloss on the stone that will still be there when something with a mouth arrives to wonder about it.
- **A blinkered hood.** This sounds absurd. It is the single most important item on the list. See below.

## Breathing gear

- **P100 particulate filtration, minimum,** for the zinc oxide fume. The coin-taste at the back of the tongue is your dosimeter: taste is tolerable, a *sweetness* or a dull ache behind the sternum means you have already taken a dose and will have chills and a fever in six hours. Metal fume fever won't kill you. It will make you stupid at altitude on a mountain that hasn't finished being a shape yet, which might.
- **A carbon monoxide monitor on the chest.** The trees are reducing carbonate. Something is coming off besides oxygen.
- **A condensate-capturing exhalation cartridge.** Desiccant on the outbreath side, sized for the duration, with a sealed reservoir. This is not comfort equipment. This is the whole ethical apparatus of the trip. Test it at temperature beforehand; desiccant beds saturate and then quietly stop working, and the failure mode is invisible.
- **Absolutely do not exhale under the eastern overhang.** That is the coolest square metre in the room. It is a condensation surface. If your mask fails anywhere, it fails there worst. Plan your route so you never come within three metres of it, and plan it in advance, because in the moment you will want to look at the drop, and looking at things is done with the face.

## Instruments, and their limits

Cause runs loose in there. You will hear the click of a falling leaf and *then* watch it fall. Your recordings will come out with the sounds in the wrong order, or blank, or fine. Assume degraded records; bring redundancy; do not build the trip around bringing back evidence, because you will not bring back evidence.

- **Two mechanical clocks and one electronic. Expect disagreement.** Do not use the presses from below as a clock — the interval is an hour or a day and does not repeat evenly.
- **One hard exit alarm, vibrating, worn against the skin, set before entry, not adjustable from inside.**
- **A steel tape and a rangefinder.** Without particulates you will misjudge every distance in the room; the far wall looks touchable and is eleven paces. This matters when you are reaching for balance near unstruck quartz.
- **A bead string.** Twenty beads on wire, worn on the wrist. Names do not adhere in there — you will christen a kiln, mean it, and arrive at the next one having lost the word. You cannot rely on your own memory even for *having already counted*. So you carry a physical past tense with you: one bead moved per completed task. It is crude and it works, because the beads are yours and have a history.
- **Pre-printed task cards, numbered, no names, laminated, tethered to the wrist.** Write them at home, weeks ahead, in the voice of someone who does not trust you. *Card 4: you have not yet looked at the pyrite. Do not pick it up. Move a bead. Go to Card 5.*
- **Nothing to write with.** You will not be leaving marks.

## The blinkers, and the trees

The three smelter trees are one tree held at three points. If you get all three inside your visual field and try to attend to them together, you will take a headache like a struck bell — a fast, whiting-out, knee-folding one. You will be standing on hot stone studded with door-length feldspar laths and blades of quartz that cut like glass because they *are* the original edge.

So: **restrict your field of view mechanically.** A hood with side baffles, or a horse-blinker arrangement, so that no accidental turn of the head can present you with the full trio. Practise walking, kneeling and standing in it beforehand. Practise until it is boring.

Related discipline, to be drilled until it is reflex:

- **Do not go into the corners to test them.** The southwest corner will flatten into generic placeholder grey while you watch, and it will finish itself two seconds ahead of your foot if you approach, and you will want very badly to catch it out. You cannot. What you can do is walk fast into an area whose specifics have not been decided yet while wearing thick gloves and a restricted hood. Look at it from across the room, out of the side of vision, and let it wait.
- **Hands crossed and tucked** as the default posture. Touch nothing. Your skin oils on an unweathered feldspar face would be the oldest stain in the room by a margin of everything.
- **Take nothing.** Not the six-millimetre threaded pyrite nut under the overhang. Not a vitrified seed lying on the stone waiting for weather that has not been invented. You will construct, in there, a very convincing argument that the seed is *stranded* and that you would be *rescuing* it. Refuse it in advance, in writing, on Card 4, in your own handwriting from three weeks ago, because the version of you that is dehydrated and dosed with zinc and standing in a room with no past tense is not the version that should be making that call. Taking the seed out gives the seed a past. That is the one gift it cannot refuse and cannot survive as itself.

## Finding the way in, and the way out

The fissure admits you only when you have stopped looking for it. This is trainable, barely, and it is much easier with a partner.

Method that works: **plant a decoy errand.** Two travellers; one deliberately loses a small object on the approach slope and the other searches for *that*, honestly, with real attention, while the first sits down and stops. The cleft turns up in peripheral, unpurposed vision. Do not congratulate yourself and do not stare at it — attention will not steady it. Go in sideways, gaze soft, and accept that afterward you will not be able to say which of the two nearly-identical clefts you used.

**The exit is the same trick, performed when you are hot, frightened, half-poisoned and desperate.** That is the hardest ten minutes of the trip. Rehearse it as a drill until "stop searching" is something your body does under stress, the way you'd rehearse a rope transfer. Set the exit alarm early enough that you have slack; a person searching hard for a way out of a place that only opens to the un-searching is a person who will still be in there at dusk, when the alloy sap freezes in the veins and every tree in the room goes rigid with a long tightening *tick, tick, ticka-tick* — and you will be alone with the loudest thing that ever happens there, and it will not have been made for you.

## Time in, and the one real budget

There is more than enough of everything in there except time. Take the hint. **Forty minutes.** Less if the coin-taste sweetens, less if the CO monitor twitches, less if your condensate cartridge warms in your hand — that means it's saturated and the water is going somewhere else.

## Coming out

Stand just outside the cleft and do nothing for two minutes. The turbine bushes hum at a level below noticing; you will perceive it for the first time when it stops. That absence is the truest thing you will get from the visit, and it only happens once, on the way out, and only if you are standing still.

Then:

- **Full doff into sealed bags. Weigh them.** You went in with a shed-mass budget; find out whether you kept it. If your outer coverall comes out with a torn cuff, you must write down, honestly, that you may have ended a world's delay, and you will never learn whether you did.
- **Dictate immediately** to a partner working from a fixed checklist of questions. Do not try to narrate. Answer numbered questions. Your memories of the place have no past tense to hang on; they will slide forward and dissolve, and what survives will be whatever got attached to your bead string and someone else's voice.

## What to prepare for emotionally

You will come back without a souvenir, without a usable photograph, without a coherent story, and without the ability to say which cleft you went through. Several of your recordings will have the click before the fall, which you will find eerie and no one else will find convincing. Within a week you will half-doubt you went.

That is the correct outcome. It means you left the room in the condition you found it: an unnamed pocket nine-tenths of the way up a mountain that has not yet been given a shape, forty living things spaced generously on bare stone, one bead of water with a point of sky in it holding on under the eastern overhang, and, below, the press coming down again, patiently, making another few centimetres of world.

Your entire job was to be the visitor it never had.
