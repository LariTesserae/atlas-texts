# Preparation for the Standing Water

## First, understand what will actually hurt you

The glass will cut you and the heat will cook you and the lip will take you over, and none of these is the reason people don't come back. They don't come back because the place is a held note and a human being is a thing that can be held. Nine or ten days is the figure everyone repeats. You will not feel the ninth day coming. You will feel *reasonable* on the ninth day. You will feel that it would be unmannerly to walk out on someone mid-sentence.

So the whole of your preparation is really one preparation: **arrange, in advance and outside yourself, to be removed.** Everything else on this list is housekeeping.

## The extraction contract

Do this before you buy a single strap.

Find a person in your home world who does not love you very much — affection makes people indulgent — and who is reliable in the boring way. Give them a date. Give them money. Give them written, witnessed, unambiguous instruction that on that date they cross and take you off the mountain, and that if you argue, if you reason with them, if you are *lucid and persuasive and calm*, they should regard that as the symptom it is. People who come back report that they were never raving. They were serene. They explained that they only needed a little longer.

Do not appoint a fellow enthusiast. Do not appoint someone who will want to look at the lake.

Second layer: your rations. Carry food and water for **eight days and not one day more**. Not a reserve, not a margin, not a "just in case." Your supply is your clock and it must run out while you still have legs. Eight days out, six days on the rim, and the empty pack is what walks you down. People who packed generously are the ones who cut steps into the glass.

Third layer: leave your way home *below*. Cache your crossing-anchor an hour down the mountain, out of the sound — far enough that you can hear a stone click when you drop it. There are two reasons. The first is that a road out which requires you to descend into quiet is a road you can still recognize as a road. The second is the revision problem: do not let anything that can be edited sit in a place that edits things.

## Your body, in the months before

**Ears.** Get them looked at. Anything already wrong — a perforation, a bad stapes, tinnitus you've learned to live with — will be made permanent. You cannot avoid the sound; you can only decide how much of the small machinery survives it. Take proper attenuating plugs, the moulded kind, plus a second sealed pair in a pocket you don't open for any other reason, because you will lose one and the loss will feel unimportant at the time.

But know the limit of them: plugs protect bone and they do not protect you from the sound, because the sound is not arriving. It is the medium. Plugs will, however, do one dangerous thing — they will let you hear your own blood, which is exactly what a null sounds like. **Never identify a null by ear.** Read the water. Gooseflesh everywhere, glass in one place, that's a null, and your ears have no vote.

**Heat.** Train in hot water. The lake is blood-warm at the top and warmer beneath, which means your body has no gradient to shed into, which means you will cook pleasantly and without complaint. There is no shiver to warn you, no gasp, nothing. Take a mechanical timer — spring-wound, loud, worn on the wrist — and set it before every immersion, and obey it as a law rather than a suggestion. Twelve minutes at the surface. Six below two fathoms. Nothing below three, ever, whatever the columns show you.

**Altitude.** Give yourself four days on the shoulder of the mountain before you go up. The warm wind coming out of the fall will lie to you about how thin the air is.

**Feet and hands.** Whatever you do they will be wet the entire time, because the rain falls upward and never stops. Pack twice the foot care you think absurd. Powder, spare liners, a night discipline of drying by body heat inside your bag. Feet are what carry you down on day eight, and rot is slow and painless and argues for staying.

## Clothing, footwear, skin

The rim is fresh volcanic glass in lobes and ropes, and underwater it grows in blades and fans and rosettes. It does not cut the way stone cuts. You will not feel it. You will look down and find four openings in your palm.

- **Boots:** stiff-soled, high, with a hard rand. Then over them, for any shoreline work, a gaiter of heavy leather or cord matting. The shoreline is where the new glass buds and splits, and it is also where the red-brown film is, which squeaks and slides.
- **Cleats or a cord-wrapped sole** for the film. It is not moss and it does not behave like moss; it is more like walking on a wet nerve.
- **Full-cover swimwear.** Never go into the water bare. A weighted long-sleeved suit of heavy cloth, cuffed at wrist and ankle, and canvas gloves. You are going to drift above the blades and the current will decide, and cloth means the difference between being abraded and being opened.
- **Sleeves and a hood on land, too.** Not for sun — the light is that of a sickroom with the curtains drawn — but because you will fall against things.
- **A serious wound kit,** weighted toward the kind of injuries that don't announce themselves: a mirror to inspect your own back, haemostatic packing, sutures, and a routine of stripping and checking yourself twice a day at fixed times whether or not you believe you're hurt. Make it a ritual. Rituals with fixed times are your friends here.

## Rope, and the direction of down

Learn this before you arrive, because on the rim you will not be able to think it through in the noise: **down is toward the loudest place.**

At the lip, the loudest place is the fall. Therefore at the lip, down is outward. Not metaphorically. Your body's own weight will lean you over the edge, gently and constantly, and the updraft lifting your hair will make it feel like buoyancy rather than the approach it is.

Therefore: **you are on a rope at the rim. Always. No exceptions, not for a moment, not to look.** Static line, anchored to something at least thirty paces back from the lip, and understand that the anchor itself may be gone by tomorrow because the mountain is still going. Re-set your anchors every single morning. Carry more line than is reasonable — a hundred paces of it — plus bolts or pitons that bite fresh glass, which is to say, plenty of them, because they will not bite well.

Near a null, down becomes negotiable, and things go up. This includes gear. This may include you. So:

- **Everything is leashed to you, and every leash has a cutaway.** A short blade on your chest, a second on your hip, both openable one-handed with either hand. If your pack decides to rise while tethered, it will take you with it, and you will have about a breath and a half to disagree.
- **A weight belt** for any swimming near smooth water, and a belt buckle you can hit blind.
- **A ground anchor and a short leash for null work,** if you insist on doing null work. Most people should not. The only defensible reason to enter a null is to observe the whip-weed, and the weed has been observed.

## The null discipline

Write these on something you can read without thinking. Laminate them. You will be tired and loud inside.

1. **Nulls are found by eye.** Smooth water in gooseflesh. Never by hearing.
2. **Nulls move.** The one at your camp at dawn is forty paces east by evening. Never camp on glass-smooth water, never sleep where the surface was calm, never leave your kit in a place because it was quiet there.
3. **Mark null positions on a hard chart with times**, and mark them from a fixed landmark on the rim — and know that the landmarks are provisional too, because the shoreline is never twice the same.
4. **If the roar returns suddenly and your ears are wet,** you were in one and have been walked out of it. Sit down. Check your count of everything on your person. Check it once, aloud, and then stop checking.
5. **Do not put anything in a null that you intend to keep as evidence of itself.** Not a sandal, not a cairn, not your notebook, not your hand for longer than it takes to feel the sag, not another person.

## The revision problem, and what to write things on

Things left in the silence come back *revised*: one more strap, one more stone, and the memory adjusts to match. This is checkable. Nobody wants to check twice.

Prepare like this:

- **Keep one control object.** Something with countable parts — a knotted cord, a string of beads, a comb. Count it, photograph it, record the number in three places at home with your extraction contact, and then never count it yourself. On your return, your contact counts it once and does not tell you the result unless it matches. That way you have data and you do not have the other thing.
- **Carry a second, sacrificial record on your body** — punched metal tags, not paper — stating your name, your date of arrival, your intended departure date, and the names of two people waiting. Wear it against skin, under everything, and take it off for nothing. Your name is the first thing this place quietly stops needing. Notice that of all the people who came here, we have their stairs, their doorway, their bath house, and not one name.
- **Keep no journal you plan to reason from.** Keep a tally only: a stroke per day, cut into the strap of your pack with a knife, made at a fixed hour, in the loud. Prose invites theories. **Bring no hypotheses and form none.** The stairs are older than the mountain and the tree is older than the mountain and there is no reward for noticing this a third time.

## Rules for the water

- Never swim alone, and never swim tethered to a person — tether both of you to the shore, separately.
- Never swim in the hot columns. The shrimp can have them.
- **Do not roll over at five fathoms.** Everyone does. If you do, look once, come up, and do not describe what you saw to your partner, and do not go back down to check whether it is still walking. If you want to test it — and you will want to — do it from the rim, in air, where you can see the far side: change your pace, stop, reverse. Most people find the figure changes too. A few find it doesn't. Neither result is good for you and both of them will make you stay longer.
- **Do not drink the lake.** This is the single hardest rule and the one most worth keeping. The water is warm and clean-seeming and the silt is only silt. The birds arrive exhausted, and drink, and then walk the rim as sober as clerks for days, and then go up. You do not know that the two facts are connected. You do not know that they aren't. Carry your own water, boil it out of the snowmelt below if you must resupply, and if you run out, that is the end of the visit and not a problem to be solved.

## The built things

**The nine steps.** Count them from the shelf downward, once, on the first day, with your partner counting independently and neither of you speaking the number aloud until both are finished. Write the two numbers separately. Then leave it. Do not descend them. They go nowhere; there is nothing under the water for them to reach; and the treads are worn concave, which means feet, which means many feet, which means a long time, and you are not going to be the one who fixes that.

**The doorway on the western rim.** Decide before you arrive whether you will pass through it. Decide in your own house, in quiet, in writing, and stick to the decision.

If you decide yes: do it on the first day, while you are still a visitor and still slightly frightened, and **duck.** Duck deliberately and much lower than necessary. It is low enough to require it. Nobody ducks. That is the whole of the reported phenomenon and it is worth more attention than it gets. Ducking is an assertion that you have a size and that the size is yours.

If you decide no: stay ten paces off it and route your circuit accordingly.

**The ringing boulder.** Do not put your forehead against it. If you must — if you have come all this way and the whole point was the boulder — do it once, for the count of three, with your partner's hands on your shoulders and a second rope on your harness, and with both of you agreeing beforehand on the number three and what will happen at four.

The reasoning: down is toward the loudest place, and that stone is the loudest thing in the basin, and it is only loud in bone. Which raises a question about your skeleton that has not been answered and is not going to be answered by you.

**The cairns.** Three or four. Do not count them. Do not add to them. **Do not build anything at all** — no windbreak, no marker, no little stack to remember a spot by. The impulse to build is not a practical impulse here; it is the first visible symptom of staying. If you catch yourself stacking stones, that is your day count speaking, whatever the strokes on your strap say. Go down.

## The eel

There is one. It is the same one. It will come to the shallows and watch you with its whole flank.

Don't feed it. Don't name it. Naming is how you make a reason to come back tomorrow, and tomorrows are the currency this place takes.

## Time, and the shape of your day

The place has no clock: the light is the same dim green at noon as at four, the shoreline moves, the sound removes the ordinary sense of before-and-after. Bring three timepieces of different kinds — wound spring, sand, and a burning cord with marked intervals — and set your life by them like a ship.

Fixed hours to eat. Fixed hours to strip and check for cuts. Fixed hour to cut the stroke in the strap. Fixed hour to sleep, back from the water, on a shelf that you inspect before lying down and expect to be gone by morning. Eat even when you aren't hungry — hunger arrives in the time it takes to walk the rim, which means the rim is the only honest clock here, and it will be taken from you.

And set one alarm you cannot reason with: your partner's, or the timer's, or the empty pack's.

## What to leave at home

- **Music, and anything you'd call a favourite sound.** You are going to lose it. Whatever tune you carry in will be scoured out and replaced by the long complicating middle of the sentence, and you will not get the tune back for months.
- **Recording equipment.** Or, if you must bring it, arrange for it to be destroyed before you cross home. A recording of that sound, played in a room in your own world, is a piece of the unfinished thing set going in your house. It will not finish there either.
- **Any open question you care about.** Do not go up there with a grief you haven't closed, a letter unsent, a question you want the universe to answer. The place is itself an unfinished thing and it will find the unfinished thing in you and they will hold hands. The people who came here brought questions. They left stairs.
- **Anything you love that can be carried.** It will go in a null or it will go up.

Bring instead a closed loop. A promise with a date on it, made to a person who will be publicly inconvenienced if you fail. Something small and ordinary and *completable*. A debt. A wedding you are standing up at. Some obligation with a **verb already in it.**

## Coming down, and after

Leave in the morning, downhill, without a last look at the lip. Walk until you can hear a stone click. Stop there, sit, and wait an hour before touching your anchor, because the first quiet will feel like a null and you should let your body learn that it isn't.

Afterward, for a month at least:

- Work somewhere noisy. Machines, markets, weather. Total silence at home will not feel like peace, it will feel like a pocket, and you will start watching the surfaces of things for smoothness.
- Sit with people who finish their sentences crisply. You will find yourself leaning forward at the end of other people's clauses, waiting. This passes. Mostly.
- Have your contact check the control object once, and accept the result, and do not check it again.
- And when you begin — and you will, around the second or third month, usually at night — to think that you were unfair, that you left too early, that it was so warm there and so small, that you could have crossed it before lunch and you were almost certainly one or two days from hearing how it ends:

Read your own letter. Write it now, before you go, in your own hand, and give it to the unaffectionate reliable person, and make it say the true thing, which is this:

**It has been coming for as long as the water has been standing up. It was not going to finish for you.**
