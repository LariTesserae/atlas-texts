# Preparing for the Weeping Shelf
### A practical briefing for an ordinary human, first visit

---

## 0. Understand what kind of place this is

It is not a wilderness and it is not an alien world. It is an industrial civilisation of maybe forty cities living on top of a chemical process. Everything difficult about it comes from three facts:

1. **The rock is being made and unmade around you at a rate you can measure.**
2. **The whole landscape works by letting gas out of water.** That gas is not always breathable.
3. **There is no plant life at all**, which means no wood, no fibre, no fruit, no shade, and no vitamin C.

Everything below follows from those three.

---

## 1. Six weeks before you go

**Altitude.** The lip of the scarp is 2,000–3,000 m and the plateau climbs gently from there. Warm, wet, low-pressure air is a trap: you will not *feel* like you are at altitude, because you are sweating in a shirt at 28°C. Acclimatise properly, and add margin for the second problem below. If you have any history of pulmonary or cerebral oedema, don't.

**Carbon dioxide.** The plateau's ambient CO₂ is chronically elevated — the terraces exist *because* CO₂ is coming out of solution all day, everywhere. Combine that with thin air and you get the standard newcomer's fortnight: dull frontal headache, breathlessness on stairs, poor sleep, a vague feeling of having been mildly insulted. It passes. Locals have deep chests and very good chemoreceptors and think you are being dramatic.

**Hearing.** Get a proper audiogram before departure and another when you return. You are going somewhere with a permanent 24-hour ambient roar. Nobody there will tell you this is a problem, because they cannot hear you ask.

**Teeth.** Get everything fixed. Dentistry on the Shelf is competent but the water is a fluoride and arsenic delivery system and dentists there are mostly in the business of managing stain, not decay. (The cliff is described as "the colour of old teeth." This is not a coincidence anybody up there finds funny, so don't.)

**Immunisation and biosecurity — take this seriously.** You are visiting the densest concentration of birds in any world. It is a functioning influenza reactor with a hundred square kilometres of standing waders per colony and a guano industry that aerosolises everything. Get every avian-adjacent vaccination your home has. More importantly: **run a proper quarantine in both directions.** You are an immunological stranger arriving among people who have never met your pathogens, and you are a potential vector carrying something novel back to a world with poultry in it. Two weeks isolation before, two weeks after, and don't be clever about it.

**Fitness.** The switchback ramps are two vertical kilometres. Down is easy on the lungs and murder on the knees; up is the other thing. Most travellers ride the caravans; the caravans still make you walk the wet sections.

## 2. The three things that actually kill visitors

### Gas in hollows

This is the big one and it kills more outsiders than everything else combined.

CO₂ is heavier than air. The dissolution country is full of dry valleys, dolines, sinkholes and shafts, and on still mornings these fill with invisible, odourless, unbreathable gas that lies in them like water in a bath. You can stand in a hollow with your head in good air and your knees in a lake of death. **Bending down to tie a bootlace has killed people.** Children die first. Dogs die first. That is why there are no dogs on the Shelf.

Then there is H₂S, which is worse in a nastier way: it *does* announce itself with the boiled-egg smell, but at dangerous concentrations it paralyses your sense of smell within a minute or two, so the last thing your nose tells you before it fails is that everything has got better.

Practice:
- Buy a **flame-stick** at any trailhead — a stub candle on a long wire hook. Lower it before you descend into anything. If it dims or dies, you do not follow it.
- **Never enter a hollow alone, and never one you cannot see over the rim of.**
- Do not sleep in a low place, do not shelter from rain in one, do not go down into an interesting-looking shaft to look at the pretty banding.
- Cellars in Aggrade are gas traps by design. If a stairwell has a chain across it and a chalked mark, the mark is a date and the chain is not decorative.
- If someone collapses in a hollow, **do not go in after them.** That is how you get two bodies. This is the single most common way rescuers die there, and locals will physically restrain you.

### Thin crust over hot water

Freshly deposited travertine over a boiling seep can look exactly like solid pavement. It is a shell a centimetre thick over water at 90°C. Scalding injuries on the Shelf are amputation-grade and infection follows fast in that mineral broth.

Carry a **staff** and use it constantly, the way you'd probe snow. Do not step where nothing has stepped. If a surface is unnaturally smooth, unnaturally clean, or gently steaming, it is not for you. Follow the stilt-crabs; they know.

### The water itself

Warm, alkaline, bitter, loaded with carbonate, sulphate, fluoride, arsenic and lithium depending on the district. Drinking it untreated will not drop you where you stand; it will give you a fortnight of gut trouble and, over years, mottled teeth and stiff joints. Boiling *concentrates* the bad parts and welds your kettle shut.

**Only distilled water, and only honest rain.** Every household has a still; every district has a condenser tower. Buy or hire a small personal still on arrival — they're cheap, ubiquitous, and roughly as culturally loaded as a wallet. Carrying your own still marks you as a competent adult. Asking to borrow someone's marks you as a child.

Do not bathe in a wound. Do not swim with an open blister.

## 3. Kit

**Bring:**

- **Boots**, heavy-soled, with a spare pair. *Karren* — the fluted, weathered dissolution rock — is as sharp as broken glass and will open a thin sole in a day. Meanwhile terrace mud and mat film are frictionless. You want stiff soles, deep lugs, and high ankles.
- **Goggles or dark glasses**, wraparound, two pairs. Under the cloud lid the light is a diffuse white glare bouncing off pale rock and ten thousand mirrors. It is snow-blindness weather with none of the cold to warn you. Also, lime dust in the eye is a chemical burn.
- **Respirator**, with fine-particulate filters, plus a supply of simple cloth masks. Aggrade's air is quicklime dust, mat-coal smoke and sulphur. White-lung is the local occupational disease and the local reason people cough for forty years and then stop.
- **Ear protection**, moulded, plus a second set. And a **sleep set** — the deepest, dullest ear-defenders you can find. Your first three nights under a colony you will not sleep at all.
- **Acid.** A jug of dilute acetic or citric, and a spare. This is your descaling kit; you will use it every night on boots, buckles, buttons, watch, teeth of zips, lens rims. You can buy sulphuric locally, cheap, from the sulphur works — it is a household staple, sold by the jug, and it will take your skin off if you're casual with it. The gentle stuff you bring yourself.
- **Grease.** Alkaline water strips the oil out of your skin. Within a week your knuckles and heels will crack, and cracks in a mineral wet environment go septic. Lanolin, tallow, whatever you like — greasing yourself morning and night is not vanity there, it's hygiene. Locals use rendered bird fat and smell like it.
- **A month of multivitamins**, weighted heavily toward **C**, plus iron. Also a mild osmotic laxative. See §4.
- **Metal, needles, small tools, spectacles, lenses.** Excellent trade goods. Metal is imported and dear.
- **A photograph, painting, or pressed sample of a forest.** Read §7 before you decide this is sentimental nonsense.
- **A written instruction about your body.** Morbid but necessary. See §8.

**Do not bring:**

- **Seeds. Anything living. Anything that could be.** They cannot grow — the chemistry forbids it — but the fear is real, the inspection at the ramp-head is thorough, and being caught with a viable seed is a serious offence rather than a joke.
- **Anything wooden you would mind losing**, in either sense. See §6.
- **Leather boots you love.** They'll come home stone.

## 4. Eating

The Shelf's food chain is: mat → fly larvae and brine shrimp → birds. You will eat at every level of it.

**Expect:** eggs in enormous variety and quantity; boiled, salted, pickled, preserved in lime, dried and powdered. Bird — roasted, potted, rendered, smoked over mat-coal. Shrimp paste, shrimp meal, shrimp everything. A grey-brown flatbread bulked out with **chitin flour** ground from insect shell, which is nutritionally inert and exists to give your gut something to push against. Cloudy sour **pool-beer** fermented from mat sugars.

**The critical item: green scrape.** The fresh top skin of managed mat, harvested wet, eaten raw with salt and acid. It is slimy, sour, faintly sulphurous, and looks like something that came out of a drain. It is also, along with raw liver and roe and fresh blood, essentially the only source of vitamin C on the continent. **Eat it.** Refusing it politely for three weeks is how outsiders develop bleeding gums in a city of people who cannot understand why the visitor's teeth are falling out when the cure is served at every meal.

- **Never eat unmanaged mat.** Wild mat from an unstamped pool can be loaded with hepatotoxins and worse.
