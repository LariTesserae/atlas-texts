# Preparing for the Young Range

## What you are actually going to

Read the brief again and notice what it doesn't say. It doesn't say the mountains are dangerous. It says they're *unfinished*. That's the whole problem in one word.

You are going to a working factory floor with no railings, no lights, no supervisor, no exits marked, at 55% of sea-level oxygen, where the floor plan is redrawn weekly and the machinery has been running for ten thousand years without maintenance and will run for a million more. Nothing there is hostile. Nothing there is aware of you. That is worse.

Two facts should organise all your preparation:

**One: it will not be the cold that kills you. It will be the wet.** Air at +1°C, saturated to the dew point, with wind, is a more efficient way to remove heat from a human being than dry air at −25°C. The Antarctic plateau lets you stay dry. This place does not. Everything you own becomes damp — the brief tells you so — and damp at one degree above freezing is a slow, patient, undramatic killer that works on you while you're admiring the geese.

**Two: your instincts for reading terrain are calibrated to old mountains and they will be wrong here.** On Earth you read stability without knowing you're doing it: lichen means that block hasn't moved in a century; rounded means water and time have tested it; soil and moss mean the slope has settled. Here there is no lichen older than a hundred years, no rounding, no soil, and every surface is a fresh fracture face. Your eye will report *this whole slope looks freshly collapsed* and your eye will be correct, and that report is useless as a discriminator because it is true of everything you can see. You have to replace the instinct with a rule, and the rule is: **every block is untested, every slope is at repose, nothing holds.**

## Phase 1: the body, starting eight weeks out

The valley floor is at 4,900 m. There is no lower ground within reach; the marsh *is* the bottom. You cannot do what mountaineers do, which is acclimatise on the way up, because there is no way up — you arrive at altitude, all at once, through a door.

So acclimatise at home, before you leave. This is the single most important item of preparation and the one most travellers skimp on.

- **Minimum: four weeks living above 3,500 m**, with a final two weeks including days at 4,800–5,200 m. Six weeks is better. The ventilatory response comes in days; the haematological response — the extra red cells, the shifted oxygen dissociation curve — takes three weeks and is still improving at six.
- **If you must use a hypoxic tent**, budget ten weeks at eight-plus hours a night, ramping to an equivalent of 5,000 m, and understand you'll be worse off than someone who did it for real, because sleeping hypoxic and living normoxic gives you a fraction of the adaptation.
- **Arrive pre-adapted or don't arrive.** Stepping through a door straight from sea level to 4,900 m is an acute exposure of the kind that produces high-altitude pulmonary oedema in otherwise healthy people within twelve to thirty-six hours. There is no descent available and no rescue. It is a death sentence with a two-day fuse.

**Medical pre-work.** Have your teeth done — a root canal at 4,900 m with no evacuation is a genuine emergency. Get imaging if you have any hernia history. Understand your own AMS susceptibility from prior exposure; if you have a history of HAPE or HACE, do not go. Carry acetazolamide (prophylactic, and know the dose), dexamethasone and nifedipine (rescue only, for a single purpose: buying hours), and know that at 4,900 m with nowhere to descend, the rescue drugs are buying you time to reach the door, not time to continue.

**Fitness, but the right kind.** Not maximal power. You need the ability to work all day at 40% of your sea-level output without going anaerobic, to carry 18–20 kg over rubble, and to recover overnight on poor sleep. Load-carrying on uneven ground, long slow distance, and — genuinely — ankle strengthening. You will walk exclusively on fresh angular talus and saturated gravel for the entire visit.

**Accept the clock.** Above 4,800 m you do not recover; you deteriorate. You will lose weight, sleep badly, heal slowly, and think worse than you think you're thinking. Ten days is a good trip. Fourteen is the edge. Beyond three weeks you're just watching yourself come apart in the dimmest light you've ever lived in.

## Phase 2: the hazards, ranked honestly

1. **Hypothermia** — wet, wind, low calories, flat light concealing how long you've been standing still.
2. **Immersion foot** — twelve hours of cold wet feet is enough to start it. It ends trips. It can end toes.
3. **Rockfall** — the brief tells you the schedule: freeze-thaw quarrying delivers a fresh scatter at dawn; the glassy aprons detach around ten. Three or four clatters an hour, unlocatable. A helmet is not optional and it is not sufficient.
4. **Glacial lake outburst** — "some drain in a single afternoon when the ice dam fails." That is not a rising river. That is a slurry front carrying boulders.
5. **Altitude illness** — mitigated by Phase 1, never eliminated.
6. **A minor injury.** A rolled ankle. In this place, with no rescue, a rolled ankle is a category-1 hazard. Price it like one.
7. **Falling into moving water.** Milky, opaque, one degree, powerful, and you cannot see the bottom or the depth of anything.
8. **Crevasses and moulins**, if you go on the ice at all.

Note what isn't on the list: animals, weather events, other people. The valley isn't trying. It's just running, and you're standing in the feed chute.

## Phase 3: the wet problem, solved properly

The clothing principle here is inverted from normal mountaineering. You are not managing *cold*; you are managing *moisture*, of which there is an infinite supply at every temperature. Nothing you wet will dry — there is no sun, the air is at its dew point, and your fuel budget is finite.

**So the strategy is not "dry it later." It is "never let it get wet."**

- **Synthetic insulation only.** No down, anywhere, for anything. Down in a saturated environment is a fashion accessory. Take continuous-filament synthetic — heavier, bulkier, and it insulates at 70% when soaked, which is the only number that matters.
- **Vapour barrier liners.** Unfashionable, deeply effective, and this is exactly the environment they exist for. VBL socks against the skin, VBL liner in the sleeping bag. You'll feel clammy. Your insulation will stay dry for ten days instead of three.
- **A hard shell with real sealed seams**, and a second one. Not a light one. It will be worn essentially all day, every day, and abraded on rock that behaves like broken glass.
- **Rubber for the hands.** Wool liner gloves inside unlined rubber or neoprene fisherman's gloves for all wet work — seeps, water collection, camp, rope handling. Then dry insulated mitts that never, ever touch water. Four pairs of liners. You will destroy gloves on fresh-fracture limestone faster than you believe.
- **Footwear is the crux.** Not leather doubles — they wick and never dry. Rubber-bottomed pac boots with removable liners, plus VBL socks, plus three liner-sock changes. Two removable boot liners so one can sleep in the bag with you while the other is worn. Inspect both feet twice daily, morning and night, every single day, for maceration and whiteness. Antifungal powder. This is not fussiness. Immersion foot is the most likely thing to end your trip.
- **Dry camp kit, sacred and inviolable.** One set of insulation and socks in a sealed dry bag that never leaves the tent. Ever. Not for a quick step outside. Discipline about this is the difference between a hard trip and a rescue that isn't coming.
- **Ground insulation, over-specified.** Saturated near-freezing gravel is a heat sink like nothing you've slept on. Closed-cell foam *plus* an inflatable, combined R-value 7 or higher.
- **A sleeping bag rated to −20°C** even though the air will be near zero, because you will be wet, hypoxic, calorically depleted, and not generating the heat a rating assumes.

## Phase 4: water, fuel, food

**Water is everywhere and almost all of it is wrong.** The outflow is skimmed-milk turbid with rock flour that "will not settle out of still water for a month." That flour will clog a pump filter in litres, blind a UV steriliser completely, and — ingested in quantity over days — will give you an abrasive, miserable gut.

- **Drink from the spring lines, not the river.** The seeps at the bedding planes are filtered by the rock itself: cold, clear, and the single best resource in the valley. Plan camps around seep access.
- If you must use turbid water: **settle it, flocculate it, then treat it.** A small bag of alum or a purpose-made coagulant sachet costs nothing and turns a litre of grey slurry into clear water in twenty minutes. Then a cloth pre-filter, then chemical treatment or a rolling boil. (Water boils at about 84°C there; that's still well above pasteurisation, so boiling works fine — it just costs fuel.)
- **Hydration is a smaller problem here than at most high altitudes**, and this surprises people. Standard high-altitude dehydration comes from breathing hard through bone-dry air; here the air is saturated, so your respiratory water loss is a fraction of normal. You will still need 3–4 litres, but you won't feel the desiccation. Don't let the absence of that familiar signal make you complacent.

**Fuel: liquid, not canisters.** Petrol or white gas, a pump stove you can field-strip, and spare jets and seals. Canisters underperform badly in sustained cold and damp. Budget ~150 ml per person per day — you're mostly heating, not melting, because liquid water is abundant. **There is no firewood. There has never been firewood. There is no combustible material in the valley of any kind.** Every joule you burn, you carried.

**Food: hot, fatty, and constant.** 4,000–5,000 kcal/day, and you won't want any of it — appetite suppression at altitude is severe and it's what makes people cold. Eat on a schedule, not on hunger. Weight the ration toward fat and simple carbohydrate; protein digestion is expensive and poorly tolerated up there. Hot sweet drinks are not a comfort item here; they are thermal management, and you should plan six a day.

## Phase 5: light, scale, and time

The light will disorient you in three separate ways and you should rehearse each.

**Flat light destroys depth.** Shadowless diffuse illumination at roughly 200–800 lux means the ground has no shape. You will step off ledges you didn't see and stumble over boulders you read as flat. Carry **two trekking poles and probe constantly** — poles become depth perception in flat light. Take **amber or rose contrast-enhancing lenses**, not dark ones, and a clear pair for the dimmest hours. And take real UV protection anyway: at 4,900 m under cloud, with snow albedo, you can still burn corneas and the underside of your chin.

**No scale references.** No trees, no buildings, no soil, no vegetation of familiar size. An 1,100 m wall will read as 250 m and you will plan a two-hour approach that takes nine. **Do not trust your eye for distance at all.** Use the altimeter, use a map bearing, use time-and-pace, and put a brightly coloured object in every photograph or you'll never be able to read your own pictures afterwards.

**Circadian collapse.** Three hundred lux from wake to sleep, dimming rather than brightening through the afternoon, no sunrise, no sunset, no colour change. Your clock will unmoor within four days. Countermeasures: a **10,000-lux therapy lamp for twenty minutes at wake time** (small, light, worth its weight three times over), a wind-up or solar watch, and a rigid schedule you keep even when it feels arbitrary. Expect periodic breathing at night — the Cheyne-Stokes cycling that wakes you gasping. It's normal. Acetazolamide helps.

## Phase 6: navigation in a landscape with no memory

**The floor of this valley is not a map. It is a process.** Channels appear overnight. Moraine ridges shift. The lake you camped beside drains in an afternoon and becomes a grit plain. Cairns fall over, get buried, or wash away. Footprints in the flush last a hundred years but tell you nothing about a route that no longer exists.

- **Navigate exclusively by the skyline.** The walls, the arêtes, the cols, the summits — those are your permanent features on any human timescale. Take careful compass bearings to three named skyline points from every significant location, and record them. Never navigate by anything below the trimline.
- **Maintain a running sketch with times, bearings and altimeter readings.** Waterproof notebook, pencil, not pen. Assume no satellites, no signal, no digital anything that matters.
- **Re-survey the route daily.** The way in is not the way out. Check it going out, not on the way in.
- **Calibrate the altimeter every morning** against a known point; barometric drift in this weather is substantial.

**Portal discipline — the multiverse-specific item.** Mark your arrival point against **bedrock, not sediment**. Do not mark it with a cairn on the marsh, or a flag on a moraine crest, or anything that sits on the valley floor, because the valley floor is a moving fluid on a timescale of days. Find the nearest exposed bedrock spur, record the bearing and distance from it, record three skyline bearings, and record the altimeter reading. Then make the mark itself high-visibility and chromatic — **magenta or fluorescent orange**, because there is no such colour anywhere in the valley and it will be the only saturated hue within thirty kilometres. And place a second, redundant mark two hundred metres away on a different bedrock feature.

Then rehearse returning to it in fog. Because cloud forms *in* the valley, at eye level, out of nothing, and it will do so at the worst moment.

## Phase 7: the daily rhythm

The valley has a timetable. Learn it and obey it.

- **Pre-dawn to first light.** The coldest, most stable hours. Best glacier travel. Best river crossings — meltwater flow is at its overnight minimum, and a braid that's thigh-deep at four in the afternoon is calf-deep at six in the morning. Also: **this is when the geese come over the col.** If you are there in the right week, be in position on a ridge with a view of the notch, dressed for two motionless hours, before there is any light at all.
- **First light to about noon.** The quarrying shift. Freeze-thaw has been loading the walls all night and the releases come with warming; the ice aprons let go around ten in a crash of glass. **Stay out of the run-out zone of every wall.** Run-out is further than you think on fresh talus — plan for two-thirds of the wall height as a horizontal buffer, minimum.
- **Midday to mid-afternoon.** Cross open ground. Work the flats. Note the dust lifting off the drying silt on the wind.
- **Mid-afternoon onward.** It gets dimmer, not darker — a slow grey draining. Cloud forms in the valley. Water levels peak. Be at camp, dry, with a hot drink, before you want to be.

## Phase 8: camp

Camp siting is the most consequential decision you'll make each day, and there are only four criteria:

1. **Above the flood.** Thirty vertical metres above the valley floor, out of any channel, out of any flat that looks like it was recently a lake bed — because it was. Know your uphill escape line and be able to run it in the dark.
2. **Out of every rockfall cone.** Not "mostly out." The fresh blocks at the foot of the cliffs each morning are the hazard's own annotation of its range; camp beyond the furthest of them, and then further.
3. **On bedrock or coarse old gravel.** Never on the moss flushes — eighty years to establish, and your tent footprint will be legible for a century. Never on fine saturated silt, which will suck your pegs through and soak your floor.
4. **Within reach of a clear seep.**

Take a **double-wall tent with a real bathtub floor and a large vestibule**, plus a separate groundsheet. Condensation management is the whole game; you need to be able to cook and change under cover without soaking the inner. Peg into gravel with wide-surface stakes, and back them with rock piles, because nothing there is consolidated enough to hold a thin peg.

## Phase 9: the life, and your obligations to it

You are stepping into a place with almost no biomass, almost no nutrients, and — crucially — **almost no decomposers**. This is not a wilderness with a resilience budget. It's a pioneer system with nothing in reserve.

- **Carry out everything, including faeces.** Solid waste will not break down in any meaningful timeframe at 4,900 m in this cold. Sealed double-bag system, planned for the full trip, weighed and budgeted.
- **Urinate on bare gravel or into flowing water, never on moss.** A single trip's nitrogen deposition is a meaningful fertilisation event in an oligotrophic flush.
- **Walk on rock and gravel, always.** Route around the moss. The springtail grooves on the ice carry a hundred thousand organisms per square metre; you will not avoid all of them, but you can avoid the dense patches, which are visible as a spill of pepper.
- **Decontaminate before you go.** New boots or boots scrubbed sterile — soil in lug treads carries seeds and spores. Inspect and clean every Velcro closure, every strap, every fold. No open food, no crumbs, no organic packaging. You are the most plausible vector this valley will ever encounter.
- **Do not interact with the vertebrates.** The blue sheep are working with no margin. The wolf on the shelf is doing arithmetic and the sum does not include you; don't change the inputs. The choughs will approach you and they will steal anything shiny, and that's on you.

**And take a 10× hand lens.** It's forty grams and it is by far the best instrument you can bring. The springtails, the red snow-algae, the crustose lichens growing a millimetre a decade, the fossil shell sections in the tilted slab at 5,200 m — all of it is at the wrong scale for the naked eye, and all of it is the actual content of the place.

## Phase 10: the mind

Prepare for this as seriously as for the cold, because it catches people flat.

The brief names it exactly: *the place is hollow. You feel it as a kind of ringing in the attention.* Four kilometres of ice, eleven hundred metres of wall, and no organism visible anywhere in that volume. Combine that with 300-lux grey light, a silence deep enough to hear individual sand grains, and mild chronic hypoxia, and you get a genuinely altered state — a flattening, a sense of unreality, occasional strong emotion arriving from nowhere, and a peculiar inability to judge how long you've been standing still.

- **Don't go alone.** Two minimum, three better. Not only for rescue — for the correction of each other's judgement, which will degrade before you notice it degrading.
- **Talk out loud. Keep a log.** Write down what you did and when, and read yesterday's entry each morning. Written records are the only reliable check on hypoxic cognition, which feels completely normal from the inside.
- **Build the day around tasks**, not around feelings, because feelings there will be unreliable.
- **And then, deliberately, schedule an hour of doing nothing at all** — sitting at the toe of the ice, not photographing, not moving, letting the hush of the outflow and the occasional unlocatable clatter be the whole content of your attention. Because that hour is why you came, and if you don't schedule it you will spend the entire trip managing gear.

## Phase 11: the no-rescue rule

There is no helicopter. There is no other party. There is no descent to thicker air. Your margin is the door you came through and however far you are from it.

The practical translation: **accept roughly one-tenth of the objective hazard you'd accept on a normal Earth trip.** Turn around earlier than feels reasonable. Don't cross the glacier if you're uncertain. Don't go up the ramp to the fossil slab if the wall above has been talking. Don't cross the braid at four in the afternoon because you're tired and want dinner.

Carry a **serious wound kit**, disproportionate to what you'd normally take, because wound healing at 4,900 m is dramatically impaired — a small cut on a hand, on fracture-sharp limestone, in permanent damp, will become a problem that does not resolve while you are there. Clean and dress everything immediately. Cover your hands.

And fix your **absolute turnaround**: a date and time by which you are back at the portal regardless of weather, regardless of whether you've seen what you came for, regardless of how you feel. Write it down before you leave. Give it to your partners. Don't renegotiate it in the field, because the person doing the renegotiating will be hypoxic, cold, and behind on calories.

## What to actually do, once you're standing there

A short list, in priority order, for an ordinary human with ten days:

- **Put your hand flat on a weeping bedding plane** and hold it there until it hurts. That's a sea coming out of a mountain.
- **Walk up to the fossil slab at 5,200 m** and look at the coiled shell sections. Warm shallow water, five kilometres of lift, four millimetres a year, and no time to weather. It's the single most compressed statement the valley makes.
- **Be under a wall at a safe distance at ten in the morning** and watch an ice apron detach — the soft crash of glass.
- **Get low on the ice with the hand lens** and find the springtails in a meltwater groove.
- **Find one black crustose lichen** and measure its diameter. Then work out its age. Then work out that the soil it's making won't exist until long after your species has stopped being your species.
- **Be on the ridge before first light in the goose week.** Ninety seconds a skein, all morning, in air with a third the oxygen, and then the sky is empty again.
- **Sit still for one full hour at the toe of the ice** and listen for the groan. It comes at long intervals and you feel it in the sternum, not the ears.

---

## Coming home

Descend properly on the other side — don't go from 4,900 m to sea level and a busy street in an afternoon if you can avoid it. Sleep for a week. Eat.

And prepare, mildly, for the sensory re-entry. People come back from places like this with a temporary intolerance for noise and colour and clutter — the ordinary chromatic racket of an inhabited world reads as unbearable for a fortnight. It passes. What tends not to pass is the recalibration: the sense, ever afterwards, that the landscapes you grew up calling *ancient* are finished products, worn smooth and soft and settled, and that you've now stood inside one at the stage where the geometry of the deep earth is still showing through — raw, dripping, grinding a mountain into milk in almost total silence, in a light like a north window in November, with nobody there.

Go in the goose week. Stay dry. Come back.
