# Preparing for the Late Plain

## First, understand what you are actually going to

Most people book this trip thinking *desert*. It is not a desert. A desert is a place where water is scarce. This is a place where water has been *absent as a category* for longer than your species has had a backbone, on a surface that is closer to being outside the atmosphere than inside it, under a machine ecology that makes and unmakes architecture faster than you can walk across it.

Treat it as a vacuum excursion with a floor. Everything below follows from that.

Also understand this: nothing you do there will matter, in either direction. You cannot help the plain and you cannot hurt it. What you *can* do is leave something behind that the dust will spend the next geological interval making larger and worse, and that will not hurt the plain either. It will only be true forever. Adjust your ethics accordingly — they will need to operate without consequences to lean on, which most people find is where their ethics were secretly keeping all their weight.

## The body problem

**Pressure.** Nine per cent. Call it nine kilopascals. That is above the boiling point of your blood at body temperature — barely, by about two and a half kilopascals — which is the entire margin between "dangerous place" and "place where the word *place* stops applying." You cannot acclimatize to it. Nobody has ever acclimatized to it. Sherpas do not acclimatize to it. Even breathing pure oxygen at ambient pressure will not do it: after your lungs take their cut for water vapour and carbon dioxide, there is nothing left over to put into your blood. You require a pressure suit. Full stop, no negotiation, no "just for a few minutes at the rim."

**Denitrogenation.** You will be stepping from a one-atmosphere departure lounge to nine kilopascals in the time it takes to walk through a gate. That is a bigger pressure ratio than a spacewalk. If you skip the prebreathe you will get decompression sickness within the hour, on a plain where the nearest recompression chamber is in another universe, and the bends at nineteen kilometres is simply a slow way of dying while conscious and in a great deal of pain and unable to make a noise anyone can hear. **Four hours minimum on pure oxygen before transit, six if you are over forty, eight if you flew or dived in the preceding two days.** Bring the mask. Sit still. Read something.

**Suit selection.** The local standard, and it is the local standard for a reason, is a hybrid: hard shell for torso and helmet, mechanical counterpressure — an elastic skinsuit, laced or shrink-fitted — for the limbs. Two reasons.

1. Mobility. You are going to walk twenty to thirty kilometres a day across abrasive pavement, and a gas-pressurized limb fights you at every joint for the entire trip.
2. Sweat. A counterpressure limb passes water vapour outward. It is damp. It is, in the relevant sense, *breathing*.

That second reason is not comfort. It is the single most important survival specification in the region, and it gets its own section.

**Eyes and skin.** You are above most of the ozone. The sunlight is not filtered, not scattered, not softened. Dual-layer visor with a hard UV cut, and a spare visor in the pack, because the polymer degrades visibly over a two-week crossing and a crazed visor in that light is functionally blindness. Any skin that sees sun through a gap will burn in ten minutes and keep burning.

## The respiration mark

The dust will copy a glove and not a hand. It has never been observed to model anything that respires.

Nobody knows what it is reading. The leading guesses are humidity, carbon dioxide gradient, thermal irregularity, or some combination that nobody has been able to falsify, mostly because the experiments that would falsify it are the experiments where you find out by dying. What everyone agrees on is the operational rule:

**Make sure the plain can tell you are alive.**

A fully sealed, perfectly dry, hard-shell suit is a *thing*. It is an artefact, an object, and it is exactly the sort of object the dust finds interesting: complex, articulated, novel. Veterans will tell you stories about early crossings in rigid suits and what the people inside them woke up inside of. The stories vary in their details and they are all the same story.

So:

- **Damp limbs.** The counterpressure skinsuit passes vapour. Keep it passing. Do not over-dry your loop.
- **Exhaust routing.** Vent your carbon dioxide over the outer shell — most commercial rigs have a shoulder manifold that runs the exhaust in a thin film across the back and the crown of the helmet. It looks silly. It is not decorative.
- **Keep moving.** Whatever the dust is reading, it reads a moving thermal source as alive more reliably than a still one. A person standing perfectly still for forty minutes at the edge of an active field is, by local reckoning, praying.
- **The wrist window.** Some old hands carry a palm-sized patch of bare skin at the inner wrist, under a cuff they can open for thirty seconds. It is a terrible idea — that skin swells, splits, and never fully heals — and about a third of long-haul guides have one. Do not copy them. Do note that they think the risk is worth it, and ask yourself what they have seen.

## Water

There is none. Not scarce. None. Not in the rock, not in the dust, not in the air, not under the pavement, not at any depth anyone has bothered to drill, in any direction, forever.

You will carry every gram you drink, and you will lose water at a rate that will frighten you, because at nine kilopascals of bone-dry air, every breath you exhale into a partially open loop is a small permanent donation to a world that will not even accept it as frost. **Closed-loop recovery is mandatory.** Budget four litres a day for the loop's losses, not for your thirst — your thirst is a rounding error next to your respiratory losses, and it will lie to you besides, because in that dryness you do not feel thirsty until you are already in trouble. Drink on a clock. Set the clock to something audible. Do not trust your body to notice.

The frost in the shadows is real, it appears for roughly one hour before dawn, and it sublimates rather than melts. You can harvest it with a cold plate and a scraper. You will get perhaps a hundred millilitres for ninety minutes of miserable, freezing, pre-dawn work. Do it once so that you understand what the cushion plants are doing out there, and then stop taking it from them.

## Heat, cold, and the absence of anything in between

Your sunward side will be in a warm bath and your shadowed side will be radiating into a sky the colour of a bruise. In an atmosphere this thin, convection barely mediates; radiation runs the entire thermal economy. The result is that you can develop a burn and a cold injury on the same limb within the same hour.

- **Rotate.** Every twenty minutes, present a different face to the sun. Make it a discipline. Guides call it *turning the roast* and they are not joking, exactly.
- **Emissivity control.** Suits sold for this run come with reversible outer panels — low-emissivity silver, high-emissivity black. You will be flipping panels several times a day. Practise until you can do it without looking.
- **Night.** The nights are the real hazard. A camp pod at nine kilopascals is essentially a thermos and holds heat well, but the wind still strips it and the sky is a near-perfect heat sink. Bring redundant heating. Bring a second, independent, chemical heat source that requires no power at all. The plain has killed more people by patient overnight cooling than by any of its interesting features.

## Electricity

This is the part travellers underprepare for, because it does not look like a hazard until it happens.

At this pressure, air is a much worse insulator than you are used to. Gaps arc that would never arc at home. Your suit will accumulate triboelectric charge continuously from the dust hissing past it — dust which is, remember, made of grains that *latch by charge*. On a cool night you will see the corona standing over the assembly fields in silent lilac sheets, and sometimes you will see it standing over *you*, off your antenna, off your helmet crown, a soft violet fire with no sound and no heat.

- **Bond everything.** Every metal component of your rig on a common ground. Drag a bleed strap.
- **Pot your electronics.** Anything with exposed high-voltage tracks will fail. Buy local, or buy rated for low-pressure operation, and carry paper backups of your navigation data.
- **The ionizer wand is your most important tool after the suit.** The dust lets go the instant charge redistributes. A wand that redistributes charge is therefore a tool that makes dust let go of you — of your visor, your knees, your seal grooves, your ration pack. Carry two. Test them daily. If yours fails and you get caught in a settling field, you will find out what it is like to be slowly mittened.
- **No radio during active corona over your position.** You will not be heard anyway.

## Sound, and the fact that there isn't any

You cannot shout for help. You cannot hear a companion scream. At nine kilopascals the air will not carry a human voice past a few metres, and what does arrive arrives flat and close and wrong, like speech in a padded room.

So:

- **Radio, with hand-signal fallback, with tether fallback.** Three layers. Practise the hand signals until they are reflex, in the suit, with gloves on, in bad light.
- **Tether in active fields.** Not for arrest — for *communication*. Two tugs, three tugs. It is medieval and it works.
- **The hiss.** The grain-flow makes a continuous papery *shhhh* at ankle height. You will stop hearing it after about an hour. Around day four you will start hearing it again and you will never stop. Everybody goes through this. Earplugs make it worse, because then you hear your own blood instead. The working solution is a metronome — most guides run a soft tick in the helmet at rest pulse. It gives your auditory system something with a structure to hold onto. Bring one. Bring the spare battery.

## Navigation

A flat plain with no haze plays two tricks on you and they compound.

**Trick one: everything is closer than it is.** With no atmospheric scattering to soften distance, a hundred-and-eighty-metre tower five kilometres away presents to your eye as a fifteen-metre object at eight hundred metres. Travellers set off to "have a quick look" and walk until nightfall. Range-find everything. Never trust your eye. Never trust your eye *after* you have been told not to trust your eye, either, which is the part people get wrong.

**Trick two: there is nothing to navigate by.** No slope you can feel — eleven kilometres before gradient becomes a factor. No magnetic reference that means anything. No landmarks except the markers, and the markers are not aligned to any compass; they are aligned only to each other, in a network that is not a grid and not a line and does not, to anyone's satisfaction, resolve into a shape.

Carry all three of:
- **Inertial**, as your primary. Modern units drift acceptably over a two-week crossing.
- **Celestial**, as your check. The horizon is hard and visibly curved and there is no haze — it is the best sextant horizon in the multiverse, and the skill is worth learning for that reason alone.
- **The marker almanac and a bearing plate.** Each marker sights to between three and five others. From any marker, two bearings give you a fix. The almanac is cheap, it has not needed updating in living memory, and it has saved more lives than the pressure suits.

## Camp discipline, or: leave carefully

The dust copies what it touches, and each copy is larger than the last, and the copies march downwind in a diminishing series of faithful ruins, and the whole thing recurs for as long as the wind blows, which is forever.

This is not a mystical prohibition. It is litter management on a world with a broken printer.

**Absolute rules, and they are enforced by every guild and caravan on the rim:**

1. **Pack out everything.** Every wrapper, every filter, every broken buckle. Anything left becomes a monument. A dropped cup becomes a colonnade of cups by dusk and a range of cup-shaped hills by the next year.
2. **Never leave a mechanism.** Hinges, latches, anything with articulating parts. The dust is very bad at mechanisms and very persistent about them, and a half-copied hinge the size of a house, collapsing and being recopied for ten thousand years, is a thing certain unlucky people have had to walk past.
3. **Never leave a shape with a hole in it.** See the doorway on the western third. Forty kilometres of dun-powder lintels, each generation further from a memory of a door that was not from this world. That was one artefact. One.
4. **Sleep contained.** Sleep in the pod, on the pod's floor, not on the pavement. Sleeping on your side on open ground will get you five grey copies of your own body by morning, each larger, all of them coming apart. People have woken to this and not been right afterwards.
5. **Drag your camp out.** When you break camp, rake the site — a light bar dragged across the pavement, twenty seconds, redistributing charge and surface dust. It disrupts the templates. It costs nothing.
6. **Walk abreast, not in file, when the ground allows.** A single-file trail is a tread pattern repeated a thousand times, which is precisely the sort of periodic thing the dust loves, and there are places on the plain that are now low ranges of footprint hills because a survey party in the old days walked nose-to-tail for four hundred kilometres.

**And the rule under the rules.** The dust does not copy what respires. It follows — and every crossing party carries this knowledge like a stone in the boot — that it *does* copy what has stopped. If a member of your party dies on the plain, you have somewhere between four and eight hours. Everyone who crosses signs a carry-out clause before departure, and everyone means it when they sign it, and you should read it before you sign rather than after, and you should know that the clause exists because of what happened the times it didn't. Carry the body out, or burn it in a slag ring, or get it below the escarpment. Do not leave it. Do not leave it *for a few hours while you go for help*. There is no help, and by the time you return there will be something four metres long lying in your friend's posture with your friend's face rendered in grey powder, and behind it another, and behind that another, going downwind into the shimmer.

## The specific advice for travellers like you

You are coming by gate. This makes you a unique hazard on the Late Plain and you must understand why.

**Do not open a transit aperture in the open air.**

The dust copies what it touches. It has spent a geological interval making progressively worse copies of a doorway that somebody once left standing on the western third, and the consequence is only forty kilometres of harmless powder ruins, because that door was inert stone.

Nobody knows what happens if the dust gets a good long look at an aperture that *works*. The prevailing view among rim authorities is that it is almost certainly incapable of reproducing the function, and that the "almost" is doing an unacceptable amount of labour in that sentence, and that the correct number of times to run the experiment is zero.

**Standard practice:**
- Open below the escarpment, in the lee, under rock, and walk up. It costs you two days.
- Or open inside a shielded transit tent: a sealed, grounded, dust-excluded volume, which you strike and rake within the hour.
- Never at a marker. Never within sight of the doorway. Never, for any reason, at the listening station.

And on the listening station: put your palm on it if you like. Feel the cycle — one every eleven seconds, patient, older than everything. Then take your hand off and go away. It is still working. Its aperture tracks something. It does not answer, it is not for you, and the single most reliable way to become a cautionary anecdote on the rim is to be the visitor from elsewhere who decided that *it is not for you* was a challenge.

## Going home

**Quarantine is not a formality and the port will not let you skip it.**

You will be carrying grains. They will be in your seal grooves, in the weave of your outer layer, inside your pack's stitching, in the hinge of your visor. They are dormant makers with an eroded mandate and no remaining sense of *what*, only of *make*, and they work dry, and they need nothing from you.

Expect a full seam-by-seam ionization strip, expect to surrender your outer shell, expect to be in the wash cycle for six hours, and expect to be asked, under oath at some ports, whether you left anything on the plain. Answer honestly. They are not asking about litter. They are asking whether there is now something out there being slowly enlarged, generation after generation, in the shape of a thought you had.

## What to prepare that is not equipment

Two weeks on the Late Plain does something to people, and it is not the obvious thing. It is not loneliness and it is not fear. It is this:

You will spend fourteen days watching an entire civilization's worth of architecture rise and fall, hundreds of events an hour, cathedrals and lung-trees and fluted towers with stairs on their outsides, in perfect silence, on a floor that has not moved since before there were eyes — and none of it will mean anything, and none of it will be remembered, including by the thing that made it, and it will not stop, and it has never stopped, and it will not stop after you leave.

Some people find this unbearable. Some people find it the most peaceful thing they have ever seen. A significant minority find both, in alternation, several times a day, and those are the ones who come back.

The preparation for that is not a piece of gear. It is deciding, in advance, before you go, what you think a thing has to do in order to have been worth doing. Take a clear answer with you. You will get it tested every eleven minutes, silently, at a scale of a square kilometre, for as long as you stay.

And bring the metronome. Genuinely. Bring the metronome.
