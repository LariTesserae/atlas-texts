# Preparing for the Rise
### A practical handbook for one ordinary human, one visit

---

## First, the honest summary

This is not a hostile world. Hostility implies attention. The Rise has none to give you. It is a place that has not yet invented anything that could notice a body — not weather, not water, not decay, not life. You are the only thing on it that is wet, soft, and finished.

Everything in your preparation follows from four facts, in this order of lethality:

1. **The air is not enough.** You will be inside a sealed garment the whole time, and that garment is your only body.
2. **Everything is sharp and nothing has been blunted.** The first fact and the second fact are in direct conflict. This is the central problem of the Rise.
3. **The light comes from underneath**, which means your eyes will lie to you about depth, about distance, and about which black surfaces are floor and which are hole.
4. **The ground is growing.** Your route out is not your route in.

Everything else — the cairns, the crusts, the eleven-minute breath — is what you'll actually remember. But it isn't what kills people.

---

## Before you leave: the body

**Prebreathe.** Your suit will run low, somewhere between 25 and 40 kPa, because a higher-pressure suit is a stiff suit and the Rise demands fine footwork. That pressure differential from a sea-level origin means decompression sickness. Budget two to three hours breathing pure oxygen before transit, sitting still, bored, resenting it. Do not shortcut it. There is nowhere on the Rise to be bent.

**Get your ankles and your eccentric strength up.** You will spend the entire visit walking on interlocking plates that grate and tip. Nothing is loose enough to skate on and nothing is solid enough to trust. The muscle you need is the one that catches a rolling ankle, repeatedly, for eight hours, while carrying life support. Train on talus, at night, with a pack. If you can find a scree slope of freshly broken glass, use that; you won't, but that's what it's like.

**Cut your nails. Trim everything. Tape your rings.** Anything that can snag inside a suit becomes a hotspot, and a hotspot becomes a blister, and a blister at twenty kilometers from the door becomes the reason the trip ends badly.

**Antiemetics, and test them at home first.** The plateau's swell is subsonic, roughly an eleven-minute period, and it arrives through your feet and pelvis rather than your ears. Some people feel nothing. Some people feel a hand pressing on the diaphragm and then a slow, seasick tilt in a world that isn't tilting. You will not know which you are until you're standing on it. Carry the pills where a gloved hand can reach them through the suit's feed port.

**Dental check.** Air trapped under a bad filling at 5 kPa does unkind things.

## The suit

Assume a full-pressure suit. Assume you will never open it.

**The outer layer is the whole argument.** Standard orbital-grade softgoods will not survive the Rise for one day. There is no dust here to soften a landing and no rounded stone to brush against — every single contact you make with the world is with a fresh conchoidal edge that has never been touched by water, wind, or time. You need an abrasion-and-cut overshell: aramid weave over a mail or scale layer at knees, shins, forearms, elbows, hips, and the outside of both boots. It will be heavy. Take the weight.

**Gauntlets:** three layers, and expect to retire a pair per excursion. Assume any handhold you grab will cut the outermost layer. The discipline is not *avoid cutting the gloves* — that's impossible — it's *always know which layer you're on*. Check them at every scheduled stop. Log the damage. Two layers gone on one finger means you go home now, cheerfully, without argument.

**Boots** are the specialist item. Soles must be rated for continuous contact at 80–120 °C and brief contact well above that; ceramic-tile or ablative-plate construction, full-length rigid shank, and a toe rand that you accept as sacrificial. The ground is warm everywhere and hot in places you will not predict, and the heat comes up through the soles and *stays*. Bring a second set of insoles, sealed inside the suit, to swap by feel if your feet start cooking — and know in advance that you probably won't manage it.

**Thermal management is inverted from everything you have trained for.** The air is cold and getting colder with altitude; the floor is warm and sometimes much worse. You will be simultaneously shedding heat from your shoulders and absorbing it through your feet. Suits designed for vacuum work assume a uniform sink. This one needs a split loop: insulate the upper body, actively cool the lower. If you only have a uniform system, tune it warm and manage your feet by never standing still on a hot plate for more than ninety seconds. Learn to shift weight like a wading bird.

**Never kneel. Never sit on the ground. Never crawl.** Carry a small folding stool with insulated feet. It will feel absurd in your pack. It will be, by hour six, the best thing you own.

## Breathing and the chemistry

Scrubbers rated for **sulfur dioxide and hydrogen chloride**, in addition to the usual CO₂. The Rise's vents are dry, and dryness is a mercy right up until the gas meets the inside of a human being, where there is plenty of water to make acid out of. A suit breach near a vent is not a slow hypoxic fade; it's a chemical burn to the eyes and the upper airway, immediate and disabling.

**Carry a gas monitor with an audible and haptic alarm**, external sensor at chest height and a second at ankle height. The heavy gases pool. A hollow in the floor can hold a lethal lake of CO₂ and SO₂ that is completely invisible — no shimmer, no smell reaching you, no billow, because there's nothing to billow into. The vent columns you *can* see are the polite ones.

**The oxygen paradox.** There is almost no free oxygen on the Rise, so nothing burns — except that you are carrying a tank of it and walking on rock that is, in places, at six hundred degrees. A high-pressure oxygen leak onto hot basalt is the one true fire hazard on a world without fire. Route your lines inboard. Inspect fittings before every excursion. If you smell anything at all inside the suit, you are already in a situation.

## Seeing

**Your eyes will be wrong here, and you must plan around that rather than hope to adapt.**

All light comes from below. Every face that is normally lit is dark, every overhang is bright, every shadow points up. Your visual system reads shape-from-shading with a hardwired assumption of light-from-above, and it will invert bumps into pits and pits into bumps with total confidence. A raised lip of glass will read as a groove. A groove will read as a step. You will place a foot wrong not because you were careless but because you were looking carefully.

**Bring a thermal imager and make it your primary navigation sense.** This is the single most important item after the suit. In thermal, the Rise is legible: hot cracks blaze, fresh black skin over a crack glows warm, cool stable slabs read dark. The killer here is the crack that has skinned over — a sheet of new glass a few millimetres thick, black on black, lying across a metre-wide fissure that is at a thousand degrees underneath. It is invisible to the eye. It is obvious in IR. Run the imager as a heads-up overlay, and when the overlay fails, stop walking.

**Carry a probe.** A tungsten-tipped pole, 1.5 m, used ahead of every step onto anything you did not personally see form. Tap-tap-step. It feels ridiculous on open glass pavement. Do it anyway; the pavement is where the skinned cracks are.

**Bring a laser rangefinder and use it constantly**, not because you need the numbers but because you need to recalibrate. With no haze, your distance estimates will be off by factors of two to five, and they will be *confidently* off. A ridge you judge at four kilometers is at forty. A cairn you judge at fifty meters is at three hundred. People have walked themselves into an unrecoverable oxygen deficit chasing a stack of slabs that looked like a ten-minute detour. Range everything before you move toward it. Say the number out loud.

**Your own lighting should be low-mounted and warm.** A bright white helmet lamp will destroy the glow-network entirely and leave you inside a small bubble of ordinary geology, blind to the diagram. Use a dim, warm, chest-mounted light for footwork and keep the helmet lamp for emergencies only. The Rise is legible by its own light if you let your eyes stay dark-adapted, and the diagram of cracks is the only map the world offers.

## Navigation

**Inertial first, celestial never.** There are no stars. There is no sun. There is no magnetic field worth trusting over a shallow magma body. Bring a good IMU, expect drift, and correct it against ranged fixes on major terrain.

**Terrain features expire.** The plateau is inflating; terraces tilt, fissures open across your outbound track, talus reorganizes. Do not plan a return along a remembered route. Plan a return along a *bearing corridor*, with fallbacks, and range-check it outbound: every thousand paces, turn around and photograph the way back as it will appear from that side. You will be astonished at how little you recognize it.

**You leave no tracks.** There is no dust, no soil, no sand — nothing takes a print. This is a strange, specific loneliness the first time you look behind you and find that the world has not registered your passage at all.

**Retro-reflective markers, tethered and numbered, and you pick up every single one on the way back.** Do not use cairns as markers. Ever. See below.

**Mark your entry point with an active transponder on a wide tripod**, not with a stack of stones, and place it on the largest, flattest, most boring slab you can find, well away from any glowing crack — then check its position against your IMU each time you pass, because the ground beneath it is rising and may be rising unevenly.

## Sound, and how to get it back

Airborne sound barely works. What you hear as "the plateau ticking" is the romantic account; through a sealed helmet at 5 kPa you will hear essentially nothing but your own fans and your own pulse, and that silence is one of the harder things about the place.

**Bring geophones.** A contact microphone strapped to a boot, or a small spike geophone you can set on a slab, will give you the Rise's actual voice: the tick of contracting glass, the knocks of accommodation, the long groan of a terrace deciding. This is not a luxury. It is a safety instrument — the sound of a crack propagating toward you arrives through the rock long before it arrives anywhere else — and it is also, frankly, the reason to come.

**Use the rock as backup comms.** If radio fails between partners in broken ground, a simple tap code through a boot into the slab carries surprisingly far. Agree on the code before you go. Three taps, pause, three taps: *I am fine, I am coming to you.*

## Water, and the thing you must not do

The Rise has no water. Not "little" — none. There is no landform anywhere on it that a fluid shaped. This is not an inconvenience to you (you carry your own); it is a fact about the world that makes you dangerous.

The crusts around the vents are chemistry a few hundred million years short of becoming biology. They are running the same arithmetic that life runs, in a dry medium, slowly. You are a warm sack of liquid water, dissolved organics, salts, and about forty trillion bacteria, standing in the middle of a prebiotic experiment.

**Therefore:**

- **Nothing vents.** No urine dump, no sublimator plume, no condensate purge, no exhaled anything. Closed loop, full stop. If your suit design cannot avoid venting water vapor, do not take that suit here.
- **Nothing spills.** Double-contain every fluid. A burst hydration bladder at a vent field is not a minor mishap; it is an uncontrolled introduction of the one reagent this world is missing, at the exact site where the chemistry is most poised.
- **Nothing stays.** Everything you carry in, you carry out, including broken gear, shed glove layers, and the three centimetres of outer shell that a slab took off your shin. Sweep your stop points.
- **You do not bleed on the Rise.** Which is another way of saying: do not get cut. Which is the whole of the safety brief.

I am aware this sounds like ceremony. It isn't. On a dead world, contamination is vandalism. On this one it may be creation, and you do not get to decide that for a planet on a day trip.

## Sampling

Take glass and basalt if you must: tongs, titanium tins, thermal soak time before sealing, and expect the sample to shatter a plastic container simply by being hot and having edges. This part is ordinary.

**The crusts are not samples.** Understand this before you get there, so you don't waste time being disappointed. A frond of native sulfur and metal chlorides growing off a vent lip is not an object; it is a *process* in a thermal and chemical gradient. Break it off and you have a handful of grey flaking powder and a lie. Whatever made it interesting was the difference in temperature across two centimetres of its own length, and you cannot bag that.

So: photograph them. Time-lapse them — you can watch one add a millimetre in the span of a single eleven-minute swell, and that footage is worth more than any specimen. Bring a small rig you can leave for an hour and retrieve. Measure the gradient. Sniff the vent with the gas train. Record the geometry precisely enough that someone else can model the growth.

And then leave them alone, because one of them competing with another one, smothering it, is the most nearly-alive thing in that entire world, and you are a tourist.

## The cairns: rules of engagement

You will find one every hour or so of walking. Waist-high, three to nine slabs, chosen for flatness, set square, largest at the base. Unmodified stone. No tool marks anywhere on the plateau, because there has never been a tool on the plateau.

**Do not dismantle one. Do not rearrange one. Do not remove a slab.**

**Do not take a slab from one cairn to another, even to "restore" it.**

**Do not build anything.**

That last one is the hard one, and I want to be direct about it: the urge to stack slabs on the Rise is close to universal. It arrives around hour four, usually after a long stretch of quiet walking, and it does not feel like an urge — it feels like a perfectly reasonable idea you happen to be having. You have flat stones, you have hands, you have a landscape that keeps almost being architecture. Everyone gets it. Some people have not resisted it, and that is exactly why nobody can now say anything reliable about who built what.

The stratigraphy is the only honest evidence: vent frost lies *over* the top slab of some cairns and *under* the base slab of others, which means the building spans the plateau's whole short life and is presumably still going on, somewhere past the cracks you can see. The structures are never near each other. Whatever does this keeps its distance from itself — and, so far, from us.

**If you see a structure being built, or see anything at all that is moving under its own arrangement:** do not approach, do not close the distance, do not attempt contact. Record from where you stand, range it, log the position, and continue your planned route. You have a finite oxygen budget and no mandate. The correct behavior on the Rise is the behavior the Rise itself models: keep your distance from yourself.

**And decide before you go what you think the cairns are.** Not because your conclusion matters, but because if you arrive without one you will form it on the ground, at hour six, alone, in the dark, with the swell coming up through your feet — and whatever you decide under those conditions you will believe for the rest of your life.

## The schedule

**Go short. Go twice, rather than long once.** Six to eight hours on the surface. No overnight camps — there is no night to camp through, no dawn to wait for, and sleeping on a substrate that cracks is an act of faith rather than a rest.

**Bring a loud, insistent, stupid clock.** Something that chimes every fifteen minutes whether you want it to or not. The Rise has no diurnal cue, no weathering to read age from, no wind to mark change, and a breathing rhythm of eleven minutes that your body will quietly adopt as its metronome. Time dissociation here is fast and complete; people have reported an hour of walking that instruments record as four. The chime is not a convenience. It is the only clock in the universe that belongs to you.

**Plan turnaround on consumables, not on objectives**, and set it at 40% remaining, not 50%. The return leg on the Rise is reliably slower than the outbound: the terrain has changed, you are tired, your gloves are worse, and your feet are hot.

## What it will do to you

Nobody comes back from the Rise talking about the hazards. They talk about three things.

**The quiet.** Not the silence — the *quiet*, which is different: a world full of activity, all of it inaudible. Terraces tilting three degrees with the sound of a distant door. A county-sized magma chamber inflating without comment. You will develop the persistent sense that something enormous is happening behind you.

**The uplighting.** Standing on a high terrace with the matte black overhead and the whole plateau drawn beneath you in orange lines, the world reads as a diagram of itself — a schematic hanging in nothing, describing a surface you infer rather than see. It is the most beautiful thing many travelers ever see, and it is beautiful in a way that does not include you and never will.

**The rehearsal.** After some hours you will stop being able to unsee it: the colonnades of columnar joints, the fissure roofed into a room, the terraces stepping with a regularity that keeps failing to be quite regular, the mineral fronds arranged in gardens, the crusts competing at the vents. Gardens, buildings, organisms — all of them at low resolution, in stone, meaning none of it yet. The Rise is a world doing sketches. You have arrived a few hundred million years before the deadline.

Most people find this exhilarating. Some find it unbearable, and the ones who find it unbearable usually can't say why afterward. If you are the kind of person for whom *meaninglessness* is a live and personal problem rather than an abstract one, go with a partner and tell them in advance.

## Compact checklist

**Wear:** full-pressure suit, low-pressure rating; cut-resistant overshell with mail at knees/shins/forearms/hips; triple gauntlets ×3 sets; high-temp rigid-shank boots with sacrificial rand; split thermal loop, cool below, insulate above.

**Breathe:** CO₂ + SO₂ + HCl scrubbers; closed-loop, non-venting; dual gas monitors, chest and ankle; inboard O₂ routing; prebreathe before transit.

**See:** thermal imager (primary), dim warm chest light, helmet lamp (emergency only), laser rangefinder, tungsten probe pole.

**Navigate:** IMU with drift log, numbered retro markers (all recovered), entry transponder on a wide tripod, bearing-corridor return plan with fallbacks, back-facing photographs every thousand paces.

**Hear:** boot-contact geophone, agreed tap code, chiming clock.

**Carry:** insulated folding stool, spare insoles, glove-layer log, patch kit rated for glass punctures, irrigation fluid and acid-neutralizing eyewash, antiemetics reachable through the feed port, titanium sample tins and tongs, time-lapse rig.

**Do not carry out:** any crust. **Do not carry in:** any exposed water. **Do not leave:** anything at all.

**Do not build.**

---

One last thing. When you are standing on the highest terrace, with the black overhead and the orange diagram running clean to a horizon with no softening on it, and the swell coming up through your boots, and a stack of nine slabs three hundred meters off — ranged, logged, patient, unexplained, and slightly warm —

you will want to walk over and put a tenth slab on top. Just to see.

That's the whole preparation, really. Everything else is gear.
