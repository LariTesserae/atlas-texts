# Field Preparation: *The Fen That Finished Thinking*

## Notes for an ordinary human traveler

---

## 0. The three sentences

Before anything else, learn these well enough to recite them while cold, wet, and frightened:

1. **Sound is the only channel that runs forward.** Everything else here — light, shape, distance, your recent past, your sense of being one continuous person — is subject to revision. If you want something to persist, put it in sound.
2. **The valley does not attack. It files.** There is no predator, no malice, no trap. There is a very old, very patient sorting habit that takes whatever arrives and finds a place for it in the mud. You are something that has arrived.
3. **You are the only manufactured object for thirty kilometers.** Nothing here has a seam. Everything you carry has thousands. Plan accordingly.

---

## 1. Whether to go at all

Some people should not.

**Hard exclusions.** Tinnitus, hyperacusis, Ménière's, prior acoustic trauma. Photosensitive or vestibular epilepsy — the ground-tone sits in the infrasound band that produces nausea, chest pressure, and free-floating dread in ordinary nervous systems, and does worse in unusual ones. Any dissociative condition. Any recent head injury.

**Soft exclusions, more important than the hard ones.** Do not go while grieving. Do not go within a year of a death that you are still arguing with. The Reprise includes, in every iteration, one or two sounds you did not make and did not hear. Over a long enough stay it will eventually include something you have been wanting to hear. It is not doing this for you; it is a badly-worn recording of a valley that has heard a great many things. But you will not experience it that way at two in the morning with your face in wet brass-colored moss. The fen's most reliable casualty is not the one who drowns. It is the one who decides to stay another day, and then another, because the playback is getting better.

Also: get your teeth checked. The chord lives in the back molars. Loose crowns, cracked fillings, and recent root work will ring for the entire visit and can become genuinely disabling by hour thirty.

---

## 2. The exposure budget

Vertebrates that wander down from the hills either leave within a day, or are not seen again *in a way that can be described*. Take that sentence literally; it is the most carefully phrased warning in the literature.

- **First visit: twelve hours, rim-adjacent, first terrace only.**
- **Experienced, with a partner and a narrator discipline: thirty-six hours maximum.**
- **Absolute ceiling, for anyone, ever: sixty hours.** Past this the corrections in the Reprise stop being funny.

The valley is thirty kilometers across the waist and you will make one to two kilometers an hour on good tussock and two hundred meters an hour in alder carr. Do the arithmetic before you fantasize about the fifth terrace and the downward-going lights. **Nobody needs to see the bottom of the sloughs.** The people who went to look are the reason we have a sixty-hour ceiling and no one who can explain it.

---

## 3. Entry and exit doctrine

**Never anchor a return point inside the basin.** This is non-negotiable and has cost people their way home.

Your gate, beacon, anchor stone, sigil, keyed device — whatever your crossing apparatus is — is an *artifact*, and artifacts set down in the fen are found again later, elsewhere, translated into grains of clay holding the object's shape to the micron and functioning not at all. It will still look like your anchor. It will still have the scratch on the housing. It will be mud.

**Site the crossing on the hill rim, above the vapor line, outside the seep.** Walk in. Walk out. Accept the extra kilometers as the price of having a door.

If your method requires the anchor to be on your person, tether it to your body with a doubled cord, keep skin contact, and *touch it consciously at least once an hour*. Attention appears to count as possession. Things you are actively holding are not yet "set down."

**Site your exit east of your entry.** Distance here is conserved approximately and drifts eastward; each out-and-back traverse returns five to ten percent short, so the valley quietly moves you east of where you insist you are. Over two days of working a terrace this compounds into a kilometer or more of confident error. Leaving a generous eastern margin turns that error into a tailwind instead of a search.

---

## 4. Clothing and the wet

You will be wearing the valley within a hundred paces. There is no staying dry. The entire clothing strategy is therefore **"wet but warm,"** not "waterproof."

- **No cotton anywhere on your body.** Wool or synthetic pile, next to skin and as mid-layer. Wool keeps working saturated; it is also a material the valley recognizes, which matters (see §6).
- A **breathable hardshell** is nearly useless against condensation but very useful against the rain-pulses, which arrive four or five times between one tide-breath and the next and hit like a thrown bucket. Take it. Expect to be wet under it.
- **Vapor-barrier socks** over a thin liner, under a thick wool sock. This is the single highest-value item in your kit.
- **Chest waders are a trap.** A bog is not a river. If you go through a quaking mat in waders you will invert. Wear boots you can swim out of, and accept wet legs.
- **Brimmed hat, not hood.** Hoods amputate your hearing, and hearing is your clock, your map, and your continuity.
- Bring **twice the dry sleepwear you think you need**, sealed in doubled dry bags, and treat it as sacred. It is not clothing. It is your only heat.

---

## 5. Thermal plan

The cold stones hold the basin six degrees below what the latitude wants. Combine that with total saturation, continuous evaporation, and a man standing still in sphagnum at ankle depth, and you have the classic profile for the least exotic death available here: **hypothermia at eight to twelve degrees Celsius**, which kills more travelers across the multiverse than ice ever has.

- **Never stand still for more than about a minute.** You will sink to the ankle with that slow swallowing sound, and the peat will start drawing heat out of your feet at a rate you will not notice until your hands stop working. Move, shift, step, rock.
- **Never sit or lean against a cold stone.** They are painfully below air temperature, permanently. A minute of contact through wet wool costs more core heat than an hour of walking gains. Approach them, look, keep your distance at arm's length, and note the fern-crowded pool at the base: that pool is meltwater-cold and much deeper than it looks.
- Eat far more than feels reasonable. **Six thousand calories a day**, heavily fatted, in small constant amounts. Cold-wet burns like labor.
- Carry a **chemical-heat rewarming kit and an emergency bivy**, and practice deploying both with numb hands before you leave home.

---

## 6. Kit doctrine

Four words: **tethered, redundant, expendable, boring.**

**Tethered.** Everything on a cord to your harness. Everything. The knife, the light, the bottle, the whistle, the spare light. Objects set down are not lost in the ordinary way; they are recycled into the ground's vocabulary, and no search will recover them as objects.

**Redundant.** Assume attrition. Three light sources. Two whistles. Two knives.

**Expendable.** Bring nothing you love. Bring no heirlooms, no wedding ring, no photograph, no gift from a person who is gone. There are travelers who go specifically to leave something — who unwrap a small object on the third terrace and set it down and walk away, and find comfort in the idea that its shape is now spelled out in clay somewhere for a very long time. That is a legitimate reason to come. Just do it deliberately, once, knowing what it costs, and not by dropping a glove.

**Boring.** Prefer materials the valley already knows how to spell: wool, leather, horn, wood, bone, waxed canvas. Machined aluminum, circuit boards, welded seams, and right angles are conspicuous here in a way that is difficult to justify but consistently reported. Gear that looks like it grew is gear that is left alone longer.

**Packing list, condensed:**

- Probing pole, 1.8 m, wood, with a wide foot. Your third leg and your depth gauge. Use it before every step onto unknown lawn.
- Bog shoes or short snowshoes for sphagnum crossings.
- 20 m floating line + a lightweight throw bag. Bog extraction is a rope problem.
- Filtered earplugs (–15 dB musician's type). **Never full occlusion.**
- Personal gas monitor (CH₄/CO₂/O₂). Peat hollows hold pockets. Do not lie down in a dip.
- Stove and fuel for the entire trip. Nothing here will burn. Do not attempt to dry and burn peat — see §12.
- Water filter, plus boiling capacity. Fen water is tannin-brown, cold, and biologically quieter than you'd expect, but not safe.
- Indelible skin marker. Write your name, your date of entry, your exit bearing, and your partner's name on your forearm every morning. Ink on skin is worn, not carried; it stays.
- A small brass bell or a wooden clapper. Not for signaling. See §10.
- Foot care kit: three spare sock sets, antifungal powder, tape, and the discipline to use them.
- Lozenges and a litre of extra water per day for your voice.

---

## 7. Feet

Immersion foot will end your trip and can end your feet. Every six hours, without exception: find the driest tussock available, sit on your pack, strip both boots, dry each foot completely, powder, inspect, fresh liner, replace. **Twelve minutes. Set it by the Reprise if you have to.** People skip this on day two because they are tired and the whole place is wet anyway. Those are the people who come home in a chair.

---

## 8. Hearing, and the chord

The fen is loud. Everyone is surprised. Ten thousand seeps, a hundred terrace falls, sheet-drainage hissing through sedge, and hammering rain-pulses, over a continuous low chord you feel in the sternum.

- Wear **filtered** plugs from the moment you descend, and remove them only deliberately.
- Expect **infrasound symptoms** by hour four: mild nausea, chest heaviness, a persistent sense that someone is standing just behind you and to the left. This is physiology, not the valley. Bring ginger or your antiemetic of choice and say the symptom out loud when it starts, so your partner can confirm they have it too. Naming it defuses most of it.
- **Do not linger between three or more cold stones.** The overtone stacking in a stone cluster is a genuinely beautiful thing — a struck bell still ringing from a strike long ago — and it produces measurable disorientation within about twenty minutes. Visit. Listen. Leave. Do not camp.
- Hearing protection has a second function: the Reprise arrives twelve to fifteen decibels above the original. Your own cough, at fifteen up, from all directions, in the dark, is startling enough to put an unprepared person into the water.

---

## 9. Navigation

Everything visual lies here, quietly and consistently.

- **Light is spent.** It goes down and does not come back. Your headlamp will have perhaps a third of the throw you expect; distant things are not dim, they are *absent*.
- **Shadows lag about a third of a second**, which corrupts your depth perception exactly when you are judging a step across black water. Do not trust your eyes for the edge of a slough. Probe it.
- **Sloughs are mirrors from above and voids from the side.** They reproduce the sky with total fidelity. More than one person has walked into what they read as a hole in the ground with clouds behind it, and more than one has walked into what they read as sky.
- **Footprints fill instantly.** You cannot backtrack by sign.
- **Dead reckoning drifts east**, five to ten percent per traverse, cumulative and undetectable from inside.

So: **navigate acoustically.**

The ground-tone is not uniform. It is quietest at the centers of the migrating sedge rings and it acquires characteristic overtones near stone clusters. Experienced parties build a rough acoustic map: they learn three or four stone-clusters by their chord colour and can place themselves by the intervals between them, the way you might place yourself by three church bells in a valley at home.

Practically, for a first visit:

- Work in **out-and-back legs of no more than ninety minutes**, always along a terrace riser, which is a continuous audible feature — the risers hiss and fall constantly and you can follow one by ear in zero visibility.
- Deploy a **sound line**: at each turn, your partner stays put and rings the clapper at a fixed interval while you advance to the limit of clear hearing, then you swap. Slow. Boring. It works, and it is immune to the drift, because it never asks you to believe a distance.
- Keep the **wall of hills on your peripheral awareness** even though they "read as suggestion rather than boundary." The dimming direction is the best compass you'll get.

---

## 10. Time

Your watch will keep running and will tell you nothing about the valley. Keep it anyway; you need it for your own intervals.

The valley's one honest clock is **the tide**: roughly twice a day, without moon, without sea, the water rises eleven centimeters and falls. On the inhale the peat creaks — a real sound, like a wooden ship. On the exhale every current in the basin runs backward for an hour and the duckweed reassembles into the *same* patterns it held that morning. Not similar. Same.

**Mark the tide on your own body.** A grease-pencil line on your boot, or better, on your shin, at the waterline of a chosen slough edge. It's the only gauge that can't be translated into clay while you sleep.

Do not try to time the Reprise. It has no fixed period, and each one is different, and they have been worn smooth by so many million iterations that they arrive less as events than as weather. Treating them as a clock is a good way to lose your sense of duration entirely.

**The reversal hour.** Opinions differ. My advice, and the majority view: **keep moving and keep talking through the exhale.** That hour is the valley running its restore, putting everything back where the pattern says it goes, and a person standing motionless and silent inside a determinism that thorough is a person offering to be included. Be noisy. Be irregular. Be obviously not part of the morning's duckweed.

---

## 11. The Reprise: protocol

Every so often, the last minute happens again, in sound, louder, and slightly wrong. Rhythms cleaned up. One or two things included that you did not make.

The protocol is short:

1. **Do not stop walking.** The commonest injury here is a stumble during a first Reprise.
2. **Do not answer it.** If you hear a voice, do not reply. Not because anything will come — nothing will come — but because your reply enters the record and will be in the next one, and now you are having a conversation with your own echo, and that is a loop you can be inside of for a very long time.
3. **Say aloud what you actually heard, to your partner, immediately.** "I heard my cough, the rain, and a door." Your partner says theirs. Compare. The discrepancies are normal and the *habit of comparing* is the safety measure. The moment you stop reporting is the moment you have started curating.
4. **Log the additions.** Not for science. So that at hour thirty, when the additions start being things you want, you have a written record of how ordinary they were at hour six.

When the Reprise begins to sound *better* than the present — cleaner, better-timed, more like how things ought to have gone — **that is your recall signal.** Not a reason for concern. A reason to leave, now, on the day you notice it.

---

## 12. Continuity: the loop discipline

Here is the reasoning that underlies all the rest of it.

Animals do poorly here because an animal requires an unbroken thread of self, and the thread keeps getting picked up in the wrong place. The creatures that persist are the ones whose lives already loop: the frogs, whose entire existence is a repeated call, and who thrive. The single eel with the notched fin in two sloughs kilometers apart within the same hour. The two herons who are one heron at two ages, hunting the same shallow and ignoring each other with elaborate care.

And the valley reuses solutions. Eleven identical plants in a stand of a hundred, same insect damage on the same leaflet, four meters apart — not copies, just a limited number of ways to be a plant *in that spot*.

So you want two things at once, and they seem contradictory until you see how they fit:

**Be regular in schedule, so the acoustic record holds you.** Sound goes forward reliably; nothing else does. Make noise on a strict interval — every ninety seconds, minimum, awake or being woken. Talk. Narrate what you are doing. Sing badly. This is not morale. This is how you keep a continuous thread in the only medium here that carries threads forward. Parties that go quiet for an hour report gaps: they arrive somewhere with no memory of the crossing and their partner has a different account of it. Train your voice before you come. Eight hours of continuous talking is harder than eight hours of walking.

**Be irregular in content, so you don't get simplified.** Never say the same sentence twice. Never take the same route twice. Never sleep in the same spot twice. Never walk in an even rhythm for long — deliberately break your gait every few dozen paces. The Reprise cleans rhythms up; that is its habit, and it is gentle, and over enough hours it will offer you a cleaner version of your own walking and your own breathing, corrected, held steadier than you have ever managed. If you take it you are easier to store. **Stay syncopated.**

A useful drill: every hour, your partner asks you a question whose answer only you could know and which you have not given before. Not a password — passwords repeat. A fresh fact, out loud. If the answers start coming faster and smoother than they should, call it.

---

## 13. Sleep

Sleep is a break in the thread. It is the single most dangerous thing you will do here.

- **Ideal: don't.** A twelve-hour visit needs no sleep. A thirty-six-hour visit can be done on two ninety-minute naps with discipline.
- **If you must: never alone, and never in silence.** One person sleeps, one person narrates aloud, continuously, for the whole nap. The narrator is not standing watch against anything. The narrator is holding the sleeper's place in the sound.
- Sleep on a **raised platform or floating pad** on a tussock, never on open lawn, never in a hollow (gas), never within ten meters of a cold stone (heat).
- **Never sleep twice in the same place.** Don't give the valley a second chance to find a way of being a human in that spot.
- Expect strange, orderly, deeply pleasant dreams. Report them to your partner on waking, out loud, before you do anything else.

---

## 14. Water, food, fire

Filter and boil everything; the tannin taste is harmless and unavoidable. Bring all your food. **Eat nothing from the valley** — not the berries on the carr, not anything. It isn't a poisoning risk so much as a category risk: the fen's rule is that arriving material gets translated into its medium, and you do not want to test which direction that runs.

Bring all your fuel. And do not, under any circumstances, dry and burn peat. Set aside every other argument: the engineering here is in the ordering of clay platelets across ten thousand hectares, the specification is the ground itself, and a peat fire in a drained cut can smoulder underground for years. Burning it is not camping. It is arson against an archive.

---

## 15. Moving through it

- **Probe before every step onto unknown ground.** The water table is two centimeters under your notion of the ground and your notion is wrong.
- Sphagnum lawns are forty centimeters of held water over uncertainty. Weight distribution, bog shoes, and the pole.
- **The drowned forest is not scaffolding.** Those gray trunks are felt now, structurally. They hold up nothing, including you.
- **Alder carr is impassable.** Not difficult — impassable. Every branch furred with liverwort and hung with dripping beard-lichen, tangled solid. Route around it. Budget an hour per kilometer of detour.
- **The old trails through the carr** — softened, exactly the width of something large, kept open by continuous use, with no user — are the fastest travel in the valley, and you may use them. Travel them noisily, never at the reversal hour, never twice in the same direction, and do not stop in them.
- Horsetail colonies at chest height will hide the ground entirely and whistle faintly in wind. Pole first, always.

---

## 16. Groups

Two to four. Below two you have no narrator. Above four you cannot keep a count.

**Count by touch, aloud, on a fixed interval.** Not by sight. Sight is where duplicates happen, and there are duplicates here: eleven identical plants, one heron at two ages, one eel in two sloughs within the same hour.

If you see a member of your party in a place where they cannot be:

- **Do not approach. Do not call out to it. Do not photograph it.**
- Say, out loud, to your actual partner: "I am seeing Marta at the second riser." Let your partner answer. Let the answer be in the record.
- Turn and walk away perpendicular to the sighting. Keep talking.
- Do not discuss the resemblance afterward in the valley. Discuss it on the rim.

If you see **yourself**, the procedure is the same, and you should terminate the visit at once, regardless of what remains on the schedule. Not because anything bad has begun. Because the valley has found a second way to be you in that spot, and you would like to be gone before it decides which one to keep.

---

## 17. Manners

This is probably the fossil of a mind. Possibly the mind is still there in the way a habit is still a person. Either way:

- **Do not core the peat. Do not take samples. Do not chip a cold stone.** (You can't, but people try, and the trying is the offense.)
- **Do not leave anything, ever, that you did not intend to leave.** It will not litter. It will be translated, faithfully, forever, into an arrangement of grains that is the exact useless shape of your dropped lighter.
- **Watch what you say aloud.** You are writing in the only medium the place uses. The Reprise wears things smooth over millions of iterations, but it starts with what you gave it. There is an argument — I find it persuasive — that the "one or two sounds you did not make" are simply very old visitors, filed, cleaned up, and running down the terraces ever since. Speak as though you were leaving a sound that will outlast your species. You probably are.

---

## 18. Coming out

Walk out on the day you planned. Not the next one. The most common failure mode of a well-prepared expedition is a perfectly reasonable extension.

**On the rim, before the crossing: thirty minutes of silence, sitting down, facing away from the valley.** This is not ceremony. Your auditory system has been in a continuous roar with a structured low chord for a day or more and it needs to come down before you ask it to do anything else.

**Then, within the first hour, debrief out loud into a recorder on the other side.** Everything. This is your only data. Your photographs will be strange and useless. Your instruments' stored readings are an open question. What you will reliably bring out is what you can *say*, so say it while it is fresh, and say it messily, in full sentences, with the hesitations left in.

**Aftercare, first two weeks:**

- Expect your **breathing to have been adjusted.** Many returnees find, for days, that their breath is a little steadier and more even than they have ever managed. It feels wonderful. It is not yours. It will fade. Let it.
- Expect **auditory intrusions** — brief, quiet, self-limiting. Report them to someone who knows where you've been. They should shorten and stop within about ten days. If at three weeks you are still getting Reprises, and especially if they are getting *better*, get help, and do not go back to fix it.
- Expect **grief with no object.** Extremely common. The place is enormously old and finished and gentle and it does not need you, and something in a person responds to that with mourning. It passes.
- Check your feet. Check your feet again.
- **Do not return within six months.** And when you do return, take someone who has never been, because they will still be surprised by how loud it is, and their surprise is a better instrument than any of yours.

---

## 19. What you'll actually bring home

Nothing physical, in all likelihood, that matters.

You'll bring the memory of black water that reproduces the sky so exactly the sloughs look like tears in the ground with clouds behind them. A gray forest standing in ranks, holding up nothing. A chord in the back of your teeth. An hour of ticking at dusk as the whole valley sheds its dew, and the root-glow coming up blue-green through forty centimeters of moss, and two herons who are one heron, facing opposite directions.

And, if you stayed long enough to be worth including, the last Reprise of your last evening will have your breathing in it — cleaned up, corrected, held a little steadier than you have ever managed — repeated on afterward for a few seconds after you have stopped.

Walk out during those seconds. Don't wait to hear how it ends.
