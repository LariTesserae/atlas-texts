# Preparing for the Weeping Shelf
### A practical briefing for an ordinary traveller

---

## 0. The single sentence to hold in your head

**Everything here is slow, everything here is loud, everything here is wet, and nothing here is alive in the way you expect.** Almost every mistake visitors make is a failure to take one of those four seriously enough.

---

## 1. Before you leave home

**Get your teeth done.** This is not a joke and it is not incidental. You are going to an industrial-era society at altitude, three days' climb above the nearest lowland town, with excellent mineral engineering and rudimentary dentistry. Whatever is marginal in your mouth will become urgent there. Same logic applies to hernias, gallstones, anything that might need a surgeon.

**Get a hearing baseline.** Audiogram, both ears, documented. You will want it on your return, and you may want it in a lawsuit.

**Get a chest film.** Same reason. The Shelf's two occupational diseases — lime burn of the airway and guano-dust fungal infection — both show up as changes from baseline, and "before" is worth a great deal.

**Vaccinate against everything avian your home medicine offers,** and accept that it won't be enough. A continent-sized bird colony is a continent-sized influenza reactor. Also arrange rabies-equivalent prophylaxis if your world has an analogue: the hatchet-beaked runners are not common near settlement, but they are fast, warm-blooded, and unafraid.

**Train your legs on stairs, not on flats.** The ascent is switchbacks. The city is vertical. You will climb more in a week on the Shelf than in a year at home.

**Spend a month sleeping badly on purpose.** Sleep with a fan, a radio hiss, something constant and loud. You are going to lose a lot of sleep to the roar in your first week, and people who have practised lose less.

---

## 2. Timing

The Shelf has no seasons. It has:

- **The daily lid.** Cloud from roughly the third hour to the ninth. Warm, close, drizzling, blindingly bright in a diffuse grey way. This is the miserable working part of the day and the dangerous part for exertion — high heat plus saturated air means your sweat does nothing. Do your hard walking before the lid closes.
- **The tear.** Late afternoon. The famous hour. Everything stops. Shops half-close, arguments pause, crews down tools. Do not schedule anything for the tear; you will be stood up, politely and universally. Go and look at it instead. Every day. It never gets old and the locals will think worse of you if it does.
- **The night rain.** Soft, almost every night. Plan your camp for it and your laundry around it. Rooftop-caught rain is the only water anyone drinks; collecting it is a household ritual and offering someone a cup of your own catch is a small, real courtesy.

If you can time your visit for **the long blush**, do. It's a minor festival, four months long, moving eleven kilometres a day westward — which means your town gets perhaps a week of it. There is drinking, colour-dyed lime whitewash, a lot of standing on rims at the tear staring at rust-red water. It is the closest thing the Shelf has to a holiday that isn't a civic-engineering milestone.

---

## 3. The material logic of your luggage

Learn this rule and apply it to every item you pack:

> **If it is wood or plant fibre, it does not exist here.
> If it is soft and porous, the water will turn it to rock.
> If it is metal and cheap, it will corrode; if it is metal and good, someone will want it.**

**Leave behind:** anything wooden you'd be sad to lose (it *will* be handled, admired, and possibly quietly stolen — not from greed, from disbelief); cotton or linen you need to keep supple; leather boots with stitched soles; anything with a felt or fabric gasket; a watch with an open case.

**Bring, deliberately, as gifts and currency:** a dozen cheap wooden pencils. Dried fruit. Peppercorns. A bolt of plain cotton. A small wooden box. These are not trinkets there — they are marvels, and a pencil opens more doors on the Shelf than a coin does. A visitor who hands a foreman a wooden-handled knife has made a friend for the duration.

**Do not bring living plants or viable seeds.** Not because they'd survive — they wouldn't, there is no soil — but because the Shelf's scholars are genuinely, professionally anxious about anything introduced to the mat, and being caught with seeds is the fastest way to be escorted back down the ramps.

---

## 4. Clothing and footwear

**Boots are the single most important purchase, and you should make it locally.** Import boots fail on the Shelf in two contradictory ways at once: *karren* — the fluted, blade-edged dissolution rock between terrace fields — will cut a soft sole to ribbons, while wet mat on travertine is as slippery as oiled glass. Local boots solve both: hard chitin-plastic soles with iron studs, high ankle, replaceable sole plates, no stitching below the waterline. Buy two pairs. Buy spare plates. Break them in on the ramps.

**Above the boots:** oilcloth. Buy that locally too. Your own waterproofs will work for four days and then the membrane will mineralise shut and you'll be wearing a bag. The local oilskin capes are designed to be re-oiled nightly, and re-oiling your cape is simply something you do in the evening, like brushing your teeth.

**A brimmed hat with a drip gutter.** The local design has a rolled rim that runs water off behind you. Wear it everywhere outdoors and *especially* in the ramp tunnels, where the ceiling drips continuously. Untreated hair under a week of that drip becomes stiff, then gritty, then genuinely difficult; many long-haul caravan workers simply shave. An oiled cap is the compromise.

**Glacier glasses, and a spare pair.** This is not optional and it is where visitors most often suffer. Altitude UV, bone-white rock, ten thousand mirrors of standing water, and a cloud lid that scatters light at you from every direction at once. The burns come from *below* as much as above: under the chin, the nostrils, the underside of the ears. Zinc paste on those spots, every morning, all week. "Lime-blindness" is what they call the resulting keratitis, and it is a night of your eyes full of broken glass.

**Gloves.** Thin, tough, and replaceable. You will be touching hot alkaline water, sharp rock, and occasionally quicklime, which combines with the sweat on your skin to make a slow chemical burn you won't feel starting.

---

## 5. Water: the four kinds

Learn to keep them straight, because two of them are drinks and two of them are not.

1. **Roof-rain.** The only thing you drink. Tastes of nothing and slightly of gypsum plate. Treat it anyway — it's collected off surfaces that birds fly over.
2. **Still-water.** Household distillate. Flat, safe, faintly metallic. Every lodging has a still and its condition tells you a lot about the lodging.
3. **Pool and seep water.** Warm, alkaline, bitter, mineral-loaded. It will not kill you today. Drunk regularly it will wreck your kidneys over months, and boiled it will cement your kettle shut in a week. **Do not boil it. Do not brew tea in it. Do not use it to rinse your mouth.**
4. **Vent water.** Near the sources, near-boiling and sometimes sulphurous. Scalds every year. The give-away is not steam — everything steams — but the absence of mat. **Where the mat stops, the water is too hot to live in, and so are you.**

**Bathing.** Bathing in the warm pools is one of the great pleasures of the place and the locals do it constantly. Two rules: **nose clips**, always — warm alkaline freshwater is the classic habitat for amoebic organisms that enter through the nasal passage and are essentially untreatable — and **do not submerge your head**. Also: don't bathe in a pool that has birds in it, don't bathe in a pool directly below a colony, and rinse in rainwater afterwards or your skin will dry out and crack in the folds.

---

## 6. Air: the three invisible things

The Shelf's geology is a gas engine, and three of its gases will kill you quietly.

**Carbon dioxide.** The whole plateau exists because deep water is degassing CO₂ into it. That gas is heavier than air and it pools — in sinkholes, dry valleys, cellars, the bottoms of unventilated stairwells, and in still hollows on calm mornings. There are places on the Shelf where a man can walk through a dip with his head in good air while his dog dies at his ankles. Locals know the bad hollows in their district the way you know which stair creaks. Visitors do not. **Never descend into a hollow, a dry pit, or an old cellar without a local, a light, and a test.** The traditional test is a candle on a string; a modern gas meter is better; either is infinitely better than curiosity.

**Hydrogen sulphide.** The underworld smell. It lives in the black bottom layer of the mat and in certain seeps. The danger is that your nose stops registering it above a certain concentration — the smell going away is the *worst* sign, not a good one. Don't dig into mat, don't linger in enclosed seep-caves, and if the eggs smell suddenly stops, move uphill and upwind immediately.

**Everything the city makes.** Mat-coal smoke, kiln plume, quicklime dust, ammonia from the guano works. Indoors, mat-coal braziers put out carbon monoxide; the locals have a healthy fear of a closed room and a cold night. Carry a CO alarm if your worldline allows it; if not, follow the local practice of a permanently cracked vent, which will make your room cold and save your life.

**Bring a proper respirator** — particulate plus acid gas — and wear it in three places without fail: the kiln districts, anywhere downwind of the guano works, and anywhere dried bird-dropping crust is being disturbed. That last is the one visitors forget. Dry guano dust carries fungal spores, and the resulting lung infection is the classic tourist souvenir that shows up three weeks after you get home and is misdiagnosed as pneumonia twice before anyone works it out.

---

## 7. Noise

You cannot overstate this and you should not try to tough it out.

A major colony roar is not a background. It is an object. It is physically present, it comes through the floor, and it is constant to within a few percent *for decades*. Near a big field you will not be able to hear a shouted warning, a vehicle, a fall of rock, or your own companion at four metres.

**Bring three levels of hearing protection:** good foam plugs for sleeping, moulded plugs for daily wear, muffs over plugs for the colony edges. Bring more plugs than you think — they mineralise, they get lost, and you will give some away.

**Bring a light-based signalling method,** because whistles are useless. A mirror in the daytime, a shuttered lamp at night; agree three signals with your companions before you go out: *stop*, *come*, *trouble*. The local hand-language is simple and worth an hour of study — most of it is arm-length gestures designed to be read at distance through drizzle.

**Expect a week of ragged sleep, irritability, and a strange flatness of mood.** That is normal and it passes. Expect also that you will start shouting without noticing. Everyone does. The locals find it endearing in visitors and are famously loud abroad themselves.

And expect the reverse on the way home. **The silence when you descend the ramps is the part nobody warns you about.** Travellers report a phantom roar for two or three weeks, a ringing that isn't tinnitus but memory, and a genuine unease at the quiet. Arrange your first few nights back somewhere with running water or a fan.

## 8. Food

There are no plants. Work through the implications before you arrive rather than after.

**The Shelf diet** is eggs, bird (roasted, salted, potted), shrimp paste, dried fly-larvae meal pressed into hard cakes, mat-bread (chewy, dark, tastes of pond and iron, genuinely not bad with enough salt), fat, and more salt. It is high-protein, high-fat, and almost carbohydrate-free.

Three practical consequences:

1. **Scurvy.** With no fruit and no vegetables, the Shelf gets its vitamin C where circumpolar peoples do — from fresh meat, organs, and lightly-cooked eggs. The local habit of eating bird liver and drinking the pot-juices is not a quaint custom; it is the reason they aren't dying. **Eat the offal. Don't over-cook things. Bring vitamin tablets anyway** — a six-week supply is tiny and weighs nothing and removes the whole question.
2. **Your gut will complain.** No plant fibre at all, plus unfamiliar microbes, plus hard water residue. Expect a rough four days. Bring rehydration salts and don't be heroic about it.
3. **Bird livers in quantity are a bad idea** in some worlds for vitamin A reasons. Eat them the way locals do — as a component, not a feast.

Drink more than you think. The lid hours dehydrate you without any sensation of sweating, because you never dry.

---

## 9. Moving across the country

**Take a guide.** This is not conservatism. The terrace fields are visually repetitive at eye level, the lid removes the horizon for six hours a day, the roar removes sound as a navigation aid, and the hazards — thin crusts over voids, dead-air hollows, near-boiling seeps — are exactly the sort that look like everything else. Guides are cheap. Being winched out of a sinkhole is not.

**Carry a probe.** A metal or bone staff, chest-high, and use it constantly in unfamiliar terrace country. Old rimstone can roof over a drained pool with a crust that holds a bird and not a person.

**Read the mat.** It's the best instrument you have. Green-black and thriving: ordinary warm water, safe. Rust and purple dominant: different chemistry, usually fine. **Thin, patchy, or absent: too hot, too acid, or too gassy — do not proceed.** A bare white basin in the middle of a living field is a warning sign, not a beauty spot.

**Watch the birds, too.** They are a colony-scale sensor. A field that empties has emptied for a reason.

**Do not walk on the rims.** Two reasons. First, a rim is centuries of deposit a hand's-breadth wide and you can destroy a hundred years of it in a step. Second, the rims are where the birds nest, and the colonies are the nitrate works, and the nitrate works are the economy. Disturbing a nesting rim is not a *faux pas*; in most districts it's a fine, and near the big guano concessions it's worse than a fine. Walk in the pools, not on their edges. That's what everyone does.

## 10. Daily maintenance (the thing nobody tells you)

Every evening, without exception, you must **de-scale**.

Everything you took into the water is now carrying a microscopic layer of new stone. Left alone, that layer compounds. In four days your boot laces are rods, your buckles won't move, your camera focus ring is seized, your tent zip is a solid line, and your trousers stand up by themselves.

So: **bring acid.** Citric acid powder is ideal — light, cheap, non-alarming to customs of any kind. Vinegar works. A stiff brush, a shallow basin, rainwater rinse. Twenty minutes a night on hinges, threads, zips, laces, straps, lens barrels, and boot welts. Locals do this automatically and will be baffled that you didn't.

**Corollaries:**

- Don't leave gear on wet ground overnight. It bonds. People have genuinely lost a pack to a seep-floor.
- Don't sleep on the ground in wet country. Use a cot, a rack, a slab, anything.
- Watches: sealed case only. Mechanical watches die here in a fortnight.
- Keep one set of clothing that never leaves your lodging, so you always have something soft.

## 11. In Aggrade

**Lodging.** Ask for a room on the **second or third floor.** Ground floors are constructed as sacrifices — they are future cellars, and everyone knows it, so they are plainly finished, damp, cheap, and where they put visitors who didn't ask. The good rooms, the dry rooms, the rooms with the view of the tear, are upstairs. This is the most useful single sentence in this briefing.

**Read the doorpost marks.** Every significant doorway has a column of small horizontal scratches with dates — the record of the ground coming up to meet the building. Asking about them is a *good* conversational move, roughly equivalent to admiring someone's family photographs. Householders will happily tell you which of their ancestors laid which step and how many centimetres the century has cost them.

**Understand that the city is negotiating with its own drainage, permanently.** Pump-houses everywhere. The pumps make a slow four-beat that you will hear beneath everything and will come to find comforting. If the pumps stop, that is an emergency; follow the crowd and go uphill.

**Streets are washed constantly** because an unwashed street becomes rock. Don't walk on a freshly-scoured section; don't stand in the sluice-run; do tip the washing crews if you've made a mess.

**Don't throw anything away outdoors.** Litter on the Shelf isn't litter, it's *stratigraphy*. A dropped wrapper in a seep becomes a fossil inclusion that somebody's great-great-grandchild will quarry out and be annoyed about. Carry it out. The local ethic on this is fierce and entirely rational.

And accept the corollary: **your footprints are permanent.** If you stand in a wet flat and don't move, you are signing the rock. Some visitors find this delightful. Some find it appalling. Both reactions are correct.

---

## 12. Manners and mind

**Speak up.** Literally. Visitors are constantly asked to repeat themselves and assume they're being snubbed. They're not; the room is just loud and the listener's ears are forty years into a colony.

**Do not be in a hurry.** The calendar here has a unit for *the time it takes a doorstep to become a step*. Civic projects are planned across four generations. A deal that would take you an afternoon takes three days, and the three days are not padding — they're how trust is established. Visible impatience reads roughly as we read spitting on the floor.

**Don't flinch at the quarry-history.** They mine lime out of their great-grandparents' streets, and the historians are literally quarrymen, and they will tell you cheerfully about cutting through a shopfront and finding a dog's footprint. It's considered only mildly morbid. Join in. Ask what was in the layer. They love that question.

**Don't solve the long blush.** You will want to. Everyone does. Within an hour of learning about the wave you will have a theory, and it will be one of the eleven theories in the published literature, and the person you offer it to will have heard it from four visitors this year. They have a whole genre of joke about the outsider who has worked it out. Enjoy the blush; don't explain it. If you are a genuine specialist, go to the observatory house and be treated seriously — but go as a student, not a saviour.

**Avoid plant metaphors.** Half of your language's abstractions are botanical — *roots*, *branching*, *growth*, *fruitful*, *seeds of an idea*, *a flourishing trade* — and none of them land. Trade-speech carries a few borrowed and mangled. Their own metaphors are mineral and sedimentary: things *accrete*, *encrust*, *bed down*, *come up to meet you*, *go to lime*. Listening for that shift is one of the pleasures of the place.

---

## 13. The thing you should actually do

Three recommendations, in order.

**One: hang something in a seep.** A key, a spoon, a small figure, a glove. Leave it a month; come back; it will have a stone shell on it, the exact shape of the original, bone-white and faintly translucent. This is the classic Shelf souvenir, sold at every market and better if you do it yourself. There are seeps outside Aggrade with a hundred strangers' objects dangling on wires like an orchard of stone fruit.

**Two: stand in a colony at the tear.** With muffs on, at the edge of a hundred square kilometres of standing birds, when the lid rips and the light comes in flat. It is the loudest, brightest, strangest ten minutes available to a human being anywhere, and you will spend the rest of your life failing to describe it.

**Three: find a road-cut and read it.** Any of them. The banded layers are the bird-and-rim arrangement written down — the same partnership, tens of thousands of years deep. It's the moment the place stops being scenery and becomes an argument.

---

## 14. Going home

Descend slowly and expect the reverse-shocks in this order:

1. **The quiet**, which will feel wrong for weeks.
2. **The smell of plants**, which will hit you somewhere on the lower ramps and may — genuinely, this is common, don't be embarrassed — make you cry.
3. **Softness.** Grass, moss, a wooden banister. Travellers report touching tree bark for a long time.

Then get the follow-ups: **hearing test against baseline, chest film against baseline**, and see a physician at three and six weeks about any cough, fever, or fatigue, and *tell them where you've been*, because the guano fungus is invisible to a doctor who isn't looking for it.

And decalcify your gear properly, in a long acid soak, before you put it away. Otherwise you'll open the bag a year later and find that the Shelf came home with you and quietly turned your boots into a pair of white stones, which is exactly the sort of joke that place makes.

## Compact packing list

**Non-negotiable:** glacier glasses ×2 · brimmed drip-hat · oilcloth cape (buy local) · studded cut-resistant boots ×2 (buy local) + spare sole plates · respirator + filters · ear plugs (many) + muffs · nose clips · metal probe staff · signal mirror and shuttered lamp · citric acid + stiff brush + basin · zinc paste · headlamp + spares.

**Medical:** vitamin C and multivitamin · rehydration salts · broad-spectrum antibiotic · antifungal cream · saline eye wash (lime) · burn dressings · barrier/lanolin cream · your own painkillers · a written note of allergies in trade-speech.

**Instruments, if your worldline permits:** four-gas meter (CO₂, CO, H₂S, O₂) · CO alarm for the room · altimeter and compass · waterproof notebook.

**Trade and gifts:** wooden pencils (a dozen) · a small wooden box or handled tool · dried fruit · peppercorns or salt-spice · plain cotton cloth · a printed book · lowland coin and a letter of credit.

**Leave at home:** anything wooden you love · fabric you need to stay soft · seeds or live plants · an open-cased watch · your normal walking speed · your assumption that anything you set down outdoors will still be a separate object next month.
