# Preparing for the Sill

## First, decide honestly what you are going up there to do

The Sill kills very few people quickly. That is the problem with it. Almost everything that goes wrong on the plateau goes wrong slowly, comfortably, and at body temperature, and by the time it is obvious it is already several days past the point where you could have walked out of it. There is no rescue. There is no signalling — the acoustics forbid it, the canopy hides you, and there is no fire, so there is no smoke. The Sill is the largest place in any world where you cannot be found.

So set the shape of the trip before you set the gear. A ten-day radial walk from a rim camp, out on a bearing and back on its reciprocal, is an expedition. Thirty days inland is a serious undertaking with a real chance of failure. Anything beyond that is not travel, it is emigration, and you should treat it as such and say your goodbyes properly.

## The question you must answer before you leave home

Everything on the Sill is one organism. That is not poetry; it is a logistical fact with two possible answers, and which answer you get determines whether you carry twenty days of food or two hundred.

Before you go, get sap and tissue assayed. You want to know three things: whether the Sill's chemistry can feed you, whether it can poison you, and whether it can *eat* you. My strong expectation, from the descriptions — thin sweet sap, faintly metallic, a plateau leached of minerals for an unthinkable duration — is this: the sap is drinkable, mildly nutritive, and cumulatively toxic in the way that a slow metal load is toxic. The soft fauna are probably digestible protein. And the green cast your skin takes on within a day is probably *epiphytic rather than infectious*: the Sill grows **on** you, politely, the way it grows on everything, without any interest in your insides.

If the assay says otherwise, cancel. A place with compatible pathogens and no fire is not survivable.

## The body: altitude, heat, and the impossibility of drying

Four thousand metres, and warm, and saturated. This is a rare and treacherous combination. At altitude your body expects cold and gives you the discomfort that makes you behave sensibly. The Sill gives you no discomfort at all until you fall over.

**Acclimatise in the low country, high up, for at least three weeks.** Not at the base of the wall — in the foothills, as high as you can get and still sleep. Arrive at the rim already adapted, because the climb itself will take days and you do not want to be learning hypoxia on a wet cliff.

**Understand that you cannot cool yourself.** Air at blood temperature and total humidity means sweat does not evaporate. Everything you have ever known about pacing is wrong here. You will overheat while soaking wet, in dim green twilight, feeling pleasantly warm, and the first symptom will be that your judgement gets soft. Plan on eight to twelve kilometres a day and long midday halts. Move in the cooler hours, which is to say the hours just after the night rain.

**Carry more salt than seems mad.** This is the single most underestimated item. The plateau is leached of minerals; the water is nearly distilled organic tea; you will sweat continuously and drink enormously. Hyponatremia has emptied more Sill parties than the ambush hunters ever have. Bring electrolyte salts in sealed, single-dose form, and a discipline about taking them whether or not you feel you need to.

**Skin is your true expedition limit.** Constant wet macerates you. Feet first, then groin and armpits, then hands. The protocol is unglamorous and non-negotiable:

- Twice daily, strip, abrade gently with a coarse dry cloth, especially between the toes and in every fold. You are removing the beginning of the Sill.
- One pair of socks, sealed, kept dry in a vapour-barrier bag, worn only while sleeping. Dry feet for six hours a night is the difference between a thirty-day trip and a ten-day one.
- Silica gel in generous quantity, in a sealed regeneration jar — and accept that you cannot regenerate it, because you have no heat. Budget desiccant as a consumable, like fuel.
- Nails cut short. Any cut, anywhere, is a wound that will not close. Take wound-closure strips, tissue adhesive, and an unreasonable amount of waterproof dressing.

## The approach and the wall

For two days before the escarpment you are inside falling water that never lands. Pack for total immersion from the moment the sky turns grey-green. Everything in dry bags, bags inside liners, electronics in hard cases with desiccant. Your last cooked meal is at the base of the wall. Eat it slowly and mean it.

The climb is four thousand metres of permanently wet rock in permanent cloud, and it is the only honest rock left in the country, which means it is also the only place on the whole journey where you can be hurt fast. Hire local guides. There are routes; there are people who know them; the low country has grandmothers who remember, and the older guides carry lore about the plateau that you will not find written anywhere.

Two things about the wall that nobody tells you:

**You will arrive at the rim wet through and with soft hands, and softness is exactly wrong for the descent.** Build into your plan a sealed hard-shelter day at the rim on the way out — a day of doing nothing but drying and rehardening before you commit to the cliff. People die on the way down with their fingertips turned to fruit.

**The rim is your only landmark in the world.** Cache there, generously. And fix your entry point by whatever means you trust most — the weeping face is a thousand-kilometre-long featureless line, and coming back to the *wrong* part of it is functionally the same as not coming back.

## Fire: the decision, and there is only one right answer

Carry no ignition source of any kind onto the plateau. No lighter, no ferro rod, no matches, no pyrotechnic signal, no lithium cell that can be short-circuited without a fuse, no firearm.

This is not only respect. It is self-interest. Beneath you are several hundred metres of peat and soft brown coal, warm, exhaling, and dry in places you cannot see. Peat fires are not put out; they go underground and burn for centuries. And the same plateau periodically vents accumulated pockets of gas straight up through the mat you are standing on. A flame plus a *whumph* is a fireball with you inside it, and then a fire that outlives your civilisation.

The practical consequence is that you must design a **cold expedition**. No cooked food, no boiled water, no drying by heat, no warmth as morale, no light by flame, no cautery. If you want hot food, the only acceptable technology is a sealed, flameless exothermic heater — a chemical pouch with no open reaction, carried in and carried out spent. Take a few, for the two or three nights when hot food will hold a person together.

## Movement: travel it like a glacier

The quilt holds you — a hand's depth, closing behind — until it doesn't. Thin patches, hidden veins, the raised lips of lakes, and the ambush hunters that have grown into the surface and become a patch of ground with a mouth in it.

- **Rope up.** Ten to twelve metres between people, as on crevassed ice. Practise extraction from a soft, gripping, sucking medium before you go; it is not the same as a snow-bridge rescue, because the substrate closes on you and holds. Practise it with weight.
- **Probe.** A long, light pole, used every step in doubtful ground, and used as a matter of habit even in good ground. The hunters cannot chase. They only need you to put a foot down without asking first.
- **Spread your load.** Wide, flexible flotation underfoot — the local woven quilt-shoes are better than anything you will bring, so buy a pair at the rim and copy their geometry. They are not for the good mat; they are for the three seconds when the mat is not there.
- **Never stand still for a quarter of an hour.** Shift your weight, move your feet, check your laces. The Sill will grow over your boot tops while you are admiring the ceiling, and it is not a threat, it is a courtesy, and it is exactly how people lose an hour and then a day.
- **Avoid rhythm.** Airborne sound dies instantly, but the peat and the water carry low frequency beautifully — it is how the fauna court each other. A marching cadence is a broadcast. Walk unevenly.

## Camp: never sleep on the ground, because there isn't any

Hang. Hammock and tarp, slung between columns, two to three metres up. Sleeping on the quilt means waking up partly incorporated, and it means putting your face at the level where heavy gas pools.

- Use **two independent anchor systems**, and inspect your knots every morning. The Sill grows over cordage in a single night and will happily keep your rope.
- Put a **vapour barrier and desiccant** in the sleeping system. The six dry hours are medicinal.
- Rig your tarp not for rain — rain does not reach you — but for **drip harvesting**. Canopy drip, filtered through twenty storeys of plant, is the cleanest free water on the plateau.
- Camp **away from lakes** and away from any hollow. Carry a simple oxygen meter and hang it at face level. At four thousand metres you have no margin at all for displaced air, and the gas releases come without warning at intervals of days.
- If you smell eggs, or if the lamp-bells all around you light at once for no reason you can name, **move upslope and upwind immediately and do not discuss it first.**

## Water and food

Drink drip and tapped column-sap in preference to anything standing. Tap like a sugar maple: a small spile, a clean bottle, a plug in the wound afterwards. The sap is the safest liquid on the Sill, and because it is faintly metallic you should ration it against your assay's cumulative limit rather than drinking it as your whole supply.

Filters clog instantly on organic load. Carry many coarse pre-filters and expect to burn through them. Chemical treatment is nearly free in weight; use it even if the assay says nothing up there can infect you, because assays are done on samples and you will be drinking the place.

Food: retort pouches, fats, and things that do not care about warmth. Nothing that moulds. Nothing that requires water to reconstitute unless you enjoy eating soup made of the plateau. If local protein is cleared for you, the soft fauna are easy to take — the browsers do not flee, and there is no sport in it — but it must be eaten raw and immediately, and you must accept that this is a real risk you are taking for calories.

## Navigation and the treachery of the structures

There is nothing to navigate by. Relief of tens of metres across a thousand kilometres. No rivers. No summits. Canopy that hides the sun and closes the sky. Ground that heals your tracks in a season and swallows your cairns.

- **Go in on one bearing and come out on its reciprocal.** No improvised shortcuts. Ever. Two compasses, checked against each other daily, plus a pace count kept on beads by two people independently.
- **Paper rots.** Polymer notebook, pencil.
- **Use the Long Flower as a baseline if it is anywhere near you.** A blossoming front hundreds of kilometres long, running north–south, moving under two kilometres a year, is the single most reliable line on the plateau. It is a compass and a calendar and it will still be there next month, which is more than can be said for anything else.
- **Do not navigate by the structures.** This is the rule that has killed the most careful people. The doorframes, the benches, the four steps, the low wall around nothing — some are the last teeth of drowned villages, but many are the Sill repeating a shape it has enveloped, in slow annual accretion, again and again, a little further on each time. You will mark a distinctive threshold-stone and then find it again, and it will be the wrong one, and it will be genuinely identical, because the plant does not know what a door is; it knows what a door is *shaped like*. A landscape that helpfully reproduces your landmarks is worse than a landscape with none.

Touch nothing at these sites. Photograph, measure, leave. And understand the corollary: **whatever shape you leave on the Sill, the Sill may learn.** A party that camps in one place for a season may be giving the plateau a new vocabulary word. Somewhere, in nine hundred metres and ten thousand years, something the shape of your tent.

## Discipline: sound, light, scent

You will be the loudest object in the history of this place, and none of it is by voice.

- **Light:** a good noon here is dusk. Any lamp you bring is an event. Use deep red, use the lowest setting that works, and hood it. The lamp-bells brighten for an hour after any disturbance of the air, so you travel inside a slow-moving announcement of yourself whether you like it or not. Accept it; don't add to it.
- **Scent:** courtship and hunting here are conducted by smell. You are a foreign smell of enormous intensity — soap, synthetics, cooked food residue, sunscreen, insect repellent. Go unscented in everything, and store your food sealed, not because of bears but because you do not know what a hundred-million-year-old organism does when it detects something entirely new nearby.
- **Sound:** shouting is useless and clapping is unnerving. In dead acoustics your voice returns nothing and your brain, deprived of reverberation, will begin supplying sounds that are not there. This is normal, it happens in anechoic chambers within hours, and it is one of the main reasons solo travel on the Sill is a bad idea.

## The mind, which is the real hazard

The Sill is warm, soft, dim, silent, sweet-smelling, and nothing in it is in a hurry. It does not attack you. It *includes* you. People do not usually die on the plateau; they stop. They shift camp a few hundred paces a year and walk with the flowering and never see ordinary country again.

Guard against this the way you would guard against cold:

- **Set a hard turnaround by food, not by feeling**, and write it in the notebook before you leave the rim, and have your partner countersign it. Half your food gets you in; half gets you out; there is no third half.
- **Go with at least one other person, and preferably three,** with an explicit agreement that any one of you may call the turnaround without argument or explanation.
- **Keep a written log with dated entries.** Not for science. For evidence, later, that you are still the person who walked in.
- **Talk about the Long Flower beforehand.** If your visit coincides with the front, you will walk into twenty kilometres of white and liver-red blossom, doubled light, unbreathable sweetness, and silent clouds of pollinators. Carry proper respiratory filtration and adrenaline for anaphylaxis, because the air in that band is not air, it is pollen. And know in advance that some fraction of people who see it do not want to leave it, and that this is not a failure of character but a known effect of the place, and that it should be planned for the way you plan for altitude sickness.

## Leaving

Come out on your reciprocal bearing. Take your dry day at the rim in a sealed shelter and harden your hands before the wall. Descend into rising noise — you will have forgotten what echo is, and the first hard sound in the low country will feel like an assault.

Decontaminate in both directions. Scrub everything, burn nothing on the plateau and burn what you must at the bottom, and accept a quarantine — you are carrying spores and fragments of a single organism that has never met anything, and it has never met anything that could carry it away.

And expect a bad month afterwards. The ordinary world will seem loud, hard, dry, bright, and rushed, and food will taste of smoke, which you will find you had forgotten was a taste. That passes. Mostly.

**Short list of things people forget:** salt; desiccant as a consumable; a second compass; a probe pole; one sealed pair of dry socks; wound closure you can apply to wet skin; polymer paper; a partner authorised to end the trip; and no fire of any kind, not even the small one in your pocket you were sure would be fine.
