# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Room Under the Blackthorn

## First, the honest summary

You are going to make a short dive, in water no deeper than a ladder, in a farm field, in an ordinary country, in ordinary weather — and it is one of the more dangerous things a person can arrange to do.

Everything that makes it dangerous is mundane and would be dangerous anywhere: an overhead environment, zero visibility, a fresh and unstable ceiling, a flooded bell whose exit hole is smaller than the room and therefore findable only by luck or by line, dense entangling growth, seven-degree water, no rescue, no chamber, no one coming. Cave divers die in exactly this, every year, in worlds with no anomalies in them at all.

The anomaly does not add a hazard so much as remove your tools. Understand this clearly before you go: **the room takes away your ability to do things that take a while.** Not your ability to act — your ability to *sustain*. You can start; you can arrive; you cannot proceed. Every scrap of technique you own that lives in the middle of a motion is going to be eaten.

So the preparation is mostly this: convert everything you need into *states and objects*, and take nothing that depends on *process*.

## The organising principle

Say it to yourself until it is boring.

**In that room, only the near is real. Everything else is a conclusion.**

Short-path phenomena survive intact: touch, pressure, cold on the skin, the sound of your own teeth on the mouthpiece conducted through the jawbone, the single contraction of your heart. Long-path phenomena are eaten: light across a room, sound across a room, a bubble's rise, a careful search, a slowly unspooling reel, a pull-signal on a rope, a thought held in place for half a minute.

The room's appetite has a grain to it. Things shorter than about a blink go through untouched. Things longer than about a held breath are too big to swallow whole and get nibbled instead, so that they arrive bitty and incomplete. In between — from a heartbeat up to a slow breath, which is precisely the size of most human competence — things simply do not happen. They are found to have happened, or found not to.

The shrimp die because a swimming stroke is exactly that size.

## Before you leave your own world

**Get properly trained, in a world that has training.** Full cave certification is the floor, not the ceiling. You want sump experience specifically: short, shallow, silty, low, with a line. You want a hundred hours of blacked-out drills — lost line, lost buddy, entanglement, valve shutdowns, gas sharing with your eyes taped. The room will not let you *perform* a drill. It will let you be a person for whom the drill is already finished. That only happens after years.

**Train your hands to make maps.** Spend a winter in a swimming pool with a blindfold and a pile of junk on the bottom, building rooms in your head out of pressure and edge and temperature. Down there, this is not a backup sense. It is the only seeing there is.

**Learn to count with objects.** A knotted cord, a bead line, a row of shifted rings on a bar. You will not be able to count seconds, or breaths, or drips — the counting itself is the kind of process the weed takes. You can count knots, because a knot is a thing, not an interval.

**Get your shots.** This is a cattle pasture with standing water in it. Leptospirosis is the one that kills farmers; the peat and the slurry carry the rest. Tetanus, current. Tell whatever doctor you use afterwards that you dived in farm water, and say it twice, because they will not write it down the first time.

**Get fat, or at least insulated.** Seven degrees is a serious cold, and the heat maths down there does not come out the way the tables say. People come up colder than their bottom time allows for. Nobody has a good account of why. Dress for four times the exposure you plan.

## Arriving: the six people

Six people know about the room. You will not be the seventh by turning up in a van with tanks.

This is a quiet, wet, agricultural world with long memories and short patience. Budget months, not days. Be a plausible person in the nearest town — a hydrologist doing subsidence surveys, a cousin, a student of drainage, anything dull and checkable. Find the farmer first; the field is his, the liability is his, and he has already had one insurance man and two curious boys and he is sick of it. Bring him something useful rather than something impressive: a proper hazard fence, a gate hinge, a day's work.

The six will find you. They are not a society and they have no rules written down, but they have two, and they are absolute: **you do not take a light, and you do not tell.** If you can't hold to both, don't go down, because you will be found out and the room will be closed to you and to everyone after you.

## Kit: what to take, and what will not work

**Gas: open circuit, twinned or sidemount, two entirely independent supplies.** Sidemount for preference — the hole is cart-width but the room shelves out under a raw ceiling and you will spend time on your side in a metre of clearance.

**No rebreather.** A scrubber works by dwell: the gas must sit against the absorbent for a certain number of seconds. The room will not give it those seconds. Divers have had breakthrough in minutes, tasting the sweetness, and have not recognised it because the taste itself was clipped short. This is the single firmest piece of advice on this page.

**No clock.** Not mechanical, not quartz, not sand. Every timepiece is a device for counting intervals, and the intervals are being taken. Watches come up wrong in both directions and inconsistently, which is worse than wrong in one. **Your gas gauge is your clock.** Plan in thirds by pressure and turn on pressure alone. Learn to read the needle by touch — count detents, or file a notch — because you will not see it.

**Lights: take two, expect them to be ornaments.** In the shaft they work. Below about five feet the beam becomes a hot coin at the lens with nothing beyond it, and the temptation to stare into it is enormous and wasteful. Carry them for the descent, for the exit, for the walk back across the field in the dark. Do not build any procedure around them.

**Nothing chemical, nothing rate-dependent.** Glow sticks flare and die. Heated undersuits fail — the battery's chemistry needs dwell like everything else. Adhesives will not cure; tape will not bond; a repair made down there is not a repair. Fix everything on the grass.

**Line: thick, braided, felt-through-gloves, with tactile direction markers.** Tie off in daylight to a driven stake or a vehicle, *not* the blackthorn (it will hold and it will also lace your forearms open, and blackthorn punctures go septic and stay septic). Secondary tie-off at the lip. Belay in the shaft. Then run your line and **do not lay it from a reel** — a reel is a process, and processes get eaten halfway. Pre-laid, pre-knotted, carried in as a finished object in a bag. Your route is a thing that already exists before you go down.

**Gloves: thin.** This is a real cost — hands in seven degrees, bare or nearly — and you take it because touch is your only sense and mitts turn a room into a fog. Most of the six dive with dry gloves and thin liners and accept twenty minutes of usable hand.

**Helmet.** The fracture is months old and still sharp enough to open a scalp or a line.

**A slate and a grease pencil.** You cannot see it. Mark it anyway, in tallies, big, by feel. A mark is an object and survives. The making of it does not, which is fine.

**Bead cord on the wrist.** One bead per event you want to remember. You will not remember otherwise. This is not superstition; this is the whole memory strategy.

## The surface site

The rim is an overhang. The hole in the turf is narrower than the room beneath it, which means the ground you are standing on at the edge has nothing under it but water and weed. **Do not stand at the lip.** Kit up ten yards back. Bridge the rim with a ladder laid flat or scaffold planks to spread the load, and put your tender's weight on the plank, not the grass.

Come in a dry spell. After rain the shaft runs silt, the field is a bog for the carry-in, and the lip calves.

The blackthorn will have opinions about your head and your hoses. Prune nothing — the six will not forgive you — but rig a groundsheet over the worst of it.

## Signals: make them spatial, never temporal

A pull-code is a rhythm. Rhythms are shapes in time. Two pulls and three pulls arrive at the surface identically, as one ambiguous yank, and your tender will guess, and his guess will be a coin toss with your life on it.

So: **one cord per message.** Three light cords clipped at your chest, tagged by knot-count so you can tell them apart by feel. White: *I am well, paying out.* Red: *coming up.* Black: *come and get me.* Your tender holds three ends and needs no arithmetic.

Buddy contact underwater is the same problem. Abandon squeeze-codes. Use **place**: a hand on the *wrist* means all right; on the *elbow* means we are leaving; on the *shoulder* means something is wrong. Location is not a duration. Location survives.

## In the room

**Never both hands free.** Three fingers on the line at all times, or a palm flat on stone. When the between goes out from under you — and it will, usually in the first few minutes, as a lurch of *I was there and now I am here and I did not come* — the thing that keeps you is that your hand was already holding something before the gap opened.

**Plan nothing that takes a while.** Do not plan to "search along the north shelf." You will not experience searching; you will experience having searched, with no result you can trust. Instead, plan to *touch the third block*. Objects, endpoints, positions. Then go to the next one.

**Do not chase your exhaust.** Your bubbles do not rise so much as appear at the ceiling, and they appear where the ceiling happens to be, which is mostly *not* over the hole. Pockets of your own used air collect under the shelved roof and they are a beautiful lie: your head breaks into air, in a cave, and every animal instinct says *saved*. It is a foot-deep pocket of your own carbon dioxide under six feet of clay, and people have stayed in one until they were stupid. The only way up is the line.

**Your breathing will feel wrong before anything else does.** The pause at the top of an inhale — the small ease where a diver lives — is exactly the right size for the weed and it goes first. What remains is the sensation of being hurried by nothing at all: no wall closing, no air running out, just the flat impossibility of anything having room to happen. This is the room's characteristic panic and it does not feel like fear, which is why it is dangerous. It feels like *urgency* and it makes people do the fast thing. The antidote is the ritual: hand on line, feet on floor, one bead.

**Your pulse will feel held.** The gap between beats is inside the grain. Most people report the heart as a single continuous pressure rather than a knocking. It unclenches on ascent, in the shaft, sometimes violently. Expect it; it is not a cardiac event.

## Your own tunnel is your breadcrumb

The weed closes over you, but slowly — a body-shaped absence holds open behind you for something like an hour before it seals. This is the one navigational gift the room gives, and the six use it constantly: if you lose the line, you have not lost the room, because the room is holding a mould of where you have been.

Trust it with caution. It is an hour of *room* time, and you have no way of knowing how much of that you have spent.

## Things not to touch

**The lights.** Forty plums of specific afternoon hanging in the fronds, cool and firm and loose in their settings, and every single person who has been down there has wanted one, and one or two have taken one, which is why the six have a rule. The weed around a gap goes the grey-brown of boiled nettles within a day and does not come back. There is not much reason to think the room is anything other than the weed. Look at them for as long as you like. They do not light your hand and they will not light your house.

**The basin under the iron ring.** The weed there is white foam, the finest and densest in the room, which means it is the hungriest place in the room. Hands put into it come out having been idle for a while. Two people report a mild permanent change afterwards — an inability to wait comfortably for ordinary things: kettles, queues, other people finishing sentences. Small, and it did not go away.

**The iron ring itself.** It is the best-looking belay point down there and you must not clip to it. It is corroded, it is set in a ceiling that failed once already, and it is directly above the basin, and a room like this may well be *about* something, and if it is, that is the part it is about.

**The worn step.** No reason. Everyone leaves it alone. Follow the custom.

## The doorframe

You are a multiverse traveller, so this is presumably why you came, and you should not go through it on your first dive, or your second.

The rule the six keep, and it is a good one: **one hand on the jamb at all times.** Never both hands off the stone. Go through as far as your waist, take what you came for, come back. It is a frame, not a passage; there is nothing beyond it but the same room at another hour, and the thing that will kill you is the temptation to turn round and look at the room from that hour's side.

Do not run your line through it. Your line's far end belongs to a different afternoon and the man holding it is not there.

Take a sample bottle and a thermometer you can read by notches. Grit, temperature, a degree of difference — that is what comes back, along with the occasional impossible petal. Expect no vista. Expect wet black nothing that is subtly the wrong wet black nothing.

## The animal

Nine times since February, a clean tube the width of a wrist opens through the weed at walking pace and goes away under the north slabs.

If it passes near you, note which way and then let it go. Do not follow it; it leads under the slabs and out of the room and you will run out of line and gas in a gap that is already closing. And do not — this seems too obvious to write and is therefore worth writing — get into it. The tube opens ahead of nobody and closes behind nothing. Whether you would qualify as nothing is not a question you want answered experimentally.

## Coming up, and coming back

The ascent is three seconds of nothing, which is a relief, and then you are in the shaft and the daylight resumes, not brightens — *resumes* — and the world is suddenly full of enormous quantities of between, and it is horrible. Everything takes forever. Your tender's sentence goes on and on. Divers call the first hour the afterdrag and it passes, mostly, by evening. Do not drive in it.

**Debrief immediately, on the grass, out loud.** Your memory of the dive is a slideshow with grey between the frames and the grey is still spreading. Have your tender ask the same fixed list of questions every time, in the same order, and answer before you take the suit off. Read your beads. Read your slate. This is the only record that will exist; the cameras come up with stills and grey, and the audio comes up as clicks.

**Then decontaminate, in daylight, thoroughly, and mean it.** Strip everything. Check hood seams, cuff folds, fin strap buckles, mask skirt, the inside of gloves. The fronds are as fine as fennel and they cling, and a traveller who carries a fragment of duration-eating weed out of this world and into a wetter, warmer one is not an explorer but a vector. Burn what you find, on the spot, and do not take this kit onward to another world without a season in between. If you find a shrimp in a fold, it will not rot, and it will hang in a jar in your house like a comma for as long as you own the jar, and you should think carefully about whether you want that.

## On when to come

The break is only months old and still sharp enough to cut a hand. The grass roots are white and drinking. The corner past the doorframe has only just begun to lean.

None of that lasts. In a decade the fracture will be rounded, the roots will be gone, and that corner will have a different floor than the rest of the room, and the weed will have grown sideways into it without commenting. The room is two hand-widths larger in every direction than it was in February, and it is not in a hurry, and it is not going to stop.

Come in late August, if you can choose. Late in the afternoon. Stand at the plank and look at the yellow going through the blackthorn leaves and into the brown water, and understand that you are watching it being collected.

Then go down, and touch four things, and come back up.
