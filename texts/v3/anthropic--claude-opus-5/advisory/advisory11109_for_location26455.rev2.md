# Preparing for the Flat Above the Weather

## First, an honest framing

Most hostile places kill you with an event. This one has no events in the sense you mean. It kills by continuation — by being the same for longer than you can be the same. Everything on the list below exists to preserve a boundary: the line between you and the substrate. The plateau does not attack that line. It simply doesn't recognize it, and given time, it is right.

Budget your visit in days, not weeks. Everything else follows from that.

---

## The body

**Altitude.** Three thousand meters is not extreme. Three thousand meters with no possibility of descent is. There is no "get them down" on this plateau; the nearest loss of altitude is two hundred kilometers away and probably isn't real, given how distances behave here. Acclimatize *fully* before you step through — a minimum of seven to ten days at or above three thousand, in your own world, with a pulse oximeter and a log. Carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, and a portable hyperbaric bag if your party can bear the weight. It is the single heaviest item that might justify itself.

The specific danger: at ninety-six percent humidity, every early symptom of pulmonary edema is also just *what breathing is like here*. Wet lungs, air hunger, the sense of drinking rather than inhaling, a cough that produces something. Everyone will have all of this on the first day and nobody will be able to tell who is sick. Therefore you do not diagnose by feel. You take oximeter readings on a fixed schedule, twice daily, written down, and you enforce a numerical turn-back threshold agreed before entry by people who were warm and dry when they agreed it.

**Wet.** You will never dry. Understand this as a physical law, not a complaint. There is no sun, no wind worth the name, no dry surface, no dry air to evaporate into. Nothing you wet gets un-wet until you leave.

This changes clothing doctrine completely. You dress the way polar travelers dress for long cold-wet work: **vapor barrier against the skin.** A thin impermeable layer worn next to the body, so your own sweat stays on you instead of migrating out and destroying your insulation. Over it, wool or high-loft synthetic that insulates when soaked. Over that, a shell you accept will be beaded with condensate inside and out, permanently, because the moisture isn't coming from the sky, it's coming from *everywhere at once*, including you.

Cotton is a suicide note. Down is a joke.

**Feet.** Immersion foot is the most likely thing to end your expedition and the most likely thing to maim you permanently. You are walking in standing water for the entire visit. Vapor-barrier socks, a boot you can drain, and a religious nightly ritual: one hour, inside shelter, feet out, dried with a chamois, powdered, inspected by someone who is not you, then sealed into a sacrosanct pair of dry socks that never touch the ground. If the dry socks get wet, that is a serious incident. Treat it as one.

**Skin.** The same logic everywhere the body folds. Macerated skin is breached skin, and this is not a place where you want a breach. The amphibians carry a photosynthetic film in their hide and think nothing of it. Whatever gives them that film is in the water, on the moss, in the air you are drinking. Keep your barrier intact. Check the groin, the armpits, behind the ears, between the toes, daily, out loud, with a partner.

**Water and salt.** The plateau is saturated with water that is acidic, tannic, nearly sterile, and nearly mineral-free. You can drink it filtered and you will not get sick. You *will*, over days, wash your electrolytes out of yourself, because peat water is essentially distilled and you are drinking a great deal of it. Bring salt. Bring more salt than you think. Cramping, apathy, and confusion at day four are almost always sodium and almost never mysticism.

(There is an argument among the few people who've written about this place that drinking the plateau's water accelerates incorporation. There's no evidence. There's also no evidence against it. Carry enough of your own water for the first forty-eight hours if it lets you sleep.)

## The ground

There is nothing to stand on. Absorb that operationally.

- **No anchors.** No pitons, no rock, no dead-man in soil, no tree. Your shelter must be self-tensioning or staked into peat with long flukes that will creep. Assume every guyline loosens overnight.
- **No dry seat, no dry table, no dry floor.** Bring a platform. A closed-cell foam raft, a lightweight sled with a deck, something you can set a stove on, sit on, sleep on, and drag. This item will feel absurd on the packing list and will be the most valuable object you own by day two.
- **Flotation for the feet.** Bog shoes — wide splayed pads, the grazers have the right idea — or you will exhaust yourself in the first three kilometers. Fifteen kilos of gear on a quaking surface at three thousand meters in saturated air is a brutal energy budget. Plan on eight kilometers a day and be pleased with six.
- **Pools.** The flats look uniform and are not. Between hummocks there are pools with no bottom you'd care to find, and there is no rock strainer, no bank, nothing to grab. Probe ahead with a pole. Move roped when the surface begins to shiver at more than an arm's length from your footfall.
- **Runner-roots.** The boilers pump sap kilometers out under the peat, hot. You will occasionally cross a line of ground that is warm and firm and strains with a sound like a ship's cable. It is tempting to walk along one — better footing, warmer. Do it briefly if you must. Do not camp on one. Runners under load fail, and when they fail they whip.

**Fire.** Wet peat won't burn. Dry peat burns for years, invisibly, underground, unstoppable. Your stove is the only thing in your possession capable of doing durable harm to a place that has worn smooth every other vulnerability. Cook on the platform, on a base plate, never on the surface, never in a hollow, never near a weeping dome where the moss has locally dried at the crown. This is the one discipline the plateau can't repair around you on its own schedule, and you should keep it for reasons of simple decency.

## Navigation

Forget metric. The plateau has more inside it than around it. Closed traverses won't close, dead reckoning accumulates nonsense, and any map you draw will be a map of your route rather than of the country. Do not fight this. People who fight this walk inward.

**Navigate by sound.** Every boiler has a voice — the whistle pitch of its turbine shafts, the tempo of its piston knock, the interval and depth of its valve cough. From any point you hear four at once. This is not scenery; this is your instrument panel. Before you travel, sit at each boiler you intend to use and *learn it*: record it, listen to it, name it, be able to pick it out of the mix blindfolded. Carry a good directional microphone and a contact mic. Your bearing to three known voices is a fix, and it's the only kind of fix that behaves.

**Travel boiler to boiler. Never into the gaps.** The engines are three to eight kilometers apart. That is a day's move with margin. The emptiness between is where the excess interior lives. If you find yourself equidistant from everything and the voices have gone vague, stop, sit down, and wait for a valve. Do not improvise a shortcut.

**Mark by ear, not by eye.** There are no cairns. A flag is invisible at four hundred meters in green fog and will be incorporated within the year regardless. Depots and entry points should carry a wind chime, a whistle, a small mechanical noisemaker — something the plateau's own murmur can't swallow. Moor your entry point to a boiler if you possibly can. Retracing your steps does not guarantee arriving where you started; hearing your own bell does.

**The plumb line.** Bring one. It works. A weight on a cord is the only instrument on the plateau that has never once been reported to misbehave, and after two days of no verticals — no horizon break, no edge, no bone — your visual system will start manufacturing them at the edge of sight. A real vertical you can hold in your hand is worth more than its mass. So is a stone. Carry a small mineral thing in a pocket: granite, quartz, anything. It is the only rock on the plateau. It is also, and this is not a joke, the item most people report gripping when they needed to.

**The pilgrims.** The small dun animals all move one way, always, everywhere, and do not arrive. Whatever they are, they are the visible shape of a one-way leak in the local causality. Cross their line perpendicular whenever you can. If you must travel parallel, **travel against them.** It is slower, it feels wrong, it is uphill in a way that has nothing to do with slope, and it keeps you the kind of thing that comes back. Do not walk with them for more than a few hundred meters "to see." The seeing is not the problem. The wanting-to-continue is.

## The boilers

They are the only warmth in a hundred kilometers and your best shelter and camp anchor, and they explode.

- **Standoff.** Camp no closer than four hundred meters from a boiler wall unless you are hypothermic and making a deliberate trade. Ruptures throw a hectare of scalding pulp laterally. The vent column goes straight up and flattens; the danger from a *healthy* boiler is the overpressure valve, and the danger from a *sick* one is everything.
- **Reading a sick engine.** The hoop bands look strained, no longer flush. There is weeping at a seam that isn't the general sweat of the country — it's clear, it's warm, it's sticky. The turbine pitch has been creeping up over hours. And the smell: the ordinary boiler smell is hot bread and cider and tannin. A *sweet* smell, a sharp one, a vinegar edge — that's a chamber going wrong.
- **The birds are your alarm.** They nest inside the moving parts, and minutes before a wheel seizes they leave, reliably, for reasons nobody can account for. A seized wheel is the front end of an overpressure event. **If the shaft birds come out, you go.** No discussion, no packing, no retrieving the stove. This rule has to be pre-agreed and pre-drilled, because when it fires you will be asleep in the amphibian warmth and your body will not want to believe a bird.
- **Steam.** The vent columns are live steam at two hundred meters and they hang, near the throat, in the shape they will make before they make it. Do not read that hanging as a safe outline. It is a preview, not a boundary.
- **The amphibian carpet.** Thousands of animals on the lower walls for the heat. Harmless. They will go absolutely silent when a valve blows, and then resume all at once, and the resumption is physically staggering at close range — a wall of sound that can genuinely unbalance you on soft ground. Sit down before you're startled off your feet. Bring earplugs, but understand the trade: plugs dull the birds, the valve warnings, and your acoustic navigation. Use them only inside a shelter with someone else awake.

## The thinning

This is the part you cannot train for, only rehearse procedures for. Agree the procedures before entry, in writing, in your own handwriting, and carry the paper.

**On meeting yourself, or a duplicate of a companion.** Two of a thing are both real and both the only one, and within an hour there is one and no wound. The doctrine, such as it is: *do not interfere.* Do not touch the other. Do not warn it. Do not try to establish which is original — there isn't an answer and looking for one is how people come apart. Both of you continue your existing plans. Set, before entry, a standing rendezvous rule: a fixed time, a fixed boiler. Whoever is there is you. Whoever is there is your friend. Accept it and make tea.

**On speech near the throats.** Your sentences will begin arriving a half-beat before you commit to them. The specific harm here is not the pre-echo; it's stopping. Abandon a sentence within earshot of a vent and you will hear the rest of it finished in your own voice, saying the thing you decided against, and people do not recover their footing quickly from that. So: **finish every sentence you start.** If you don't want to finish it, don't start it. Within a kilometer of a throat, prefer hand signals and a slate. Establish the signal set before entry and drill it until it's boring.

**On recording.** Your devices will capture things that happened twice, or in the wrong order, or that didn't happen. Timestamps are not trustworthy. Run redundant recorders, label everything by *event* rather than by clock, and — this matters — **do not attempt to reconcile discrepancies in the field.** Seal the data. Reconcile at home, in a room with corners. Field reconciliation is the single most common precursor to someone deciding not to leave.

**On time.** There is no dark and no day. The light thickens and thins on a schedule your eye can't hold, which means your circadian rhythm has nothing to hook into and will dissolve in about seventy hours. Impose an artificial one immediately and ruthlessly: a bright lamp at a fixed hour, eyeshades and plugs at a fixed hour, meals on the clock whether you're hungry or not, a written log entry at fixed intervals whether anything happened or not. The log is not for science. The log is a thread back to sequence.

## Knowing when to leave

Nothing here is preserved and nothing here is lost. Anything that arrives becomes tissue. You are anything.

Set a hard departure date before entry and treat it as a law rather than a plan. Beyond that, watch for the signs. They are not dramatic:

- A film on the skin where the skin has stayed wet. Not dirt. It doesn't come off with the first wipe.
- Your breath beginning to hang in the shape it will make, out on the flats, well away from any throat.
- Finding your own footprints ahead of you, going the pilgrims' way.
- Dreaming the murmur — the whole surface draining into the whole surface — and waking rested.
- And the reliable one, the one that comes first and gets missed: **the flatness stops being unbearable.** On arrival, the absence of verticals is a low-grade misery; your whole visual system is hunting for bone and finding only body. If one morning you notice that the horizon running off unbroken in every direction feels *sufficient* — that it feels like enough, that you have no complaint against it — pack. That day. That is not peace. That is the plateau having found the edge of you and started to smooth it, the way it smoothed the mountains, the way it smoothed the laws.

## What to bring that isn't equipment

Something with a hard edge and a made shape. There is not one manufactured object on the plateau — no wall, no path, no relic, no scratched initial — and after a few days the absence works on you in a way that's hard to name. A knife, a watch, a book, a coin. Something that a person decided on.

Something loud that you control.

A companion who will say the turn-back number out loud when you don't want to hear it.

And the stone.

---

## Last thing

Go for the boilers. Sit at thirty meters with the heat coming off a sixty-meter lung of laminated heartwood, in the green dusk, with ten thousand throats going and the water rising and falling in both directions and four engines whistling at you from over the edge of the world, and understand that nobody built any of it and nobody is running it and nobody was ever meant to see it.

It is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with you. That's the whole gift. Don't mistake it for an invitation, and don't stay to be included.
