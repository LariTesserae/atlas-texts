# Briefing for the Drowned Cordillera

## Before anything else: understand what you are

You are the fastest thing that will ever have been in this basin. You are also the first agent of erosion.

Everything you have been taught about "wilderness travel" assumes a landscape that repairs itself — that rain will wash out your bootprints, that wind will scatter your scent, that a current will carry away your silt. None of that is true here. There is no wind. There is no current. There is no rain that strikes anything. Diffusion is the only transport mechanism in the basin and it operates on a timescale of months.

Which means: whatever you do, stays. Where you do it. In the shape you left it.

Hold that in your mind for a week before departure until it stops being a slogan and becomes a physical instinct. Everything below follows from it.

---

## Timing

The Turning runs on roughly an eleven-year cycle. Get the phase from your entry broker and do not take their word for it — the cycle drifts, and the last three recorded Turnings came early. Look for the signs yourself on arrival: a warm bruise in the deep water, interfaces that have stopped running dead level and have begun to sag a few centimeters over a kilometer.

**Best window:** years four through seven after a Turning. The lid-meadows are mature enough to bear weight and read clearly, the water has re-sorted itself into sharp layers, visibility is at maximum.

**Worst window:** the Turning itself, and the four months after. Not merely because of the buckling — although a lid-meadow that tears loose above you is a hundred tonnes of matted leaf descending at the speed of an hour hand, which is slow enough to watch and, in an enclosed cleft, slow enough to be inescapable.

The real hazard is gas. You have fifteen meters of anoxic leaf-felt on the basin floor, warm, sparse in bacteria but not sterile, sealed under two hundred meters of water that has never once overturned except on schedule. There is carbon dioxide down there and probably methane, dissolved under pressure, and an eleven-year accumulation with nowhere to go. When the layers exchange, some of it comes up. Usually gently. Not always. Camp on the highest ground you can find, carry a CO₂ meter and an oxygen sensor with an audible alarm, and if you smell cut grass and iron on a still night, go up. Not sideways. **Up.**

## The air above the water

The basin is at altitude and the air is barely air: warm, saturated past saturation, mist that drips upward off leaf undersides because the droplets are effectively neutrally buoyant.

Three consequences.

**Hypoxia.** Acclimatize properly — two weeks minimum at comparable elevation before you arrive, or bring supplemental oxygen and expect to use it while sleeping. The warmth lies to you. You will not feel like you are high.

**You cannot cool down.** Sweat does not evaporate in air already carrying more water than it should. Above the waterline you will be working in a sauna. Plan all summit activity for the hours the mist is thinnest, drink continuously, and treat any headache as heat illness until proven otherwise.

**You are breathing fog.** Over days, that matters. Take a hydrophobic filter mask for the summit camp and wear it while you sleep. Travelers who skip this come home with a wet, low cough they keep for a month.

Bring a dehumidifier for your gear tent, or accept that everything you own will be damp for the whole trip and that nothing will ever dry. Electronics in sealed cases with desiccant, opened only under the tent fly, never at the shoreline.

## The rock

Freshly made stone that has never been weathered. The arêtes are edge-sharp; the conchoidal fracture surfaces are obsidian-grade. Kevlar-palmed gloves at all times above water. Hard-soled boots, not approach shoes; the andesite will slice a soft sole in a morning.

**Do not anchor to anything.** There are flakes the size of doors hanging off those cliffs held by friction alone and the patient drag of water. They have been waiting. You are a new variable. Tie off to nothing you have not personally hammered, and on the summits prefer freestanding camp systems weighted with your own ballast bags over anything driven into the rock.

If you feel the mountain groan — and you will, once or twice, as a pressure against the sternum rather than a sound — stop, look up, and count. The flake comes afterward. Underwater it descends slowly enough to swim out from under, *if you are watching.* This is the single best argument for never diving below an overhang and never diving with your attention entirely on your camera.

## Breathing, below

**Closed-circuit rebreather. Non-negotiable.** Open-circuit scuba is banned by most reputable outfitters and should be banned by your conscience.

Your exhaled bubbles do not go to a surface. They go to the underside of the nearest lid-meadow and *stay there*, as silver pools, lifting and tearing decades of knitted leaf-litter, and they remain for years marking your route like a slug-trail. A single careless open-circuit dive can put a hole in an interface that the basin will need the better part of a century to close.

Beyond the ethics: you will want the duration. Dives here are long by nature — you move slowly, you stop often, you spend hours doing nothing at all — and rebreather gas is warm and moist, which suits an environment where you will be immersed for six hours at a stretch.

Train on the unit for a year before you come. Train in *caves*, not in open water. You will understand why in the next section.

**Bring more scrubber than you think.** Bring a second unit. There is no rescue.

## The stack: buoyancy, and why the water has shelves

Five or six layers, each slightly denser than the one above.

The practical result is the strangest and most delightful thing about diving the Cordillera: **every interface is a shelf.** If you are trimmed neutral for layer three and you sink a little into layer four, the denser water pushes you back up. You do not hover at an interface by skill. You *rest* on it. You can let go of a camera and it will sit there in mid-water at exactly the depth you left it, perfectly level, indefinitely.

Learn to exploit this:

- Interfaces make ideal decompression platforms. No effort, no drift, no depth variance. The world does your stops for you.
- They make workbenches. Lay out your tools on the boundary as though on a glass table.
- They make excellent long-exposure camera platforms. Nothing moves. You can shoot thirty-second exposures hand-free at eighty meters.

But understand the trap. **Your weighting is only correct for one layer.** Descend through a boundary and you become heavy; ascend through one and you become light. Bring a fine-adjustment buoyancy system with 50-millilitre precision and a written table of your trim setting for every layer, verified on your first checkout dive. A coarse BCD will have you yo-yoing through interfaces, tearing lids, and stirring sixty years of pollen into a cloud that will not settle in your lifetime.

And know that the trap applies to your gear, your line, and — this is unpleasant but you should hear it — your body. A drowned diver in the Drowned Cordillera does not go to the bottom and does not come to the surface. They stop at the layer that matches them and hang there, warm, unrotting, for decades. Recovery is possible and has been done. Carry the depth-logged locator beacon your outfitter offers. Wear it. It is not for you.

## This is an overhead environment

It does not look like one. It looks like an open, sunlit, green-gold lake.

It is a cave.

Above you is a ceiling of matted leaf-litter kilometres wide, and the older mats are dense enough that you cannot punch through them in a panic. You pass upward through a lid by pressing a slit open with your hands; the mat dimples, lets you through, and **closes behind you.** From the other side there is no hole. There is a hundred hectares of identical backlit veined leaf-ceiling and no way out except to find a natural gap that may be a kilometer away in any direction.

Therefore, full cave protocol:

- **Continuous guideline from the point of entry.** Always. Even when the water is so clear you can see four hundred meters and the idea of getting lost seems absurd. Clarity is exactly what kills people here; it persuades them they are outdoors.
- **Take every centimeter of line back out with you.** Nothing biodegrades on a human schedule. A line left behind is a permanent installation.
- **Two-person minimum for any lid penetration**, one above, one below, both on the same line, both watching the puncture.
- **Thirds rule, applied to scrubber and bailout, not just gas.**
- **No markers, no arrows, no cookies.** Navigate by depth and by rock grain. The depth gauge is the truest instrument you have; in a world stratified all the way down, the vertical is the only direction with honest information in it.

## Moving

Forget everything about efficient propulsion. There is no current to fight and nothing to be efficient *for*.

The forest is arranged in tiers that hover a hand's width apart and tear on contact. A fin kick — even a good frog kick — throws a wash that will shred a blade four meters across and destroy a structure that has stood unmoved for thirty years. A diver propulsion vehicle is unthinkable; it would cut a corridor through the strata like a fire through a library.

**Move with your hands only.** Slow sculling, long glides, horizontal trim, fins locked and still. Fins should be small, stiff, and largely decorative — many experienced Cordillera divers dispense with them entirely and use fingertip-and-palm work exclusively, which is slower and infinitely more beautiful.

Budget 150 meters an hour for forest transit. Do not resent this. The place is built at that speed.

Nothing here has ever had to avoid anything. The gliding animals cannot dodge you; the clock-snails cannot be startled; the drifting colonial clouds will not move out of your way. Naivety is total. **You can kill anything in this basin without meaning to and without noticing.** A single clock-snail's grazing track is a hundred meters of pale meandering line and a hundred years of work. One incautious fin-stroke erases it.

## Noise discipline

The quietest lush place you will ever visit, and sound carries in water further than you can imagine.

Your rebreather solenoid clicks. Your regulator creaks. Your buoyancy inflator hisses. Your knee cracks. All of these will be audible, cleanly, from four hundred meters, in a basin where the ordinary acoustic events are the *tick* of a gas chamber equalizing and, once a week, the shearing of leaves.

Tape everything. Silicone-damp every hard contact. Go through your kit on the summit before the first dive and eliminate each individual noise, one at a time; you will find eleven or twelve you did not know you had.

For communication, use a simple tapper and an agreed code — voice comms are unnecessary and grotesque. Three taps carry a kilometer.

## Contamination, both ways

**Outbound (you to the basin).** This is the most consequential aspect of your visit and the one travelers take least seriously.

The basin floor holds fifteen meters of compacted, glassy, barely-decayed leaf-felt going slowly toward coal, because the local bacteria find it unappetizing. That is fifteen meters of *entirely unexploited carbon*. A single vigorous terrestrial mold or saprophytic bacterium, introduced, could find itself in possession of a resource base the size of a mountain range with no competitors. This is not a theoretical risk. Two basins in the recorded literature have been closed to visitors for exactly this reason and one of them is now a brown lake.

Therefore: full gear sterilization on arrival, documented. No organic materials in your kit — no wood, no leather, no cotton, no untreated rope. No fresh food below the waterline and none at all if you can manage it; dried and irradiated rations only. Everything you excrete gets sealed and carried out, including on the summits. No sunscreen — it slicks the interfaces and floats there. Cover up instead with tight synthetic layers and a wide hat.

**Inbound (the basin to you).** The water is thick with pollen, spore dust, and shed leaf-scales. Cross-reactive allergy is the most common medical incident in the Cordillera by a wide margin. Take a full-face mask, not a half mask with exposed lips and cheeks. Carry antihistamines, and carry epinephrine even if you have never reacted to anything in your life. Rinse in sterile water before you unseal.

Do not drink the lake. It is faintly sweet and looks purer than anything you have ever seen and the sweetness is dissolved organics of unknown chirality. Bring water or distill it with heat.

Do not eat anything. There is nothing here that is food, including the things that look like food.

## Navigation, and the false cordillera

Mirror interfaces reflect the peaks upward. Standing on a submerged ridge you will see the real range, and above it the range inverted, and above that a second, fainter inverted range, tiered into the high water. On a good day the basin appears to contain a hundred and twenty mountains. Forty of them exist.

Travelers have set off swimming toward peaks that were reflections. Some have gone a long way.

Correctives:

- **Depth first.** A reflection cannot fool a depth gauge. If the "peak" you are heading for implies an ascent your gauge does not confirm, it is not there.
- **Snell's window.** In water this still the window overhead is perfect and unblurred — a clean circle showing the sun, the mist, and the black wet summits, compressed. It follows you. It is a reliable up-indicator and a decent compass if you know your sun angles.
- **Bearings taken from above water only,** before descent, from a fixed summit station, and written down.
- Bring a magnetic compass *and* an inertial unit. The local field is weak and behaves oddly near the newer intrusives.

## The psychological brief

Please read this section twice. It kills more visitors than the rock does.

The Drowned Cordillera is a landscape at maximum sensory saturation and near-zero event rate. It is unbelievably full and almost nothing in it happens. You will see a gliding animal perhaps once a month. You will hear a sound perhaps once a day. Nothing moves, nothing sways, nothing falls, nothing calls.

Human nervous systems are not built for this, and they respond in a characteristic way, which the guiding community calls **stack fever**: a rising, unbearable compulsion to *make something happen*. To kick hard. To tear a leaf. To break a hanging flake loose and watch it go down through the tiers shearing everything on its way. To shout, just to hear the basin do something back.

It arrives typically on day five, it presents as irritability and a feeling of being *mocked* by the stillness, and it is entirely manageable if you are expecting it and entirely dangerous if you are not. Countermeasures:

- Come in a pair or a trio with an explicit protocol: any member may call a stop, ascend, and sit on a summit for a day, no questions and no shame.
- Train stillness before you come. Meditative practice, freediving apnea work, long-exposure photography, anything that rewards the ability to hang motionless for forty minutes without needing the world to reciprocate.
- Bring an interior life. Books, work, a problem you are chewing on. The basin will not entertain you. It has no interest in you at all, which is most of its point.
- Keep a written log with timestamps. The absence of events makes time feel corrupted; a log restores a spine to the week.

## The stairs

On the north face above the third interface there are eleven regular steps rising to a flat landing that stops in mid-air, and beside them a lattice of twelve guy-threads hung with leaf-litter that reads unmistakably as a rope bridge with the planks rotted through. The water is very still. The reflection puts a second flight above the first, ascending where the other descends.

Nobody built it. There are no makers in the basin. There have never been tools here, or fire, or anyone at all.

You will not believe this while you are looking at it. Your visual cortex is going to hand your conscious mind a finished and confident verdict — *people were here, they left, it was a long time ago* — and it will keep handing you that verdict every time you look, for as long as you look, no matter how thoroughly you have talked yourself out of it beforehand.

Prepare for that the way you would prepare for altitude or cold: not by trying to prevent it, but by knowing its shape in advance. Go to the stairs late in the trip, not early. Go with someone. Do not go alone and do not go at the end of a long dive when you are narced and tired and the mind is loose. Sit on the landing for an hour if you want to — it is one of the great hours available anywhere in the multiverse — and then leave without touching the threads.

And this above all, for the sake of everyone after you: **drop nothing there.** A lost carabiner will not sink to the bottom and will not wash away. It will settle onto an interface and hang, level and motionless and perfectly preserved, beside a staircase that already looks like a ruin, for a thousand years, waiting to be found by someone who will not be able to talk themselves out of it either.

The false impression that someone came through here once and left before the leaves fell is the only construction in the Drowned Cordillera. Don't add to it.

## Coming home

Expect a re-entry period. Most travelers report two to three weeks of what is informally called *noise sickness*: a raw, skinless irritation at wind, at traffic, at leaves moving, at the way objects on Earth refuse to stay where they are put. Some report a milder and more durable effect — a permanent recalibration of what "fast" means, and an unexpected impatience with things that decay.

Both fade. Neither is harmful. A number of people consider the second one the most valuable thing they brought back.

Leave the drone. Leave the music. Leave the urge to be noticed by a place that has never noticed anything.
