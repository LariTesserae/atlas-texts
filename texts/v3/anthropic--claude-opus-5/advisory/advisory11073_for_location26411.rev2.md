# Field Preparation: The Increment

## Advisory grade: **hard**. Not for a first crossing. Physically survivable, cognitively corrosive.

---

## 1. The single thing you must understand before anything else

You are going to a world where *sameness is identity*. Not similarity — identity. If you take two things that cannot be told apart, they are not two things.

Almost every preparation below is a consequence of that one fact, and almost every fatality is a consequence of forgetting it for about four seconds.

The practical corollary, which kills travellers from safety-conscious cultures more than anything else:

> **Redundancy does not work on the Increment.**

Your backup rope, if it came off the same spool as your main rope, is your main rope. Your spare fuse blows when the first one blows. Your second oxygen bottle empties as the first empties. Your two identical carabiners are one carabiner wearing two positions on your harness, and it will fail in both.

Every redundant system you carry must be **heterogeneous**: different manufacture, different material, visibly and tactilely distinct, ideally different design philosophy. Two ropes from two worlds. A steel knife and a ceramic one. A mechanical lamp and a chemical one. Pack as though you were assembling a collection rather than a kit.

---

## 2. Six months out: the body

**Altitude.** You will be living above a cloud deck that has no visible end, at a height where exhaled breath fogs and then *stays* fogged. Whatever that altitude works out to in your physiology, treat it as serious. Eight to twelve weeks of graded acclimatisation before crossing, and plan the first week on the mountain as a write-off — sit on a low terrace near a condenser wall, drink, sleep, do nothing.

**Cold-wet endurance.** Eight degrees, constantly, with no fire culture. People warm themselves at condenser walls the way your ancestors warmed themselves at hearths — which tells you how little heat there is to go around. The air is not merely damp; midmorning fog drags upslope through the terraces and condenses on every cold flange, and *everything you brush against will wet you*. Hypothermia here is slow, undramatic, and extremely common. Train in wet cold. Learn to notice the specific stupidity that precedes it.

**Hands.** Nothing on the Increment has weathered. Plate corners still carry their shear-burrs. Rolled steel keeps the mill grain. Every handrail, doorframe, ladder rung and stair edge on that mountain is as sharp as the day it left the die. Build calluses. Accept that you will be cut anyway, dozens of times, in the first month, in the fleshy web between thumb and forefinger.

**Eyes.** This is the one people underestimate. The light comes from *below* — six hundred kilometres of cloud floor throwing sunlight upward, all day, unrelenting. It is a kind of snow-blindness that comes at you from your feet.

Commission, in advance, the following: goggles or spectacles with a **heavily filtered lower hemisphere**, clear or lightly tinted above, wide side shields, and a matte non-reflective underside to the frame. Buy three pairs, made by three different workshops, for the reason given above. Add a **brimless cap with a stiff lower flange** — the locals' design, copy it exactly, it is three centuries refined. Bring high-factor sunblock and apply it to the places you have never in your life sunburned: under the chin, under the nose, the undersides of the earlobes, the shelf beneath the brow, the insides of the nostrils.

**Vestibular training.** Lit from below, with shadow pooling *on top of* everything, your visual system will insist the world is upside down. Most travellers get two to six days of low-grade inversion nausea and a persistent sensation of falling upward. Vestibular habituation exercises help. So does an antiemetic. So does keeping your eyes on your hands.

## 3. Six months out: the habits

These take longer to install than the fitness does. Start early.

### Stop counting aloud.

This is not superstition, and it is not merely rude. Enumerating identical things in company applies *pressure*. Say *one, two, three* over three concordant cups and something strains — and occasionally something splits, and then you have committed an act that the Nominators train a lifetime to perform deliberately, badly, by accident, in a stranger's kitchen.

Practice for months. Learn to move through the world estimating: *a handful, a stair's worth, about a shelf, enough*. Learn the local quantity-words, which are all vague on purpose. Do not count stairs while climbing. Do not count money at a stall. Do not count out medication into your palm while talking. Do not count reps. Do not tap a rhythm.

Bring no ticking watch. Bring no metronome, no pedometer, no device that displays an incrementing number in company. If you must time something, use a sand-glass or a sundial-equivalent — something that measures without enumerating.

### Learn to notch.

Every stairwell on the Increment is the same stairwell. Thousands of addresses, one object. Climb it inattentively and you will emerge four districts over and sixty metres down, still climbing, with no sense of having gone wrong.

The whole civilisation's answer is **local defacement**: scratch it, chip it, paint it, tie a particular rag to it, rub a particular grease into it. Children are taught to notch before they are taught to read.

So: carry an **address kit** at all times, on your person, not in a pack. Mine was an awl, a stub of chalk in an unlikely colour, a small bottle of scent oil (clove, in my case; choose something no one else on the mountain is wearing), a coil of mismatched wire tags, and a strip of loud fabric torn into forty pieces.

**Rule, memorised until it is reflex: mark the door you came out of, before you walk away from it.**

Practise the motion at home until you can do it without looking, with wet cold hands, in gloves.

### Learn to breathe irregularly.

Odd, but locals do it and there is a reason. Your breath fog lingers here far longer than it should — many identical exhalations are, in the way of this world, arguably one exhalation with many addresses. In a crowded dormitory the air whites out. Locals breathe in broken, arrhythmic patterns, without thinking about it, and a room full of visitors breathing in tidy metronomic rhythm will fog itself blind in twenty minutes and then get a lecture. Practise. It feels insane for about a week and then becomes automatic.

### Learn to be quiet.

Ten thousand machines run on that mountain and produce one machine's worth of noise. You will be, without exaggeration, one of the loudest objects in the world. A dropped pan is a district-wide event. A laugh carries three terraces. A sneeze turns heads at a rolling mill.

Train yourself: set things down, never drop them. Speak at half your volume. Do not hum. Leave behind anything that buzzes, beeps, vibrates, or plays. Bring soft-soled overshoes for interiors.

## 4. The kit: what to bring, and how to ruin it first

**Deface everything before you cross.** All of it. Every item in your pack should be unmistakably itself: different laces in each boot, mismatched gloves (buy two pairs in two colours and wear one of each), socks dyed four different shades, each mess tin dented in a unique place, each strap a different weave. Anything factory-identical to anything else you own is a liability.

Expect to be stopped on arrival by an inspector from a maintenance guild — they are the most feared and best-fed people on the mountain, for good reason — and expect your kit to be gone through item by item and marked. Do not argue. An unaddressed foreign object in a concordant industrial complex is, from their side, roughly what an unlabelled vial is to a biosecurity officer. Items they cannot address, they will keep.

**Medication requires special handling.** A blister strip of thirty identical tablets is not thirty doses. What it is, exactly, is disputed; what is *reported* is that travellers have taken one tablet and received either nothing or everything. Have your medications **compounded singly** before you go — hand-rolled, each dose visibly different, each marked, ideally in a sequence of shapes or colours. Liquids in a single bottle appear to be safe (concordance seems to have a lower size-bound somewhere around the scale of a made thing, though the Nominators argue about it and you should not be the experiment).

**Light.** Counter-intuitive, but you will need a lamp for the *bright* hours, and it must be mounted **low and aimed upward** — at your belt, or on a short staff. The dark places here are the tops of things: roofs, shoulders, the upward-facing cups of machinery, the interiors of any vessel. A head torch is almost useless. A small mirror on a telescoping rod, for inspecting the shadowed upper surfaces of things before you put your hand there, is worth its weight.

**Boots.** Resoleable, heavy, with metatarsal protection and a cut-resistant upper. The northern flank talus — glass swarf, ceramic chips, bright brass curl, every fragment still razored because nothing here has had time to round off — eats boots in a season and feet faster. Do not go on the talus. If you must, go with a guild escort who will tell you where the fans are still moving.

**Do not bring:** matches in a box (they are one match), a camera without prior permission (see §6), a 3-D printer or any duplicator, a firearm, coins, banknotes, anything from a vending machine, an identical twin, or a version of yourself.

## 5. On the mountain: the rules that keep you alive

**Machines are never off; they are off *here*.** A concordant lathe standing still at your address is the same lathe that a stranger forty terraces up is about to start. Locally powering-down, unplugging, or chocking a machine means nothing. Work is stopped by a **stillness**, issued by a guild, honoured at every address at once. Never, under any circumstances, put a hand, sleeve, or tool into a machine because it looks idle. This is the single most common way visitors are killed, and the locals will not warn you, because to them it is like warning someone not to drink bleach.

Similar logic: doors that are locked are locked everywhere; doors that spring open beside you were opened by someone three kilometres away; a fire in one address is a fire in all addresses, which is why there is no smoking, no open flame, and no forgiveness.

**Never enter a stairwell alone for the first month.** Hire a runner — usually a child of nine or ten, absurdly cheap, absurdly competent — who reads the notch-grammar the way you read street signs. Carry a card in local script naming your sponsor and their district mark. If you become lost: **stop climbing.** Sit down. Mark where you are. Wait. Travellers who keep climbing have turned up four days later, ninety metres away, dehydrated and unable to account for the interval.

**Mark your food before you eat it.** Press a thumbprint into the bread, scratch the skin of the fruit, notch the rim of your cup. Do not accept an unmarked vessel from anyone; it is either careless or a test. Aune cheese is good. The thumb-length beetles farmed out of the settling tanks are oily, faintly metallic, and genuinely nutritious. Fern shoots are everywhere. Water: **filter obsessively.** The condensate runs over new metal all day; endemic heavy-metal load is the mountain's great chronic illness, and you can see it in the blue gums of the old. Long stays should be medically monitored.

**Roofs.** Dark, shadow-pooled, unlit from below, structurally unweathered, full of children. Going up there is not forbidden exactly, but it is what children do, and an adult on a roof is regarded as either simple or criminal.

## 6. Etiquette, ranked by severity of the offence

1. **Never make an image of a singular.** The vault holds two hundred and six objects — the total output of a mountain-sized civilisation working for two centuries. To photograph one is, in the local understanding, to attempt to give it a second address. It is the worst thing you can do there. Cameras are best left behind entirely. If you carry one, keep it sealed and declared.
2. **Do not casually produce a mass-manufactured object from home.** This is the trap that catches every well-meaning visitor. Your ballpoint pen has no siblings in this world. It is, locally, *a singular* — a thing that is only itself. Ten thousand machines run for eleven years here to make one spoon. Pulling a shrink-wrapped spoon out of your pocket and offering it as a gift is not generosity; it is closer to detonating something. If you wish to give, give **labour, skill, story, or your time**. If you must give an object, discuss it first with your sponsor, and be prepared for the possibility that it goes to the vault or must be ceremonially destroyed.
3. **Do not count aloud.** Covered above. It is the local equivalent of casual arson.
4. **Do not ask a Nominator to split anything.** They work sleeves-pinned in a windowless hall on the ninth terrace and they go blind young. Do not stare at their eyes. Do not make jokes about being split yourself; several travellers have, and it is received the way a joke about drowning is received on a lifeboat.
5. **Do not harm a sparrow.** There is, in the functional sense, one sparrow. Eleven thousand pairs of wings turning above the fifth terrace without lag or ripple, threading one thin chirp through the mist.
6. **Feed the dog.** There is one dog. It is on every terrace, it is very old, it is permanently and mildly wet, the whole mountain feeds it and nobody owns it. Feeding it is the closest thing the Increment has to a greeting. Do not name it. Do not try to take it anywhere.
7. **Old aunes are people.** The goats scar themselves on the sharp edges of the world and split, slowly, over a lifetime, into individuals. An old aune is entirely itself, is left alone, and may have a name. Young ones are commons. Ask before you touch anything with four legs.
8. **If the bell rings, stop.** It happens perhaps three times a century, at accessions and certain deaths. You will hear it wherever you are, all the way down to the cloud terraces, and you will understand immediately why nobody works the day after. Do not be the foreigner who works the day after.

## 7. The personal hazard nobody warns you about

You are a multiverse traveller. That makes you the most singular object on the mountain — no siblings, no pattern, no addresses but the one. Two things follow.

First, you are safe from concordance in a way the locals are not, and you will be tempted to get sloppy about the rules. Don't; the rules also protect *them* from you.

Second, you are, by their reckoning, worth more than a district. A few people will want to interview you. A very small number, in the long history of such visits, have wanted something more permanent, and there is a low room with a real stone floor where things that are only themselves are kept on shelves. Travel with a sponsor. Sleep where you are known.

And the precaution that has no exceptions: **never let two of you be there.** Not simultaneously, not sequentially, not in different years, not a twin, not a clone, not a close variant self from an adjacent line. What happens is disputed and the accounts are bad. Deface yourself before you go, in any case — a scar, an asymmetric haircut, a distinctive smell you maintain daily, a habit of speech. Locals do this instinctively. You should too.

There is also a soft hazard, and it is the reason experienced crossers cap stays at about three months. Travellers report **thinning**: a creeping sense of being a type rather than a person, of one's gestures belonging to a pattern that one merely occupies. It is not dangerous physically. It is deeply unpleasant. The treatment is the local one — small violent acts of distinction. Keep a journal in your own handwriting. Keep one object that is unmistakably, uselessly yours. Say your own name aloud, alone, once a day. Get cut, occasionally, and let the scar stay.

## 8. Coming back

Your gear will now be covered in addresses. Declare all of it. Anything you acquired on the mountain that has sharers must stay — removing one address of a concordant object is understood as theft of the entire object, and the guilds are unsentimental about it. Do not buy a "singular" from a market; they are fakes, and buying one insults every person who spent a generation making a real one.

Then budget a month for the return.

You will not be able to count out change. You will find yourself notching your cutlery at home, scratching your own front door, tying a rag to a lamppost. You will walk into a supermarket and see an aisle of two hundred identical boxes and feel something in your chest go quiet and wrong, and you will have to leave.

This passes. Mostly.

## If you remember only five things

1. **Redundancy must be heterogeneous.** Two identical things are one thing.
2. **Mark the door before you walk away from it.**
3. **Never count aloud.**
4. **A machine that is stopped here is not stopped.**
5. **Your cheap spoon is a holy object. Keep it in your pocket.**
