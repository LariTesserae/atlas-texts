# Preparing for the Shelf

## First, correct your idea of what you are visiting

You are not going to a landscape. You are going to a temperature, and a machine, and a hiss.

Almost everyone who plans a trip like this plans for the water and forgets the *duration* of the water. There is no beach to sit on at the end of the day. There is no tent floor, no dry sock, no ten minutes by a fire. From the moment you arrive to the moment you leave, you are standing in cold water up to your navel, breathing wet air, in light the strength of a bathroom at three in the morning. Every problem you have there will be a slow problem. Nothing on the Shelf is trying to hurt you. It will simply keep subtracting from you at a steady rate, and your entire preparation is the arithmetic of making your reserves outlast your stay.

Budget a year of preparation for a month on the Shelf. That ratio is not conservative.

## The body, twelve months out

**Arrive fat.** This is not a comfort measure. Assume the water is somewhere between seven and eleven degrees — "cold enough to ache" means cold enough that immersed skin stops complaining and starts going quiet, which is worse. You will be immersed to the waist continuously for weeks, which means roughly half your surface area is bleeding heat into an infinite heat sink, forever, with no recovery period. You cannot shiver your way through that; shivering is a sprint and you need a distance runner. What you need is subcutaneous insulation on your thighs, hips and lower back, and a metabolism you have trained to eat freely without complaint.

Put on ten to fifteen kilos of ordinary fat over the preceding year. Do it with a doctor who understands you are not asking for advice about aesthetics. You will lose all of it and more, and the losing is the plan.

Alongside that: **cold-water habituation**, three or four sessions a week, in a pool or tank chilled to your target temperature, standing waist-deep rather than swimming. You are not trying to become tough. You are training out the initial cold-shock response — the gasp, the hyperventilation, the panicky peripheral clamp-down — because on the Shelf you will step off a weir lip into shoulder-deep sunken course at some point, and the gasp is what drowns people in a metre of water.

**Feet.** Nine months of barefoot walking, escalating. The stone of the Shelf is kind — every corner spent, every block a low dome — but you will be walking on it with skin that has been waterlogged for a fortnight, which is skin with the tensile strength of wet bread. Boots are the wrong answer twice over: they fill with peat grit and grind your soles into ulcers, and if they are leather you have strapped a dead animal to your feet in a world that has no animals and no word for hunting. Go barefoot. Go barefoot for months beforehand, on cobbles, on wet cobbles, until your soles are thick and your toes can grip. You will need the toes. The grooves between blocks are toe-hooks, and the walk there is a rolling gait — dome, groove, dome — that the feet learn faster than the mind. Learn it early. **Your gait, not your face, is what marks you as impossible.**

**Gait training** deserves its own hours: chest-deep or waist-deep walking against resistance. Rig a pool with cobbles and hang bolts of loose cloth in it to imitate the black hair-forest, which does not part for you so much as tolerate you. Real Shelf walking speed is perhaps a kilometre an hour and costs you as much as an hour's hillwalking. Recalibrate every distance you had in mind. A "visit to the Shelf" is a visit to one province of one terrace-band, and even that is ambitious.

**Ears.** The thing most likely to end your trip is not hypothermia or the wells. It is otitis externa. Water in the ear canal, warm, dark, forever. Get your ears examined and cleaned, and pack a great deal more acidified drying drops than seems reasonable — and understand they only work if you can get the canal dry, which you can't, so the regime is prophylactic and religious: after every full submersion, drops, tilt, wait.

**Teeth.** Every piece of deferred dentistry, done. An abscess out there is a death sentence with a two-week fuse, because there is no evacuation that does not require you to walk to it.

**Vaccinations and cover:** tetanus above all — you will be handling anaerobic peat with macerated hands. Broad-spectrum antibiotics, and specifically something that covers cold-water skin organisms; chronic wet-skin infection is the endemic disease of that world and it presents as a nothing, a small raised nodule on a knuckle, that is still there four months later.

**Shave everything.** Hair on the Shelf never dries and is a substrate. Head, body, all of it, and keep it shaved. Nails filed very short and rounded; soaked nails tear off sideways.

**Dark adaptation.** Spend the final month living at very low light: no screens after dusk, no bright rooms, learn what your eyes can actually do at the bottom end. You will find you have far more night vision than you believed and that you have been throwing it away all your life. On the Shelf, at the brightest noon of the brightest year, you can see your hands and the pale scum-line under the weed. That's it. That is the budget. Learn to live inside it before you go, because the alternative is a lamp, and the lamp is a moral problem — see below.

## Kit

Design principles, in order: **it floats, it is silent, it is greasy, it leaves nothing behind.**

- **Immersion insulation, waist-down.** Thick closed-cell neoprene long-johns, high-waisted, plus a thin hooded top for the drizzle band. Not a drysuit: you will urinate constantly (cold diuresis is savage there) and you cannot service a sealed suit in a world with nowhere to stand. Bring two full sets and a proper patching kit, and accept that neoprene stinks and degrades and that you will be sealing seams by feel in the dark.
- **A tide-mark solution.** Where the waterline crosses your body, you will develop a belt of chronic chafe and maceration. This is the single most reliable injury on the Shelf. Bring a waist band of smooth, greased, seamless material, and bring far more grease than you think — heavy, non-animal, unscented. Grease is not optional. Grease is the local technology: they press oil from sheetmoss tubers for their lamps, and you will find yourself understanding, quite quickly, why an oil is the most valuable substance in a world made of water.
- **A pole.** Non-metallic, wrapped so it does not clack, taller than you. You will use it constantly — probing for peat sinks, for slumped copings, for the sudden shoulder-deep. Never take a step onto unread floor. The peat in the basins is metres thick, soft, and it is entirely capable of taking you to the sternum and holding you there while you get cold.
- **A tow-float.** Do not carry a pack. A pack drowns you when you go down and it destroys your rolling gait.
