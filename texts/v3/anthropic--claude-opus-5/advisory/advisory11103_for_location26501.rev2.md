# Preparation for a Visit to The Throat, Station 4

## What you are actually going to

Understand the shape of the problem before you pack, because the kit list follows from it and nothing else does.

You are going to a room in which there is no water, no time, no speech, and no room. Four of those are survivable. The one that kills people is the second.

Everything in the Throat runs at a constant: the note, the wind, the wheels at just under two revolutions a second, the hiss, the twenty-two degrees on every bell. A human being inside that constant loses the ability to estimate duration almost immediately. Nothing ticks over. Nothing gets brighter or dimmer. Your own body's clocks — hunger, chill, the shift of light — are all either absent or frozen. The recorded fatalities in the Throat are, without exception, people who intended to stay eight minutes and stayed fifty, and who at minute fifty were not frightened, because by then they had stopped producing the fluid you need in order to be frightened.

Plan the trip around that fact. Everything below is downstream of it.

---

## Crossing in

**Do not translate directly into the pocket.** It is a filled volume. There is no air-space in it to displace and nowhere for your mass to go; at best you arrive interpenetrating a laminated body of sulphur-carbon glass that is harder than your bones, and at worst you arrive inside the flue, where a four-hundred-metre chimney of moving air will take you either up through the cornice gap or down into the fracture-field, and both of those are a closed casket.

**Do not translate into the flue's mouth for the "view."** Several travellers have. The pressure gradient is gentle by mountaineering standards and lethal by pedestrian ones.

Arrive on the **windward side of the divide**, at least a day's walk out, ideally two. There are three good reasons and they all matter:

1. **Altitude.** The south shoulder sits high enough that unacclimatised arrivals are hypoxic before they reach the slot, and hypoxia and dehydration have the same early symptoms, which means you will misread the one that is about to hurt you. Give yourself a minimum of four days at height, preferably a week, with a sleep-low night in the middle.
2. **Drying.** The transition from wet air to Throat air is savage if taken in one step. Walking the divide lets your mucosa adapt: your nose will thicken its secretions, your eyes will up-regulate the oily layer of the tear film, your lips will start to cornify. None of this is enough. All of it helps.
3. **The note.** You will hear it for two hours before you arrive. That is not incidental — it is the acclimatisation you need most. Travellers who come in fast and meet the note at full volume in an enclosed space frequently panic, and a panicking body in the pocket does enormous damage to something that has never been damaged.

**Cross in dry.** No dripping gear, no wet boots, no full hydration bladder slung outside a pack where it can weep at a seam. Whatever you bring that holds water will be the most interesting object on the mountain, and the garden will find it.

## Your body, three weeks out

- **Get your dental work checked.** You cannot speak in there. You cannot report a cracked filling. And the dry cold, the abrasive dust and the constant low-frequency drive are unkind to teeth.
- **Stop wearing contact lenses.** Not "bring glasses too" — stop, three weeks before, and let the cornea recover. A soft lens in the Throat dries to the eye and comes off with epithelium attached. There are people who lost sight in one eye for a fortnight over this. Prescription inserts in sealed goggles, or nothing.
- **Build a hydration baseline.** Not a single frantic litre at the slot. Two weeks of consistent, salted, boring intake, so that you enter with full interstitial reserve rather than a stomach full of water you haven't absorbed yet. Learn what your own urine looks like at 5,000 m when you're doing well, because that is your only real instrument up there.
- **Treat any open wound, however small.** A split knuckle, a hangnail, a shaving cut. Blood in the pocket is water in the pocket. The response to blood is the response to breath, only larger, faster, redder, and much harder to stop looking at. It is also the one form of the bloom that occasionally does not shatter cleanly, and you do not want to be the traveller who left a mark.
- **If you are menstruating, do not go in.** Go the following week. This is not a moral position; it is the same rule as the wound.
- **Cut your hair short or bind it flat.** Every hair you shed is a fibre the size of the garden's own fibres, wet with your scalp oil, and it will be found and used.

## Kit

### Worn

- **A smooth, non-shedding outer shell.** Tightly woven, seams inward, hard face. No fleece, no pile, no felted wool, nothing with a nap. You are going to be inserted into a solid mass and dragged against it along your entire body, both directions. Whatever your outer layer sheds, it sheds in there.
- **Nothing that dangles.** No trailing straps, no accessory cord, no ice-axe leash, no camera lanyard, no loose sling. Tape down every webbing tail. The shaft passes through the dressed wall on a plain bearing and the wheels are on the other side of it, and the only plausible way a human being has ever had, or will ever have, of stopping the wheels is by feeding something soft and stringy into the gap. **DO NOT STOP THE WHEELS** is not a superstition and the fingernail annotation beneath it is not a joke; it is a report, filed by somebody who understood that the plate was the only place to file it.
- **Dielectric layers.** Rubber-soled boots, insulating gloves, an underlayer that does not conduct. You are a bag of salt water at earth potential walking into an economy that runs on charge. A well-grounded human inserted into the pocket is a short circuit across the only thing the garden eats. You will not kill it — it is older and better at this than you — but the local bracts within a body's radius of you will dim, and it takes them a long while to come back, and the polite thing is to go in insulated. It is also the safe thing: at the terminal block you will feel the hairs stand on your wrist, and there are travellers who have had a much firmer conversation with those cables than that.
- **Sealed goggles with a foam or silicone gasket.** This is the single most valuable object you will carry. They will not fog — nothing fogs in the Throat — and what they do is trap the humidity coming off your own eye so that your tear film survives twenty minutes instead of five. Tint: none. You are going into a room lit by corona discharge at roughly the brightness of a match seen across a field.
- **A heat-and-moisture-exchange mask** over nose and mouth, with a fine particulate filter. The filter is for silicosis: the flue carries ground feldspar and olivine, and the spool-fern makes its own silica and sheds it. The HME element is for you, and it will hand back perhaps a third of the water you breathe out. Note the trade: **the mask also prevents the bloom.** That is deliberate. You wear it the whole time and you take it off once, for the breath, on purpose, at a moment you choose, and then it goes straight back on.
- **A soft cap or hood covering all hair, taped at the brow.**

### Carried

- **Two vibrating timers.** Not one. Not audible ones — you will not hear a beep under the note and you may not feel it either through shell fabric, so wear one against the skin of the wrist and one against the sternum. Set them for eight minutes and twelve minutes on your first visit, not twenty. Twenty is the outside limit for an experienced body, and your body is not experienced.
- **A soft, deformable pack** that can be passed through the slot after you and pulled through by a strap. Frames do not go through the slot. Neither do rigid bottles.
- **A wax tablet and a blunt stylus.** For communication that you don't want to leave behind. The stone by the slot is for initials and nothing else; a place with six sets of initials and no dates has been treated well for a very long time and you are not the person who starts writing sentences on it.
- **A partner, and an ankle tether.** The tether is thin, smooth, non-fraying line, tied off *outside* the slot to a body, not to rock, and the codes are the Throat's standard: **one squeeze, yes. Two squeezes, out.** Two squeezes is not negotiable, is not a suggestion, and is answered by going, not by finishing what you were doing. A pull on the tether means the same as two squeezes. If the tether goes slack and stays slack, your partner comes in.
- **A jar with a wide mouth and a tight lid.** See below.
- **A red-filtered headlamp with a mechanical dimmer.** White light in there is an act of vandalism against your own night vision and you will spend the next quarter of an hour, which is your whole visit, unable to see the glow you came for.
- **No stove, no flame, no vented cells, no flash photography.** The flame for obvious reasons, the flash because it will cost you your dark adaptation and because the corona does not photograph the way it looks.
- **No food.** Nothing organic, nothing damp, nothing with a sugar on it. Eat outside and rinse your mouth and spit outside, well clear of the fracture-field.

### Waiting outside, staged at the slot

Two litres of water with electrolytes, already opened, already warmed against a body so you are not fighting a frozen bottle with dead hands. A windproof bivouac shell. Eye ointment. A saline squeeze bottle. Something to sit on. You will need all of it and you will need it within ninety seconds of coming out.

---

## The approach and the wait

Do your business — all of it — at least two hundred metres down the shoulder and downwind. Nothing you excrete goes anywhere near the flue or the fracture-field. The flue is a chimney and it inhales.

Then sit at the slot, in the cold, in the dark, for a full twenty minutes before entering, and do nothing. This is the dark adaptation and it is also the last adjustment to the note. Notice, while you sit, that you have stopped being able to tell whether the note is outside you. That's normal. It is the reason you cannot speak in there; the tone sits exactly at the bottom of the human voice and eats it, and the first time you try to say something and hear nothing come out is unpleasant enough that it is better to have expected it.

---

## Going in

Shoulder first. Left arm extended over your head, head turned, right arm at your side, and then a long slow corkscrew. The pack goes after you, on the strap, pulled in hand over hand. Practise this before the trip against something with real friction — a packed haybale, a sleeping bag stuffed to bursting and pinned between two boards — because the sensation is genuinely unlike anything in ordinary life and the time to discover how you respond to it is not four hundred metres above a fracture-field.

The growth will close behind you and take hold of you along your whole body at once at about the pressure of a firm handshake. It is dry, it rasps, and it is not doing anything to you; it simply fills the volume you are trying to occupy and has nowhere to go. **Do not thrash.** A thrashing body in there breaks stems in the dozens, and a stem breaks like a cigarette and does not grow back, and it also wedges you, and the extraction procedure for a wedged traveller is to cut the garden, with a fine saw, for as long as it takes.

Breathe against the pressure. It will not let your chest expand as far as you would like. This is survivable and it feels, for the first thirty seconds, like it is not.

---

## Inside: the protocol

- **Move only your eyes for the first minute.** The chamber will resolve out of the dark as your pupils open and the corona comes up: a shell-coloured light, violet at the fibre tips going to pale rose, and under the mountain's note the second sound, the hiss like sand on paper, and inside the hiss the tick of every crossing bract. Give yourself that minute. It is most of what you came for.
- **Touch nothing structural.** Not the bells, not the fern coils, not the foil-bract in the high corner. Do not test the stems. Someone always wants to know what the grey powder looks like and the answer is in every account ever written and does not require a fresh casualty.
- **The station is for looking at.** Read the maker's ghost on the dynamo casing with your fingertips, by all means; that is what it is for now. Do not touch the terminal block. Do not touch the shaft. Do not put anything — not a hand, not a light, not a glove, and above all not a line — past the dressed wall.
- **Leave the artefacts.** The glove, the cup held four feet up in the fern coils, the plate. Everything in that room that was ever brought in is still there in the exact posture it was left in, because there is no rot and no appetite and no weather; the assemblage is intact and you are, briefly, inside a complete record. Take one item and the record has a hole in it forever.
- **Watch your timers.** Both. Not "notice when it buzzes" — look, deliberately, on a count. You will not feel the time passing. The lips-splitting at ten minutes and the small dry nosebleed at fifteen are the honest signals, and the tin-and-pennies taste at the back of the throat is the one that means you are already late.

### The breath

Do it at minute six, or whenever you have decided in advance, and not "when it feels right," because nothing in there will ever feel like the right moment; there are no moments.

Mask down. Face forward into the growth. Breathe out, slowly, warm, from the bottom of the lungs.

The bloom comes up in seconds: white, soft, wet-looking, visibly growing, the only fast thing in the mountain and a frank obscenity in a room where nothing has moved for an age. Watch it. It will run under a minute, then stiffen, whiten further, go brittle and shatter, and the wind will take the pieces down the flue and out of the world.

If you want a souvenir, this is the only ethical one: hold the open jar below the bloom and catch what falls. It is yours — it was your own water and your own carbon — and it is the one thing the mountain was going to throw away regardless. Cap it before it powders. It keeps indefinitely, because of course it does.

Mask back on. Now start moving out.

---

## Coming out

Reverse corkscrew, pack first this time on the strap, and expect the slot to feel narrower going out than coming in, because it does, because you are stiffer.

At the slot: goggles off last, after the saline. Rinse both eyes. Ointment. Then drink — slowly, a mouthful at a time over twenty minutes, with salt, not a litre in one pull, or you will vomit it and be worse off than you started. Sit for an hour in the bivouac shell. You will feel wrung out and oddly light and rather pleased with yourself, and about half of that is chemistry.

Initials, if you're leaving them, go on the smooth stone by the slot, at chest height, without a date. That is the convention and it is a good one, and after twenty minutes inside you will understand exactly why nobody up there has ever seen the point of a date.

---

## Aftercare, days one to ten

- Expect a grey cough for three to five days. If it is still grey at ten days, or if there is blood in it after the second day, get imaged. Silicosis is cumulative and unglamorous and does not care that the place was beautiful.
- Expect nosebleeds. Humidify your sleeping air. Do not pick the crusts; they are half mineral and they take skin.
- Expect corneal grittiness for forty-eight hours. Expect actual pain if you cheated on the goggles.
- Expect the note. This is the part people do not warn each other about, out of a sort of embarrassment. You will hear it for two to three weeks — in extractor fans, in tyre noise, in the pause before sleep — and it will not be unpleasant, and it will fade, and a fortnight after it has gone you will notice that you miss it.

---

## The actual hazard

It is not the dehydration. Dehydration is arithmetic and you can do arithmetic.

It is that the Throat is a fixed point, and multiverse travellers are, as a population, unusually badly equipped to be near one. You spend your working life in places that are going somewhere — accumulating, decaying, ripening, falling apart, at minimum getting later. Here is a room that took everything erosion could take a long time ago and arrived at final form, and is now simply at rest: loud, cool, glowing, packed to the walls, not waiting and not sleeping and not going on. You put your face in it and it converts your breath into something extravagant and joyful for fifty seconds and then sheds it and is precisely, to the last nine centimetres of quill-moss and the last twenty-two degrees of bell, exactly what it was.

Nothing added. Nothing owed.

People find this intolerable and people find it the most restful thing they have ever encountered, and a surprising number of them find it both, and those are the ones who come back, and keep coming back, and start shaving the twenty minutes up to twenty-five and then thirty because there was no time in the room and it didn't feel like thirty.

So set the rule before you go, while you are still somewhere that has weather, and give the rule to your partner rather than to yourself: **two squeezes means out, and out means now, and the tether is held by the person who has not been inside.**

Go twice. Come back with a jar of white powder that used to be your own breath. Don't go a third time in the same year.
