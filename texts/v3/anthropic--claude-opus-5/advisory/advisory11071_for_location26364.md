# Preparing to Visit the Equilibrium City

## First, understand what kind of trip this is

It is not exploration. There is nothing to find. It is not survival travel, though it will try to kill you incidentally, the way a cathedral would kill you if you slept in it for a month. It is not mountaineering; nothing here needs to be climbed, and the stairs are better than your local ones.

It is closest to a visit to something that is *holding a shape*. Your entire preparation should be organised around two facts: **you are the wettest, loudest, most temporary thing that has ever been there**, and **the place has no interest in you whatsoever, including no malice.** Everything that goes wrong will go wrong because you brought it with you.

Plan a short first visit. Six to nine sleeps. People who plan three weeks come back with something missing from the middle of them.

---

## The body: getting there able to breathe

The range stands above the level where weather can occur. Whatever the local numbers, treat the air as equivalent to 6,000–7,500 m: thin, absolutely dry, and so clean that it carries no information — no smell, no pollen, no smoke, nothing for your nose to do for the rest of your life.

- **Acclimatise for six to eight weeks in your own world** at real altitude. Do not use a hypoxic tent and call it done. There is no low camp at the destination — below the peaks there is only the powder sea, which is not a place you can descend to and possibly not a place at all. You cannot retreat downward. All of your margin has to be in your blood before you step through.
- **Carry supplemental oxygen anyway**, closed-circuit if you can manage the mass, and use it for sleep. Sleeping hypoxic in silence, in dim violet light, in a room with no echo, is how travelers acquire the visitors that walk with them for the rest of the trip.
- **Send a five-minute test arrival first.** Step through, breathe once, take a pressure and gas sample, step back. Do not trust the numbers to be *consistent* — some of the load-bearing relationships up there are not strictly maintained, and altitude-versus-pressure may be one of them — but you want to know whether this is a mask trip or a bare-face trip before you commit a week to it.
- **Know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral oedema in your companion, not in yourself.** You will not notice your own. And understand in advance: an evacuation across ground where a street can take one step or an afternoon is not an evacuation. It is a hope.
- **Cold is radiative, not convective.** The stone is cellar-cool everywhere, at all hours, which means you can sit on it, sleep on it, lean against it without being drained. But the sky at the zenith is nearly black and it will pull heat off the top of your head all day. Hat. Hood. A reflective outer layer. Insulate upward, not downward — the inverse of everything your mountain instincts will tell you.

## Water: the one true taboo

There has never been water here. No rain, no dew, no dissolution, no rounding. Three hundred million years of right-angled cleavage unsoftened by a single drop. That is *why there is a city*.

You are a bag of water at 37°C with a leaking mouth.

- **Sealed hydration only.** Bite valves that self-close, no open bottles, no cups, nothing you can slosh. A spilled litre is a geological event: the first solution feature in the history of the range, a rounded rim on a right-angled edge, permanent.
- **Do not spit. Do not urinate on stone.** Sealed catheter or sealed bottle systems, everything packed out. Solid waste double-bagged, weighed in and weighed out.
- **Your breath is a small rain.** In the hollow interiors, where the air does not move, you will fog the stone. There is nothing to be done about this except to keep your interior visits short, to breathe away from surfaces, and to never, ever exhale onto the crust. Accept that you leave a faint, apologetic weather behind you wherever you sleep.
- **Budget 5–6 litres a day** and be prepared for that to be optimistic. The dryness works on you constantly: nosebleeds, cracked lips, a cough that starts on the second day and stays. Bring nasal saline gel, occlusive lip balm, unscented ointment for your knuckles and the corners of your eyes. Use them sparingly and never let a drop fall.

## Dust

Twice a day the thermal column below breathes, and the streets run ankle-deep with dry grey rivers of powdered feldspar. It parts around your boots. It is warm-cool. It is silent. It is one of the most beautiful things any traveler has ever walked in, and it is Mohs 6 abrasive powder finer than flour.

- **Full-face respirator with P3/HEPA cartridges** for the tides, plus spares. Feldspar dust in a human lung is a permanent installation.
- **Sealed boots, high gaiters, taped cuffs.** Nothing with exposed bearings, ball joints, zips or telescoping parts. Everything mechanical you bring will be destroyed by the end of the week; bring the version you don't mind destroying.
- **Goggles, and a second pair.** The dust does not sting, which is the danger. You won't blink it out.
- **Learn the tide schedule immediately** and never camp in a plaza. Plazas fill to a depth and then drain through the square passages into the interiors, where the dust lies in flat grey lakes for a few hours. If you are in a low chamber when a lake forms, you are in the lake.

## Silence: this is the part people fail

The grazers have eaten this range down to near-nothing over three hundred million years, and they are still working. Within twenty metres of a feeding one, sound does not merely soften — it *fails*. You strike two rocks and feel the impact in your wrist bones and hear nothing, not even the shape of an absence.

What remains is a dry sift like someone turning a page in the next room, from every direction, always. Then your own blood, which becomes loud, then intolerable, then ordinary in about three days.

**Train for this at home.** Genuinely, formally train.

- Book time in an anechoic chamber. Two hours minimum, several sessions, alone. Most people cannot manage forty minutes the first time. Learn what your own body sounds like from the inside so that you meet it as an acquaintance and not as an intruder.
- Do a silent retreat, or the closest thing you can get. Not for the spirituality — for the specific skill of not being alarmed by an unfilled hour.
- **Do not bring music.** Not for reverence; for calibration. Anything you use to plug the silence will make the silence louder when it stops, and up there it always stops.
- **Do not bring a recorder to "capture the quiet."** It comes back with holes in it. People find those holes disturbing in a way that outlasts the trip.

**Communication must be non-acoustic**, because near a grazer you will simply be mouthing at each other:

- A short tether between companions — three metres, hard-linked, with an agreed tug code. Two tugs stop, three come here, continuous pull emergency.
- Hand signals rehearsed until they are boring.
- Bone-conduction units if you have them; they work when air doesn't, sometimes.
- A slate and a graphite stick. Low-tech, dust-proof, silent.

## Light and eyes

The illumination is that of a corridor at four in the morning, permanently. You can see well. You will never feel you have seen enough — that feeling is not an error, do not chase it.

- **Dark-adapt and protect it.** Everything you bring should be red-filtered and dimmable to almost nothing. One careless white beam costs you forty minutes of adaptation and, worse, makes the pale interior crust-glow invisible for the rest of the hour. The interiors are lit by that buttermilk luminescence and by nothing else. It is enough. Learn to trust it.
- **Bring one bright light, sealed, unused, for emergencies only,** and keep it on someone else's harness so you cannot reach for it out of nerves.
- Expect visual instability in the low light: drifting, faces in the joint patterns, movement at the edges. Normal. Sleep, then check again.
- **Do not photograph the peaks for navigation.** Two frames of the same skyline will not contain the same number of summits. Photograph details — a fracture, a stain — never counts.

---

## Time: keep a physiological ledger

The sun crosses the violet sky at a rate that varies and sometimes pauses at the meridian, dimming, as though thinking. Distances and durations have no exchange rate. Your watch will be telling the truth about something, but not about here.

**Your body is honest.** Use it as the clock.

- Ration by *event*, not by day. Number your meals and eat only when actually hungry. Number your sleeps. Number your water bladders and mark them as you open them. Your food count is your calendar, and it is the only calendar that will hold.
- Bring 100% more consumables than your route suggests. A street can take an afternoon.
- **Local clocks that do work:** the twice-daily dust tides, and the terminator. The ticking line of cooling rock crosses a peak in about an hour and it is the most reliable metronome in the range.
- Grow something measurable. A shaved patch, a marked fingernail. Travelers who come back arguing about how long they were gone are the ones who kept no body-record.

## Navigation, or the refusal of it

The city has fewer places than it has locations. You will find the same plaza — same chipped fourth step, same ring-stain, same forked lintel — forty kilometres and two days apart, and if you left a mark in the dust in one, it will be in the other, aged by exactly your walking time.

Names slide off within a day or two and quietly redistribute themselves onto other summits.

So:

- **Do not map. Do not name.** Both are wasted effort and both are how the obsessive spiral starts. Describe instead: "the portico with the ticking that comes late." Keep the description in your notebook, not in your mouth.
- **Navigate by sightline, not by path.** The air between peaks is so clean the next district looks painted on; you can read the joints in its stairs from nine kilometres. Keep your entry point visible. Prefer out-and-back radials over traverses.
- **The dust-mark trick is your only real instrument.** A small deliberate sigil drawn in dust — a private one, five strokes, unmistakable — tells you whether you are somewhere new or somewhere recurred. Use it constantly. Then let the tides erase it, which they will.
- **Never mark stone.** Dust marks are temporary; a scratch in stone is the first true alteration in three hundred million years, and it will appear in every recurrence of that place, forever, because they are not copies. That is the one act of genuine vandalism available to you. Do not take that souvenir either — the flake you pocket is a flake the equilibrium does not get back.

## The thinned rules: standing orders

Above the contour where the crust changes colour, the world stops maintaining distinctions. It doesn't tear. It relaxes. Consider staying below that line on a first visit; there is more than enough city beneath it.
