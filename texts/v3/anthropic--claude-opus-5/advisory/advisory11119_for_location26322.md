# Preparing to Visit the Cistern at the Top of the Rise

## The one-sentence version

You are going to a place where nothing wears, nothing shouts, nothing bites, and nothing forgives — and you are the only animal on the guest list, so plan less for survival than for *not leaving marks*.

---

## Months out: the body

**Acclimatize to altitude.** The basin sits above the cloud deck, and the air up there is described as "thin" and "bright," which in practice has meant anywhere from 2,600 to 5,000 metres depending on which season of cloud you arrive in. Do not arrive fresh off a lowland world and start hauling gear up a hoist. Spend at least a week at comparable elevation somewhere convenient. Carry a pulse oximeter, know your own numbers, and know the difference between a headache from the ascent and a headache from the ozone at the bus bars, because you will get both and they need different responses.

**Then re-learn diving at altitude, in warm water, in slow motion.** If you intend to go below the first weir — and everyone does — your decompression planning has to be altitude-corrected, and your profiles will be long, shallow, repetitive, and warm, which is the exact recipe the tables handle worst. Water at thirty-three degrees does not feel like work. You will stay down far longer than you meant to. Set hard clock limits before you get in, because nothing in the basin will remind you: there is no chill, no current, no sound, no hunger, and the light does not change.

**Train your buoyancy until it is boring.** This is not a comfort matter, it is the central ethical skill of the trip. There is a false floor of suspended leaf litter three metres above the real one, a hundred years in the making, and in places it has thickened into meadow and things have rooted in it. One careless flutter kick will open a hole in that meadow that will take a century to close, in silence, with nobody's name on it but yours. You want to arrive able to hover motionless for ten minutes, turn without using your fins, and travel by pulling gently along a guideline. Practise until your instructor is bored of you. Then practise the same thing while carrying something in both hands.

**Then practise it again across a density step.** The basin is stratified in three layers, and the boundaries are firm enough to lie on. This wrecks weighting. Trim yourself neutral in the warm bright top and you will be corked out of the middle layer like a float; weight yourself to sink through the halocline and you will be a stone when you come back up. Divers who have not rehearsed this get *pinned* — hanging under a shimmering ceiling they can see the sky through, kicking gently upward, going nowhere, unable to call anyone. Learn the trick with the small transfer weights and the two clip-points. Learn it in a pool with a salt layer, not here.

**Practise silence.** Spend some days in earplugs. Spend an afternoon in a float tank. Find out now what your own tinnitus sounds like at full volume, because it will be at full volume, all the time, for the whole visit. Find out now whether you are one of the people whose nervous system tolerates that and whether you are one of the people whose doesn't. Perhaps a third of first-time visitors get some version of what the crew call hushwater panic: the ears keep casting for sound, find nothing, and the body reads *nothing* as *smothered*. It comes on hardest in the weed columns, which close behind you without noise. It passes if you stop and hold a frond and count. Know that in advance.

**Learn the wrist speech.** In the basin a voice dies within an arm's length; the polymer eats it. So the builders speak by touch, wrist to wrist, and they made the system up within a month and never wrote it down, which means you cannot learn it properly before you go and you *can* learn enough to be teachable. Get a tactile alphabet — any of them — and drill it with a partner until you can do numbers, directions, *up*, *down*, *stop*, *wait*, *I am not all right*, and *how long*, blindfolded and plugged. Then drill line-pull codes: one pull, two, three, the long slow drag. Then, and this is the part travellers neglect, get used to being held. Ordinary conversation here begins with a stranger closing a hand around your forearm. If you flinch every time, you will spend the visit being spoken to slowly, like a child.

## Weeks out: the quarantine

Read the card on the jar in the gauge house before you decide how seriously to take this. Seven nematodes and one rotifer, arrived on a leaf, found in a filter basket, and *discussed*. Not destroyed — discussed. A card was wired to the neck, a tally was kept, and the tally has been amended twice, which means somebody counts them.

That is the animal kingdom of this world, and you are proposing to walk into it wearing your own.

You cannot make yourself sterile. Accept that first, out loud, so you stop pretending. Every adult human carries mites in the follicles of the face; you will shed them, and some will land in warm water and die and one might not. What you can do is reduce yourself to the smallest animal event you are capable of being:

- **Gear:** smooth-skinned, seam-minimal, sealable, boil-tolerant. No wool, no leather, no felt, no cork, no neoprene with fabric facing, no hook-and-loop closures — those little plastic forests hold eggs and spores and will not give them up to a rinse. Everything you take goes through a full hot cycle at the station below, twice, with the second cycle witnessed. Zips get flushed. Reel spools get stripped and re-wound.
- **Food:** sealed, cooked, seedless, fruitless. No nuts in shell, no whole grains, no live cultures, nothing dried on a stalk. The crew's own rations are dull for a reason.
- **Waste:** all of it comes out with you. All of it. There is a container system, it is not dignified, and you will be briefed on it in detail by somebody who has stopped finding it funny.
- **Yourself:** crop your hair short, clip and clean your nails, skip the beard, do the nasal and ear rinse they hand you, and use the lavatory before you get in the cage. This is not fastidiousness. It is the local equivalent of taking off your shoes.
- **No sunscreen in the water.** At that altitude, above cloud, on a reflective plate of standing green light, you will burn in twenty minutes — and the basin's surface behaviour is engineered to the part-per-million, and a slick of your emollients across it is a real perturbation, quite apart from what the actives do to liverworts. The answer is fabric: long sleeves, gloves, a hood, and a broad hat on the rim. Glacier glasses, not sunglasses. The glare comes up off the jade as hard as it comes down out of the sky.

Then decontaminate *outbound*, just as hard. The flora here is a catalogue of everything life ever invented for reproducing without a courier — spores, clones, split-and-split-and-split, pollen in long sticky threads that find their way by drifting. That is a description of a magnificent invasive. Do not carry the Cistern's garden home in the cuff of a glove.

## What the place will actually do to you

**It will let you fall a very long way.** Understand the shape: a pale column forty metres across at the shoulder, and set on it a basin you could walk the rim of in nine minutes — a mushroom, wildly overhung, with cloud underneath and no ground under the cloud that anyone has usefully described. The rim is flooded to within a hand's breadth of the top course, so "walking the rim" means wading a slick lip furred with liverwort and moss, with unfrosted glass weirs on your inside and open air on your outside, at altitude, in wind, wet. Nine minutes dry. Forty minutes done properly. You wear a harness and two lanyards and one of them is clipped to the ring line at all times, including while you re-clip the other one, including while you are being spoken to, including while you are taking a photograph. There are bolts. Use them.

**It will drown you quietly.** This is the hazard that kills visitors, and it kills them because it does not look like a hazard. The damping polymer means surfaces do not break — they bulge, dimple, and reseal. Throw a wrench in and it goes with a sound like a door closing in another building.
