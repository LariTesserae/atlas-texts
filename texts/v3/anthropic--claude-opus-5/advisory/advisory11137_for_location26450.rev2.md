# Preparing for a Visit to the Breathing Room

## First, understand what you are to the room

You are three things, all of them unprecedented here: the loudest object that will ever have entered, the brightest, and the dirtiest.

The heat will try to kill you and the chimney will try to keep you, and those are the risks *to* you and they are manageable. But the risks you pose are larger and less reversible. A room like this — one entrance, no outflow, a closed vertebrate population in the low hundreds, an amphibian assemblage that has never met a stranger — is a sealed jar. Everything you carry in, you introduce permanently. Prepare accordingly: the heat plan is two paragraphs, the decontamination plan is two pages.

---

## Six to twelve weeks out: the body

**Heat acclimatization is not optional.** The room sits at 31–38 °C in saturated air. Your sweat will not evaporate. It will bead and sit and run off you and accomplish nothing, and your core temperature will climb on a slow ramp you cannot feel until it is a problem. Unacclimatized, you have perhaps forty minutes of useful function before your judgment starts going soft — and you will not notice your judgment going soft, because that is what it means.

Spend ten to fourteen consecutive days in humid heat before you cross: sauna, steam room, a hot tent, ninety minutes a day at moderate exertion, hydrating deliberately. Acclimatization buys you plasma volume, an earlier and more generous sweat response, lower sweat sodium, a lower resting heart rate in the heat. It roughly doubles your safe working time. It decays in about a week, so do it *immediately* before, not two months before.

**Measure yourself against the slot.** The chimney narrows to a sideways squeeze at seven meters down, and there are six cut steps at the worst pinch because it *is* the worst pinch. Chest depth matters more than weight. If you cannot pass through a rigid 28 cm gap with your arms extended overhead and your chest deflated, do not go. Wedging yourself in a wet, warm, unventilated vertical slot with your diaphragm compressed is one of the ugliest deaths available, and there is no one within a day's walk who can extract you.

**Train your dark adaptation.** Full rod adaptation takes forty to fifty minutes and a single glance at a white light destroys it in a quarter second. Practice sitting in true darkness for an hour. Practice moving in it. Learn what your own night vision is actually capable of, because you will be relying on it and most people have never used theirs.

**Medical.** Pre-exposure rabies series, if your world's bats carry anything like a lyssavirus — two hundred animals overhead, and a bite from a small bat can be missed entirely. Tetanus current. And be honest about immune status: a chamber with a bat colony, guano, and a warm damp floor is a textbook histoplasmosis site. If you are immunocompromised, or on steroids, or pregnant, this is not a place you visit; it is a place you read about.

## Three weeks out: decontamination

This is the part that matters, and it is boring, and you will be tempted to do it approximately. Don't.

**Chytrid.** Somewhere between three and six hundred frogs live in the fern axils of this room, in three species, in a population that has been genetically and epidemiologically isolated for a very long time. *Batrachochytrium* zoospores survive in wet mud, in boot treads, in the seam between a sole and an upper, for weeks. One introduction ends them, and the ants and the snakes and the geckos follow the frogs down. Comparable logic applies to whatever your world's equivalent of *Pseudogymnoascus destructans* is: a two-hundred-bat colony that does not echolocate and cannot relocate is not a population that survives a novel fungus.

The protocol, and do all of it:

- Take **only gear that has never been in a cave, a wetland, or a watercourse.** New, or dedicated-and-quarantined.
- Everything hard-surfaced — boots, harness hardware, water bottles, camera body, rope — gets scrubbed clean of organic matter, then a full-strength disinfectant contact soak (Virkon S at 1%, twenty minutes, is the standard), then rinsed with distilled water, then dried completely.
- Everything soft — clothing, rope, webbing, pack — gets laundered, then **dried at 60 °C for at least an hour**, or sun-dried to bone-dry and bagged. Dry heat is the more reliable killer.
- Bag everything separately and do not open the bags until you are standing at the slot.
- **Fresh boots for the descent.** Carry them down in a sealed bag; change at the lip. The plain's caliche dust on your soles is a lesser matter than what's in the treads from wherever you were last.

**Yourself.** Shower with unscented soap. No deodorant, no sunscreen on your hands, no lotion, no DEET or picaridin, no insect repellent of any kind, no tobacco, no alcohol wipes once you're at the site. You are entering a room where the entire courtship, navigation, and predation grammar of nine hundred animals runs on volatile chemistry, pooled in a stable warm layer under the ceiling. A repellent plume in that layer is a fog horn. And whatever is on your skin ends up in the condensation and then in the pool, which is ten centimeters deep at the rim and holds the only free water in a thousand square kilometers.

Go in olfactorily blank. You will smell like a mammal, which is fine; the room has known mammals. Just don't smell like a chemical plant.

## The week before: weather, and the one thing that will actually kill you

The slot is the room's only entrance, its only exit, and its only drain.

Every few years a surface storm sends a brown column of water down that chimney hard enough to strip the lower ferns off the walls. You do not want to be in the chimney for that, and you do not want to be in the room either, because the chimney is where you would have to go.

So: **know the catchment.** Not the sky above you — the sky forty kilometers upslope. On a caliche pan, water sheets and travels and arrives without warning under a clear sky. Establish, for your visit window, that there has been no rain anywhere in the drainage for three days and none forecast for two. If you cannot establish that, don't descend. The seep running fast is your signal that something happened upstream recently; if you're already down and the seep quickens or the pool clouds, leave immediately, and leave *before* you finish the thought.

Secondary: schedule around the fig. If it's fruiting, the room goes briefly frantic — bats, beetles, ants, the civet-thing — and while the pillage is conducted at the volume of a page turning, it is the one period when you are genuinely in the way. If you want to see it, accept that you should see it from the wall, sitting, not moving at all, for a long time.

## Equipment

Bring less than you think. Everything you carry is heat you have to move and grit you have to keep off the floor.

**Air first.** A dense, warm, mineral exhalation out of floor fissures in limestone is exactly how CO₂ enters a chamber, and this chamber has one opening at the top and none at the bottom, which means no through-draft — just a lazy chimney convection that may or may not be clearing the low end. Carry a **CO₂ meter and an O₂ meter**, and read them *at floor level*, and read them at the northern low end by the pool, which is precisely where a heavy-gas pocket would sit and precisely where you will want to go. Above 3% CO₂ your breathing deepens and you feel it; above 5% you get headache and confusion and you will rationalize both as heat. Above 8% you sit down and don't get up.

Look at the bone shelf when the pool is low. Bones sorted by size, frog and gecko and bird — that reads as water-sorting, and probably is. But if the assemblage includes things that had no business dying at a waterline, believe the gas meter over the interpretation.

**Light.** One dim red or deep-amber headlamp, the lowest setting that exists, with a diffuser, worn low and pointed at your own feet. A single white beam in a chamber whose plants have spent two centuries learning to survive on twice-bounced bottle-green scatter is a flashbang. It will not kill anything, but for the geckos, the moths, the whole nocturnal reading of the room, it is an event without precedent.

Bring a backup light in a sealed pocket. Tape over every status LED, every watch face, every screen. A charging indicator on a battery pack is, in that dark, a star.

**Photography**, if you must: no flash, ever. A small tripod, a fast lens, long exposures on the ambient wedge of sun during its forty minutes on the floor. Skip infrared illuminators — you don't know what reads near-IR down there, and the point isn't the picture.

**Rope.** Seven meters is short enough to tempt you to free-climb it, and the rock is glassy, warm, and *slightly greasy with algae*. Free-climbing it on the way down is stupid; free-climbing it on the way up, heat-loaded and soft-handed, is how people die in caves. Rig a hand line. Anchor problems: caliche and split stone are miserable rock, so use a vehicle, a stake array, or a large deadman, not a piton, and absolutely **do not put hardware in the lip.** That lip is ten thousand rains of rounding and an unknown number of hands. Pad your rope where it runs over the edge — for the rope's sake and the edge's.

And do not run anything through the polished cord-groove at the pool rim. That groove is an artifact. Someone cut it with a water-skin line over the course of generations. It is one of five human marks in the room and it is not your bollard.

**Water.** Four liters minimum, plus electrolytes — you will sweat continuously and absorb nothing back. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst; thirst lags badly in humid heat. **Do not drink the pool.** Beyond the obvious microbiology, that pool is the water budget of the entire chamber, and there are blind white shrimp in it that constitute a lineage. And keep it out of your nose: warm, still, shallow fresh water is thermophilic-amoeba country, and there is no hospital.

**Comms.** Nothing radio will get through seven meters of limestone and a dogleg. Run a physical tug-line to a surface partner with an agreed code — one pull *fine*, two *coming up*, three *come get me*. Have a partner. Have a second person who knows your window and knows the coordinates and knows when to worry.

## Going down

Change boots at the lip. Take a minute up there before you commit; the thread of moisture leaning east in the afternoon is the room breathing out, and it is worth standing in.

The air changes at about the fourth meter. It goes from oven to bathhouse in the space of a body length, and it is disorienting in a specific way: your body reads it as relief for about ten seconds, because the dryness stops, and then it reads it as suffocation. That reversal is normal. Breathe through it. Descend slowly — you want your hands dry-ish and your heart rate low when you reach the floor, because everything after that is subtraction.

Stop on the last step. Don't step off it into the room. Stand there for five full minutes in the dark with your light off and let your eyes start to open, because the first thing the room does, and the only warning it gives, is that it is much fuller than you were prepared for and you will not see that until you can see.

Watch where you put your feet. Frogs, snails the size of plums, whole ant highways. There is no path.

## Inside: the discipline

**On sound.** You will find that you cannot bring yourself to speak, and that is right, but understand *why* so you don't relax into it later. The dome is a whispering gallery — a sharp sound comes back four times, magnified, from four directions. And the animals here have spent something on the order of a hundred thousand years being selected for silence, which means they have no acoustic defenses, no habituation, no category for a loud noise that isn't a catastrophe. A dropped carabiner is, to that room, a supernova.

So: tape anything that can clink. No metal on metal. Move your feet, don't place them. If you cough, cough into your elbow and your chest. Agree with your partner on hand signals beforehand, in the daylight, where you can see each other — because you won't be able to see each other well down there and you won't be willing to speak.

And plan your **silent emergency**. If someone turns an ankle, or the gas meter alarms, or the seep quickens, the instinct is to shout. Decide in advance that you won't: a hand on the shoulder, two squeezes, and both of you move for the chimney. Write it down. Rehearse it once. The room is a very persuasive teacher of quiet and a very bad place to improvise.

**On touch.** You can put a palm on the wall, and when you do you will be covering four species, so choose the spot. Don't take samples. Don't collect the sherds — four sherds, one with a fingernail-impressed rim, and they are the entire archaeological record of the place. Don't clear the fern off the topmost step; that fern is the most informative object in the room, because it dates the leaving.

**On the pillar.** The hollow worn into the largest fig pillar at shoulder height, hairless and dark and slightly concave — that one you may touch. Wash your hands of everything before you come down and then put your hand where the hands went. It is not a shrine and no one asked you to, but it is the only gesture in the room that is continuous with the people who made it, and it costs the room nothing. A hand's oil on fig bark is a thing that pillar has absorbed ten thousand times.

**On time.** Set a hard clock. Acclimatized, at rest, on the *cool* side: ninety minutes to two hours is reasonable. Sitting against the warm east wall where the vents run at 38 °C, much less — that wall is beautiful and it is a slow oven, and the feeling of sweat simply sitting on you is your thermoregulation reporting failure. Take your pulse every twenty minutes. If your resting heart rate goes above about 110 and stays there, you are banking heat, and you have a seven-meter wet chimney to climb.

**The rule that keeps people alive in this room: leave while you still feel fine.** The exit is the most demanding physical act of the trip and it comes last, when you are hottest, softest, and most reluctant. Budget for it as if it were twice as hard as it looks. It is.

## Going up, and after

Rest at the lip before you walk out onto the pan, because you will step from a bathhouse into a white oven and your body will spend a confused minute deciding what to do. Drink. Then walk, because there is no shade for forty kilometers and standing around on caliche at midday is its own separate way to die.

Bag your descent boots. Disinfect everything again on the far side — not for the room's sake this time, but so you don't carry a piece of its microbiology home, or into the next place.

And then: don't publish it. Not the coordinates, not the waypoint, not the photographs with anything identifying in the frame. There is no hill to announce it and that is the only defense it has ever had. A place that survived because nobody could find it does not survive being findable. If you take a party, take two people, not eight. If you go twice, go years apart.

---

## What to actually expect

You will prepare for hours and the room will not notice. It has no mechanism for noticing. Nothing in it needs you to be quiet, or clean, or careful; those are entirely things you are doing for reasons of your own, and the room will not acknowledge them or reward them.

What will happen is this. You will sit down on the cool side with your light off, and for twenty minutes you will be a person in a hot dark cave being uncomfortable. Then your eyes will open up and the count will start — geckos on the west wall, frogs in the fern axils, the moth haze eight meters up hanging like slow brown smoke — and it will keep going past every number you were ready for. The drip will stop being a texture and resolve into individual events and you will find you are tracking them without having decided to. Something will walk across the back of your hand on many legs.

And a frond eight centimeters from your ear will tremble for about a minute and stop, and something will have been said.

The main thing to prepare for is that it wasn't to you, and that this is fine, and that the several hours you spent scrubbing your boots were the only part of the visit that was ever going to matter to anyone but yourself.
