# Preparing for the Sounding Flats

## First, throw away the word "loud"

The single most dangerous thing you can bring into that valley is the assumption that you are going somewhere noisy. Noisy is a concert. Noisy is a jackhammer. Those are ear problems, and ears can be plugged.

The Flats are not an ear problem. At the levels you will be walking through, air stops behaving like a medium for sound and starts behaving like a machine part in contact with your body. It moves your chest wall. It shakes your abdominal contents against each other. It finds the gas in your sinuses, your middle ears, your gut, your bad molar, and it drives that gas like a piston. Your ears are, frankly, the least of it — and they are also the organ you will most definitely sacrifice, at least in part, whatever you do.

So prepare as though for **diving, or altitude, or a radiation field**. Three ideas transfer directly:

1. **You have a dose budget, and it is cumulative and irreversible.** Log it. Respect it.
2. **Never hold your breath.** A closed glottis is a sealed cavity. This is the cardinal rule, and it is the same rule for the same reason.
3. **The environment does not care about your intentions and will not warn you.** There is no signal that you are getting hurt. The pain arrives afterward, or not at all.

Everything below is downstream of that reframe.

## Medical screening: who simply cannot go

Get an actual workup, six months out, from someone who understands blast physiology if such a person exists in your home branch. Disqualifying or near-disqualifying:

- Any history of **spontaneous pneumothorax**, bullous lung disease, or significant asthma. Your lungs are a bag of gas in a resonant thorax. The Flats will look for them.
- **Ear surgery, perforation history, stapes prostheses, cochlear implants.** Anything mechanical in the middle ear becomes a hammer.
- **Untreated hernias**, recent abdominal surgery, adhesions.
- **Kidney or gallstones.** Lithotripsy is an acoustic procedure. The valley does it for free, at times and intensities of its own choosing, without analgesia.
- **Retinal thinning or detachment history.** The vitreous gets shaken.
- **Seizure disorders**, particularly photosensitive or audiogenic.
- **Pregnancy.** Do not. Amniotic fluid transmits beautifully and a fetus has no dose budget.
- **Pacemakers, gas-filled implants, recent dental work, dentures, poorly-seated crowns.** Get a full dental clearance and have every void under every filling checked and packed. A microscopic air pocket under an old amalgam becomes, in an antinode belt, an unforgettable experience.
- No **scuba, no flying, no altitude** for a week before entry. Show up with your tissues in equilibrium.

Also get a **baseline audiogram** and a **baseline chest film**. Not so you can prevent the damage. So that, afterward, you can measure it honestly and decide whether to go a second time.

## The six months before

**Train your core, your neck, and your jaw.** Standing upright on stone in a 40 Hz field, your skeleton is a column with a heavy mass on top, and columns have modes. A strong trunk and a relaxed, unclenched jaw make the difference between coming home with a stiff week and coming home with a compressed disc and a cracked molar. Learn to stand *loose* — knees soft, never locked. Locked knees weld you to the floor plate.

**Learn a tactile language.** You will not speak for the duration. Bone-conduction collars work, after a fashion, because your skull's internal noise floor is lower than the air's — but they are fragile, they fog, they fail, and they make every conversation feel like shouting down a well. Pick a sign system, adapt it for **gloved hands and large arm shapes** (fine fingerspelling is useless in gauntlets), and drill it with your team until it is automatic under stress. Add a **back-of-hand tactile code** for the eight things that matter: *stop, go, down, slide-coming, hush, I'm hurt, look, home.*

**Practice not speaking.** This sounds trivial. It is not. You cannot hear your own voice out there — not the air-conducted part, not against that floor — and the reflex is to push harder, and harder, until you have shredded your vocal folds without ever producing a sound that existed. Veterans tape a strip of cloth loosely under the chin as a reminder. Spend a month at home in enforced silence outdoors. Learn what the impulse feels like a half-second before it fires, so you can kill it.

**Practice writing on wet paper.** Everything will be damp. Waxed notebooks, graphite, never ink.

**Build or buy a lateral line.** This is the most valuable item you will carry and I want to give it its own section.

## The sense you have to purchase

Every animal in that valley knows where it is. You will not. Your body has no organ for reading an interference field, and the "something in you" that starts reading pressure through the ribs after a week is real but crude and arrives far too late to keep a two-week visitor alive.

So buy the sense. A **pressure-tap belt**: eight to twelve sealed taps around your waist and shoulders, differential, feeding a set of skin tactors — vibration, mild electrotactile, whatever you can wear against wet skin without burning it. It tells you the *gradient*: which way the field rises, which way it falls, how fast. Nothing more. It is not a map.

Wear it for three weeks before you travel, in a vibration rig or simply in traffic, until your brain stops interpreting it as buzzing and starts interpreting it as *direction*. Sensory substitution takes about that long. If you arrive with the belt still feeling like a gadget, it will be noise on noise and you will ignore it, and then you will step off a node.

Pair it with a **phase map** of the valley sector you are visiting, updated within the year — the nodal lines drift as the criers walk their pattern seaward — and a **wrist unit** showing local SPL, a running dose total, and a countdown to the next pitch-slide. Carry three of those countdown timers, from three different manufacturers, on three different parts of your body. The slide is the thing that kills visitors, and it kills them for exactly one reason: they were surprised by it.

## The suit, and the trap inside the suit

You want closed-cell foam: a full compression garment, thickest over the chest and abdomen, that damps tissue shear and keeps your soft organs from developing their own opinions. Over that, a smooth outer shell with no flapping surfaces — anything broad and loose will be found, driven, and torn off you, and the edge of a torn panel at 150 dB will cut.

Then: a **helmet with a proper chinstrap**, sealed goggles, deep-insert plugs *and* muffs (not for survival — for preserving what residual hearing you can), a **mouthguard**, and boots with siped soles for the wet gloss stone, which is genuinely as slick as it looks.

And here is the trap. You are standing in still, windless air at thirty degrees and ninety percent humidity, sealed in foam. **There is no evaporative cooling. There is no breeze. There is no thermal gradient anywhere in that valley to save you.** More visitors are carried out of the Flats with heat illness than with acoustic injury, by a wide margin, and they are carried out wearing exactly the protection that was supposed to keep them safe.

Therefore:
- Work the cool hours. Dawn and dusk are forbidden (the slide), so your windows are mid-morning and late night.
- Phase-change cooling vest, with spare packs recharging back in the town.
- Aggressive hydration with salt. You will not feel thirsty — thirst signals are calibrated for dry air — and you will be losing liters.
- Hard time limits per excursion, set in advance, honored even when you feel fine. *Especially* when you feel fine.

## Your lungs are the long-term bill

Here is the thing nobody mentions in the brochures, because it doesn't happen dramatically.

The valley floor is made of mineral that was suspended in fog and shaken out of the air onto every hard surface. You are a hard surface in that fog. Every night, your gear grows a micrometer of stone rind — on seals, on hinges, on goggle glass, in the eyelets of your boots. Brush everything down nightly with a stiff brush and a mild acid wipe, or in ten days your zippers will be geology.

Your alveoli are also surfaces. They are wet, warm, enormous in area, and the air going into them is carrying aerosolized mineral in a fine size fraction at high concentration, all day, every day, with no wind to ever clear it. The long-timers call the result **flats lung** and it is a slow pneumoconiosis with no treatment.

**A properly fitted respirator with a fine-particulate filter is not optional and is never removed outside the town.** Not for a moment. Not to wipe your face. Fit-test it at home, bring three, bring a hundred filters, and change them on schedule rather than when breathing gets hard — because breathing always gets hard out there, and you will not be able to tell the difference.

## Gear rules

- **No sealed cavities.** No vacuum flasks, no rigid canteens with airspace, no glass, no sealed instrument housings that aren't rated. Use collapsible bladders. Fill everything completely.
- **Nothing hollow that can be driven.** A mug clipped to your pack will sing itself to pieces and take the clip with it.
- **Everything damped and potted.** Pack electronics in gel. Void spaces are failure points.
- **Thread locker on every fastener.** Screws back themselves out of things in the Flats. This is not a metaphor.
- **Lanyard everything.** Anything dropped near a blowhole is gone, and anything on your head is negotiable. The chinstrap is not a joke; people lose hats to the chimneys and then lose their tempers going after them.
- **Mechanical watches do not survive.** Solid-state only.
- **Drones do not fly.** Nothing with rotors does. Ground robots need the same potting you do.
- **Microphones are useless** except as pressure sensors. Do not plan on recording anything in the ordinary sense. Plan on *plotting* things.

## The eleven-minute drill

Twice a day the whole field walks forward and for eleven minutes the geometry of the world is undefined. Antinodes move through places that were safe. Standing water leaves the pans in vertical sheets. Every cavity in your body gets swept, slowly, by a search function.

The drill, rehearsed until it's reflex:

1. **Ten minutes out:** stop moving toward anything. Choose your spot now.
2. **Five minutes out:** be in the town, or on a mapped node, or — if caught — in a damp pan.
3. **Get off your feet and get horizontal.** A standing human is a compliant column with a skull on top and it amplifies the vertical axis brutally. Lying flat breaks the column. Lie in the shallow water if there is any; fluid coupling is kinder than skeletal coupling.
4. **Open your mouth.** Keep it open. This vents the pharynx so it can't act as a closed resonator, and it keeps you from clenching.
5. **Breathe shallow and continuous. Never hold.** If you feel the field "find" your chest — and you will know the sensation instantly, it is unmistakable and horrible — do not stop breathing. Keep the airway open. A sealed lung in a moving antinode is how people die here.
6. **Do not get up early.** Eleven minutes means eleven minutes, plus two for luck. Your timers are on your wrist, your chest, and in your boot for this reason.

Rehearse this at home, on a floor, with a stopwatch, twenty times. You want the body to do it while the mind is still catching up.

## Walking, and what you look like to the locals

Stay on the **node-ribbons**. They are the highways for good reason and they are visible from a distance as green against gray. Off-ribbon travel is expensive in dose and should be planned like a dive: in, task, out, on a clock.

**Three people minimum.** Tethered in the loud belts, in line of sight always. Buddy checks every twenty minutes: pupils, nosebleed, gait, and a two-question written exam that you write yourself before the trip — because the first thing acoustic and heat stress take is your judgment, and you will not notice it going. Any disagreement between team members gets written down and settled on paper. You cannot argue out there, and the temptation to just grab someone and pull is how people get hurt.

**Quietholes.** They drift, tens of meters wide, at walking pace, and they are the most seductive thing in the valley. After four days of continuous bombardment, walking into a quiethole feels like taking off a lead coat. Your whole body wants to sit down. Your whole body wants to stay.

Do not. Look at the ground in one and you will see the ring of bones. Everything in the valley that can feel relief comes here, and what hunts here has learned that. **Mark the rule in advance and give one team member the sole job of enforcing it: five minutes in a quiethole, timed, then out, no discussion.** And never, under any circumstances, sleep in one. Nothing that sleeps in a quiethole wakes up.

**The hush.** Dog-sized, low, with a soft dorsal array, and it hunts by being a hole in the world. Its ambush doesn't work on you the way it works on a gulper — you don't navigate by field, so you won't be confused by the absence. You'll be *delighted* by it. Which is functionally identical: you will stop, and stand, and enjoy the sudden quiet, and that is precisely the behavior the hush is fishing for. **Treat any unexplained pocket of relief as a contact.** Back out the way you came, watching low.

**And understand what you are, to them.** You are a body-sized void in the field, moving. You disrupt the wave the way a hush does, minus the sophistication. To a gulper herd feeling for the shape of the world against their flanks, you register as a small predator walking up the ribbon. Do not approach a herd, ever, and never come between a gulper and its calf — the cow cannot see you properly, she can only feel the hole you make, and three meters at the shoulder of confused mother is the end of your trip. Give herds the full width of the ribbon or wait. They move at a walking pace all day; you have time.

Wear something with a broken, non-uniform outline if you can. It muddies the shadow you cast.

## The town

Arrive at the town. Base out of the town. It is the only silence for forty kilometers and it is the reason a human visit is possible at all.

Protocol:

- **It is not a ruin. It is a running machine with nobody in the control room.** The fountain works. The lights come on at dusk, one in six, amber. The orchard netting gets repaired. Behave accordingly: you are a visitor inside functioning infrastructure, not an archaeologist in a tomb.
- **Do not interfere with the maintenance columns.** They walk the arcades and will not acknowledge you. Step over them. Do not pick one up. Do not block a column and see what happens; the answer may be nothing for a long time and then something.
- **The library's reading collars stay in the library.** Racked by the entrance, in sizes down to child, which is the detail that undoes most visitors and which you should be emotionally prepared for before you walk in. Use them there. Return them to the rack.
- **Do not eat the feral orchard.** Alien biochemistry is alien even when it is sweet and enormous and hanging over your head. Bring your own food. The commissioning crew ate it and the commissioning crew were not you and did not have your gut flora.
- **Decontaminate in both directions.** You are a biological source entering a four-century-old ecosystem that has never met a mammal from another world. Scrub, seal, and don't seed the place with your skin.

Sleep in the town. All of you, every night. The temptation to camp out on a node to save a day's walking is the classic fatal economy of the Flats.

## The mind: what actually happens to you

This is the part of the preparation people skip and then regret.

**You will hallucinate.** Everyone does, usually starting around day three. The braided upper register — that cathedral-organ-being-sawn sound — is broadband, unstructured, and endless, and your auditory cortex is a pattern engine that cannot tolerate an input with no pattern in it. So it invents one. You will hear music. You will hear a crowd talking in the next room. You will, very commonly, hear your own name called clearly from a direction, and you will turn your head, and there will be nothing, and you will do it again an hour later.

**Brief the whole team on this before departure, explicitly, using these words: it is universal, it means nothing, and you do not follow it.** People who aren't warned conclude they are losing their minds and start hiding symptoms, and a person hiding symptoms is a person who will walk off the node.

**You will derealize.** Shadowless milky light with no edges, no sun position, no wind, no temperature swing, no silence, and a noise floor that never varies. Noon and four in the afternoon are indistinguishable. Nothing in the environment marks time except the twice-daily slide. Your circadian machinery will simply give up. Counter it deliberately: a bright light at a fixed hour in the town, fixed meal times, a written log with timestamps, and a rigid sleep schedule enforced by someone whose job it is. Keep a diary. Not for posterity — for continuity of self.

**And you will have to be ready for the town.** When you step through the berm after a long day in the field and the sound simply *ends* — full silence, absolute, with gulpers standing in the plaza with their eyes half shut and gliders roosting in the school colonnade by the thousand and the fountain running — most people cry. This is normal. Schedule nothing for the first twenty minutes after re-entry. Let it happen.

## How long to stay

Shorter than you want. **Ten to fourteen days is a sane first visit**, with perhaps twenty to thirty cumulative hours actually out on the loud ground and the rest in the town, on the rim, or in the node-thickets.

Set the dose budget in advance, in writing, with the whole team signing it, before you have felt the place. You will want to extend. Everyone wants to extend. Decisions made out there about how much more of it you can take are made by a brain that has been shaken continuously for a week and cannot hear itself think in the most literal available sense.

## Coming home

Budget a week of re-entry and warn your household.

- **Tinnitus is near-universal** and takes weeks to fade; some of it won't. Get the post-trip audiogram and compare it to your baseline honestly.
- **You will carry a phantom 40 Hz** in the sternum for one to three months. It comes back strongest at night.
- **Silence will hurt.** This one surprises people. After two weeks of unbroken sound, an ordinary quiet bedroom feels like sensory deprivation and many returnees cannot sleep without a low hum generator for a month or more. Buy it before you go, so it's waiting for you.
- **Your balance will be strange** for a few days. Don't drive immediately. Don't climb anything.
- **Go see a pulmonologist at six months**, even if you feel perfect. Especially if you feel perfect.

And then there is the last thing, which no packing list handles. A significant fraction of people who visit the Sounding Flats start planning the return trip on the journey home. Something about it — the ground being made under your feet at nine meters a year, the eleven thousand criers singing a note commissioned four centuries ago by people who built a town for fourteen thousand and then left because the work no longer needed them, the swept arcades and the repaired netting and the lights coming on at dusk for nobody — gets into travelers and does not come out.

Know that about yourself in advance. Decide now, while you can still hear, how many times you are willing to go.

## Two things to actually do while you're there

**Be on the rim at dawn, at a safe elevation, off the stone.** The slide happens, and for eleven minutes the fog in the pans is combed into the standing wave — a hundred kilometers of interference figure, hyperbolas over hyperbolas, sharp as an etching, drawn in water vapor across an entire valley. There is nothing like it in any branch I know of. Bring a wide lens and a tripod you have potted in gel, and then put the camera down and just look, because the photograph will not survive contact with the memory.

**Bring a hand lens and a fine stylus, and play the stone.** Cut a terrace face — a fresh one, a handspan high — and under magnification it is banded in laminae a few micrometers thick, one per node, night upon night, four hundred years of them. It is a phonograph record of the valley recording itself. With patience and a steady hand you can drag a stylus across an hour from three centuries ago and feel, through your fingertips, in a place where you cannot hear anything at all, the exact note the criers were singing on a night before anyone you love was born.

Bring the slice home. Declare it. It is the only souvenir worth the weight.
