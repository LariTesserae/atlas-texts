# Briefing: The Chord Room

## First, understand what kind of world this is

Most dangerous places attack you. This one only *subtracts*. It has no teeth, no cold that kills in minutes, no gradient to fall down, nothing that wants anything from you. What it does is take, steadily and without malice: your hearing, your tear film, the water in your breath, your voice, your sense of elapsed time, and — if you stay long enough — your conviction that you are a separate object from your surroundings.

Plan accordingly. Everything below is water-conservation, sensory armour, and mechanical enforcement of decisions you make while still sane.

Also understand: it is a **completed** world. Nothing there is in the middle of happening. You will be the only event. This is a practical fact, not a poetic one, and half of the protocol below follows from it.

---

## Who must not go

- Anyone whose livelihood is in their ears. Musicians, sonar operators, physicians, anyone with an existing threshold shift or tinnitus. The imprint is real (see *Afterward*).
- Anyone with asthma, reactive airway, chronic sinus disease, or a recent respiratory infection. Cool + bone-dry + continuous airflow is a bronchospasm engine.
- Contact lens wearers. Not "bring solution." Do not wear lenses. Prescription goggles or spectacles under sealed goggles, both strapped.
- Anyone with cracked enamel, a failing filling, recent dental work, or temporomandibular pain. The western drone couples into the mandible. People have gone in with a hairline crack and come out with a tooth in pieces and no way to tell anybody.
- Late pregnancy. The low register is felt in the abdomen, and nobody has good data.
- Anyone photosensitive, migraine-prone with visual aura, or given to derealization. The white sky with no gradient plus a floor of pulsing black circles is a provocation.
- Anyone going alone on a first visit, and anyone going with a partner they have not physically trained with.

## Training, weeks before

1. **Learn the tactile code until it is reflex.** Not written on a card. Reflex. Practice it blindfolded, gloved, cold-handed, and in a genuinely deafening environment — a boiler room, a turbine hall, beside a waterfall, in front of industrial fans. A minimum working grammar:
 - one squeeze on the forearm = *yes / acknowledged*
 - two = *no*
 - long hold = *stop, wait*
 - pull toward = *come with me*
 - flat palm on your own sternum = *I am unwell*
 - three rapid taps, repeated = *we leave now, no discussion*
 - hand cupped over partner's eyes = *close your eyes, glare*
 Agree that the *leave now* signal is never questioned, never overridden, and never debated by slate. Whoever gives it is right by definition.
2. **Acclimate to noise you cannot escape.** An hour at a time. The thing that breaks people is not volume; it's the absence of any pause, and discovering that they cannot hear themselves shout. Discover that here, at home, where you can walk away.
3. **Test your ear protection in that same loud place, seated exactly as you will wear it.** You will not be able to hear whether your plugs are properly seated once you're inside, and you will not be able to ask.
4. **Rehearse a mute task.** Give yourself a job to do in the Room and practise doing it silently, gloved, tethered, with your eyes half-shut. Purposeless presence there degrades a person within the hour. Work is armour.

## Kit

### Ears — double protection, no compromise
Custom-moulded plugs (silicone, not foam; foam behaves badly in that dryness) *under* deep-cup over-ear muffs, strapped, with the headband secured so it cannot be knocked off. If you can bring powered noise-cancellation, do — it works best precisely on the sustained low drone, which is the component that makes people nauseated and afraid.

Accept in advance that **the lowest register will get through anyway**, by bone. It is not a failure of your kit. You will feel the drone in the sternum and back teeth for the whole visit. A soft mouthguard reduces the dental transmission and is worth its bulk.

Bring ointment for the ear canals; in that air, wax dries to shellac and plugs can come out badly.

### Eyes
Glacier goggles or welder-grade shade, **sealed all round**, side shields, dark enough that you can look at the clay without squinting. Two tiers of tint if you can — you will want darker than you think. The seal is doing double duty: it holds a pocket of your own humidity against the eye, which is the difference between an unpleasant hour and corneal damage.

Preservative-free saline in single-use vials and a **gel** lubricant (plain drops evaporate off the surface before they help). Learn to instil them one-handed, gloved, without lifting the goggles more than a finger's width.

Bring a spare pair of goggles. Strapped to your body.

### Airway and water
The single most valuable item you can carry is a **heat-and-moisture-exchange mask** — the kind that captures the water in your exhalation and gives it back on the next breath. It warms the air, it stops your lips and nostrils cracking in the first hour, and it halves your insensible loss. Wear it from arrival. Breathe nose-only if you can.

Water: carry all of it. There is none there, and this is the hard-edged version of that statement — **there has never been any**. Rigid, sealed vessels with a bite-valve or a hose you control with one hand. Electrolytes, mildly.

**Drink to a timer, not to thirst.** Thirst lags badly in air that dry, and there are no other cues — no sweat, no sun, no shade, nothing changes. A vibrating alarm every ten minutes; a mouthful each time.

### Skin
Heavy occlusive balm on lips, nostrils, eyelids, ear rims, knuckles, before entry and reapplied at the halfway alarm. Cover everything else with cloth. Long sleeves, high collar, hood.

**Natural fibres.** Wool and cotton. In that dryness synthetics build enormous static charge, and there is nowhere to ground it: your hair will stand, dust will plate onto you, and — worst — you and your partner will spark at each other every time you touch, which garbles the tactile code exactly when you need it most. Anti-static treat everything you can.

Insulate more than the temperature suggests. Cellar-cool plus constant airflow plus maximal evaporative loss means you will chill faster than you expect and you will not notice, because you never feel damp.

### Feet, hands, and the tether doctrine
Boots with **wide, stiff, flat soles** and no protruding heel. You are walking on an open grid of holes, some wide enough for a fist, and a rounded lip will not stop a narrow sole from wedging. Ankle support. Gaiters — the talc gets in everywhere.

Thin gloves, tethered.

Then the rule that matters more than any other item of kit:

> **Nothing you carry is untethered. Nothing.**

Wrist loops, chest loops, retractors, cord — every vial, every lamp, every slate, every pencil, every bottle, your goggles, your mask, your spare goggles. Empty your outer pockets to nothing. Remove rings and earrings (dry fingers shrink). Cut off loose buttons. Tape over anything that can come loose.

This is not because losing an item inconveniences you. It is because of the next section.

## The prohibitions

**1. Do not put anything down a bore. Ever. For any reason.**

Not a pebble, not a coin, not a marker, not a length of string to measure depth, not a cup of water, not a glowstick to see how far it goes. You are standing on the ranks of a tuned instrument that has held one chord, unaltered, for longer than your species has had hands. An object lodged in a pipe is not litter. It is a *detuning*. It could put a whistle, a buzz, a dead note into the world's one sound, permanently, with no one to come and clear it. If there is a single act available to an ordinary human that deserves the word desecration, it is this one, and it is available to you accidentally, by a slip of the fingers, in the first ten seconds.

Tether everything.

**2. Do not urinate, spit, bleed, weep freely, or spill.**

Your body is the wettest thing in that universe by an unimaginable margin. Void completely before entry; plan the visit inside your bladder's honest window; for long work use absorbent garments and carry everything out sealed. If you cut yourself, cover it immediately and do not let it drip. A spilled mouthful of water would be the first water that world has known and it would leave a mark on the clay that outlasts every civilization you have heard of.

**3. Walk a line, not a wander.**

Your footprints are the only new thing in that world and the wind blows the wrong way to erase them — permanently, not "for a long time." A party of six should leave one path. Single file, on the leader's prints, in and out along the same arc. Choose your route before you're dazzled.

**4. Do not touch the rosettes. Do not take a filament.**

A leaf pushed aside stays pushed aside, open to the sky, forever, in the exact attitude of your careless hand. A snapped filament lies on the clay as a bright thread until the stars go out. If your work genuinely requires a sample, take dust from inside your own footprint — never a crescent from behind a rim. Those crescents are the only clock the world has, and every one of them is a reading.

**5. Never put your ear, your eye, or your mouth over a bore.** The airflow is steady and cool and it will feel like an invitation. Its provenance is unknown lungs.

**6. Test the air before you unmask.** Arrive on your own supply if your method allows. The atmosphere is very probably fine — the bowl is open and continuously flushed — but "exhaled by something enormous you will never see" is not a certificate of purity.

## Light, marking, and writing

Bring a lamp, hooded, tethered with real cordage — but expect it to be nearly useless. The white sky is brighter than anything you can carry and there are no shadows to read. Its only real job is signalling in a whiteout of your own dazzle, and even that fails against the glare.

Ink dries in the pen. Use graphite on a rigid slate, or a stylus on wax. Adhesives and tapes go brittle. Paper is not just unreliable, it is a hazard — anything light will be lifted by the upward exhalation and taken over a bore.

Write your slate before you enter, and write on it the things you will forget:

> THE DREAD IS PHYSICS.
> DRINK.
> WE LEAVE AT THE THIRD ALARM.

## Conduct: the first ninety seconds

Arrive already sealed, plugged, goggled, masked, gloved, tethered. Do not adjust your kit inside; there will be a strong temptation, in the first minute, to lift the muffs "just to hear it properly." People who do this lose measurable hearing and gain nothing, because there is nothing to hear: it is one sound and it does not change and it will not change if you listen harder.

Then, before you look at anything else: **look at your feet.** Establish where the fist-wide bores are on your route. Take twenty seconds. The instinct is to look up at the walls of black mouths immediately, and it costs people their footing.

Then find your partner's forearm and exchange one squeeze each. That is the whole conversation. It is worth more than it sounds.
