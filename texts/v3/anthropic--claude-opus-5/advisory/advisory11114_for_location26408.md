# Preparing to visit the Sill

## First, an honest filter

Before anything else: decide whether you are a candidate. The Sill is not a hard place because it is hostile — it is a hard place because it removes the sense you have spent your whole life outsourcing your survival to, and replaces it with one you have never trained.

Do not go if:

- You have asymmetric hearing loss, chronic tinnitus, recurrent otitis, or a perforation. On the Sill your ears are your eyes, and a phantom ring is a phantom wall.
- You have a coagulation disorder, or take anticoagulants. The stone cuts without pain and does not stop the bleeding for you.
- You have had altitude pulmonary or cerebral oedema, or reactive airway disease triggered by cold.
- You cannot tolerate enclosed unbroken sound. Some people are broken by the shimmer within four days. There is no way to know in advance except to test yourself, and you should test yourself.
- You need to see faces to feel that you are among people.

Deaf travellers can go, but never alone and never onto the open terraces; in the local frame you are both blind and mute, which is a condition they have compassion for and infrastructure for, and which costs money.

## Twelve months out: the body

**Altitude.** Three and a half thousand metres, and the air is thin in oxygen fraction as well as pressure — reckon on an effective 4,200–4,500 m equivalent. There is nowhere lower to stage on, because above the Sill there is only more Sill. So you acclimatize before you travel: eight to ten weeks of hypoxic sleeping, or a genuine high-altitude residency, ending no more than a fortnight before departure. Carry acetazolamide and dexamethasone and know the doses cold, in the dark, by feel, from a labelled-by-shape case. Practise finding them blindfolded, in gloves, at 2 a.m.

**Ears.** Have them cleaned, imaged, and audiogrammed. Get a baseline. Wax that would be trivial at home is, on the Sill, a smear across your visual field. Treat any sinus issue that might swell a Eustachian tube. Learn to equalise silently.

**Blindness, as a skill.** This is the long training and the one people skip. Spend at least four months living blindfolded for progressively longer stretches — not "in a dark room," but blindfolded in a busy stairwell, blindfolded carrying a load, blindfolded eating, blindfolded urinating, blindfolded finding a dropped object on a floor you have been told is sharp. Take formal instruction in cane technique and in human echolocation: palatal clicks, the four-metre pillar, the doorway, the wall that is soft versus the wall that is glass. You will never be as good as a six-year-old Hanthan. You need to be good enough that your body stops screaming.

**Click discipline.** You will be issued a clicker at the gate and told to use it as you would breathe, and this is not a metaphor — it is a legal and practical description. Train the habit for months before you go: a mechanical clicker in your hand, running while you talk, while you chew, while you think, while you carry something heavy, while you are frightened. The failure mode is universal: humans go quiet exactly when they are startled, and startle is the moment a hole in the hologram gets someone killed. Drill it until being frightened *makes* you click faster.

**Fitness, of a specific shape.** Hanthan streets are ladders. Bridges at nine or ten levels, no plazas, nothing level because nothing can be levelled. Train stairs, ladders, and one-handed climbing while carrying, at altitude, in gloves. Train grip endurance. Train quadriceps for descent, because descending blind is slower and costs more.

## Six months out: the mind

**Expect hallucinations.** In total darkness the visual cortex does not politely idle; it invents. Somewhere between day three and day twelve most travellers begin to see things — geometric lattices, drifting fields of colour, faces, rooms that aren't there. This is release hallucination, it is normal, it is not madness, and it is not information. Know this in advance in your bones, because at 3,500 m in the dark, at hour forty of not sleeping, "am I losing my mind" is a question with real consequences.

**Expect the light you actually get to matter enormously.** The sour green of a thick mat. The blue flicker of coldfire. You will find yourself staring at these, hungrily, for long minutes, and you will find them beautiful in a way that embarrasses you. Let it happen. But do not walk toward coldfire: the flicker means new lattice, and new lattice means the sharpest surface on the plateau.

**Circadian anchoring.** There is no day here. Nothing on the Sill will tell you what time it is except the city's schedules, which follow a cycle you should learn cold before arrival. Bring a bright light panel for private use in your own damped chamber, a tactile chronometer with an hourly haptic pip, and the discipline to obey them both. Travellers who let their clock drift free lose a fortnight without noticing.

**The silence contract.** You cannot sleep in the hologram. Nobody can; that is why silence is plumbed and metered. Budget for damping the way you would budget for oxygen: it is the largest single line item of a Sill visit and it is not optional. Reserve and pay for your damped hours before you arrive, in writing. And understand what you are buying: the locals regard a damped chamber the way you regard a windowless cell in the pitch dark, and they will find your appetite for silence slightly pitiable and slightly obscene. Some landlords upcharge for it out of distaste. Pay.

## Kit

Think of the kit as solving four problems: **cold that does not blow, brine that does not freeze, stone that is all edge, and life that colonises everything.**

**Hands.** Puncture-resistant, cut-resistant, over-length gauntlets sealed to the sleeve, with a thin liner underneath and at least four spare pairs. Aragonite needles a centimetre long go through ordinary leather like it's paper. You will not feel it. Assume every glove is a consumable.

**Feet and knees.** Sealed rigid-soled boots with a plate midsole and full gaiters; knee and shin armour. Never kneel bare. Never place a bare palm. Never sit down on the ground without putting something between you and it.

**The brine.** This is the killer that reads as a nuisance. Brine at minus fifteen is *liquid*, wets like water, wicks like water, and cannot be shaken off as frost — it stays on you as a saturated film pulling heat out at forty times the rate of air, and it precipitates crystal inside the fabric weave, turning your own clothing into an abrasive. So: no absorbent outer layers, ever. Smooth sealed shells, taped seams, and a fresh-water rinse-and-warm protocol at every threshold, practised until it's reflex. Immersion — a foot through a shelf into a pool — is a full emergency: strip, flush with warm fresh water, rewarm, and do not walk it off. Carry the flush bottle *on you*, not in the pack.

**Breathing.** A heat-and-moisture-exchange mask is close to mandatory: cold dry thin air at exertion will strip your airway and your hydration. Add a fine particulate filter for aerosolised mat matter and mirabilite shatter, and — this is the part people forget — a gas monitor, worn high and low. There is no wind under the fog. Hydrogen sulfide, methane and carbon dioxide pool in terrace basins, in hollows, in dead-end tunnels, and above all in fresh excavations into the buried Hanthas. Sulfide will kill your sense of smell before it kills you, and the whole plateau smells of eggs anyway, so your nose is useless as an alarm. Two monitors. Different manufacturers.

**Do not obstruct your own emitters.** A mask muffles your clicks and your voice; a hood over your ears blinds you. Get a rig designed around this: throat- or chest-mounted emitter with an unobstructed port, open-ear acoustic transparency, and — critically — *frequency-selective* hearing protection rather than plain attenuation. You must protect your hearing across a month of continuous 85–95 dB shimmer while retaining the bands you navigate in. Have this fitted and tuned by someone who has done it before, and accept the trade: your maximum safe stay is set by your ears, not your lungs. Thirty days is generous. Forty-five is reckless.

**Containers.** Everything you own must close hard. Egg cases go into any crevice narrower than a fist, and your pack straps, boot cuffs, mask folds, and cable runs all qualify. Hard cases, smooth surfaces, polished plate where you can get it — polish is not vanity here, it is pest control. Expect to be colonised. There is no exclusion, only management.

**Emitters and instruments — get them licensed.** Any active device you carry that makes sound is writing on the city's map. An unlicensed sonar imager, ranging pole, or drone is not a curiosity; it is graffiti across a street sign, and in the wrong place it is manslaughter waiting. Register your devices at the gate, accept the band you are assigned, and never exceed it. If you want to record, record passively.

**Lamps: bring them, hide them.** Light is private. A lamp in a street is an act of intimacy; a lamp turned on a stranger is assault. Carry a shielded, shuttered, deep-red hand light for emergencies and for your own chamber, with a physical cover, and treat switching it on in company as you would treat undressing in company: only with permission, only among people you have that kind of relationship with. Most travellers' worst social injury on the Sill is a reflex — a stumble, a light, a face.

**Water and food.** Everything on the surface is brine. Fresh water is condensed, distilled, and expensive, and the drinking lines have larvae in them, seasonally, as a matter of course. Filter everything, twice. Bring your own calories: figure 4,500–5,000 kcal a day for cold at altitude plus the fact that Hanthan interiors sit at four degrees, which is a temperature you will be *living* in, not visiting. Local protein is superb and cheap and rich in cold-adapted enzyme suites that a fraction of visitors react to violently; introduce it in tiny amounts over days, with antihistamines and adrenaline within reach. Assume elevated arsenic and heavy metals in anything harvested near a vent mouth; ask which vents, and believe the answer.

**Medical.** Beyond altitude drugs: hemostatic gauze in quantity, wound closure, an irrigation kit, and broad antibiotics. The characteristic Sill injury is a wound you did not notice, packed with mat slick, which is anoxic sulfide sludge. Nothing in that biosphere wants to infect you biochemically, but mechanically it will foul a wound beautifully. And the brine itself will grow crystal in open tissue if given hours.

## Culture, and the ways you will give offence

- **Sound is public, light is private.** Internalise the inversion. It is fine to be loud, constantly, at strangers. It is not fine to look at them.
