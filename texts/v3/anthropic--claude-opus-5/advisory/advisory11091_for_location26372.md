# Preparing for the Shelf

## 1. Understand what kind of trip this is

You are not going somewhere difficult. You are going somewhere *slow*, at altitude, in permanent damp, with no fire and no impact. Almost everything that goes wrong for visitors goes wrong because they arrived fit and loud and prepared for an expedition, when what was required was the preparation of a guest in a monastery that happens to be four hundred kilometres wide.

Two facts should organise all your planning:

**There is no heat.** A coal in a stone cup is a household's entire inheritance. You cannot dry anything, cook anything, or warm yourself after a soaking. Wet is not an event on the Shelf; it is the ambient condition, and it is 3,000+ metres up.

**There is no percussion.** Not "they prefer quiet." There is no impact sound anywhere in a city of half a million rooms. Every hard-edged object you own is, in that acoustic, a scream.

Plan around those two and the rest follows.

## 2. Condition the body, starting months out

**Altitude.** Arrive via at least ten days of staged acclimatisation, ideally on the approach slopes below the rim. Do not helicopter or gate directly onto the tableland. The Shelf offers no descent for relief — it is flat to every horizon anyone has walked to and back from, so if you develop cerebral or pulmonary oedema up there, the only way down is the way you came in, days off. Bring acetazolamide, bring dexamethasone, bring nifedipine, and bring the discipline to turn around.

**Cold-wet, not cold-dry.** Train in the worst weather you can find: 3–6 °C, raining, wind, no fire at the end of it. Learn what your body does at hour nine of that. Learn to sleep in it. The Shelf will not freeze you; it will cool you by two degrees a day for a week until you stop making good decisions.

**Feet.** This is the injury that ends most visits. The surface is damp gritty limestone; the locals go barefoot on it and have soles like horn. You do not. You need six months of graduated barefoot walking on wet abrasive ground, plus a maceration-prevention routine you can actually perform in the field: rotate two pairs of felt socks, wring and body-dry the off-pair against your torso, air the feet at every halt, and carry antifungal powder and a fungal cream in generous quantity. Trim toenails short and square before departure. If your feet go white and wrinkled and stop hurting, you are already in trouble.

**Eyes.** The albedo is the story here. Wet white stone below, luminous cloud above, terrace films acting as mirrors — the light comes from every direction including up your nose. You will get photokeratitis in an afternoon without protection, and you will get it *under* ordinary sunglasses because they don't block reflected light from below. Bring side-shielded glacier glasses, dark. Bring a spare pair. Then also acquire or make a local-style woven brow-visor blackened with soot, and wear both; the visor is what people read as a face, and it cuts the overhead glare that glasses don't.

Sunburn will appear on the underside of your chin, inside your nostrils, on your lower lip, and — if you talk with your mouth open, which you will, because at half a metre everyone does — on your palate. Zinc paste. Heavy lip protection. Note the constraint in §7: whatever you smear on yourself ends up in the drinking water, so choose mineral, inert, and small quantities.

**Kidneys.** Extremely hard water, altitude diuresis, cold-induced diuresis, and low fluid intake because you're never thirsty in the damp. That combination makes stones. Drink far more than you feel like drinking, all day, every day. Bring citrate. If you have a stone history, reconsider the trip; there is no analgesia on the Shelf and no way off it quickly.

**Teeth.** You will be conducting every conversation of your visit with your face thirty to fifty centimetres from another face. Get dental work done beforehand. Bring a soft brush and no paste that you'd mind drinking.

## 3. Condition the habits

These drills feel absurd at home. They are the actual visa.

**Voice.** Practise for weeks. Speak at a volume that is inaudible at two metres and perfectly clear at fifty centimetres. This is not whispering — whispering is hissy and, in a place made of hiss, illegible. It is full voicing at very low power, with the consonants carried by precision rather than force. Have someone stand two metres away and tell you when they can hear you at all. Then learn to *lean* — the whole-body inclination that says *I am now speaking*. There is no word for calling out. There is no substitute for the lean.

**Silence of the body.** Record yourself moving around a room and listen. Every click, tap, set-down, and rustle is something you must eliminate. Practise setting objects down as if they were sleeping animals. Practise standing up without pushing off a wall. Practise coughing into your own elbow-felt, which you will do often at altitude and which is forgivable but ugly.

**Stillness.** Sit for four hours doing nothing, in the cold, without a device, more than once. On the Shelf, waiting is not idleness or delay — it is the entire industrial process. The making of every wall around you consisted of choosing a place and then *not interfering* for nine years. A visitor who fidgets, who asks how long until, who fills the lid-hours with activity, reads as somebody who breaks things.

**The half-metre.** Have friends hold conversations with you at that range, with eye contact, for an hour. Note when you flinch. Get over it before you go, because pulling back mid-sentence is the local equivalent of turning your back.

**Gradient sense.** Every stair, arcade, prayer, and verb tense is oriented up-gradient (young, future) or down-gradient (dead, past). The tilt is faint and the light is shadowless and you *will* lose track. Get it into your body: the water always runs toward the dead. Watch a runnel; that's your tense. As backup, carry a plumb bob on a thread, which is discreet, silent, and needs no explanation. Do not carry a device that beeps.

The error you must avoid is grammatical: speaking of an infant in the tense of the down-gradient, or of the ruins in the tense of arrival. Locals will not correct you. They will simply have heard you say something horrible.

## 4. Pack for softness

The packing principle: **nothing that strikes, nothing that cuts stone, nothing that burns where it can be seen.**

- **Everything in felt.** Sew felt sleeves over your water bottles, your instrument cases, your boot soles, your stove if you bring one. A metal flask set down on limestone is a bell.
- **Kill every fastener.** No velcro — the long tearing rip is, in that acoustic, the single most offensive sound a visitor can produce. No zips that click. No buckles that clack. Cord, toggles, and bone.
- **Clothing:** thick wool and felt, in layers you can wring out; a vapour barrier next to the skin for sleeping so your insulation doesn't slowly drown; a wide felt hat and the visor. Nothing waterproof-crinkly. Nothing bright — you'll be a hole in the world's colour scheme; undyed cream, grey, peat-brown.
- **Shelter:** a small quiet tent, but *no pegs driven into stone*. Anchor with weighted felt bags of peat or grit, placed. Never hammer anything into the Shelf, in the city or a hundred kilometres out on the unbuilt terraces. Habit is the point.
- **Heat:** chemical warmers, worn inside clothing, never shown. If you bring a stove, use it only well out on dry ground, out of sight, and understand what you are doing: burning in an hour more fuel than a household owns. Some travellers choose to bring no stove at all, on the grounds that a thing you cannot use without indecency is dead weight.
- **Tools:** leave the steel behind. A knife that cuts stone is not a tool here; it is a weapon against the city itself, and it will be read that way. Carry a bone withe-knife. If you must have steel for medical reasons, keep it in a closed pouch and never take it out where it can be seen.
- **Bring:** cord, spare felt, needle and sinew, a bone comb, dark goggles, a plumb bob, a folding basket, and a lot of fat (see §6).
- **Do not bring:** anything that flashes, anything that ticks, anything on wheels, soap, detergent, DEET, dyes, or a drone.

If you are documenting: silence the shutter completely, or don't. And do not photograph the tall grey bird.

## 5. Arrive at the right hour, and live by the lid

Plan to step onto the plateau in mid-morning — after *the lid opening*, before the flat glare of noon.

Your day will be shaped by two fog hours. At dawn a sharply bounded white layer forms at the level of the third tier: the lower city disappears, the upper city floats in sun. At evening it sinks into the streets. During those hours nothing is done. You go indoors, you sit close, you drink chalky water, you talk at half a metre. Do not use the lid-hours to catch up on your notes, pack, walk about, or explore. Being visibly busy then is the clearest possible statement that you have misunderstood everything.

Also: those two hours are when you will fall. In fog above the third tier the terraces are identical, and at noon there are no shadows at all, so a rim a finger high and a drop of four metres look the same. Move in mid-morning and mid-afternoon light, with your eyes low, and take the rate of a person who has nowhere to be.

## 6. Walking without damage

- **Never step in a seep or a terrace film.** Your footprint is a dam. A rim is a finger high and represents decades of standing water; break one and you drain a district's work and change where the stone grows for a century. Use the stairs, the channels, the walkways. When crossing unbuilt terrace country outside the city, cross at the natural spill-lips and step *along* the rims, never across them.
- **Never dam, even temporarily.** Not for a photograph, not to fill a bottle, not to make a washing pool. Water is guided, never stopped. To stop a seep is to kill a district's future, and there is no version of "I was only going to leave it a minute."
- **Never strike.** No knocking on a wall to test it, no tapping a rim, no dropping a pack. If you do strike something, someone will come and stand very close and say something quiet. The correct response is to lean in, listen, and not explain.
- **Getting attention:** drop a pebble into a runnel and wait for the person downstream to look up. Learn this before you need it. Carry three smooth pebbles.
- **Navigation:** the ruins are down-gradient. A four-day-wide band of white wreckage, then hummocks indistinguishable from geology — that's your south and your past. Unbuilt glittering terraces are up-gradient. Beyond that: seep-lines run with the seams, and the plateau is featureless in a way that eats confidence. Do not go out alone beyond sight of tiers. There is no rescue, no shade, no fuel, and no landmark.

## 7. Water and waste — take this more seriously than anything else

The water table is **a hand's depth** below the entire city. Everything you put on the ground is in everyone's cup by next week.

- Drink from the seeps at their emergence, up-gradient of habitation, and be aware that the terrace films below the city are farmed, lived in, and full of shrimp, snails, scum and people. Bring a filter (ceramic or hollow-fibre) plus chemical treatment, because you cannot boil. Expect the filter to lime up and die — bring three.
- The water is ferociously hard and tastes of chalk and slate. It also scales the inside of everything, including, over a long stay, your kettleless flasks and possibly your kidneys. Drink constantly anyway.
- **Pack out all excrement.** All of it, all visit. Bring sealed carry bags and enough of them. Ask, quietly, where the locals put theirs — it will be the dry quarters, worked into peat — and follow that practice precisely rather than inventing your own.
- No soap, no shampoo, no sunscreen slick, no insect repellent, no toothpaste, no antiseptic wash near water. You clean yourself the way everyone does: wet moss, grit, and abrasion. You will smell of wet felt and moss within three days, like everyone else, and this is fine.

## 8. Food

The local diet is pressed and fermented blue-green scum from the terrace films, handfuls of inch-long translucent shrimp, ramshorn snails, a parsnip-like taproot, and — rarely, ceremonially — a grey fish that took eleven years to grow. Eat what you're offered, slowly, and never comment on the quantity.

But do not plan to live on it. It is low in fat, low in calories, and you will be burning enormous amounts of energy staying warm in a wet 4 °C at 3,200 metres. **Bring your own fat and calories**, densely, in soft silent containers: ghee, nut pastes, hard fat, dried fruit. Eat them privately, without display. Also bring a broad-spectrum vitamin, particularly A, D, B12 and C, and thiamine if you'll stay past a month.

Two cautions. Fermented cyanobacterial mats can carry hepatotoxins; if a jar tastes strange or has a metallic bitterness, stop, and note that your gut has no local co-evolution. And do not decline the eleven-year fish.
