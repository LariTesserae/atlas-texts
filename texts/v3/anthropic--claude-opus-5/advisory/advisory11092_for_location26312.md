# BRIEFING: THE SILL
### Preparation notes for an ordinary human, first visit

---

## 1. The thing that will actually kill you

Not the sharks of the mid-canopy, not the ground opening, not the Knappers. **Wet-bulb temperature.**

Air at blood heat and one hundred percent saturation means your sweat does not evaporate. It just runs. You are a hundred-watt heater at rest and a four-hundred-watt heater walking, and on the Sill you have no radiator. Core temperature climbs about one degree per hour at rest, faster if you're moving, and at three degrees over baseline your judgement is gone before you notice it's gone — which in total darkness, on glass, three kilometres from camp, is the same as death.

Everything else in this briefing is subordinate to that fact. **You are not visiting a forest. You are making dives into a warm ocean, and the ocean is air.**

Plan accordingly:

- **Sortie length: three hours down, hard limit.** Two if you're carrying anything.
- **Base camp above the canopy**, on a windward cinder-cone flank at two hundred metres or more, where the air actually moves and the wet-bulb drops the three or four degrees that make sleep survivable.
- **Bring a sink.** The plain has infinite energy and no cold. The only cold things in the world are the leaves — that is literally their function, they are radiators — and the condensate that runs off them. Learn to use them: press your forearms and the back of your neck into a fern-mat when you stop; drink cold leaf-water constantly, because internal cooling is the only cooling that works. A litre of 20°C water is worth more to you than an hour's rest.
- If your budget allows a powered cooling garment, bring it, and bring two. If it doesn't, accept shorter dives and don't argue with yourself about it in the field.

---

## 2. Three months of preparing your body

**Heat acclimatisation.** Non-negotiable. Ten to fourteen consecutive days of ninety-minute sessions in a sauna or heat chamber at 40°C and high humidity, working lightly, finishing the block within a week of departure. You'll expand plasma volume, sweat earlier and more dilutely, and buy yourself perhaps forty minutes per sortie. Those forty minutes are the difference between a good trip and a body bag.

**Feet.** This is the second-order killer. The rock is unweathered volcanic glass and it will open a boot sole like paper; the air is a fungal incubator; your skin will macerate in four hours. Get boots with a full-length composite or steel midplate and an aggressive lug — mountaineering-grade, sized up one to accommodate swelling — and break in **three identical pairs**. Toughen your feet with tincture of benzoin daily for a month. Learn to do a full foot inspection by feel alone, in the dark, in five minutes, because that is how you will be doing it.

**Fitness.** Not endurance — you will never run far. You need load-bearing strength for slow, careful movement over broken lobes while carrying twenty kilos, and you need the capacity to carry a partner two hundred metres uphill.

**Medical.** Standard multiverse panel plus: broad-spectrum antibiotic course carried, systemic antifungal carried, tetanus current, and a serious conversation with your physician about the fact that no vaccine on your homeline addresses anything you'll meet. Assume the microbial exposure is total and novel. In practice, the commonest injury outcomes are (a) glass laceration going septic within eighteen hours and (b) respiratory mycosis from guano dust, presenting two weeks *after* you get home.

---

## 3. Three months of preparing your senses

You are going to a world where two of your primary channels are removed and the third is jammed. Retrain now, not there.

**Dark mobility.** Take a blind-mobility course. Genuinely. Learn long-cane technique — two-point touch, constant contact — and practice it blindfolded on rough ground until it is boring. On the Sill your cane is your eyes, and a cane also tells you, through the handle, whether the thing in front of you is basalt, litter, root-mat, or a hole. Learn tongue-click echolocation to the point where you can find a doorway. You will not become a bat, but you will stop walking into trunks, and that alone raises your survival odds.

**Practise walking on things that die.** This sounds absurd until you've done it. The floor is a living carpet; you will kill dozens of animals per step and the surface will *click and give* beneath you like walking on shells. People flinch, and flinching on glass is how you fall. Walk barefoot-in-boots across a gravel bed of dry pasta in the dark until the sensation stops meaning anything.

**Noise tolerance.** Sleep for a week with a pink-noise generator at 85 dB. Learn to hold a thought while being screamed at. The Sill's ambient will not damage your reason permanently, but for the first ten hours it will take your reason away entirely, and you should know what that feels like in advance so you don't mistake it for a medical emergency.

**Get an audiogram.** Baseline both ears. You will want it later, for the insurance claim and for your own honesty.

## 4. Kit, by system

### The haptic principle

Your eyes are useless and your ears are occupied. **Every instrument you carry must speak through your skin.** Vibrating watch, vibrating gas alarm, vibrating waypoint compass, vibrating comms. If a device only beeps, it does not exist on the Sill. Have this modification done before you go; do not assume you'll notice a flashing LED, because you will be looking at nothing and you will be right to.

### Hearing

- **Layered protection: deep-insert foam plugs (33 dB) under electronic muffs with level-dependent compression.** The muffs let you keep directional hearing at safe levels; the plugs catch what the muffs can't.
- Understand the limit: above roughly 50 dB of attenuation, sound reaches your cochlea *through the bones of your skull*, and no earplug in any universe stops that. At 118 dB ambient you are running at the ceiling of what protection can do. **You will come home with a temporary threshold shift, and if you stay more than a week or two, some of it will be permanent.** Budget your exposure like a radiation dose.
- **Bone-conduction earpieces plus throat microphones** for team comms. Throat mics ignore ambient noise entirely; bone conduction goes under your ear protection. This is the single best piece of kit you can carry. Being able to speak conversationally to a partner in a 115 dB world is a superpower the Knappers would consider divine.

### Sight

- **No white light on the plain. Ever.** Understand what a white light *is* here: it's a fissure opening. It is the one event the entire ecosystem is hardwired to respond to. Everything goes silent, then everything screams, and every predator in six hundred metres orients on the source. You would be lighting a flare in the middle of a stampede. If you must photograph the canopy, do it once, from the cone top, above the treeline, and accept that you have made yourself the news for the night.
- **Digital night vision with near-IR illumination** is your working sight. Nothing on the plain appears to use near-IR, so an 850nm floodlamp is effectively invisible.
- **Thermal imaging mostly fails here and you should know that before you rely on it.** The ground is body temperature, the trunk bases are body temperature, the air is body temperature, the animals are body temperature. There is no contrast. Thermal is useful for one thing only: finding fissure mouths and hot vents, i.e. finding the things that will kill you rather than the things that will eat you.
- The heat-hunting snake analogues, incidentally, are drawn to your face because your face is the only exposed warm wet thing you're carrying. **Wear a fine-mesh face screen at all times.** It also keeps the moths out of your mouth, which is not a joke; at certain hours you cannot breathe through an open mouth.

### Air

- **Four-gas monitor with haptic alarm, worn at the collar and at the ankle.** Hydrogen sulphide is heavier than air and the plain is a field of lobes and hollows; you will step down into pockets of it. H₂S also anaesthetises your sense of smell at exactly the concentration where it becomes lethal, so the moment you think "the sulphur smell has gone" you should already be climbing.
- **CO₂ pools in the same hollows.** Under a near-solid three-tier canopy in still air, there is nothing to flush it.
- **P100 respirator, mandatory within thirty metres of any fissure mouth or bat roost.** Guano dust plus warm damp plus novel fungi equals the illness that gets most returnees. It's miserable to wear in that heat. Wear it anyway.

### Feet, skin, and rot

Three pairs of boots, six pairs of wool-blend socks, and a **religious** foot ritual every time you return to camp: strip, irrigate, inspect by feel, dry with a chamois, powder, air for an hour minimum. Antifungal cream on every skin fold, every cycle. Superglue and steri-strips for glass cuts, and a genuine irrigation syringe — basalt glass leaves clean deep wounds with fragments in them, and a cut you close over a shard will abscess by the next slack hour.

Everything textile you bring will grow mould within four days. Pack accordingly: synthetics, silver-treated, and duplicates. Electronics must be potted or in nitrogen-purged housings with desiccant, and you will still lose one device in three.
