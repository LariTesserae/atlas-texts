# Visitor's Preparation Brief: The Maintenance Plain

## Read this part first

The Maintenance Plain is not a hostile world. It has no storms, no predators interested in you, no terrain that will drop you, no season that will starve you. It will nonetheless kill an unprepared visitor in about six hours, and it will do it without any change in its behaviour whatsoever, which is the thing people find hard to plan for.

Everything below follows from two numbers: **thirty-four degrees Celsius** and **relative humidity within a percent or two of saturation, permanently.**

---

## 1. The physics that will kill you

At 34°C and saturation, your wet-bulb temperature is roughly 34°C. Human beings shed metabolic heat by evaporating water off skin and out of lungs. On the Maintenance Plain, **nothing evaporates.** Your sweat glands will run flat out for your entire visit and accomplish precisely nothing except salt loss. Your breath comes back to you as warm as it left. You have no heat sink.

Also note: there is no cold *anywhere*. The gutters run warm, the plate is warm, the hatch-plates are warmer, the ceiling is the plain's own breath. You cannot find a cool rock, a cool stream, a shaded hollow. The entire world is one temperature and that temperature is slightly below the point at which you cook.

Consequences:

- **No evaporative cooling gear works.** No swamp coolers, no wet bandanas, no cooling towels, no ventilated suits. They are worse than useless; they add weight and give false confidence.
- **A passive radiator cannot dump your heat** into air that is already at your target temperature.
- Therefore: **you must carry a powered chiller and its fuel.** A vapour-compression pack rejecting to ambient at ~55°C condenser temperature works fine even in saturated air; the condenser will run streaming wet, which is acceptable. Budget for 400–500 watts of removal while walking, 120–150 at rest.
- **Phase-change vests are excursion-only.** Do the arithmetic before you rely on them: eight hours of walking is roughly 11 megajoules, which is about 35 kilograms of ice. Ice is for the twenty-minute emergency, not the day.
- **Wear core-temperature telemetry with a loud alarm, and make it your partner's job to watch it, not yours.** Heat illness on the Plain arrives without the normal warning signs. You will not feel "sweaty then suddenly dry," because you are always wet with condensate you cannot distinguish from sweat. The first symptom you notice will be a bad decision you have already made.

**Weigh yourself on a camp scale before and after every excursion.** It is the only honest hydration instrument available. And take far more electrolyte than your instinct suggests — continuous unproductive sweating, replaced with distilled or heavily-treated water, walks people into hyponatremia within three intervals. Salt is not optional here. Salt is a survival item.

---

## 2. Air, water, and what not to touch

**Do not drink anything you find.** Not seam water, not gutter water, not condensate wrung from your own clothing. The rime lives on trace metals and reduced sulfur compounds seeping out of the seams; that is what is in the water, in quantities selected by a system that has no interest in your kidneys. Bring water, or bring a distillation rig with metals-grade post-treatment and accept the power cost.

**Carry a hydrogen sulfide detector and believe it.** H₂S is heavier than air and the whole plain is graded. It collects in seams, pools in the gutter-lines, and sits deep in the intake throats. It also anaesthetises your sense of smell at exactly the concentration where it starts to matter, so your nose will tell you the danger has passed at the moment it has become lethal.

There is a free biological gauge available: **the thicker and paler the wickworm nap in a seam, the richer the sulfide bleeding out of it.** A seam furred solid with tails is a seam to walk past, not kneel beside. Learn to read this. It is the only warning the Plain offers about anything.

**Do not eat anything.** Not the rime, not the fauna. Setting aside the metals, a flange snail is structurally floor, and a gasket beetle's gut is lined with a catalytic mesh. These are not organisms that have been shaped by anything's digestion. Nothing here is food for a body like yours, and the failure mode is not indigestion.

**Mind the stray current.** The rime survives partly by shorting the potential between adjacent plates with conductive threads, which means there *is* a potential between adjacent plates, and you are a wet bag of saline lying across the seams. Insulating soles. Insulating gloves. Do not straddle a seam while handling conductive equipment, and do not lie down across one to sleep.

---

## 3. Skin

This is the injury that actually ends most visits.

You will be wet, at blood temperature, for the entire deployment. Skin held soft and wet at 34°C begins breaking down in about seventy-two hours. Feet go first, then groin, then armpits, then anywhere a strap crosses. Salt crusting at the edges of the wet areas does the rest.

- **The single most important piece of infrastructure you will bring is a dry room.** A sealed, dehumidified, actively chilled camp volume where you can get genuinely dry for six hours out of every twelve. Not comfortable — *dry*. Plan the entire expedition around it. Everything else is negotiable.
- Zinc-based barrier ointment, applied to dry skin only, before every excursion. Talc and powders are worthless; they take up water out of the air and become paste.
- Two sets of everything, one always drying.
- Inspect your partner's feet and back daily, out loud, as a scheduled task. You will not do it for yourself after the fourth interval.

---

## 4. Boots, and the fact that you are erosion

The plain is polished by the passage of countless small feet, and it is wet, and in places it is greased with crushed fauna. Hard cleats do nothing on polished sinter — they skate. You want **soft, high-friction compound soles, large contact area, and no aggressive lugs.** Bring three pairs. They will wear fast. That is correct; the soft sole abrading is the soft sole doing its job instead of abrading the floor.

Which brings up the thing nobody warns you about until you are standing on it: **you are wear.** The plain compensates for abrasion by extruding new plate at exactly the rate of loss. Your boots are a load on that budget. So is your camp. So are your dropped items.

It is not, practically speaking, damage — the system absorbs you without noticing. But most visitors find it matters to them anyway, somewhere around the fifth interval, and it is better to have thought about it in advance than to discover it while staring at your own footprints in a rime field.

---

## 5. Moving across it

**You cannot judge distance.** There are no shadows. Depth must be read from occlusion and haze alone, and the haze is uniform, so a weld eight metres high at two kilometres and a weld eighty metres high at twenty kilometres present identically. Everything is closer or further than it looks and you will not learn to correct for it.

**The single best comfort item after the chiller is a low-mounted raking lamp.** A hand or hip light set at ten or fifteen degrees above the plate, throwing hard oblique shadows in front of you. It costs almost nothing and it restores three-dimensional vision in a two-metre bubble. Without it you will step into seams, misjudge weld crests, and put your hand down onto things. With it you can see texture again. Many visitors report that the lamp does more for their morale than for their feet, and keep it burning in camp for that reason.

**Navigate by grade.** The plain is graded one part in four thousand toward the gutter-lines. A precision bubble level or a simple water-tube will give you downhill, reliably, everywhere, forever. This is your compass. Gutters run to throats; throats are on the survey maps; the network is your coordinate system.

Do not expect magnetic north to mean anything with all that turning metal beneath you. Carry inertial navigation, carry a backup, and log position obsessively — because:

**Markers do not persist.** Plate is continuously withdrawn and replaced. Anything you leave lying on the floor may simply be taken down through a seam within days, and a closed loop with no losses will treat your dropped glove as a fault to be cleared. **Tether every item you carry.** Inventory at every stop. Anchor route markers to weld flanks or stack bases, mechanically, and make them tall enough to see through the haze.

Terrain hazards, in order of how often they hurt people:

1. **Seams.** A finger's width, ankle-catching, invisible without your raking lamp.
2. **Weld crests.** Knife-sharp, and vitreous flanks are glass. Pad every rope anchor. A slip from ten metres onto sintered basalt is a fatal fall in the way that a ten-metre fall onto anything else is not.
3. **Gutter-lines during the dew tide.** They brim. Cross them before, not during, and never camp in one for the shelter. There is no shelter. There is nothing to shelter from except the thing that fills the gutter.
4. **Intake throats.** The water goes down. You would go down with it, past the crabs and the grilles, and be delivered somewhere. Tether within thirty metres of a throat lip, always, no exceptions, no matter how flat the approach looks.

---

## 6. Timekeeping

There is no day. The brightness at what you would call midnight matches the brightness at what you would call noon to four decimal places. Your circadian rhythm has nothing to grip and will come apart in under a week if you let it.

The Plain does, however, give you a metronome, and it is a superb one: **the dew tide, twice per interval, four hours, identical every time, to the same wet-mark on the same plate.** Calibrate to it on arrival. Then:

- **Run the expedition on hard clock discipline.** Fixed sleep windows, fixed meals, fixed shift changes, announced aloud.
- Install **bright, directional, warm light in camp for "morning" and dim amber for "evening,"** and be strict about it. You are manufacturing a day because the world will not give you one.
- **Put a ticking clock in the camp.** An audible, mechanical, irregular-sounding one. This is not sentimentality; it is the only acoustic event you will hear that is not water or claws.

---

## 7. The note

Beneath hearing, the machinery runs. You will feel it in the soles of your feet and in your chest. Everyone reports the same curve: you stop noticing it on the second day and you cannot stop noticing it on the fourth.

Earplugs will not help. This is not in your ears.

What helps:

- **Decouple your sleeping platform from the plate.** Hammocks slung from a frame on isolators, or a suspended cot on elastomer mounts. The single most effective intervention available, and it also keeps you off the plate, off the seams, and out of reach of whatever crawls.
- **Mass damping under the camp floor.** Sandbags work. Any sandbags. Fill them with plate dust if you must.
- **Fill the camp with other low-frequency sound.** Music with bass content. A fan. Anything that gives the note something to hide in.

Brief the whole party before departure on the fourth-day effect, by name, with the curve drawn out. People cope with it far better when they were told it was scheduled.

---

## 8. Fauna protocol

The animals are not dangerous, with two footnotes.

**They are underfoot, constantly, at forty to fifty per palm-width.** You cannot walk here without killing. There is no route that avoids it. The census is constant, so within a couple of hours a hatch-plate somewhere extrudes a replacement, wet and folded, for every one you crushed. Some visitors find this consoling. Rather more find it the exact opposite, on a delay of about a week. Decide in advance which sentence you are going to say to yourself, and say that one.

Footnote one: **vent hounds.** Blind, long, low, warm, hunting by tremor, one per twelve square kilometres. You are the largest tremor source that has ever crossed their territory. They will investigate. Reported behaviour is consistently *investigation*, not attack, and their take rate on everything else looks rationed to the point of being scheduled — but a rationed predator meeting an unscheduled animal is a situation with no precedent, and the Plain has no precedents. Move smoothly. Avoid hard rhythmic footfall. Keep a tremor decoy in the kit — a simple eccentric-mass thumper you can set down and walk away from. Do not sleep on the plate.

Footnote two: **cistern crabs at the throats.** Half a metre, unbothered, and they keep the grilles clear. They will not come after you. But they are working, and the throat lip is wet, and a startled step backward at the lip of an intake is how people die here.

Finally: **do not interfere with a hatch-plate.** Do not open, block, sample, heat, cool, or stand on one. Nothing in the record says what happens. That is not reassurance.

---

## 9. Contamination, both directions

You are an exponential organism with an exponential microbiome, arriving in a system that has been running at exactly zero growth for an unguessable duration. Full containment protocol, out and back. Sealed waste, nothing buried, nothing poured into a seam, nothing left. Assume the Plain would absorb your entire biosphere without a flicker — and assume also that you cannot prove that, and that the cost of being wrong is a world.

Inbound risk to you is low. Nothing here evolved, so nothing here is adapted to eat you. But the toxicology is industrial rather than biological, and a catalytic gut mesh does not care that it has ended up in a wound. **Treat every cut as a chemical exposure as well as an infection.** Irrigate generously with clean water you brought.

---

## 10. Duration, and the part that is actually hard

Visitors report that the difficulty is not the heat or the wet or the crawling. It is that after a while you begin to suspect you are the only event.

This is the real hazard of the Maintenance Plain and it should be planned for with the same seriousness as the wet-bulb problem.

**Recommended maximum deployment: nine to eleven intervals.** Longer stays show a consistent and unpleasant pattern.

Countermeasures, all of which sound trivial and none of which are:

- **Never travel solo.** Not for an hour. Not to the next weld.
- **Scheduled outside contact at fixed times**, even if there is nothing to report. Especially if there is nothing to report.
- **Bring change with you, since the Plain has none.** Vary the camp. Rearrange it. Cook differently. Rotate duties. Introduce small, deliberate, meaningless novelty on a schedule. Your nervous system needs *events* and will begin generating them internally if you do not supply them externally.
- **Prohibit census work in the final third of any deployment.** The surveys that counted gasket beetles on a single hexagon across a hundred intervals, and got the same number, stopped for reasons they filed under "morale." Take them at their word. Counting is how the Plain gets in.
- **Brief everyone, explicitly, on the event impulse.** Late in a long stay, people begin to want very badly to *do something the Plain will have to respond to.* Break a grille. Dam a gutter. Pry up a hatch-plate. Burn something, just to see smoke, just to make a thing that is not identical to the last thing. This impulse feels, at the time, like curiosity and initiative. It is neither. Name it in advance, agree on a two-person rule for any irreversible action, and write it down while everyone is still at home and can laugh about it.

---

## 11. Leaving

Decontaminate fully. Log and account for every item; anything unaccounted for, report, because the Plain will have taken it down and you should not pretend otherwise.

On collection: removing a specimen does not reduce the census. One is extruded to replace it. Collection here is, uniquely, free — and most researchers find that the free-ness is what bothers them, not the killing. Prepare for that in advance too.

**Reentry:** expect a week of strangeness. Returnees flinch at wind and at sunlight and at the sound of an engine. Shadows look aggressive. Then, usually around the second week, the other side of it arrives: an ordinary landscape starts to look frankly *untidy*, unfinished, wasteful, dying at the edges, full of rot and slack and accident.

That feeling passes. Let it. The rot is where the next thing comes from.

---

## 12. Short packing list

**Essential:** powered chiller plus fuel/power; core-temp telemetry with buddy alarm; sealed dehumidified dry room; electrolytes in quantity; water supply or distillation with metals post-treatment; H₂S detector; low-angle raking lamp; precision level; inertial navigation plus backup; suspended sleeping platform on isolators; soft-compound boots ×3; zinc barrier ointment; insulating gloves and soles; tethers for absolutely everything; tremor decoy; mechanical ticking clock; full containment waste system.

**Strongly recommended:** directional camp lighting on a manufactured day cycle; bass-capable audio; tall anchored route markers; camp scale; a written schedule of deliberate novelties; a written copy of section 10, posted where everyone can see it.

**Do not bring:** evaporative cooling of any kind; anything you would mind losing to a seam; a plan that depends on finding shade, shelter, cold, or news.
