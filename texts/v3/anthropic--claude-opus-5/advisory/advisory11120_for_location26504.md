# Briefing: The Drowned Flat

**Classification:** hostile-benign. Nothing here wants to hurt you. Everything here will, incidentally, if you arrive wrong.

---

## First, the honest sentence

You cannot see this place. Read the description again and notice how much of it is *arithmetic* — the decline of half a metre over a kilometre, the two hundred and eighty generations, the dune moving a hand's breadth an hour. That is not a view. That is a landscape someone assembled from ten thousand hours of instruments and cores and touch. What you will get, personally, in your own body, is arm's length of brown, then hand's length of brown, then a grey wall four inches from your mask, and a texture under your glove.

Prepare for that, not for the panorama. Travellers who prepare for the panorama drown of disappointment first and hypothermia second.

---

## Six months out: the body

**Cold.** Bottom water will run six to nine degrees, and in a benthic storm the column is mixed, so there is no warm layer anywhere to go up into. The water is loaded, which means it moves heat out of you slightly faster than clean water and abrades your insulation while it does it. You want a drysuit you have worn a hundred times, or — better — surface-supplied hot water. Do not take a wetsuit. Do not take a *new* drysuit; take the one whose leaks you can name.

**Cold adaptation** helps less than fitness does. Get your resting heart rate down. Get your CO₂ tolerance up, because you will be working hard on a bottom current and breathing against a regulator that is being sandblasted, and CO₂ retention at five atmospheres is how competent people become stupid without noticing.

**Screening.** Patent foramen ovale echo. Ears — a plain sinus problem becomes a reason you can never leave. Dental work: a leaking filling at fifty metres is an event. If you have had decompression illness, choose another world; this one requires you to be boring.

**Narcosis.** On air at forty-eight metres you will be doing the mental arithmetic of a tired man at the end of a long day, and the only thing standing between you and death here is arithmetic. Take trimix. Even a modest helium fraction buys back the part of your mind that counts line markers.

**Tactile training.** This is the unusual requirement and the one people skip. Spend three months learning to identify objects by glove in blackout. Get a tank, silt it out, and practise: sand, shell gravel, stiff clay, peat. Then practise objects: a beach pebble versus a flint core; a natural fracture versus a struck flake scar; a hazel rod versus a root. You are training to be a blind person who has been blind long enough to be good at it. Ordinary sighted people, put in the dark, use their hands like tongs. You want hands that read.

## The transit problem

This is the part multiverse travellers get wrong, because in most worlds the arrival is trivial and the destination is the difficulty.

Two facts:

1. You will arrive at five and a half atmospheres, or you will arrive at one and have to get to five and a half, and either way you contract a decompression obligation the moment you begin breathing.
2. **There is no usable surface.** The lid has had a gale standing on it for four days. Whatever is up there is a field of breaking crests you cannot be recovered from, in a world with no boat, no crew, no chamber, and no one who knows your name.

So the exit must not be through the top. Plan the return transit at depth, in the water, in the dark, on schedule, with your gas reserve intact — which means the transit apparatus must tolerate pressure, silt, and being handled by a cold man in three-fingered gloves. Test it in mud. Test it *twice* in mud. Fine grey silt is a solvent for mechanisms; it finds O-rings, valve seats, zipper teeth, latch detents, the mating faces of anything that must mate.

If your return cannot be made at depth, then you must bring your own surface: a habitat, a bell, a bailout chamber anchored into the peat, and a decompression schedule measured in days. That is a serious expedition, not a visit. Most travellers should choose the depth exit and a bottom time of forty minutes.

And build your obligation *backwards from the exit*, not forwards from the entry. The plain has no interest in your ascent profile. It will not hold still while you sort it out.

---

## The current, which is the actual killer

Depth is a bookkeeping problem. Cold is an engineering problem. The current is the thing that will hurt you.

A bottom current at "a running pace" is roughly one and a half metres per second. You cannot swim against one metre per second. You cannot hold a line hand-over-hand against one and a half for long. If it takes you, it will take you out across a featureless plain in near-zero visibility, and there is no rise, no rock, no wreck, nothing to fetch up against — just more flat, in every direction, for the size of a county. Being swept here is not being swept somewhere. It is deletion.

Therefore:

- **Go at slack.** Not "around slack" — at slack. On this bottom the usable window at spring tide is twenty to forty minutes, and in a benthic storm the gale-driven residual flow can eat the slack entirely, so that the current merely slows, reverses grudgingly, and never actually stops. Have a rule fixed in advance, in writing, at what current speed you abort, and abort at it.
- **Better: do not go during the storm at all.** The passage tells you the prize plainly. When the gale lies down, the silt fog settles out over about a day and the plain becomes *visible* for a few hours — ribbed grey field running out into green-brown dark, level, quiet. That is the only time this world can be looked at. If your schedule permits waiting, wait. If your ego insists on the storm, understand that you are paying an enormous amount of risk to experience a brown wall.
- **Stay low.** The current slackens in the last metre. Down there is also where the silt is thickest, so you trade what little sight you had for the ability to stay put. Take the trade. You will not swim this plain; you will crawl it, prone, in boots, dragging yourself by pins and handfuls of peat. Bring boots. Fins are for water; this is a floor.
- **Weight heavy** and accept the consequences: at fifty metres a heavy diver who loses buoyancy control has no forgiving options. Redundant inflation. Practise the failure.

---

## Lines

Everything is tethered. There is no exception to this and no discussion of it.

A shotline or transit marker with a strobe and an acoustic pinger, pinned into peat — peat holds, sand does not — and from that, a jackstay laid at slack on a compass bearing, and from the jackstay, your personal distance line on a reel that you have stripped and reassembled blind. Knotted markers at fixed intervals, because you will navigate by counting, not by seeing. Two reels. A line cutter you can reach with either hand.

If you part from the line, you have parted from the world. Stop. Do not swim to look. Deploy your bag, note your gas, and begin the ascent you can afford, because there is nothing out there in any direction to find.

---

## Light

Bring lights, and know what they are for.

They are not for seeing distance. Point a beam into loaded water and you get an illuminated wall of your own backscatter, brighter and less informative than the dark. Mount them off-axis on a short boom, low, raking across the bed at a shallow angle, so that ripples throw relief. Amber cuts silt marginally better than white. Have one small light dedicated to gauges only, hooded, on your wrist, and a second identical one in a pocket.

Also: understand what you are doing. Nothing here has cast a shadow in eight thousand years. The first time you rake a light across a ripple field and see the ridges stand up in relief, you will have made the first shadow on this plain since it was a plain with wind on it. People are not always ready for how that feels. Some of them cry in a helmet, which is a nuisance.

## Kit that ordinary people forget

- **Spares for everything sealed.** Assume grit ingress everywhere. Spare mask. Spare gloves — the silt sands the fingertips off cheap ones in an hour, and your fingertips are your eyes.
- **Hardwire comms if you have an umbilical.** Acoustic through-water comms function in sediment load but degrade; a wire does not care. And you will want a voice. The silence, when the tide goes slack, does the thing described: it starts recruiting your pulse and the tick of your joints to fill it. A voice in your ear is worth more than a second light.
- **A slate and a pencil**, because you will not be able to see a screen and you can write blind. Pencil, not electronics, for the primary record.
- **Pins and skewers** — long stainless pins to stake yourself, and to peg a scale bar, and to mark a find you must leave.
- **A camera you have already written off.** It will come back scoured. It will come back having recorded brown.
- **Nothing you love.** Everything that enters this water comes out abraded, filled with grey paste, and smelling of eight-thousand-year-old anaerobic peat, which smells like the inside of a cold garden.

## The mind

Three specific preparations, in order of how badly people are hurt by neglecting them.

**One: no reference.** There is no horizon, no up-light strong enough to orient by, no shadow, no landmark, no gradient. On a flat, dark, silted bed at slack water, a substantial fraction of well-trained people get a form of vertigo that feels exactly like falling in a direction that does not exist. It resolves in seconds if you have practised the drill: settle onto the bed, hold the line, close your eyes, breathe long, read your depth gauge with a finger, and let the numbers be your body's opinion instead of your inner ear's. Practise this drill in a silted quarry until it is dull.

**Two: sensory poverty.** You will be in a world that is, from your standpoint, a texture and a hiss. Prepare tasks. Task-loading is the cure for both boredom and panic: measure a thing, count a thing, follow a bank of coarse gravel along an outer bend for thirty metres and record the grain change. Travellers who arrive intending to *experience* the plain generally experience thirty minutes of brown and their own heartbeat and come back saying there is nothing there. Travellers who arrive with three small jobs come back changed.

**Three: the indifference.** This is the real thing to prepare for, and no equipment addresses it.

The plain will not register you. There is no ecology of consequence here — the turbulence is total and it changes nothing, the movement is constant and the shape is permanent. You may kneel on a hearth. You may put your hand into a bored antler socket that a person cut for a haft. You may run a glove along a row of standing hazel rods curved in the arc of a fish trap, set in a river, in the dry, by someone who expected to come back tomorrow, and who has been dead for two hundred and eighty generations, in a landscape that has since become ocean and then become nothing in particular.

And then the tide turns and the sand comes over the top of it and it is gone for a century.

You must decide, in advance and in cold blood, how you feel about that. Because the temptation in the moment is enormous, and it is not greed. It is *rescue*. You will want to take the flint core, the antler, the handful of whole hazelnuts, because you will be holding the only witnessed moment in their eight thousand years and you will know that when you let go it ends. Travellers have wrecked their decompression, their gas, and occasionally their lives over that impulse.

The advice, which you may reject: **record and leave.** Photograph, measure, position, describe on the slate, and let the plain have it back. The plain does not preserve and does not destroy; it buries and unburies, and it has been doing that faithfully for longer than the thing has been drowned. It is not a museum needing a rescuer. It is the only reason the flake scars are still as crisp as the week they were struck.

If you take something, take it because a competent archaeologist in some world will read it, and take it with a position fix, and know that you have taken a word out of a sentence nobody will ever read again.

---

## What a good visit looks like

Wait out the gale. Enter on the settling day, when the silt has fallen out of the bottom ten metres and the plain is briefly a place with a distance in it. Descend on a pinned shotline into water that goes green, then bottle-glass, then a direction. Land soft, prone, on a shell pavement that clinks under your knee like poor coins under a blanket. Lay a jackstay along the bearing of an old river you identified from someone's survey. Follow the coarse gravel of the outer bend. Find where the dunes have travelled off and left the peat bare and black. Kneel on the floor of a forest. Spend eleven minutes on a stump the diameter of a dinner plate and note the direction of its grain. Leave at your planned time with your planned gas, ascend on the line, do the stops honestly, and exit at depth into your own world with paste in every seal and grit in your teeth.

Then sit somewhere warm and understand that you have visited the most eventless violent place you will ever go, and that it has no story about you, and that this is not a failure of the place.

Bring a second pair of gloves.
