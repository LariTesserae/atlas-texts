# Preparing to visit the Bell

## First, understand what you are bringing into the room

Everything else in this brief follows from one sentence: **you are made of the four things the Bell has never contained.**

You are water. You are oxygen, in a bottle, at pressure. You are fire — in your batteries, your static, your contacts, your camera. And you are an animal, the first one, walking in with your gut flora and your shed skin and your protein.

Nothing you can do about this except be honest about it and pack accordingly. Every rule below is either a rule about keeping yourself alive or a rule about keeping those four things sealed inside you.

The second thing to hold onto: the Bell is small. It is *a room.* You will not need endurance sports. You will need the discipline of a person moving through a two-thousand-year-old instrument that is still in tune enough to be ruined.

---

## Timing your arrival

Do not arrive during the annual week. There is no ambiguity here — a chord that pulverizes the crystal fans at the mouth of the crack will rupture your sinuses and cook your exit route. The crack is your only way out, it is a shoulder-width slot, and during the hard week it is a flue.

Two ways to read the season, both from the surface:

- **The fans at the lip.** They re-grow as fast as they crumble, but they are levelled to powder in the hard week and then rebuild. Heavy, complex, deeply ochred fans with three or four generations of stagger-step overgrowth mean the last hard week is well behind you. Thin, fresh, pale, uniform fans mean it is recent — or imminent, which you cannot distinguish from a single visit. When in doubt, camp and watch for a few days; the fans grow fast enough to give you a trend.
- **The plume.** Stand off two or three miles at dawn when the air is stratified and look for the brown smudge of shed leaves. If the smudge is thickening day over day, leave.
- **The soles of your feet.** If you can feel the hum in the salt, you are already too late to go down. Turn around.

Inside, your clock is the **five-hour-and-change swell.** Plan a single working period of one swell or less. Log the crests from the surface before you commit, then descend on the falling side of a crest, so that your deepest hour is in the trough and your exit is well clear of the next rise. You want the shaft weakest when you are in the slot.

## Getting to the crack

Two hundred miles of dry salt in every direction, and the crust is not what it looks like. Those room-sized polygons curl at the edges and are glazed and brittle; the slumped place over the void tells you there are other voids, and no reason to believe they have all announced themselves. Walk on the middles of plates, not on the joints. Sound ahead with a pole. Rope up in any low place where the crust dishes.

Navigation is compromised near the crack because the heat column bends the horizon; the shimmer is your beacon and your liar. Fix your camp by a compass bearing and a cairn, not by eye.

Cache water at the camp, well upwind, and cache a second reserve an hour's walk out. The salt plain will kill you far more prosaically than the Bell will.

Set your surface station at the crack: one person who does not go down, ever, on any trip. Radio will not reach through basalt. Run a physical line — aramid, not steel, non-sparking, heat-rated well above what you measure in the throat — with a **tug code** agreed in advance and rehearsed until it is reflex. You will not be able to speak down there, and you will not be able to hear a shout, and you will not be thinking clearly for the first twenty minutes.

## The life-support problem

Take nothing about the air on faith. Probe the crack before you enter: lower an instrument package on a line and let it hang for a full nine-second cycle, several times, at several depths.

What you should expect, and what you must verify:

**Heat.** The gallery air will be somewhere in the range where a dry sauna stops being pleasant and starts being a countdown — call it sixty-five to eighty-five degrees, moving, with a radiant load from below that increases with every terrace you descend. Deeper, it gets worse fast; the plants hold liquid sulfur at 120–150°C and the grate glows cherry. **Set a hard floor before you go in** — a terrace number, chosen from your radiant-flux readings, below which nobody goes — and paint it on your forearm.

Heat, not gas, will set your dwell time. Cool actively. The elegant solution is **solid CO₂**: sublimation cooling in a vest, vented into the shaft, because CO₂ is something the Bell already breathes and will not notice. Do not use a water-ice or water-loop system unless it is welded closed and pressure-tested, and even then carry it as though it were a jar of acid, because to this room it is. Do not use paraffin or any wax phase-change medium: it is fuel, and fuel is forbidden.

**Gas.** There is oxygen in the Bell — the canes burn blue, so there must be — but the breath also carries hydrogen sulfide at concentrations no filter cartridge can be trusted with, and heat degrades sorbents anyway. Worse, H₂S will destroy your sense of smell within a minute or two, so you lose the only warning your body offers, permanently, for the duration of the visit.

Go **closed-circuit**. A rebreather with a full-face positive-pressure mask. This gives you three things at once: independence from the atmosphere, a controlled inspired temperature, and — critically — **it vents nothing.** Your exhaled water stays in your loop instead of raining onto sulfur glazes and two-thousand-year-old wedge niches. Carry an open-circuit bail-out, but understand that using it means you are leaving.

Filter your loop intake against **pollen.** During a release the air in that shell along the wall goes opaque with sulfur dust; it is not designed to be inhaled by lungs, having never previously encountered any.

**Materials.** H₂S blackens copper and silver on contact. Assume every exposed contact, connector, and solder joint in your kit fails at the worst moment. Anything electronic goes in a hermetic housing with a desiccant pack — and then you must remember that the desiccant is one more jar of forbidden water.
