# Preparing for the Cold Room

## First, understand what kind of visit this is

You are not going in. Settle that before you pack anything else, because every other decision follows from it.

Yes, the gap is a shoulder wide. Yes, the room is four paces by six and the ceiling is low rather than impossible. A determined person could get in. A determined person could also stand up inside a cathedral organ. There is nowhere in the Cold Room to put a knee: the floor is the day-stone, which is also the roof of the sixth town, and the walls from floor to gap are all street. The single square foot you would need in order to exist there does not exist.

So this is a visit made lying face-down on wet limestone in November, with your head in a hole, for four or five hours, in silence, mostly not moving. Prepare for that and you have prepared correctly. Prepare for anything more ambitious and you have prepared to be the shrew.

## Finding it

There is no cairn and there will never be one. The shelf stands a hand's width above the peat and looks like every other place where fen stone breaks surface, of which there are a hundred in a mile.

Go in the second week of October, in the hour after dawn, and walk the field looking not down but across, at knee height, into the light. The Cold Room breathes. The heavy air spills and the fen air rolls over it and for about twenty minutes there will be one place where a thin grey exhalation stands over the ground like a spilled ghost, in still air, going nowhere. That is the gap. Take two compass bearings on whatever is permanent — a pump-house, a drain corner, a willow stub — and write them down. Do not mark the ground. Do not cairn it, flag it, peg it, or tread a path to it; a path is an invitation to a dog.

Then go away and come back in November, having done the work below.

## Season and hour

White overcast. High cloud, no sun, no wind, no rain in the previous three days. Noon.

Not summer: you sweat, you steam, and the fen is full of things that would like to ride down the gap on your collar. Not spring: the wren is nesting and your face at the gap in April is a crime against an entire year. Not after rain: sodden peat squeezes underfoot, and the room's floor sits a foot below the water table on a balance you cannot see and must not test. Not in wind, even though no wind enters — wind carries chaff and seed and dust off your clothing, and the gap is a drain for everything heavier than air.

Give yourself six hours of daylight and expect to use four of them holding still.

## The plank

The three slabs fell against one another and locked. Nothing since has moved them: no frost, no flood, no root, no wind. You are the first thing in that landscape's history with the mass and the leverage to change that, and if you put a hand on a slab to brace yourself while shifting your weight, you may end the Cold Room in the time it takes to grunt.

Bring a board. Scaffold plank, nine feet, or two of them. Lay them on the peat so that they bridge the shelf entirely and touch no part of the fallen stone. Lie on the plank. Your chest, your elbows, your hips, all on the plank. Nothing of you touches the slabs at any point in the visit, including on the way out, including when your neck hurts, including when you are cold and clumsy and want to push yourself up.

Bring also: a rolled towel for under your sternum, a second for your forehead, and a folded pad for your elbows. You will not believe how much of this trip is about your neck.

## What to wear

Warm, silent, shedless, unscented, and — this is the unintuitive part — heavily insulated, for their sake as much as yours.

You radiate about a hundred watts. Held a foot above a town whose entire physics is *cold pools and does not vary*, you are a heater suspended over a snowfield. Every layer of down or fleece you put between your body and the gap is a degree the town does not have to absorb. Dress as if for an arctic hide: base layers, thick mid, an outer that does not hiss when you breathe.

Rules for the outer layer: no zips, no velcro, no crinkling shell fabrics, no drawcord toggles that tap. Bone or wood buttons, wax the laces, tape anything that can rattle. Do the sit-up test at home in a quiet room — if you can hear your own clothes, so can they, and to them it is a rockfall in the north.

Nothing scented. Stop using soap with a smell four days before. No repellent, no sunscreen, no deodorant, no balm. Do not smoke for a week; their chimneys have never carried smoke and there is no reason that should change on your account.

And: freeze your clothes. Forty-eight hours in a domestic freezer kills the mites, eggs and springtail hitchhikers you have no idea you are carrying. Brush your boots to bare rubber. Turn out cuffs and pockets and pick out the seeds. You will be introducing your body's entire ecology to a sealed room; the least you can do is bring less of it.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat or a stiff hood — worn to shade **your** eyes from the sky, held off to one side, never over the gap.

## Breath

This is the hardest discipline and the one most travellers get wrong.

Your breath is 34°C, saturated, and vast. It will go in as weather. It will settle on every polished flint face in the town and bead there, and it will take away, in one exhalation, every notice, every price, every piece of gossip, every prayer, and the town's own name, mid-morning, unfinished.

Understand what that is. It is not pollution. It is not even really a message. It is walking into a library and closing every book at once, gently, without malice, and then doing it again forty seconds later.

Preparation, beginning six weeks out: train comfortable dry breath-holds. You want ninety seconds without the CO2 flush that makes you gasp — not a maximum, a *cruising* ninety seconds, repeatable a hundred times in an afternoon. Then train the turn: hold, look, withdraw the face, exhale sideways and downwind, breathe three times, return. Practise it against a doorframe until it is not a decision.
