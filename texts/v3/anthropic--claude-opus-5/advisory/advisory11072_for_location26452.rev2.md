# Going Up to the Long Note: A Traveller's Preparation

## First, the honest part

This place will take something from you and not give it back. Even done perfectly — best protection, shortest stay, clean descent — you will come down with less hearing than you went up with, and a thin permanent ring in the quiet of your own rooms. Every harvester on the Feather Road knows this. They consider it part of the price of the cakes. You should decide, before you spend anything else, whether you consider it part of the price of the view.

If you earn your living by your ears — musician, interpreter, physician, anyone who listens for a subtlety — do not go. If you have tinnitus already, do not go; it will be made permanent and louder. If you are prone to depersonalisation, migraine with aura, or the kind of depression that resolves into stillness rather than agitation, think very hard. The plateau is not hostile. It is *finished*, and finished things are dangerously restful to a certain sort of mind.

## Months out

**Get your hearing baselined.** A full audiogram, both ears, bone and air conduction, extended high frequencies if available. You want a before-picture so you know what the plateau cost and whether it is still costing you six months later.

**Get custom plugs made.** This is the single most important purchase of the trip and it cannot be done at the trailhead. Impressions of both canals, deep-fit, solid silicone — not vented musicians' filters, you do not want fidelity, you want a wall. Order **three pairs.** You will lose one to the wind and one to a crevice and you will need the third.

**Learn the signs.** Not a phrasebook, not a card in your pocket — you will not be able to consult a card with one hand on your hood. Learn two hundred signs to fluency, including the ones that don't exist below: the flat hand tilted (*the wind is unchanged*, which is hello, which is also *nothing is wrong*), the two-finger tap on the breastbone (*I hear you*). Practise them with your travelling partner in a room with the extractor fan on, at night, in gloves. Practise them until you can sign while walking, which is harder than it sounds, and until you can sign *stop*, *go back*, *I am hurt*, *I am cold in a way I don't like* without thinking.

On the plateau you will be mute for the whole of your stay. People underestimate this catastrophically. Six or eight days without a spoken word to another human being, in a sound you cannot get out of, does something to the social animal. The signs are not a convenience. They are your only rope back to other people.

**Train for load-carrying and for leaning.** The plateau's wind will push you with roughly the force of a full jerrycan hung off your chest, constantly, from one quarter, for as long as you are there. Your downwind leg will do most of the work; your neck will ache from holding your head against the blast. Build the obliques and the neck.

**Go with a harvest party if one will have you.** Six days up the Feather Road is not a formality — it is the acclimatisation that will keep you alive on a tableland somewhere north of four thousand metres. Do not accept any route that gets you to the rim faster. Arriving fresh is arriving dying.

## Ears: the discipline

The note sits near 100 dB at the pinnacles and never drops below 80 anywhere. There is no night relief. There is no indoors. Occupational guidance would give you fifteen minutes a day at that level; you are proposing a week.

Wear **double protection continuously, from the moment you crest the lip to the moment you go back under the cloud.** Custom plugs inserted properly (jaw open, pinna up and back, seat them until your own voice goes hollow), then muffs over the top — the heavy kind with liquid-filled cushions, which seal better against the low end. Foam alone is not enough; the chord is broad and low, and low frequencies walk straight through foam and then through the bones of your skull. Thirty-five decibels of real-world attenuation is a good day. That brings you to a sane 65, which you can live in.

Two traps:

**The first is the muffs themselves.** In a forty-knot stream the cups sing — the wind tears at the shells and makes a second roar right against your ears. You must put something smooth and windproof over them: a taut shell hood, a fur ruff, a helmet cover. A hood that luffs is worse than no hood.

**The second is day three.** This is the real danger, and everyone who has been up warns about it in the same words. On the third day the brain gives up and files the roar under *silence*, and the plateau becomes calm and white and beautiful, and you will take the muffs off. Not for long. Just to feel it. Just to check something. And then again, and again, and each time you are spending hearing you cannot re-earn. Make a rule with your partner: **the muffs come off only inside a lee cavity, only once a day, only for the count of sixty, and only if the other person is watching the clock.** Sign the rule to each other before you go up. Keep it.

Wearing plugs for a week ruins ear canals. Bring drying drops (acetic acid and alcohol), keep your hands clean when you seat them, and inspect each other's ears nightly with a headlamp. Swimmer's ear at altitude, mute, six days from a doctor, is a miserable way to end a trip.

And know what protection cannot do. The chord will still come into you through your sternum and the long bones of your legs. You will feel tired in a way that has no cause you can name. You will sleep badly and dream oddly. That part is not preventable. It is simply what it is like to stand inside a very large instrument.

## Eyes and skin

Light arrives from both hemispheres: hard sun from a near-black zenith, and the whole cloud sea throwing it back up under your chin. Nothing is shaded. There is no such thing as a shadow to stand in.

- **Glacier glasses with solid side and bottom shields.** Category 4 lenses. Two spare pairs — the airborne grit will frost the lenses of the first pair into uselessness within days. Never wipe a gritted lens; blow it, then dab.
- **A slit goggle in your chest pocket.** Wood or leather, Inuit pattern, a two-millimetre slot. It weighs nothing, it cannot be scratched, and if both your glasses go you can still walk out instead of going snow-blind on stone.
- **Zinc, thick and opaque, reapplied every two hours** — and applied to the places nobody thinks of, because the light comes from underneath: the underside of the nose, the underside of the chin and jaw, the inside of the nostrils, the lower lip, the ear lobes, the backs of the hands at every angle. Harvesters come down with burnt palates from breathing through an open mouth while working.
- **A face covering that seals and does not flap.** The grit is the same grit that has polished every windward stone on the plateau to a shine. It will polish you.
- Lip balm with a sunblock, by the dozen.

## The wind, and the art of tethering

Everything you have ever learned about windy places is half-wrong here, and the wrong half is helpful. Because the wind never gusts and never lulls, **you can lean on it.** Set your weight into it at ten or twelve degrees, trust it like a wall, and walk. In a gusting place that would kill you. Here, the constancy is the safest thing about the plateau — and it is worth understanding in your body that the one event that could genuinely hurt you is the one event that does not happen.

**Tether everything.** This is the discipline that replaces "don't drop things." A dropped glove is not on the ground, it is four kilometres downwind and travelling. Lanyard the gloves to the cuffs, the hat to the collar, the glasses to the neck, the knife to the belt, the pack lid to the pack, the map to your wrist. When you open the pack, open it in the lee of a boulder and open it a hand's width at a time.

**Nothing loose, nothing that luffs.** A flapping shell will fatigue you, shred itself, and add its own noise inside your hood. Tape the cuffs. Cinch the hem. Smooth and taut.

**Wear a helmet.** Three reasons: horizontal hail, which is what knocks the swifts out of the sky; the bone-dropping raptor working the sheep carcasses; and the simple fact that a stumble on a cobble stripe with a heavy pack and a steady shove at your back is a head-first stumble.

**Walk along the stripes, not across them.** The animals worked this out and so should you. Crossing the corduroy all day will wreck your ankles.

## Shelter, water, fire

**Do not bring a tent.** You cannot pitch one, you cannot keep one, and even if you could it would be an insult (see *Manners*). Sleep as the harvesters sleep, in the lee cavities of the pinnacles: dry, out of the blast, surprisingly warm.

Choose your cavity in daylight and choose it for sound. Some of the tafoni are resonators and boom; some are dead and quiet, comparatively. A quiet cavity is worth an hour's walking. Take a closed-cell mat (the floor is stone and the stone is a conductor of both cold and chord), a good bag, a bivvy sack against the drift that sifts in overnight. Do not evict swifts. Do not sleep under an occupied ceiling; they will rain on you and you will be inhaling their dust.

**Water:** the single stream is clean-ish condensate, but do not drink downstream of the colony — the guano crust leaches. Filter and treat regardless. Off the stream, harvest rime: the horizontal ice feathers on the lee of everything, easily snapped off by the armful. They regrow to the same length, which makes them the one thing on the plateau you may take without changing anything.

**Fuel:** carry your own, liquid-fuel stove for the altitude and cold, and cook *inside the cavity* with the door of your body to the entrance. An open flame outside is not a flame, it is a rumour. Do not burn sheep dung; it is not yours and it is not much.

**Sanitation:** carry everything out. All of it. There is no soil here to receive you, and paper will travel forty kilometres and still be recognisable in a decade. Doubled bags, taped, in the bottom of the pack.

## Health particulars

- **Altitude:** pulse oximeter, a slow profile, and the honesty to turn around. Acetazolamide if your physician agrees; nothing sedating, ever, at this height, however badly the note is keeping you awake.
- **Hydration:** forty knots of dry air strips water out of you through your lungs and skin faster than thirst reports it. Drink to a schedule, by the clock, whether you want to or not. Electrolytes. Dehydration up here presents as *stupidity*, and stupidity on a plateau with a two-thousand-metre edge is the whole risk in one word.
- **Dust mask** for any time you are working in the feather drift or the guano ground. Old bird colonies breed things that live in lungs. The harvesters all cough, and they think it is normal.
- **Allergy:** if you have any shellfish or dust-mite sensitivity, do not eat the drift cakes without testing a crumb first. Chitin is chitin. Anaphylaxis, mute, six days out, is not survivable.
- **Shake out your boots.** Thousands of jumping spiders per hectare, and they like a warm crevice as much as you do. They are harmless and unnervingly attentive.
- **Midges** at the Sink, in a green ring thirty paces wide. Bring repellent for exactly that one hour of your trip.

## Safety, when you cannot shout

This is the reorganising fact. **You cannot call for help and you will not hear a call.** A whistle is a joke here. Therefore:

- **Never lose line of sight with your partner.** Not for a minute. Agree on the maximum separation and make it less than you think.
- **Carry a signal mirror.** There is an obscene amount of sun. A mirror flash crosses the plateau where a scream does nothing.
- **Carry a chemical light or headlamp on your chest, switched on before dusk,** and agree a code: two flashes *come*, three *stop where you are*, continuous *I am hurt*.
- **A bound slate and grease pencil on a lanyard.** Paper will not survive one careless second. The slate is for the things signs cannot carry: numbers, names, arguments.
- **Do not go into the fog belt casually.** The daily fog runs through the first kilometres of the plateau — which is also exactly where the rim is, and the rim is two thousand metres of nothing under a lip of welded ash. If you are in the fog belt near the edge, be roped, or be elsewhere. Better: do your rim-looking in the clear of afternoon, and camp inland.

## Manners

The harvesters' rule is not superstition and it is not conservation in the sense you know it. It is closer to workmanship: *this is a place which has finished happening, and it is bad practice to start it again.*

So: **build nothing.** No cairn. No windbreak. No tripod platform, no flagged route, no scratched initials, no line of stones to mark where you slept. The urge to leave a cairn is one of the most reliable urges in the human species and you will feel it strongly on the third day, when the note goes quiet and the plateau becomes beautiful. Do not.

Take nothing structural. A feather from beneath the colony, if you have been given leave; rime, which renews; nothing else. Do not lift a cushion plant, which is five hundred years old and will not come again in your line. Do not walk on the deep moss at the colony; it is the softest thing for thirty kilometres and the only thing here that would notice being stood on.

Greet everyone you pass with the tilted flat hand, even at distance, even if they are a stranger and you are both exhausted. It means *the wind is unchanged*, which means *all is as it should be*, which is the only reassurance this place offers and the only one it needs.

And when someone signs something to you, tap the two fingers to your breastbone. *I hear you.* Nobody hears anything. That is precisely why it is said.

## The mind, and the going down

Budget your days and fix your descent date before you go up, and write it on the slate, and do not renegotiate it on the plateau. Decisions made inside the note are not made by quite the same person who made the plans.

Expect, roughly: two days of misery, in which the sound is an assault and you will privately want to leave; then the reclassification, after which everything becomes still and enormous and unreasonably good; then, if you stay past a week, a soft reluctance to descend that you will justify with excellent reasons. That reluctance is the plateau's only predator. It has killed people gently.

While you are there, do the things this place uniquely permits. Sing at the very top of your voice, for as long as you like; no one can hear you, including you. Find a wingless cricket on a stone in the evening and answer its abdomen-drumming with your knuckle, and see what it does. Sit in the lee of a pinnacle at dusk and watch a river of a million birds turn above forty hectares of moss, and understand that everything alive up here is eating a thin invisible snowfall of insects that came up out of a forest four kilometres below and cannot go back.

**Coming down:** arrange, in advance, a week of nothing. You will be functionally deaf for several days and the chord will stay in your skull for a month. Have someone meet you at the bottom of the Feather Road who signs. Do not schedule conversations, negotiations, or anything requiring you to be certain you have understood. Be careful crossing roads, in worlds that have them.

Get the audiogram again at six weeks. Compare.

And then, at some point in the following year, in a quiet room at two in the morning, you will hear it — the same chord, unchanged, no gusts, no lulls — and you will understand that you did not actually leave, that a small part of you is still standing out on the stone stripes in the doubled light, inside something that was already complete before you arrived and is complete now that you're gone.

Pack accordingly.
