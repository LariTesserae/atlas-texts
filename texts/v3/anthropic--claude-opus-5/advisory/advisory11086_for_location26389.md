# Briefing: Visiting the Sheet

## Understand what you are actually preparing for

You are not preparing for altitude, cold, or wet, though you will get all three. You are preparing to spend time in a place where four of your most deeply automatic assumptions have been worn blunt: that heat spreads, that sound carries, that causes precede effects, and that two similar things are two things.

Almost everything that goes wrong for visitors goes wrong because they packed for the weather and not for the physics.

Budget three weeks of preparation minimum. Budget a season of aftercare.

---

## Body: before you go

**Altitude.** The sky is dark blue at noon and the loud stars are out all day. Treat this as extreme elevation: eight weeks of graduated acclimatisation, a full cardiopulmonary workup, and a hard honest look at your pulmonary artery pressure. Carry acetazolamide and a way of descending, and know in advance that *there is no descending*. The lowlands are a rumour in the oldest songs. If you develop pulmonary oedema on the plateau, your only exit is your entry point, and your entry point may not be where you left it (see **Measurement**). This is the single most likely way for a visitor to die, and it kills people who felt fine on day four.

**Eyes.** The whole world is a mirror three millimetres deep. You are receiving high-altitude ultraviolet twice, once from above and once from below, and the below-dose arrives at an angle your eyelids and brow-ridge have never had to defend against. Get side-shielded, deeply tinted, high-blocking lenses and a broad soft hat, and wear both from first light. Carry a spare pair — but see the warning about identical lenses under **Individuation**. Pack anaesthetic eye drops and accept that if you get arc-eye out on the flat, you will be blind for two days somewhere with no shade and no landmarks.

**Feet.** Your feet will be in moving water for the entire visit. Not soaked — *submerged*, continuously, at a depth of three millimetres, for weeks. There is no dry ground anywhere on the plateau except the town mounds and the tops of the cushions, which you must not stand on.

Boots: one-piece, seamless, welt above the waterline, insulated, and soled with something that grips wet polished stone *and* thin ice, because you will cross the boundary between the two dozens of times a day as the ice-tide drifts. Bring a small pry-bar. If you stand still in the shadow of a wall for twenty minutes, the Sheet will freeze around your soles and you will be pinned; this is comic the first time and lethal if it happens while you are hypothermic and alone.

Foot care is your evening religion. Nightly: off, dry, inspect, powder, oil, fresh socks. Immersion foot is the injury that ends most visits early, and it is entirely preventable and entirely your own fault.

## Individuation: the most important thing in this document

**Bring nothing in pairs. Bring nothing mass-produced in multiples. Bring nothing identical to anything else in your possession, and nothing identical to anything already on the plateau.**

Your world makes things by making them the same. Screws, batteries, cartridges, pills, coins, buttons, tent stakes, carabiners, printed pages, socks, gloves, contact lenses. On the plateau, sameness is not a manufacturing achievement; it is an unfinished sentence, and the world will finish it for you. Weeks, sometimes days: the two things begin to be one thing that is in two places. Scratch one, the other is scratched. Lose one, and you have lost half of the other.

So: before departure, you will spend an entire evening making your kit **ugly and singular.** Every screw gets a different notch. Every battery a different band of paint. Every tent stake a different bend. Left boot and right boot get visibly different lacing, different dye, different scuffing — they came off the same last, and that matters here more than it has ever mattered anywhere. Files, punches, enamel, sandpaper, a hot needle. Nothing leaves your kit-bag looking like its neighbour.

Some specifics:

- **Spectacles.** Two lenses ground to the same prescription, mounted in the same frame, in the same orientation, held rigidly together for weeks, is the worst object you could possibly design for this place. If you can, bring a different correction in each eye and etch the rims differently. If you cannot, bring a monocle and a patch. People have come home unable to explain why one eye now sees what the other eye sees, and where.
- **Binoculars.** Same problem. Leave them. Take a small telescope.
- **Medication.** Identical tablets in an identical blister strip. Decant into a single pouch, and if the dose matters, weigh it rather than counting it. Assume the pills in the strip are, by week three, fewer pills than the count says.
- **Anything with a mechanism containing repeated parts.** Chains, ball bearings, magazine springs. It will not fail dramatically. It will simply stop having as many parts as it needs.
- **Yourself.** You are bilaterally symmetrical, and you have been walking around for decades assuming that this is free. On the plateau, do not clap. Do not press your palms flat together. Do not perform symmetrical exercises at length. The locals wear a ring on one hand and a thread on the other wrist, they never gesture with both arms in the same shape, and they will flinch if you clap — you will find out why on the second day, when your hands feel briefly like one hand and you have to look at them to be sure.
- **Do not travel with an identical twin.** Do not travel with a twin at all. If you are a twin and your sibling has ever been to the plateau, do not go.
- **Do not dress your party in matching gear.** This is why local clothing is deliberately, almost aggressively mismatched — every garment on the plateau is a one-off, and a group of visitors in identical outfits reads to a townsperson roughly the way a group of people wearing each other's faces would read to you. Come deliberately motley. It is both a safety measure and a courtesy.
- **Do not visit twice in the same clothes.** Actually: do not visit twice, until you have talked to someone who has.

## Heat: unlearn everything

Temperature exists but no longer argues. A hot thing and a cold thing in contact will sit and sulk for hours.

Consequences you must drill into yourself before you go:

**Nothing has a tell.** A pot off the fire is not cooling. It is not steaming, because the air above it isn't being warmed either. Blow on your soup for ten minutes and it will scald you exactly as much as it would have. **Carry two thermometers** (individuated), test everything, and never, ever, use the back of your hand as an instrument. Visitors' hands are always the first thing to get hurt here, and because gradients are lazy, a burn goes on being delivered long after the contact ends — you will have several seconds of "that's fine" followed by a wound.

**Conversely, you will not be warmed by fire.** Sitting by a flame does almost nothing. Chemical hand-warmers do nothing. A bag of hot stones in your bedroll does nothing useful and may burn you across six hours without ever feeling hot.

**Warmth must be generated inside your insulation, or delivered at low temperature over long duration.** In practice: work; shiver; eat; and use a body-temperature-plus-a-little water bladder held in continuous contact for hours. Learn to warm up by moving, and learn that when you stop moving you will not cool down either, which means you will sit in your own sweat inside your clothing and become quietly, slowly, undetectably hypothermic without ever feeling a chill.

**Plan to eat cold.** Cooking is a multi-hour project and the fuel is not yours to burn — there is no wood, no dung, and the only combustible on the plateau is slowly-harvested dried cushion, which is somebody's water supply. Bring calorie-dense food that requires nothing. Bring more than you think. You are working hard, cold, and at altitude, and you will not notice how much you are burning.

## Sound: you cannot call for help

A shout dies at forty paces as if it hit a curtain. Whistles, horns, alarms, your voice at full volume: forty paces. That is the whole of your acoustic world.

**Therefore: rope up.** Not for falling — there is nothing to fall off. For staying in the world with your companion. Glacier-travel discipline: a marked line between every pair of travellers, with an agreed tug-code learned by heart before you arrive. Two tugs *stop*. Three *come to me*. A long steady pull *I am in trouble*. Practise it at home, blindfolded, until it is reflex.

**Bring a slate and chalk.** This is, without exaggeration, the most useful object you will carry. It is how you will speak to locals across a room, how you will negotiate, how you will ask directions, how you will apologise. Bring several slates, all different shapes.

**Learn to speak close.** Conversation here happens at a distance your culture reserves for intimacy or violence. Faces near, voices low and level. Look after your teeth and your breath as a matter of basic courtesy; you will be talking into people. Do not raise your voice when you are not understood — raising it does nothing but signal that you are new. Step closer instead.

**Learn the handful of touch-signals** used in crowds, and the placard conventions, and the cord-tug etiquette in markets. Any surveyor will teach you for the price of listening.

**Learn the surveyor's greeting.** You state, aloud, the distance to the nearest cairn. You are corrected. You thank the person who corrected you. Do this properly and unembarrassedly and you will be treated well ever
