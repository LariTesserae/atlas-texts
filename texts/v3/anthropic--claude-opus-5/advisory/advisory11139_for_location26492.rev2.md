# Preparing for the Shelf of Combs

## First, understand what you are walking into

Not a landscape. An archive, and an interior.

Two facts govern everything else, and if you hold only these two you will behave correctly ninety percent of the time.

**One: nothing here decays.** There is no soil, no bacteria, no oxidation worth the name, no rot, no burrowing. Whatever arrives stays, and whatever stays gets laminated into stone at roughly one thin rind a year. A moth's wing that blew up here three hundred years ago is still legible at eye height. That is the fate of everything you shed — a hair, a fibre, a flake of skin, a breath of water vapour. You are not a visitor to this place; you are a **depositional event**. Plan accordingly.

**Two: sound does not travel, so the place behaves as a room.** Thirty paces and a shout is a whisper. You will hear your own clothing, your own heart, your own joints, and nothing else. There is no ambience to hide in. This does strange things to people within about the first hour and I will come back to it.

---

## Your body

**Altitude.** The acoustic attenuation described — a shout dying at thirty paces — is not ordinary mountain thinness. That is very low pressure. Treat this as equivalent to 8,000–9,000 m on a temperate world, possibly worse. The people who came here were locally adapted over generations; you are not them.

- Do not gate in cold. An unacclimatized arrival at this pressure means confusion in four minutes and unconsciousness in eight, and the confusion phase is the one where you walk off the edge. Either acclimatize for three to five weeks in a pressure-matched world or chamber, or arrive on supplemental oxygen and stay on it the whole time.
- Bring twice the oxygen your itinerary requires. The shelf is two hundred paces long and you will still want more of it than you planned, because the frost tide takes four hours and you will not leave in the middle of it.
- Know the signs of HACE and HAPE in yourself and your companions, and agree in advance — in writing, in the lowlands, while everyone is sensible — on who has authority to order a descent. Up there, the person losing judgement is the one who feels best.

**The cough.** Dry, frigid, fast-moving air over your airways for six hours produces the classic high-altitude bark. It can crack ribs. More to the point here: **a cough is a contamination event.** You will spray warm salted droplets across whatever is within two paces, onto wet calcite, and that mark will be in the stone forever. Wear a heat-and-moisture-exchange mask over the oxygen mask. It halves your water loss and it catches your worst moments.

**Water loss.** The shelf is permanently damp and will dehydrate you faster than a desert. Relative humidity means nothing when there is almost no air to hold anything. You will lose most of your water through your lungs without noticing. Drink on a timer, not on thirst. Bring electrolytes; you will need them more than you need the water.

**Light.** This is the hazard people underestimate, because it comes from below. The cloud-sea is a second sun and it aims at the undersides of things. Expect:

- Goggles with full side and **bottom** gaskets. Ordinary glacier glasses leave you open from beneath and you will be snow-blind by afternoon. Bring a second pair; a lost pair is an evacuation.
- Sunburn in the places you have never burned: under the chin, inside the nostrils, the underside of the nose, the roof of the mouth if you breathe openly, the backs of the ears, the underside of the brow. Mineral paste, thick, reapplied. Nothing scented.
- Face covering by default. It is also the fibre discipline (below), so it costs you nothing extra.

**The green seams.** The wall behind the grove carries a fibrous mineral that shreds into wool — the same stuff the old cord was spun from. In a hornblende host that is very likely an amphibole asbestos, which is the bad kind. You will be breathing hard, in dry air, near an outcrop that sheds. Wear a particulate filter integrated into your mask arrangement, keep away from the wall's cracks, and **do not handle the cord.** It is beautiful and it is half-fused into the floor and it is a lung disease.

**Cold and the mismatch.** You will be lit from two directions and cold in the middle. People get sunburn and frostbite on the same afternoon here. Your hands will be gloved the whole time for contamination reasons anyway; watch your feet, which sit on cemented rubble at the freezing point and get none of the warmth your face is getting.

## The central discipline: leaving nothing

I want to give this its own section because it is the actual work of the visit, and because people fail at it not from malice but from forgetting that there is no cleanup crew and no decay.

**Your breath is the biggest thing you bring.** An adult exhales something like 50–100 mL of water an hour, more at altitude. The hand-cut cistern down at the eastern rim fills to two inches on a good morning. A party of four breathing for six hours puts out considerably more water than the whole shelf's people ever collected in a day. On the combs nearest where you stand and rest, that vapour will freeze out and go into the rind, and it will be a thick, strangely-sourced, isotopically foreign lamina — an alien season, permanently in the record, at the exact height of a human mouth.

You cannot prevent this entirely. You can:
- Keep moving; do not lecture, argue, or rest in a tight corridor.
- Take your rest breaks at the eastern rim in open air, downwind of the grove, or against the hornblende wall.
- If your party has any duty of non-intervention, use exhale absorbers — a soda-lime or desiccant cartridge on the outflow. They are heavy and they are the difference between being a visitor and being a stratum.

**Fibres.** One hair is a three-hundred-year fossil. Wear a smooth-surfaced, sealed outer layer with no fleece, no wool, no napped fabric, no velcro, no fraying hems. Tape the cuffs. Hairnet and balaclava. Some travellers shave; it is not excessive.

**Grease and salt.** Bare skin on a comb waterproofs the flute where you touched it. The film that has run down that rib every morning for centuries will part around your fingerprint, and the comb will grow a dead stripe there, and that stripe will be visible for as long as the comb stands. Salt from sweat does worse — it drives crystallization damage into the calcite. **Never touch a comb, ever, with anything.** Gloves included, because gloves carry what your hands carried.

**Waste.** Everything out. Urine especially: a drop on wet sinter etches, then stains a red-brown lamina that reads, forever, as a season with a strange wind. Rig the bottle system in the lowlands and practise it in bulky clothing, because you will not want to improvise at the edge of a two-thousand-foot fall in a mask.

**Food.** No crumbs, no flakes, no wrappers that shed. Sealed paste tubes eaten inside a bag, the bag resealed. Dry biscuits are forbidden; the first strong gust distributes your lunch across four centuries of stone.

**Fire.** Do not. There has never been combustion on this shelf — that is precisely why the people never slept here and why there are no nights in the record. Soot on white sinter is permanent and unmistakable and it will be the single loudest thing anyone ever did here. Use battery heat or exothermic chemical packs, and carry the spent packs out.

**Soles.** Change footwear at the wall. Come up in crampons if the approach needs them and leave them at a changeover cache at the hornblende — steel on sinter scores it, and the scores are forever. Grove shoes should be soft, light-coloured, non-marking rubber, no deep lugs to carry grit up from the approach, scrubbed clean and inspected before you step in. Black rubber leaves black scuff on white stone. Make this a ritual: the changeover is the threshold, and crossing it is when the visit starts.

## Timing: the day has a shape, and you should obey it

The whole reason to come is the frost tide, and it runs once west in the morning and once east in the evening, and it takes about four hours.

The ideal itinerary:

- **Arrive in the dark, before first light**, with the changeover done and everyone settled. Use red light only, shielded, downward. Your lamp is the first artificial light this place has known; treat it like a lit cigarette in a library.
- **First light** — the combs light from within at low sun angle. This is the standing-lamps hour. It is very short. Have nothing to do at this time except look.
- **The passage of the wet line**, roughly the next four hours. Do not walk it. Find a position — the shelter is ideal, which is presumably why it is where it is — and stay put and let the boundary come to you. You will feel the frost go off the lit flanks before you hear it, a hiss that registers in your teeth. Then the dripping starts, from hundreds of overhangs at once, none of it loud, all of it close. This is the only sustained sound the world makes and you cannot hear it from more than a few paces away, so you must be inside it.
- **Midday** — dry, blazing, silent, and the worst light for looking at anything. This is the hour for the wing, which is lit from below and doesn't care about the sun, and for the archaeology, and for eating.
- **Late afternoon** — the tide runs back east, refreezing what it passes. Your gear will frost. Be off the shelf, or committed to the night, before the eastern run finishes.

**A clock note:** with two light sources and cancelling shadows, you lose all the ordinary time cues. Wear a watch. Better, learn to read the shade tide — it is a more accurate clock than your sense of the day will be, and reading it is the local literacy.

**Fog days** come about one in five and cannot be predicted from the shelf. If the cloud-sea rises, the shelf is swallowed and the light becomes sourceless, total, and so bright that closing your eyes does not help. **This is the most dangerous condition here**, because the fall is on three sides and there is no horizon, no shadow, no depth, and no sound to navigate by. Rules for fog:

- Stop moving. Sit down where you are if you are in the grove; the combs are a fence and the packed areas will not let you fall.
- If you are near the eastern rim — where the cistern and the shelter and the table are, which is exactly where you will want to be — you are in the open and you must already be on a line. Fix a guide cord from the hornblende wall to the rim area before the day starts, on every visit, without exception. Girth-hitch it to a massive fallen crown with a wide soft sling; do not drill, do not bolt, do not score. **Take the cord out with you.** There is already one piece of cord on this shelf that has become part of the ground, and one is the right number.
- Fog days are growth days. If you have the oxygen and the patience, sitting still in the white while the whole grove silently gains weight is, by every account I trust, the thing people remember longest. But you sit still. You do not walk.

## Moving in the grove

The passages are fixed and old and some of them will not accept you. Before you leave home, measure your width **in the gear you will actually wear**, pack included, and then practise threading a mock-up of the spacings with a bell hung on every upright. You want the bells silent. This sounds childish and it is the best preparation you can do.

- Pack off before any tight thread. Hand it through, or better, leave it at the wall and carry a small chest bag.
- No poles, or rubber-ferruled poles used at arm's length from stone. Metal tips score, and a slipped tip into a waist is a catastrophe.
- **The waists.** Every comb stands on a neck that may be as thin as a wrist, holding a crown that weighs what a large animal weighs. They have stood on those waists for centuries. You brushing past with a frame pack is a novel load. Assume any comb can be killed by contact and that the killing will be lethal to you too.
- Falls here are almost soundless. There is no warning noise, no rattle in advance, no cry from a companion thirty paces off that you would hear. This is not a place with good luck in it. Move deliberately, look up before you stop anywhere, and do not linger under leaning crowns.
- **Footing** is cemented rubble, vertically fluted, and it passes through wet twice a day. Wet calcite film with a dusting of grit is slick in a way your feet will not predict. And because things cast two shadows that cancel, you cannot see the shape of the ground. Use the seams — the hair-thin black lines where pillars have grown together — as your depth cues. They are the only true dark here and the only honest edges your eyes will find.
- **The hovering.** Standing figures appear to float slightly. It is optics and you will not unlearn it, and it produces a mild vestibular unease near the rim. Keep a hand on stone-adjacent reference (not on a comb — on the hornblende wall, on a fallen crown, on the line) whenever you are within ten paces of the fall.

**One social oddity to expect:** your companions' faces are lit from below. Human faces lit from below read as hostile, grieving, or unfamiliar. You will misread each other all day. Agree in advance that nobody's expression means what it looks like, and say things out loud that you would ordinarily let a face say.

## The acoustics, and what they do to you

Everything you hear is happening within a few paces. There is no distance in the sound world, which means there is no outside, which means the shelf feels like a room with a two-thousand-foot floor.

- Speech is effortful and quiet and comes out wrong-timbred. Voices sound like they are being made rather than carried. Keep sentences short.
- You cannot shout for help. A whistle is nearly useless at this pressure. Carry a signal mirror — the up-light off the cloud is constant and a mirror works beautifully here — and agree on hand signals before you go up, including the three that matter: *stop*, *come*, *I am in trouble*.
- Stay within line of sight of a partner at all times. Not within earshot. Earshot is thirty paces and it is a lie.
- Budget one full hour of no speech. Not as an exercise in reverence, though it functions as one, but because there is a sound here — the wet line's dripping — that you cannot hear while anyone is talking, and it is the only thing the world has to say.

## Water and the cistern

Someone climbed very hard, with a grey quartzite hammerstone and no metal, and pecked an oval basin three feet across and cut five channels to lead five separate drip-lines into it, and it still works. It fills to two inches on a clear morning and the sun takes most of it back.

- **Bring all your own water.** All of it. The cistern is not a resource, it is an instrument.
- Do not put your lips, your bottle, your hands, or your breath over it. If you are compelled to drink — and I understand the compulsion; this is water that has never touched soil, never passed through anything, was vapour an hour ago and dust before that — take it from a drip-line above the basin into a clean vessel, before the channel, and take a swallow, and leave the rest of the fill to the basin. It is very soft water with almost nothing in it and it will not hydrate you meaningfully. It is not for that.
- If a channel is blocked by a shard, you will want to clear it. Think about this before you are standing over it with your fingers twitching. The argument for clearing: the whole apparatus is an intervention, and the intention of the person who made it was that it fill. The argument against: the block is now also part of the record, and you have no idea how long it has been there or whether its being there is the most recent event on this shelf. My counsel: leave it, photograph it, note it, and let the person after you inherit the same decision. If you do clear it, use a piece of fallen sinter, never a tool, and write down exactly what you did and where.
- **Do the thing they did.** Sit in the three-walled shelter during the passage of the wet line and count drops. There are tallies cut into the flattest slab, in groups too large and too irregular to be days, and the best reading anyone has is that somebody sat here and counted. Counting is a different experience from watching. After about twenty minutes you will understand something about why people came up here that no description gives you, and it is not a thought, so I will not try to write it down. Do not cut your own tally. Count on your fingers and take the number home.

## The made things

Three walls of stacked sinter slabs, corbelled as though a roof were intended, facing the up-light so that it is brighter inside without one. It holds four people sitting. Sit in it — it is a room and rooms are meant to be sat in, and nothing about sitting harms it. Do not stack, straighten, or rebuild. Do not add a stone. The corbelling is a question and you should not answer it.

The two carved figures, each the length of a finger, both of them combs: fluted, waisted, crowned. When these people made images, the thing they made images of was this place. Photograph them where they lie. Do not pick them up — not because you would drop them, but because their position is data and you will not be able to put them back exactly, and there is no one to ask.

And take nothing. Not a shard, not a chip of a broken comb, not a pinch of the red dust. Every lamina is a dated year and every fallen crown is a fallen century, and this is one of the few worlds where a removed object leaves a permanent, readable hole. Many multiverse conventions class places like this as sealed records; whether or not your charter says so, behave as though it does.

## The wing

In a broken comb near the west end, about two hundred laminae deep, there is two-thirds of a moth's wing — membranous, brown, magnified a little by the curve of the stone above it, roughly three hundred years old, held at eye height, lit from below by cloud-glare.

Go to it last. Everyone who has ever stood in front of it has stood in front of it for a while, and you will not be the exception, so build the time into your oxygen calculation rather than discovering it as a deficit.

Bring a hand lens, 10×, on a lanyard, and hold it away from the stone. **Do not breathe toward the surface** — your warm breath will condense on cold calcite instantly and then freeze, and you will have added a fog layer to the only animal on the shelf. Turn your head to exhale. Better, hold your breath, which at that altitude you will manage for about four seconds, which is exactly the right length of look, repeated.

It came up from a world with air thick enough to fly in. It landed on a wet flank. The flank grew over it. That is the entire biography and it is worth the climb by itself.

## Whether to stay the night

The people did not. There is no fuel here — no wood, no dung, no oil, nothing that burns — and so there were never any nights on this shelf, and the only darkness that exists is a few finger-widths deep in the seams between fused pillars.

You, with batteries and a sealed bag, could stay. Consider carefully what you would be doing. A camp is a warm, wet, lit, human-smelling object sitting overnight in a place that has never had one, breathing its extra season into the nearest hundred combs while the frost grows around it.

If you stay anyway:

- Camp on the hornblende side, hard against the wall, on bare rock if you can find it, never in the grove.
- Expect the full night freeze, and expect your equipment to come back to you furred in the morning like everything else.
- The sky is a dark, bruised blue by day; at night, above a two-thousand-foot cloud-sea with almost no air in the way, it is as good as sky gets anywhere. That is the honest case for staying.
- You will be awake for a lot of it. The silence is not restful. It is the kind of quiet that makes your own pulse sound like an intruder.

My own advice: make your first visit a day visit, dawn to the eastern tide, the way they did it. If you still want the night after that, you will want it for better reasons.

## If something goes wrong

Accept in advance that there is effectively no rescue. The approach is a very hard climb, the shelf is unlandable in the general case, sound does not carry, and a comb-fall or an edge-fall is final. The realistic mitigations are all preventive:

- Two people minimum, four ideal, never more than four moving in the grove at once.
- Line-of-sight discipline, mirrors, agreed hand signals.
- A hard turnaround time decided in the lowlands and enforced by the clock, not the mood.
- Redundant goggles, redundant gloves, redundant oxygen.
- A recall gate or extraction marker sited at the changeover cache by the wall, on bare hornblende, where it cannot fall and cannot be lost in fog — and a rehearsed route to it from anywhere on the shelf, counted in paces, memorable while hypoxic.

---

## Going down

Before you leave, do the sweep. Take a light — even in daylight — and rake it at a low angle across the wet flanks of every comb within two paces of anywhere you stood, sat, ate, or knelt. Fibres show up brilliantly in raking light on wet calcite. Pick them off with tweezers, not fingers. Check your soles. Check the shelter floor. Check the cistern rim. Count your party's items out against the list you counted in — and make that list, item by item, before you go up, because at that altitude you will not be able to compose one.

Then pull the guide cord, take the changeover cache, and go.

---

## Afterward

Two things tend to happen to people and it is worth being warned.

The first is auditory. For a few days after you come down, ordinary distance will sound wrong — voices arriving from across a street, traffic, wind in trees. You have spent a day in a world where everything audible was within arm's reach, and your hearing recalibrates to it faster than you expect and comes back slower.

The second is harder to name. The grove is not waiting for anything. It reached its arrangement a long time ago and it will maintain it, comb by comb, waist by waist, fall by fall, for as long as dust keeps blowing up from the south, and it does not require you, or the people who came with hammerstones, or the moth. Some travellers find this consoling and some find it flattening, and you will not know which you are until you are standing in it with two cancelled shadows and no weight under your feet.

Go anyway. But go light, go clean, go quiet, and leave the place exactly one visit older than you found it.
