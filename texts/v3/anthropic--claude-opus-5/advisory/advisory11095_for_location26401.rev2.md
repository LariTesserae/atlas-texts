# Briefing: Visiting the Standing Water

## First, understand what you are visiting

It is not a lake with people on it. It is a building, and the building is alive, and the building is also the telephone, the air supply, the food, the rope, and — in a sense the locals will not explain and you should not press — the neighbour. You will be staying *inside* an organism that two hundred people have spent uncountable generations negotiating with.

Almost every mistake a visitor makes comes from forgetting this and treating the cane as scenery.

The second thing to understand: everything here is slow, and everything here is *narrow*. Two hundred people at 3,000 metres on a thin calorie budget in twelve-degree water have no margin. They can absorb a guest. They cannot absorb a guest who costs them anything. Plan your entire trip around costing them as little as possible.

---

## Your body: start six months out

**Altitude.** Three thousand metres is about 0.7 bar. That is roughly fourteen percent oxygen at sea-level equivalent, and it will cut your breath-hold in half before you even feel the cold. Arrive at the rim at least eight to ten days early and sleep low, climb high, or come up in stages from whatever gateway town you use. Acetazolamide if you tolerate it, but understand that it is a diuretic and you are about to spend your days in cold water, which is *also* a diuretic. You will be dehydrated constantly and will not feel thirsty. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

**Cold.** Twelve degrees kills unacclimatised people not by hypothermia but by the gasp. Cold-shock response: your first immersion produces an involuntary inhalation you cannot suppress, and if your face is under when it happens, that is the whole story. Spend the months before your visit getting into cold water — genuinely cold, repeatedly, until entry produces nothing but a flinch. This is the single highest-value preparation you can do and it cannot be faked or rushed.

**Fat.** Put on four to six kilos. You will lose it there. Lean travellers shiver themselves into stupidity by the third day and make the kind of decisions that end trips. The locals grease their shoulders with pig fat for a reason and you should accept it when offered; it does less than you'd think for heat and more than you'd think for skin.

**Ears and sinuses.** Learn Frenzel equalisation, properly, from someone who knows. Valsalva will fail you below ten metres and blow your sinuses out. If you arrive with a head cold, the entire vertical dimension of this place is closed to you, and the vertical dimension is the place. Do not travel congested. Do not travel with an untreated tooth either — an old filling with a void under it will produce, at eight metres, a pain that genuinely ends expeditions.

**Apnea.** Train it, but train it with a partner and never in still water alone, and then throw away your numbers, because at altitude in cold water after a day of shivering you will have perhaps forty percent of your home-world capability. Plan for that. Build no schedule that requires a good dive.

---

## Your germs: the non-negotiable part

Two hundred people, isolated, with no medicine beyond herbs and patience.

A commonplace upper-respiratory virus that inconveniences you for three days could plausibly kill twenty of them, and the ones it kills will be the old women who hold the knock-grammar and the birth-lore. This is not a hypothetical. It is the standard outcome of this kind of contact and it has happened more times than any traveller's registry likes to print.

Quarantine yourself for a minimum of ten days before you cross, alone, and cancel the trip if you develop so much as a sore throat. Take whatever immunisations your origin culture offers. Arrive healthy or do not arrive. If you cannot commit to this, go somewhere with a bigger population and a worse view.

---

## Your gear's germs: the other non-negotiable part

You are carrying, on the seams of your bag and in the soles of your boots and in the damp corner of any wetsuit you have ever worn in fresh water elsewhere, the spores and zoospores and larvae of another world.

The frogs in the nodes are an amphibian population in a closed basin with no refuge. A chytrid analogue introduced on a damp bootlace ends them in a season, and the silence that follows would be the loudest thing that ever happened to this place. Worse: a water-mould that takes the cane. A column dies in a day and a half of slow toppling. A *plant* — remember, it is one plant — might die more slowly and take everything with it.

So: every item that touches water gets dried to bone-dry, frozen, or bleached and rinsed, and then sealed. No organic material of any kind. No food with seeds. No plant fibre. Check your boot treads, your cuffs, your bag's velcro. Turn out your pockets and brush them. Do this obsessively, and then do it once more at the rim.

---

## What to bring

**Bring:**

- **Your own food, dense and fatty, quietly.** You will burn four thousand calories a day and they cannot spare them. Nuts, fat, sugar. Eat privately unless you are sharing generously, and share generously.
- **Salt.** A freshwater bowl at altitude, a diving population that sweats and urinates its electrolytes away all day. Salt is the perfect gift: precious, consumable, changes nothing, disappears.
- **Fat or oil.** Same logic. Calories and waterproofing in one.
- **Beads.** The caddis larvae cement the house-walls with dropped beads; beads clearly circulate. Small, pretty, valueless to you, decorative to them, and they end up in the architecture, which is a nice thing to leave.
- **Obsidian**, if you can source it honestly — but only a little. It comes over the rim by trade, and a visitor who dumps a year's supply of it into a two-hundred-person economy has made a mess he will not be there to clean up.
- **Wax earplugs for sleeping only.** Not for diving.
- **Acidified alcohol ear drops.** Otitis externa is the endemic misery of this place. Drops after every session in the water, without fail. A bad ear here means a month of not being able to descend, which means a month of sitting on a raft being useless in the rain.
- **Wound care, and more than you think.** Every graze on a cane-stem goes septic in constant twelve-degree water. Obsidian cuts are clean but deep. Iodine, closures, dressings that stick to wet skin. A course of broad-spectrum antibiotics.
- **A greased felt shirt from the town, accepted as a gift or traded for.** Wear theirs. It works, and wearing it says something.

**Think very hard before bringing:**

- **A wetsuit.** A 5mm open-cell freediving suit is the correct equipment for 12°C and it will double your working day. It will also make you visibly a different order of creature, and it needs weight, and there is no metal here — bring stone or fired-clay weights on a fibre belt, and expect them to be fascinating. If you bring it, bring it as a tool you use and take away, not as a marvel you display.
- **Fins.** Honestly: fins would make you the best diver in the basin within a week, and that is a social event, not a convenience. If you bring them, bring them and take them home. Do not leave them. Do not leave two.
- **Fire-making.** Fire here lives in clay bowls and is guarded like a small animal. A ferro rod is the most disruptive object in your luggage. If you give one, give it privately, to a specific host, and understand you are handing over something that will be argued about for three generations.

**Do not bring:** metal blades to leave behind, mirrors, anything loud, anything battery-powered you would be upset to lose, weapons, and any object whose absence you would mourn. Everything gets wet. Everything sinks eventually. Sixty metres is sixty metres.

## The three rules that will keep you alive under the water

**One: breathe out on the way up. Always. Without exception.**

This is the killer, and it is a killer *specific to this place*, because this place offers you air at depth.

You tap a node at eight metres. The gas is rich, cool, faintly sour, and it feels wonderful. You fill your lungs. Then you swim for the surface.

At 3,000 metres, surface pressure is 0.7 bar. At eight metres of water it is about 1.5. Your lungs will try to double in volume on that ascent. They will not be allowed to. What follows is a lung tear and gas in your arteries, and you will lose consciousness in the last two metres of a dive you thought was over, in front of people who have no idea what has happened to you because it never happens to them — they exhale on ascent the way you blink, without knowing they do it.

Rehearse it dry. Rehearse it shallow. Make it a reflex before you go anywhere near a node.

**Two: node etiquette is not etiquette.**

Never open a stranger's node. Never open one that has been opened twice — learn to read the scarring and the plug-marks before you carry a spile at all, and if you are not certain, you are not certain, and you go without. A stem breathed out too often goes soft, and the column comes down over a day and a half, and it may come down on somebody's mid-town room, and the fact that you were only borrowing a breath will not be relevant to anyone.

Carry your own spile, your own mallet, and your own waxed pith plugs, and re-plug what you open, and mark it.

Also: the gas is sour for a reason. There is carbon dioxide in it. A mild headache after a node breath is normal and fades at the surface. A headache that does not fade is your body telling you something, and the worst possible response — the instinctive one — is to go down and take another node to clear it. Don't.

**Three: never swim alone out of sight of a dwelling.**

The bowl is a mile and a quarter across. The columns are ten to twenty metres apart and they all look alike. Cloud comes over the rim in tongues and sits for an hour. Shouting is swallowed by water and flattened by distance. People here navigate by memory and by the density of the knocking, and you have neither.

Agree a lost-signal with your hosts before you need it. Learn where northeast is and keep it in your body. Do not swim at night.

---

## The knocking: learn it before you arrive, if you possibly can

The plant is the town's voice. It is never silent, and the pulse of it is the actual substance of living there.

At minimum, memorise: **two-and-one, come up. A long roll, a body in trouble. A slow triplet repeated, a birth — and during it, nobody taps idly.** Assume there are forty more signals you don't know and that half of what you hear is a conversation about you.

And then: **do not tap.** Not once, not experimentally, not to see if it works. A visitor drumming on a stem is a stranger leaning on a hospital intercom. Touch the paddle-worn spots with your hand, feel the sound come through the wood into your wrist bones, and keep your mallet on your belt until someone puts it in your hand and tells you what to say.

---

## Arriving, and manners

Come in over the northeast lip, in the ankle-deep sheet, barefoot — the stone there is polished to the gloss of an old tooth and it is slicker than it has any right to be. You will fall at least once. Fall carefully.

Stand at the lip and wait to be seen. You will be. Two hundred people, and the heron on the rafts is not the only thing here that stands still and watches.

Expect people to speak to you very loudly and all at once. This is not aggression; it is the habit of a place where you are usually far from everyone and water eats shouting, so when people are together they *are together*, hard and fast and overlapping. Expect also long hours of total silence with someone working beside you, which is not coldness. The two modes are not connected the way they are in your world, and if you try to read them the way you're used to, you will conclude that everyone here is either furious or bored. They are neither.

Make yourself useful. Cordage is endless and you can twist it badly and still help. Hauling traps requires nothing but hands and willingness. Being a guest who works is the entire shape of acceptance here, and it takes about nine days.

Do not touch the heron. Do not cut anything. Do not carve your name into a stem — it is not graffiti, it is a wound in the thing everyone lives in and which may, in a sense you will not be able to argue with, be somebody.

---

## The things you will want to do and should not

**The low town.** You will want to dive it. Twenty-five to thirty metres, tea-coloured light, silt over the doorways, an overhead environment, a blind eel-thing looping through the rooms, and a breath-hold budget halved by the altitude. Every factor stacks against you. And it is very likely a grave, or something adjacent to one — the eel is left alone, and things are left alone here for reasons.

Ask. Accept no for an answer. If the answer is yes, go with someone who knows it, go shallow, go once, and do not enter a doorway.

**The frogs.** You will not sleep for three nights. The honking begins at dusk and does not stop, and it is not a chorus, it is a scatter of hard reports at every distance, and your brain will insist for seventy-two hours that each one is somebody knocking at your door. Then on the fourth night you will stop hearing it entirely, and — this is the strange part — you will miss it forever afterward.

---

## Leaving

Do not jump worlds within twenty-four hours of a node-breathing day. If you have been taking gas at depth, even casually, you have been loading inert gas like a very slow scuba diver, and a gateway that drops you at a different altitude or pressure is a flight. Treat it as one. People have walked through a door at the rim and unfolded, on the other side, into a joint-locking, skin-mottling mess, and there is no chamber within three worlds.

And at the lip itself: go slowly. The sound falls away in about ten paces — all of it, the frogs, the applause of rain on leaf, the constant irregular wooden pulse that has been the floor of your hearing for weeks — and what replaces it is a silence so large and so cold that experienced travellers have sat down in the overflow and cried without deciding to.

Sit down on the polished stone before you do that. Let it go out slowly. Look back once.

Then walk, and expect to spend some months afterwards in a world that does not talk through wood, listening for something.
