# Briefing: Preparations for a Visit to the Equilibrium City

*For the ordinary traveler. Read the whole thing before you pack anything.*

---

## 0. First, decide honestly why you are going

This matters more than your kit, because the city has no resources, no inhabitants, no information you can extract, and no way of being surveyed. Everything you bring, you carry. Everything you learn, you learn about yourself.

The travelers who come back intact are, almost without exception, the ones who went with a small, specific, finishable intention: *walk one street to its end and return*. *Sleep one night in a chamber*. *Sit in the portico room for an hour.*

The travelers who don't come back went to **understand it**. The city is very patient with that ambition and will let you pursue it forever.

Write your intention on a card. Put the card in your chest pocket. You will need to read it later, in circumstances you cannot currently imagine.

---

## 1. The body: air, pressure, and the fact that there is no weather

Everything begins with the altitude, and the altitude is not negotiable.

Send an instrument package through first and measure the pressure. Do not take anyone's word, including this document's. The reported figures cluster around a fifth of a standard atmosphere, but the thermal column below is doing something structurally odd and the number varies with where on the range your gate opens.

- **If pressure is above roughly 0.25 atm:** you can acclimatize. Budget six to eight weeks of staged altitude work beforehand — genuinely, in a mountain range, not in a tent — and go on supplemental oxygen regardless. Your ceiling for useful thought is much lower than your ceiling for staying alive, and the city punishes bad thinking far more than it punishes breathlessness.
- **If pressure is below that:** closed-circuit rebreather and a soft pressure garment. Accept that you will be visiting the city the way a diver visits a reef: through a window, briefly, with your hearing muffled — which, as you will see in §5, may be a mercy.

The air is *ferociously clean*. No moisture, no organics, no salt. Your sinuses will crack and bleed within hours. Your corneas will dry against your eyelids while you sleep. Take a humidified mask for the sleeping hours if your system allows it; if it doesn't, take lubricant gel and use it more often than seems reasonable.

**The thermal problem is subtle and it is the thing that actually kills people.**

The stone is cellar-cool everywhere, always. Sun-facing walls, deep interiors, noon, midnight — the same cool. This sounds pleasant. It is not. It means the entire mountain is an infinite heat sink held at about 12°C that cannot be warmed, and above you is a near-black sky at the temperature of space, into which your body radiates continuously and unopposed.

You will not feel cold. You will feel *fine*, in shirtsleeves, and then four hours later your fine motor control will be gone and you will not have noticed the transition.

- Never sit directly on the stone. Never lean on it. Carry a closed-cell sit-pad on a lanyard at your hip and use it every single time, including for the thirty-second rest that turns into twenty minutes.
- Sleep on a minimum of R-8 of insulation. Double up. The pad is more important than the bag.
- Wear a hood or brimmed shell with a low-emissivity outer surface. Covering the top of your head and your shoulders cuts the radiative bleed dramatically. This one item makes the difference between a comfortable visit and a slow, cheerful decline.
- Eat on a schedule, not on appetite. Hypoxia destroys appetite. Set a timer — knowing that timers lie here, §6 — and eat anyway.

## 2. Light and the eyes

The illumination is that of a corridor at four in the morning, and it never brightens. Your pupils will be wide open for the entire visit.

This is dangerous, because a small white sun in an atmosphere with no water vapour and no dust above you is delivering an unknown ultraviolet dose into a fully dilated eye. **Do not wear sunglasses** — you cannot afford to lose any visible light — **wear clear lenses with a total UV block**, wraparound, with side shields. Take three pairs. Feldspar dust will fog them beyond use faster than you believe.

Skin: the same logic. Cover it. Not for warmth, for photons.

Headlamps: bring them, but understand that in the inner chambers the pale crust glows at the very bottom of your visual range, and a single flash of white LED costs you forty minutes of dark adaptation and shrinks the room to the size of your beam. The chambers are better seen adapted. **Use deep red, at the lowest setting that works, or nothing.** Carry white light for emergencies only and treat turning it on as a decision.

Bring a candle. Not for light — for the pleasure of something warm-coloured. You will be astonished by how much you need this on the fourth day.

---

## 3. Dust

Twice a day the thermal column breathes and grey rivers run through the streets.

The dust is powdered feldspar: hard, angular, never water-rounded, and therefore *sharp at every scale*. It is a silicosis agent and a lapping compound.

- **Respirator, P100 or better, with a proper seal.** Spare cartridges. The dust laughs at fabric masks.
- **Run every mechanism dry.** Grease and oil are dust magnets; a lubricated zipper is a zipper that will be welded shut by tomorrow. Roll-top closures, cord-and-toggle, no zips where a zip can be avoided.
- **Everything optical gets a sacrificial cover.** Lens caps, cling film, anything.
- **Learn the tide before you camp.** The plumes pour over the cols on a roughly twelve-hour rhythm, flow ankle-deep along the streets, fill the plazas to a mark, and then drain through the square passages into the hollow interiors, where they lie in flat grey lakes for hours.

That last part is the hazard. **A chamber that is dry and beautiful when you enter it can become a dust lake while you sleep in it.** Read the walls: there is always a fill-line, a subtle change in the sheen of the stone. Camp above it. Every time. No exceptions for tiredness.

The dust rivers themselves are safe to wade and one of the finest experiences available anywhere — warm-cool, silent, parting around your boots and closing behind them, braiding exactly like water and nothing at all like water. Do it. Do it in a street, not in a couloir, and not where the street ends in a square passage.

---

## 4. Everything here has an edge

Three hundred million years without water means no rounding. Nothing here has ever been smoothed. Every corner in this city — every step nosing, every lintel, every flagstone joint — is as sharp as the day it cleaved.

- Full-leather or aramid gloves, two pairs.
- Rope protectors, and more of them than you think. A dynamic rope run over an unweathered feldspar edge under body weight is cut in under a minute. This has killed people who were otherwise doing everything right.
- Hard-soled boots. Soft approach shoes will be opened at the sidewall by day three.
- A first-aid kit heavy on closure strips, tissue adhesive, and irrigation. You will cut your hands. Everyone cuts their hands. In an atmosphere that clean, cuts don't get infected — they just don't close, because there's no moisture in the air to support the process. Seal them artificially and keep them sealed.

---

## 5. Sound, and the loss of it

Nothing prepares you for this except practice, so **practice.**

Before you go, spend at least three separate nights in a proper anechoic chamber or a deep dry cave, alone, awake, with no device. You need to have already met the sound of your own blood. In the city it becomes loud, then intolerable, then — around day three or four — simply ordinary. That arc is survivable if you know it's an arc. It is not survivable as a surprise, because in the middle of it you will become convinced that something is wrong with your head, and you will be four hundred metres up and forty kilometres from your gate.

Then there are the grazers.

You will meet one perhaps twice a week: flat, slate-coloured, metre-wide, moving at the speed of a minute hand. Within about twenty metres of a feeding grazer, **sound does not exist.** Not muffled — absent. You will strike two stones together, feel the shock in your wrist, and hear nothing, not even the shape of a silence where the click should be.

Practical consequences, and they are serious:

- **Speech fails without warning.** You will be mid-sentence and your partner will simply not be receiving. Establish a full hand-signal vocabulary before departure and drill it until it's boring. Include signals for *stop*, *come*, *go back*, *I am not okay*, *I am okay*, *grazer*, *wait for me*, and *read your card*.
- **Rope tug-codes.** Three tugs, two tugs, one. Agree them. Write them on tape wrapped around your harness.
- **Never rely on an audible alarm.** Vibrating alarms only; strapped to the skin, not to the pack.
- **Grazers come to noise.** This is a tool and a risk. If you need to sleep and cannot bear your own heartbeat, a wind-up music box left twenty metres from your bivy will usually attract one by morning and buy you a bubble of perfect, absolute silence. If you want to *avoid* them — and some people find the null unbearable, a kind of sensory drowning — run silent and they will drift elsewhere over the following hours.
- **Do not touch them.** Not for their sake. Nobody knows what happens, and the two accounts that exist do not agree with each other.

Carry a small mechanical metronome. It is your grazer detector, your rule-of-physics canary, and on bad nights it is the only thing in the universe keeping time.

## 6. Time, rations, and the central problem of the visit

**The sun lies. Your watch is telling the truth about a thing that has stopped mattering.**

Up here, duration has decoupled from distance. A street can take one step or an afternoon. The sun crosses at a variable rate and sometimes parks at the meridian for a while, dimming slightly, as though thinking. You cannot plan a route by time and you cannot ration by the clock.

So ration by your **body and your consumables**, which remain honest:

1. **Eat by the packet, not by the hour.** Pre-portion every meal identically and number them. Your position in the ration stack *is* your position in the trip. "I am at packet nineteen" is the only true statement about time you will make while you're there.
2. **Water is the hard wall.** There is none. No ice, no seep, no frost, nothing to melt. Everything you drink, you carried up. Calculate your consumption honestly at altitude — it will be higher than at home, because of the dry air — and then apply the **one-third rule**: turn back when one third is gone. Not half. One third, because the return journey's duration is not knowable in advance and you must be able to absorb a street that decides to be an afternoon.
3. **Keep a growth ledger.** Mark your thumbnail with a scratch of graphite on arrival. Measure the growth daily against a card. It is the only clock in the city that cannot be argued with.
4. **The terminator ticking** — that hour-long sparse pattering as the cooling line crosses a face — is a usable tally. Not a uniform unit, but a countable event. Notch a stick.

The single most common fatal error in the Equilibrium City is not falling and it is not cold. It is a competent, careful person walking out along a street that turns out to have been three days long in the outward direction, on two days of water.

## 7. Navigating a place that refuses to be referred to

Three properties defeat ordinary mapping:

- **The peaks cannot be counted.** Twenty-three left to right, nineteen right to left, and both counts felt scrupulous.
- **Places recur.** The plaza with the chipped fourth step and the lightning-fork lintel is on the second peak and also on a peak forty kilometres away. It is not a copy; the chip is the *same chip*. A mark you leave in one is in the other when you arrive, aged by exactly the walking time.
- **Names slide off.** Whatever you call a summit, within two days you will have forgotten which one you meant, and the name will have quietly relocated.

So do not map. **Take a census instead.**

Carry two hundred small brass discs, each stamped with a unique glyph — not a number, glyphs resist the slide better, and not a word, never a word. Leave one in every distinct plaza, chamber, and stair-head you occupy. Then walk, and record only this: *which glyphs I can see from which glyphs.*

What comes home with you is not a map. It's a topology — a list of adjacencies — and it will be much, much smaller than the distances you walked. That is the correct result. **The city has fewer places than it has locations,** and the census is the only honest instrument for the difference.

Supplement with **rubbings**. Graphite on thin paper, taken from joints and lintels. A rubbing is a physical fact and resists the slide the way a name can't. Rubbings are also how you'll prove to yourself, later, at home, in a well-lit room, that the chip really was the same chip.

And: **assign one team member the duty of stopping the counting.** Someone will start trying to count the peaks. It produces a specific cognitive nausea and it is compulsive. The rule is: the moment you hear someone say a number out loud, you take their shoulder and turn them away from the skyline. This is not a joke and it is not optional.

## 8. Instrumenting the thinning

Above a contour — findable, there's a line where the crust changes colour — the world relaxes about itself. Below it, more or less ordinary physics. In between, a gradient that is not marked.

**Your shadow is the gauge.** Up high, it precedes you around corners by a second or so; stop suddenly and it runs on a little before returning. That lead is a direct readout of how much the world is bothering to synchronise.

Calibrate it. At camp, at a corner, have your partner count while you step. Record the lead in half-seconds. Then check it **every time you change altitude** and especially every time you approach an edge.

**Long lead: falling is soft. Short lead: falling is falling.** This is the only safety instrument that works here, and it costs nothing but discipline.

---

## 9. On stepping off things

Yes, it's true. High up, you can step off a four-hundred-metre balustrade and descend at the speed of settling dust, turning gently, with ample time to reconsider — and reconsideration works. Reach for a ledge nine metres away and it will be within reach, because up there the difference between *nine metres* and *within reach* is one of the distinctions that stopped being maintained.

It is, by all accounts, the most beautiful thing a human being can do.

Rules, if you're going to do it:

1. **Check the shadow lead immediately before. Every time.**
2. **Never near the crust-colour line.** The gradient is not sharp and the city gives no warning when a rule resumes.
3. **Never on the last day**, when you're tired and your judgement about what counts as "high up" has become flexible.
4. **Have a plan for where you are going to land**, because *down* is the direction in which the rules get stricter. Nobody has ever reached the powder sea; it isn't clear it's a place you can arrive at; and it is not clear what happens to someone who descends far enough to fall properly again halfway through a fall.
5. **Go roped, with a partner braced,** the first time. Yes, it's absurd. Do it anyway.

---

## 10. What not to touch

The grey-green crust on the shaded faces grows about a millimetre a century. Your bootprint in it is a scar ten thousand years deep. **Walk on bare stone and dust. Never on the crust.** Route around. Always.

Don't collect. Attempts to remove stone fail in ways that are unpleasant to experience: packs that lighten, samples found seated back in their sockets, a general sense of having been gently and impersonally corrected. The city is in exact equilibrium and it is not clear that it can be robbed. Bring rubbings, photographs, and the discs you left behind if you pass them again. That's the harvest.

Don't build. Don't cairn. Don't arrange. The urge to leave a small stack of stones here is overwhelming and it is the first symptom of §11.

---

## 11. The mind

This is the real preparation and almost nobody does it.

You are entering a city of streets, stairs, doorways, colonnades, plazas, and a portico opening on a room with a raised dais at the far end — and there has never been a hand here. Not a builder, not a tool, not an occupant. Your brain will not accept this. It is not built to. It will spend the entire visit generating a hypothesis, discarding it, and generating it again, and the hypothesis is always the same: *someone is here, or was, or is coming.*

Symptoms, in the usual order:

1. The conviction that you have just been in the adjacent room.
2. Attributing courtesy to the landscape. (It does decline to be counted *politely*. That's real, and that's the problem.)
3. Beginning to keep the city's habits: walking only at right angles, brushing your camp dust back into the pattern, timing your meals to the tide.
4. Beginning to think of the dais as *for* something.
5. Beginning to think of it as *for you.*

Stage five is where travelers stop leaving discs, choose a plaza, and become a permanent feature of an arrangement that has been in equilibrium for three hundred million years and now includes a person.

Countermeasures, all of them dull and all of them effective:

- **Go in twos or threes, never alone,** and never as a pair who defer to each other.
- **Read the card in your chest pocket every day**, aloud where possible.
- **Keep a written log of things that are untrue about the city.** "Nobody built this. Nobody lived here. It is not addressed to me." Re-read it.
- **Bring one wet, organic, unmistakably alive thing.** An apple. A lemon. A jar of moss. Look at it every night. In a world of silent, cool, unbuilt, right-angled permanence, this small rotting object is your anchor to the category of *things that happen*, and its smell, at day six, will be the most emotionally overwhelming experience of the trip.
- **Set a hard turnaround and delegate it.** Somebody who is not you holds the authority to end the visit. Agree in advance that their call is final, and agree it in writing, at home, at sea level, with a witness.

Sit in the portico room if you want to. Sit on the floor, not on the dais. An hour. Then leave.

---

## 12. Getting out

Know your gate the way you know your own front door.

- **Cache at it.** Water, food, insulation, a spare respirator, a light. Enough to survive a wait of several times your planned trip length, because the return leg's duration is not a quantity you control.
- **Mark it with three discs in a pattern you will recognise from any angle**, and with a rubbing of the specific joint pattern behind it, carried on your person.
- **Understand the recurrence risk.** The gate stands in a place, and places recur. You may arrive, footsore and certain, at the correct place — same chip, same stain, same lightning-fork lintel, your own disc lying where you left it, aged by your walking time — and find no gate. This is not a hallucination and you are not lost. You are at the *place*; you are not at the *location*. The known remedy is boring and it works: stay put, eat a numbered packet, sleep, and walk out and back in again by a different street. Two or three iterations usually resolves it. Your cache is what makes that survivable, which is why the cache is not optional.
- **Leave earlier than you want to.** Everyone reports that the desire to leave arrives days after the correct moment to leave.

And note the phrasing in every account ever written about this range: the city cannot be entirely left. Plan for that as a medical fact, not a metaphor.

---

## 13. Afterwards

Budget two weeks at home doing nothing demanding.

Expect:

- **Sound intolerance.** Ordinary rooms will be unbearably loud for four to ten days. Traffic will feel like violence. Keep the lights low and the house quiet and let people know in advance so they don't take it personally.
- **Shadow-checking.** You will glance down at corners for months. Harmless.
- **A preference for right angles** that your household will find funny and then won't.
- **The plaza.** You will dream of the plaza with the broken fourth step and the ring-shaped stain, recurrently, for years, and in the dream your disc is still there, aged by exactly the time since you left. This is the ordinary outcome and it is not a symptom of anything. It's simply that you have been to a place that has fewer places than locations, and part of what that means is that you are still, in some accounting, standing in one of them.

Do the return interview. Look at your rubbings in daylight with someone who didn't go. Keep the card from your chest pocket.

---

## Packing list, condensed

**Life support:** oxygen or rebreather per measured pressure, spare cartridges, humidified sleep mask, lubricant eye gel, P100 respirator ×2 with spares.

**Thermal:** low-emissivity hooded shell, R-8+ sleeping pad (doubled), lanyard sit-pad, insulated bivy, chemical warmers.

**Optical:** clear UV-block wraparounds ×3, red headlamp ×2, white light for emergencies, lens covers, one candle.

**Mechanical:** hard-soled boots, aramid gloves ×2, rope with abundant edge protection, harness, tape for tug-code labels.

**Timekeeping:** numbered ration packets, growth-ledger card and graphite, notch stick, mechanical metronome, vibrating alarm.

**Navigation:** 200 stamped brass discs, rubbing paper and graphite blocks, blank notebook (for adjacencies, never names), pencil.

**Psychological:** the card in your chest pocket, the untruths log, one wet living thing, a wind-up music box, photographs of people you like.

**Water:** more than you calculated. Then the one-third rule on top of that.

---

Go for a week. Walk one street. Wade one dust river. Let the terminator tick across you once and hear the mountain speak and stop. Sit on the floor of the portico room for an hour, and then get up, and then go home.

The city will be exactly the same shape when you leave as when you arrived. That's the whole of it. That's what there is to see.
