# Preparing for The Throat, Pocket Four

## First, understand what kind of place this is

Most hazardous locations threaten you. This one doesn't. It has no interest in you at all, and that is precisely the danger: nothing here will escalate, warn, or relent. The mountain will take your water at a fixed rate whether you are fine or dying. The note will not change pitch when you panic. There is no feedback loop between your condition and the environment, which means every single safety margin has to be carried in your head and on your wrist, because the place will not supply one.

Plan accordingly. This is not a survival trip. It is a **breath-hold** — a twenty-minute excursion, repeated a very small number of times, wrapped in three weeks of logistics.

---

## The year before: your body

**Acclimatize properly, on the wet side.** The Throat sits high on a divide; you want three to four weeks of graduated altitude work on the windward flank before you cross. Do not treat the crossing as the acclimatization. You will arrive on the dry side already dehydrated by the climb, and the dry side is where dehydration becomes arithmetic rather than discomfort.

**Get your blood checked.** Ultra-low humidity plus altitude plus exertion is a thrombosis recipe. If you have any clotting history, any polycythemia, any history of retinal problems, this trip is a bad idea and there is no version of the gear list that fixes it.

**Deal with your eyes now, not there.** If you wear contact lenses, leave them in another world. A soft lens in the pocket will bond to your cornea in under ten minutes and come off with epithelium attached. Get your prescription put into a sealed goggle insert, made and tested months in advance. If you've had refractive surgery and have residual dry eye, expect the twenty-minute limit to be a five-minute limit for you.

**Train your nose.** Habitual mouth-breathers lose water roughly twice as fast. Spend the pre-trip months making nasal breathing automatic under load. This is not a wellness suggestion; it is worth several minutes of pocket time.

**Rehearse the pocket blind and silent.** Build a mock-up: a slot you must go through shoulder-first, and beyond it a volume packed solid with dry brush, gorse, bundled straw — anything that grips and rasps and closes behind you. Practice inserting yourself, turning your head, freeing a snagged sleeve, and extracting yourself, in the dark, without speech, wearing your actual gloves and goggles, with a partner holding your ankle line. Practice it until the reverse move is reflexive. In the real pocket you will not be able to say "I'm stuck," and the growth holds you at the pressure of a firm handshake all over your body at once, which is very pleasant for about ninety seconds and then is not.

**Learn the touch code until it's below thought.** One squeeze on the arm: yes / I'm fine. Two: out, now. Three, if your party wants it: hold still. Nothing else. Do not invent a rich vocabulary; under desiccation stress people forget elaborate codes and then invent new ones, and their partner reads them wrong.

## The crossing

You will go up through ice and come down into nothing. Budget a full day for the transition and treat it as a gear change, not a walk.

Everything you carry over the ridge will be damp. On the far side it will dry — hard, fast, and permanently. Expect:

- **Leather to become wood.** Boots, straps, glove palms, sheaths. Waxed and oiled leather does better; new stiff leather is a mistake. (You will meet a glove in the pocket that made this transition with a hand still in it.)
- **Adhesives to fail.** Tape stops sticking. Glued boot soles delaminate. Anything laminated — bonded shells, glued-in insoles, taped seams, patched gear — is on a countdown. Prefer stitched and riveted over bonded, everywhere.
- **Wood to shrink.** Axe heads loosen, knife handles loosen, tool ferrules loosen. Check every mechanical fastener the morning after you cross, and again the next morning. Better: leave wood behind and carry all-metal tools.
- **Rope and cord to stiffen.** Not necessarily to weaken, but to handle badly and take a set. Uncoil and hank everything the day you cross so it doesn't dry into a shape you can't undo.
- **Plastics to craze.** Old plastic, previously UV-baked plastic, and anything with a hinge or a snap-fit lid. Use metal water vessels with metal caps and gaskets you have spares for.

The sky will be cloudless and cruel. There is no water vapour to soften the UV and no water vapour to hold the heat. You will sunburn and shiver at once. Cover everything; use a physical-block sunscreen and accept that it will be a mineral paste by the second day. Lips and the underside of the nose get the reflected dose and they are also the tissue you can least afford to lose.

## Water arithmetic — the actual expedition

There is no snow. There is no melt. There is no seep, no spring, no rime on the rock, no frost to scrape. **Every drop on the dry side is a drop you carried over the ridge.** This single fact determines the shape of the trip.

Working numbers for planning:

- **6–8 litres per person per day** merely to exist and work at that altitude in that air.
- **+1 to 1.5 litres per pocket entry**, consumed afterwards, immediately, not sipped.
- **Two entries per person per day is the ceiling.** Two hours and a litre between them. The third entry of a day is where the accidents live: judgment goes before strength does, and the place gives you no cue that it has gone.

So: a four-person team wanting eight total entries is a two-to-three-day window on the dry side and something between 60 and 100 kilos of water carried over a ridge. Most parties solve this with a cached dump on the col and a single-carry sprint. Some solve it by going too light, and those are the parties that come back with corneal abrasions and stories about how they only meant to stay a little longer.

**Pre-load, but pre-load with salt.** Drinking two litres of plain water an hour before an entry, in a place that will strip a litre out of you through your lungs and skin, is a fast route to hyponatremia on top of everything else. Oral rehydration salts, or a proper electrolyte mix, from the day you cross. Taste it and approve of it at home, because you will have to drink a great deal of it while feeling nauseated.

**Nothing to drink goes into the pocket.** See below. This is the hardest rule to keep and the most important.

## Gear: the short list that matters

**Respiration.** A heat-and-moisture-exchange mask over a P3 particulate filter, or a combined unit. Two functions: it stops you inhaling freshly ground feldspar and olivine (fine, angular, silicotic, and the reason people who worked the Throat coughed for the rest of their lives), and it returns some fraction of your exhaled water to you. The HME is worth minutes. Carry three filters per person; they load with grey dust faster than you'd believe.

Note the trade: the mask also means less of your breath reaches the growth, and the flush is what you came for. Practitioners handle this by working masked and then, once, deliberately, taking the mask off, putting their face into the mass, and spending the water on purpose. Do it late in the entry, not early.

**Eyes.** Sealed goggles with a foam skirt and *no vents that admit dust*. They will not fog — nothing fogs here — so seal them completely. Preservative-free gel drops in an insulated pocket, applied before entry, never during. **Never touch your face inside.** Your gloves are coated in abrasive mineral powder from the moment you enter the flue; one rub of a watering eye and you have a corneal abrasion in a room where you cannot speak, four hundred metres up a chimney, hours from anything.

**Nose.** Saline gel in both nostrils before every entry. It buys you time before the crusting starts, and crusting is what forces you into mouth-breathing, which is what doubles your loss rate. Expect a little grey-crusted blood anyway. It is normal and it stops.

**Skin.** Occlusive balm on lips, nostril rims, ear canals, eyelids, and the web of the thumb — everywhere skin is thin. A heavy petrolatum-type ointment, not a lotion.
