# Briefing: A Visit to the Pan

## 1. Understand what actually threatens you

Newcomers prepare for heat. Heat is the third-worst thing here.

**First is the alkali.** pH 10.5 does not sting. That is the entire problem. An acid tells you immediately and you flinch away; carbonate liquor feels pleasant — soapy, slippery, faintly sweet. That slipperiness is your own skin oil being saponified. It does not stop when you stop noticing it. On skin it takes hours to do its damage and days to show. In an eye it does not stop at all: alkali penetrates the cornea and keeps going into the anterior chamber, and a splash you rinsed "pretty well" at noon can be a scarred eye by the following week. Assume every wet surface in the basin is mildly caustic, because it is. Assume the dust is the same chemistry with the water removed, waiting for the moisture on your lips, your nostrils, the inner corner of your eye.

**Second is the crust.** Thin trona over 60–82°C brine. A leg through it is a full-thickness scald *and* a chemical burn *and* boot-full-of-liquor, several kilometres from a hospital with three wards shut. This is the injury that kills visitors.

**Third is the heat**, which at least announces itself.

Everything below follows from those three.

---

## 2. Timing

**Season.** Do not come during the flood, however much the mirror tempts you. Fifteen centimetres of water over a thousand square kilometres hides every thin place and every channel lip, and the reflection destroys your ability to judge ground. Come six to ten weeks after the sheet has gone: the crust is freshly sealed, the cyanobacteria are still in bloom, the flamingos are still in from four countries, and the surface can be read.

**Hour of arrival.** Insert at 04:30 local, at the rim, on the escarpment side. Two reasons. You arrive in the coolest hour of the day, so your first two hours of orientation are not spent adapting to a wall of heat. And you arrive at the tidemark — that hard line where the grey-green fuzz stops — so that your last sight of a living plant is deliberate and recent. Walk down across it on foot. It will be the most important five minutes of the trip and you only get it once.

Do not arrive in the middle. The middle is not somewhere to *arrive*.

---

## 3. Water: bring all of it

The drinking water comes by rail in a tank car once a week for two hundred and forty people. Every litre you drink here is a litre taken out of a fixed allocation from someone who lives on it. An ordinary adult working outdoors at 41°C will want eight to ten litres a day.

**Bring your own, all of it, plus forty percent.** For a week that is roughly seventy to eighty litres. This is heavy and awkward and there is no way around it. If your transit method has a mass budget, spend the budget on water and leave the camera.

Do not filter, boil, treat, or otherwise attempt to render the local water drinkable. Filtration does nothing to dissolved sodium carbonate. Boiling concentrates it. You can distil it — you are standing in a natural still and the physics is willing — but alkaline aerosol carryover into your condensate is very easy to get wrong, scale will foul your surfaces in days, and a distillate that tastes fine can still be at pH 9. If you must have a backup, bring a small packaged reverse-osmosis unit and an acid-dosing pretreatment, and test every batch with a pH strip before you swallow any of it. Treat this as an emergency system only.

Carry a separate, clearly marked **rinse water** supply: at least three litres per person per day that you are willing to pour over skin without hesitation. Hesitating over rinse water is how people end up with chemical burns. Mark those bottles with tape you can identify by feel in the dark.

And carry **sterile saline in litre bags** — eye irrigation only, minimum four litres, with a nasal cannula or Morgan lens if you can get one. If Diphoterine or an equivalent amphoteric rinse is available in your home world, bring it; it is the one genuinely superior product for alkali splash.

Electrolytes: oral rehydration salts, a sachet for every second litre. The joke writes itself — you will be dosing yourself with sodium out of a foil packet while standing on sixty metres of it.

## 4. Materials: what the basin eats

This is the section people skip and regret.

Strong carbonate solutions destroy **protein**. That means:

- **No leather.** Not boots, not belts, not watch straps, not camera cases. The text tells you plainly: by evening the leather has begun to crack. Leather here has a service life of days.
- **No wool.** Wool socks will go slimy and then go to pieces. This matters, because wool socks are what many people wear for foot health.
- **No silk, no down** (down is protein and the shell fabric won't keep the liquor out).

Carbonate is comparatively kind to **cellulose and most synthetics**:

- **Cotton** is fine — alkali is what's used to mercerise it. Loose, light-coloured, long-sleeved cotton drill is the correct outer layer.
- **Polypropylene or polyester** socks, six pairs minimum per person per week.
- **Nitrile or PVC** for gloves and boots. Not latex (degrades, and the heat).

**Footwear:** knee-height PVC or nitrile boots with a thick nitrile outsole, worn over a proper insulating insole. Avoid EVA-foam midsoles entirely: at 65°C ground temperature they compress, deform and conduct. You want something you could stand on a hot plate in. Seal the boot top with a gaiter — the failure mode is liquor slopping in over the rim while you're concentrating on your feet.

Accept that your boots are consumables. Bring a second pair. Bring foot powder and change socks at every break; you will be sweating inside rubber in tropical heat and macerated feet crack, and cracked skin is an open door to an alkali that is already sitting on the surface of it.

**Hat:** wide brim *plus* a neck and shoulder drape. And then remember that the ground reflects more than the sky delivers. You will sunburn on the underside of your jaw, inside your nostrils, under your brow ridge, on the roof of your mouth if you walk with it open. Sunscreen goes on upward-facing surfaces last and downward-facing surfaces first.

## 5. Eyes, lungs, hands

**Eyes.** The locals wear cheap wraparound goggles. Do better: sealed, indirectly-vented chemical splash goggles with an anti-fog coating, tinted or with a separate tinted overlay. Wraparounds stop dust from the front and let it in at the cheekbones. Bring three pairs — you will scratch them, because you will wipe alkaline dust off a lens with a cloth and grind it in. Never dry-wipe. Blower bulb first, always.

Carry a **personal eyewash bottle on your belt at all times**, within one second of your hand, not in the pack. The rule is fifteen minutes of continuous irrigation, starting immediately, and continuing while someone else fetches help. Fifteen minutes is longer than anyone thinks it is. Practise it once at home with plain water so your body knows the duration.

**Lungs and sinuses.** A fitted P3/N100 half-mask for any time you're on the aprons in the afternoon drift, near the bagging shed, near the calciner, or downwind of anything dry. The dust does not settle; there is no "clean air later." Expect nosebleeds anyway. Bring saline nasal spray and use it four times a day, and again before sleep.

Carry a small **H₂S monitor**. The mats are anaerobic underneath, and a seep that has been quietly reducing sulfate under a leathery crust of dead cyanobacteria can let go a lungful when you step on it. It is a small risk and a cheap instrument.

**Hands.** Look at the locals' knuckles: cracked white and not healing. That is your future in about four days. Prevention is a silicone or dimethicone **barrier cream** applied before work, reapplied after every wash, and a heavy lanolin or petrolatum ointment at night with cotton gloves over it. Keep your nails very short. Once a knuckle splits, you have a chemical burn with a delivery route, and it will not close while you are here.

## 6. You are the hospital

Three wards are shut and the port is four hundred kilometres away down a metre-gauge line. Whatever happens, the first six hours are yours.

Minimum:
- Sterile saline, litres, as above.
- **Burn kit sized for a leg**: clean cling film (the best field dressing for a scald there is — non-adherent, transparent, seals), non-adherent pads, sterile water, elastic bandage. Rinse a crust-breakthrough burn with *your own* water for twenty minutes, not the pan's.
- Strong analgesia and something for nausea, because a large burn will produce both.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics and chlorhexidine. Alkaline wounds go bad.
- Rabies pre-exposure vaccination before you travel — there are jackals and hyenas patrolling the tidemark. Tetanus current. Nothing here is likely to bite you, but nothing here will treat you either.
- Rehydration salts, and a plan for someone else to notice you've stopped sweating.

## 7. Moving: scale-blindness and crust discipline

There are no plants. You have spent your life judging distance against vegetation without knowing it. Here, an object at fifty metres has lost its colour and at two kilometres is a rumour, and you will misjudge every range you attempt by a factor you cannot predict. A walk you estimate at twenty minutes is an hour and forty in the heat.

Therefore: **carry a rangefinder, and pace-count anyway.** Log a bearing and a time before any movement off the causeway. Tell a person in the control room where you are going and when you'll be back — not as a formality; as the actual mechanism by which you survive.

**Crust rule:** stay on the causeways and on ground a local has walked in front of you this season. If you must leave hard ground, probe every step ahead with a staff, move with a second person at ten metres' separation and a line between you, and turn back at the first wet crunch. The sound changes before the surface does — it rings where it is thick and goes dull before it goes through. Learn the two sounds on safe ground near the plant on your first morning, deliberately, while someone is watching.

Do not enter the dredged channels. Warm liquor, deep, opaque, pH 10.5, and you have no idea what the cut face looks like below the waterline.

## 8. Optics, electronics, records

You want binoculars. Without them the pink line on the horizon stays a line and you never understand it. Bring **8×42, sealed and nitrogen-purged**, and a blower bulb, and more lens tissue than seems sane.

At 05:00 every cold steel surface beads with grey condensate. Anything you bring outside from a cool bag will fog and stay fogged. Keep optics and cameras in sealed bags with silica gel overnight and let them equilibrate *inside the bag* before opening. Recharge the gel daily — you have no shortage of heat.

Soda creep gets into zippers, threaded caps, battery compartments and connectors and corrodes them from inside. Anything electrical wants a sealed case; anything with a battery door wants tape over it. Bring more batteries than the maths says, because contacts fail.

**Write in pencil.** Ink pens fail in heat and the paper is damp at dawn and slick with alkaline dust by noon. Graphite writes on anything, including a wet page, and will not run when the page gets rinsed.

## 9. Biology: what not to touch, and the one real ethical hazard

The flies will part around you and close behind you and never land on you. They are harmless and they are the most extraordinary thing you will walk through. Do it slowly. Do not take a running start.

Do not handle bird carcasses — botulism moves through colonies like this. Do not walk into a flamingo concentration; a flushed flock at that density injures itself and you will have subtracted something from the basin for a photograph. Keep three hundred metres and use the glass.

And then the serious one. **Each hot-spring pool has its own fish.** A pool the size of a garage, isolated by kilometres of lethal crust, containing a population that exists nowhere else in this universe. You are a multiverse traveller: your boots and your gear carry organisms, spores, eggs and chemistry from places that have no business being here, and a single transfer between two pools — or from your home world into one — could end a species inside a season, with nobody ever knowing it happened.

So: **do not enter the pools.** Observe from the lip. If your work absolutely requires wading, use dedicated boots per pool, bagged and labelled, and bleach-and-rinse everything between sites, and accept that you are still taking a risk you have no right to take. The default is: look, don't step. Ninety-five percent of what those pools have to show you is visible from the bank with binoculars anyway.

## 10. Arriving among two hundred and forty people

There are houses for eleven hundred. You will be conspicuous within an hour of arrival and known by name within a day. Plan on this rather than resenting it.

**Go to the control room first.** Not the club, not the church, not the guest quarters — the shift supervisor. Say who you are, where you intend to walk, and when. Ask what's running today. Ask which causeway is safe. They will tell you, and thereafter you are a person who checked in rather than a person who wandered, and this determines everything about how the visit goes.

**Do not photograph the plant** until you have explicit permission from someone with the authority to give it. Company towns are jumpy about cameras, and the calciners are somebody's livelihood, not scenery.

**Bring cargo, not gifts.** The correct offering is exactly what the basin cannot make:

- **Fresh green vegetables and fruit**, as much as you can carry, in a cool box. Nothing green grows within thirty kilometres. This is worth more than money here and everyone will understand immediately what it cost you to bring it.
- Good barrier cream and heavy hand ointment, by the case.
- Decent sealed goggles, a dozen pairs. The cheap wraparounds they wear are what's available, not what anyone would choose.
- Lens tissue, batteries, good socks, sharp pencils.
- Something to read that was printed this decade.

**Match their pace.** Everyone here is slowed, the way people are in a boiler room, and this is not lethargy — it is thermally correct. A visitor moving at normal speed at eleven in the morning is a visitor who will be a medical problem by one. Slow down deliberately on day one, before your body makes you.

## 11. The day, and sleep

Structure your day the way the basin does:

- **04:30–09:00** — the working window. Cold condensate, birds coming in low over the causeways, haze whitening at seven, ground beginning to smoke at nine. Do everything important now.
- **09:00–11:00** — marginal. Short tasks, shaded.
- **11:00–16:00** — the dead hours. Even the storks stop. The crust is untouchable; the light is at its flattest and dimmest. **Do not work through this.** Lie down with wet cloth and a fan if there's power. This is not a siesta, it is a safety procedure.
- **16:00–19:30** — the second window. Shift change at six, lamps on in air still at thirty-five.
- **After dark** — go out, not far, and stand on the causeway and listen to the flamingos transiting overhead in relays for an hour. This is the thing you came for and most visitors sleep through it because they are exhausted. Nap in the afternoon specifically so that you can be awake for this.

Sleeping at thirty-five degrees with no cooling and no breeze is genuinely hard. Bring a light cotton sheet you can dampen (from *your* water — budget a litre a night for it), and a battery fan, and expect to sleep in fragments. Plan a trip one day longer than the work requires, to absorb the sleep debt.

Food: bring your own, and bring things that don't need water to prepare. Don't plan to eat much in the middle of the day; you won't want to.

## 12. The psychological brief

This is where most visitors are underprepared, because nobody warns them.

**The mismatch.** Forty-three degrees of heat arriving under the light of a failed-heating overcast afternoon. Your body will keep expecting the light to explain the temperature and it never will. This produces a low, persistent wrongness that is not dangerous but is genuinely tiring. Name it on the first day and it loses most of its power.

**Green hunger.** Two or three days in, the absence of plants stops being an observation and becomes an appetite. People report it as a physical craving. Do not try to solve it by bringing a living plant — that is a biosecurity offence against a basin that has no defences. Bring **green cloth**: a scarf, a towel, something you can look at. It sounds absurd. It works, and everyone who has done it says so.

**Exposure.** Four hundred metres of blinding pale apron with nothing on it and you visible for all of it, alone, at walking pace. Some people find this peaceful and some find it close to unbearable, and you will not know which you are until you are halfway across. Do the first crossing with someone else.

**The quiet that isn't.** A million birds producing a sound like a crowd through a wall in another building, so continuous that it functions as silence — and under it the calciner's note arriving through the soles of your feet rather than your ears. After a day you stop hearing both. When you leave, the absence of them will be the thing that wakes you at night for a week.

Bring a notebook and write for twenty minutes every evening. This place does something to the sense of scale and of time — "a rate no single human career can see" — and people come back from it unable to say what happened to them. Writing while inside it is the only reliable countermeasure.

## 13. Departure

Everything you carry out is impregnated with soda, and it will migrate into whatever you store it with and quietly eat that too.

Before you leave the rim: strip on the tidemark side, wash down with fresh water including your hair (the dust makes hair brittle and it will break off in handfuls a week later), bag all clothing separately, and leave the boots. Rinse and dry every metal fitting, every zip, every battery contact. Assume your leather is already gone; don't bother packing it.

Check your own eyes a week later, and again at a month. Alkali is patient.

And walk *up* across the tidemark on foot, the way you came down — through the hard line, into the grey-green fuzz, into the thorn scrub. Stop at the first acacia and stand under it for a while. That is the other half of the thing you came to see, and you cannot see it from the plant.

## 15. Your own rules, to match theirs

1. Everything alive here lives inside a narrow band. So do you. Yours is narrower than theirs and you did not evolve into it. Know where its edges are before you approach them.
2. Nothing you can taste is safe and nothing that feels harmless is.
3. The mineral regenerates. The pools do not, the birds do not, the species in the garage-sized pool does not. Take nothing from a seam.
4. The buildings do not regenerate either, nor do the two hundred and forty people keeping them running. Arrive with cargo.
5. Heat and dim light coexist. Trust the thermometer, never the sky.
6. The largest crowd here is quieter than the smallest machine. Be quieter than both.
7. If you have seen nothing move for a quarter of an hour but steam — good. That is the place showing you what it is. Sit down, drink, and let it.
