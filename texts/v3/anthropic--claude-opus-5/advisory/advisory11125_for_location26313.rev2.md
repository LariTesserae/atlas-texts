# Going Down to the Lower Volumes
### A preparation brief for the ordinary traveller

---

## First, correct your frame

You are not visiting a cave, a mine, a flooded quarry, or a sea. You are visiting a **machine that has begun to behave like a sea**, and nearly every mistake travellers make in the Volumes comes from picking the wrong metaphor at the bulkhead and then being unable to put it down.

If you go in thinking *cave*, you will be unprepared for the light, the weather, the distances, and the birds. If you go in thinking *sea*, you will be unprepared for the fact that the whole thing is on a schedule, that the water level is a decision somebody made in an office, and that the "seabed" is a structural element with a rated design life and an inspection interval.

The working frame — the one the crews use without ever saying it aloud — is this: **you are inside a component.** A very large, very new, very wet component, which is full of water for operational reasons, and which contains animals because somebody put them there on purpose, and which contains you because somebody signed a chit. Everything down there is subordinate to the purpose of the thing. The water rises and falls because of the purpose. The lamps dim and brighten because of the purpose. The gulls are an unintended consequence of the purpose. You, too, are an unintended consequence, and if the purpose requires it, the cell you are floating in will be drawn down to the invert on eleven hours' notice and you will need to have already gone.

Hold that frame and the rest of this brief will make sense.

---

## The identity problem (solve this before anything else)

There are no tourists in the Lower Volumes. Not because it is forbidden — nobody has bothered to forbid it — but because there is no infrastructure for a person who is not attached to a work programme. Every bunk, every meal, every hoist slot, every set of ear defenders belongs to a cost code. A human being without a cost code is a discrepancy, and in a culture built by engineers, discrepancies get escalated.

So: **arrive attached.** The three easiest attachments for a stranger, in order of plausibility:

1. **Dosing barge deckhand.** Enormous turnover, miserable work, four-month circuits, nobody memorises faces. You will handle acid and buffered brine and you will smell like a swimming pool for the rest of your life. The great advantage is mobility: a barge crosses the whole cell in a mowing pattern and you see everything.
2. **Survey assistant.** Requires you to be able to hold a rod, read a number aloud clearly, and not get bored. Pilots on the long crossings are desperate for someone to talk to. This is the best posting for actually understanding the place, because surveyors are the only people down there whose job is to *look at it*.
3. **Stock hand on the wall works.** Mussel lines, kelp trials, cress rafts. Cold, wet, repetitive, and it puts you at the coast — which, as the description says, is where everything lives.

What will *not* work: journalist, researcher, inspector, or anyone whose stated business is curiosity. Curiosity is read as audit. Audits are read as threat. You will find every question deflected with cheerful, total, impenetrable technical precision.

Learn the register. The floor is the **invert**. The ceiling is the **soffit**. The sharp edge of a casting is an **arris**. A rust stain is a **weep** or a **tear**. Water level is **stage**. You never *dive into* a cell, you *go in the water*. You never ask how deep it is, you ask what the stage is. Getting three of these right in the first hour buys you a month of being left alone.

## Your body: what the Volumes will do to it, and in what order

Plan on a four-day misery curve. Everyone has it. The crews find it funny and will not warn you.

**Hours 0–2: the descent.** Your ears will crackle continuously and your sinuses will feel packed. The invert sits some eleven hundred metres below datum; ambient pressure at the bottom is meaningfully higher than you are used to. It is not dangerous, but it is *constant*, and you will notice you are breathing slightly richer air and sleeping slightly heavier. Do not descend with a head cold. A blocked eustachian tube on a fast hoist is a ruptured eardrum, and a ruptured eardrum in an alkaline aerosol environment is an ear infection you will carry home to your own universe.

**Hours 2–8: the lips.** This is the signature injury. The aerosol off the water is alkaline, and it settles on everything, and you will lick your lips because they feel dry, and the licking is what does it. By the end of your first shift your lips will be split, glossy, and burning. There is no cure, only prophylaxis: a heavy occlusive balm — lanolin, petrolatum, something that sits there like a lid — applied *before* the bulkhead and every ninety minutes thereafter. Buy more than you think. Then double it. Then double it again, because it is the single most valuable trade good you can carry.

**Day 1–2: the hands.** Everything you touch is either a sharp new arris or a lime-crusted surface with the pH of oven cleaner. Your knuckles will crack. The cracks will not heal, because they are being washed with mild alkali eight hours a day. Barrier cream at the start of shift, wash with a mildly acidic cleanser at the end, and never, ever go in bare-handed to free a jammed line.

**Day 2–4: the ears and the head.** The bass is not loud in the way a factory is loud. It is *present*, at sixty to seventy beats a minute, in your chest and your teeth, and your brain will spend two days trying to find the animal making it. Some people get headaches. Some people get a low-grade dread they can't attribute. It passes. It passes for everyone. Then, about a week later, you stop hearing it entirely, and the first time somebody shuts down a lift house for maintenance and the cell goes quiet, you will find the silence genuinely frightening.

**Week 2 onward: the light.** A twenty-six hour lamp cycle is close enough to human circadian rhythm to feel almost right and far enough off to wreck you slowly. You will drift. Bring a watch you can set to a twenty-six hour day, use it ruthlessly, and take your sleep on the local cycle from the first night even if it means lying awake. The crews who do badly are the ones who keep a home-schedule watch "for reference."

## Medical kit — the part nobody packs correctly

- **Neutral or mildly acidic eyewash, in quantity.** This is non-negotiable. The standard reflex — flush with plenty of water — is *wrong here*, because the plenty of water available to you is the thing that burned you. An alkaline splash in the eye is a progressive injury; it keeps eating inward for hours. Carry sealed bottles of buffered wash on your person, not in a locker.
- **Mild acid rinse for skin** (dilute citric or acetic), plus plain neutral water to follow.
- **Occlusive lip balm, barrier cream, and a thick emollient for the hands.** See above.
- **Ear drops, alcohol-and-acid type**, used prophylactically after every immersion. Permanently damp ears in a warm-ish mess deck breed infections that will end your visit.
- **Antifungal powder.** Everything is wet. Everything stays wet. The Volumes are a fungal paradise between your toes.
- **Vitamin D**, at supplement doses. There is no sun. There has never been sun. The lamp strings are the wrong spectrum and the crews all know someone whose teeth went odd.
- **Decongestant and the discipline not to need it.**
- **A personal multigas monitor** if you can get one, set for O₂, CO₂, Cl₂ and H₂S. The volume of air is vast and the ventilation is forced and reliable — but acid dosing plus brine plus a bad day equals chlorine, dead-end galleries pool CO₂, and the older cress raft mats going anoxic underneath produce sulphide in enclosed spaces. A monitor is also excellent camouflage: nobody questions a person wearing one.

## Clothing and kit

**In air:** cold and saturated. Eight to twelve degrees with one hundred percent humidity and moving air is a serious thermal environment — more punishing than the numbers suggest, because you will never fully dry out. Wool base layers, not cotton. A genuinely waterproof outer, hood up by default. Two complete sets, so one can be drying while the other is soaked, and accept that "drying" is aspirational.

**Gloves.** The crews wear theirs through in a fortnight. Bring six pairs. Bring eight. The arrises are cast-sharp and have not had ten thousand years of water to round them off; every handrail, every hatch coaming, every ladder stringer is a blade with a tolerance.

**Boots with aggressive tread and a hard toe.** Wet concrete under an algal film is the second most common injury on the books after hands.

**Ear defenders, worn on a neck cord permanently.** Not "when it gets loud." The soffit rain arrives with maybe ninety seconds of warning, and when a hectare of ceiling lets go at once it is a genuine hearing-damage event, not a rainstorm.

**Eye protection with a dark option.** The glare off a lit sea is brutal and constant and comes from below as well as above — surface reflection plus the wet soffit reflecting it back down. Long shifts on open water produce something very like snow blindness. Polarised, and a spare pair, because you will lose one over the side.

**Your own light,** with your own batteries, on your own body. And a backup on the other side of your body.

**A whistle and a mechanical means of making noise.** Radios work indifferently in the cells and the ambient noise floor eats shouting.

## Navigation, and the single rule that keeps you alive

There is nothing to steer by. Understand what that means. From a small boat in the mid-volume of Cell 40 there is no shore in any direction, no sun, no stars, no swell direction, no smell of land, and the ceiling looks identical everywhere. The only reference is the **lamp grid**: red strings on one axis, amber on the other.

Learn to call your position — *red nineteen, amber four* — before you go anywhere, and say it aloud, to yourself, at intervals, like a rosary. Write it on your hand. Write it on the boat.

**Keep a hand on the string.** This is stencilled everywhere for a reason and it is meant literally as often as figuratively. A lamp string is a plumb line running from soffit to depth; it is a landmark, a handhold, a depth reference, a place to tie off, and the one thing in a cell that has an address. Losing contact with the string in fog, in soffit rain, or after a knock has dumped you off a raft is how people become statistics. They are never found, because there is nowhere for a body to wash up — the coast is a vertical wall nine hundred kilometres long.

If you take one behavioural change from this brief, take this: **in the Lower Volumes you do not move from a known place to an unknown place. You move from string to string.** Even on foot along a gallery. Even going to the head at night.

## Reading the schedule: the board, the whistles, the knocks

The Volumes kill by changing. The description is exact: *this place is younger than your assumptions about it.* A chamber dry last season is under ninety metres now. An air shaft is now a four-metre-per-second waterway that will take you into a conduit and turn you into a coring sample.

Three habits, drilled until automatic:

1. **Read the board at every bulkhead, every time.** Not the first time. Every time. The stage changes, the programme changes, and the drawings lie — a fitting with no weep beneath it is younger than the last drawing revision and does not exist on paper.
2. **Learn the whistle codes and respond to them as reflex, not as information.** Three long is drawdown. Two and a repeat is a knock in the system. There is no "I'll finish this first." Drawdown means a cell is about to start moving a province of water and you want to be on the correct side of a bulkhead before it does.
3. **Count the knocks.** Everyone does. When water hammer runs a conduit and the cell booms, the whole crew looks up and counts, and the counting is not superstition — it is the only diagnostic instrument available to a person standing on a raft. Three a shift is normal. Six means the system is misbehaving and you should be thinking about where the nearest adit is. If you are ever in a cell where the count reaches double figures, leave. Do not ask. Do not finish the task. Leave.

Notice the social function here too: the counting is communal. Joining in is how you become a person rather than a discrepancy.

## Going in the water

Don't, if you can avoid it. If you can't:

**Drysuit, always.** Not a wetsuit. A wetsuit holds a layer of alkaline water against your skin for the duration, which is a slow chemical burn across your entire body, and the crews have a word for what it does to your groin that I will not write down. Drysuit, dry gloves, hood, and a **full-face mask** so you are not tasting the cell.

**Freshwater rinse tank on the boat**, used before you break the suit seals. Lime crusts zips, seizes valves, ruins cameras and kills O-rings. Rinse-and-dry is a religion down there, observed with a fervour that will look excessive until the day your inflator valve locks open at thirty metres.

**Your dive tables are wrong.** They assume sea level. You are at roughly minus eleven hundred metres and ambient is meaningfully above one bar. Worse: the hoist out is a rapid ascent of eleven hundred metres. That is an altitude exposure. Treat the shaft like a flight. **No ascent within eighteen hours of any decompression dive**, and preferably twenty-four. This kills more visitors than the animals do, because it doesn't feel like a risk — it feels like going home.

**The sound is the hazard you will underestimate.** The cells ring. Cell 40 sits near a very low A and never entirely stops. Water couples sound into your body far better than air does; a valve slam that is a distant click at five hundred kilometres is something else entirely at five. If a knock passes through the cell while you are submerged, you will feel it in your sinuses, your chest, and your gut, and a bad one can genuinely injure you. **Rule: no immersion during gate operations. Check the programme. Ask twice.**

**The vertigo.** Two hundred metres of glassy, lamp-lit, faintly green visibility with no bottom and no surface texture produces a specific and unpleasant sensation: you are not swimming, you are *hovering above nothing, in a well-lit room with no walls.* Some people are fine. Some people panic instantly and comprehensively. You will not know which you are until you are in it. Go in on a line the first time. Go in beside a wall the first time. Give yourself a coast.

## Weather

**Soffit rain.** Three weeks in eleven, keyed to lift house maintenance. It is not rain as you know it; it is a hectare of ceiling releasing simultaneously from up to four hundred metres. The drops are big and have had a very long way to fall. It hammers the surface white, it is loud enough to damage hearing, it will swamp a small open boat, and it hurts. You can watch a front come at you across a hundred kilometres of flat water with nothing to do about it but get under something. **When in a cell during rain season, never be more than five minutes from cover.** Ear defenders on the cord, always.

**Fog.** Mornings, on the twenty-six hour cycle, lying in long combed bands aligned with the current. Beautiful, and the single most dangerous ordinary condition in the Volumes, because it removes the grid. You cannot see the strings. You can hear the knocking and nothing else, and sound in a hard-walled cell arrives from directions it did not come from. **In fog: do not move.** Tie to something. Wait for burn-off. Boats have been lost in fog thirty metres from a gantry.

## The animals

Everything lives on the walls; the middle is void. Plan your wildlife watching at a coast, and know the clock:

- **The green stripe** — four metres down, everywhere, a perfectly level band of algal fur at the illumination optimum, ringing the entire cell like a tidemark on a bath. The most beautiful single thing in the Lower Volumes, and the most slippery. Do not climb on it.
- **The silver** — hand-length schooling fish, introduced by the trillion as a trophic floor, now hanging against the wall faces in half-kilometre slabs. Swimming into a shoal is a religious experience and a navigational catastrophe; you lose all visual reference in a second. String discipline applies.
- **The squid**, which come up the wall at lamp-dim and go down at lamp-bright. This is a genuine reason not to be in the water during the transition. They are hunting, they are fast, and they have not yet learned that you are not food.
- **The eels**, one per construction joint, territorial, each inhabiting a perfectly straight horizontal crack hundreds of kilometres long with exactly two neighbours in the world. Do not put your hand in a joint. Do not put your hand in a joint. This is how the crews test newcomers and it is not a joke.
- **The lampfish**, orbiting the strings in vertical columns, already schooling tighter under amber than red after a single generation. Sit and watch these. They are the place's first original thought.
- **The gulls.** Aggressive, loud, numerous, and disease-carrying. They will rob your food from your hand. They came down the intake shafts and now breed on the lift house gantries and their chicks will live and die and never see sky. Do not feed them. Do not let them see where food is kept. Secure your mess kit as though against monkeys.

## Eating, drinking, washing

Drink only issued or treated water. The cell water is sweet, slippery, and alkaline; a mouthful won't kill you but a habit will give you a chemical gastritis. Rinse your mouth with neutral water after any immersion.

Don't eat the cress. The rafts exist to strip metals out of the water, they do it very well, and everyone knows where they've been. Eating cress marks you instantly as either a newcomer or a fool.

Fish from the stocks are eaten, variably and unofficially, depending on the cell and the year and which dosing pass went through last. Follow the crew. If the crew won't eat it this season, you don't want to know why.

Wash in neutral water at the end of every shift, dry completely, powder the feet. This is not fussiness. The single largest cause of people leaving the Volumes early is not accident but **attrition** — skin, ears, feet, hands — an accumulated wearing-down by damp and alkali that nobody notices until they can't close their fist.

## The psychology, which is the real difficulty

Physically, the Lower Volumes are manageable with the right kit. What the kit cannot help with is the **quality of absence.**

There is no sky and there has never been one. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense — instead there is that impossible receding double grid, surface reflection and soffit reflection converging, which the eye reads as distance and the mind reads as *wrong*. There is no stone that wasn't fired in a kiln. There is no old thing anywhere: every edge is crisp, every wall still shows shuttering grain, every rust weep is a hand's breadth a year and none of them reach the skirting except in the oldest galleries. Four years. All of it, four years old. A sea with no history whatsoever.

People react in one of two ways and you cannot predict which until you are there.

Some get **grid stare** — a flat, slowly deepening dissociation that comes from too many hours looking at perfect perspective, and which announces itself as boredom, then as apathy, then as a reluctance to bother with the string. This is the one that gets people killed, because it does not feel like an emergency. Guard against it with work, with routine, with the twenty-six hour discipline, and with other humans. If you find yourself in the mid-volume with no task, having not spoken to anyone in a day, and you find you cannot be bothered to call your grid reference — that is the symptom. Go to a wall. Go to a coast. Go where things live.

The others are seized by the opposite thing, and this is the reason to go. Somewhere around the second week, usually while alone on open water, you get it: **that all of this was poured on purpose, by people, every millimetre, for a reason that has nothing to do with beauty — and that it is a sea now anyway.** That the eels have made a world of a crack. That the birds have made a colony of a gantry. That the lampfish have already begun to develop opinions about amber. That six metres of concrete poured in an afternoon is now bedrock, and that the faint bright polish at the outlet throats is erosion, the first millimetre of a process that will round this place off like anywhere else in about ten thousand years, and that nobody alive will see it.

Bring something to write with. Most people need to write something down after that, and paper is scarce and damp, and the good notebooks come down the shaft with the traveller or not at all.

---

## What to bring for other people

You will need friends and you cannot buy them with local currency. What actually moves down there:

Lip balm. Barrier cream. Good gloves in quantity. Dry socks. Real coffee. Spices — the mess food is calorically correct and spiritually void. Batteries. Small sealed things: playing cards, dice, anything for the long crossings. And above all, **seeds, cuttings, and anything green that might survive.** The cell is four years old, alkaline, cool, and has not learned photosynthesis at scale; the kelp trials are half failing, and the people running them are the loneliest and most fascinating professionals in the Volumes. A traveller who arrives with a viable idea about plants will be adopted instantly.

---

## Leaving

Always up. Book your hoist slot early, confirm it twice, and know the alternate adit. If you have been in the water, observe the eighteen hours. If you have a cold, expect a bad ride.

The strangest part of the whole trip is the last hour, and I will not spoil the shape of it except to say: you will emerge into whatever passes for outside in that world, and your ears will crackle in the other direction, and you will look up.

And there will be sky there, and you will find that you have to learn it again.
