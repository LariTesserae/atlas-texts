# Preparing for the Addition

## First, understand what you are walking into

You are going to a place where nothing is worn out except the people.

Every other world you have visited has been aging at you from all sides: the rounded doorstep, the sag in the stair, the pebble, the rust bloom, the path worn diagonally across the lawn. You have never noticed this because you have never been without it. On the Addition it is absent — deliberately, expensively, nightly absent — and your nervous system, which reads age constantly and without permission, is going to spend its first week looking for something it cannot find.

Most visitors describe the result as homesickness with no object. It is not the emptiness that does it, and it is not the light. It is that the place has no past in it, and you are carrying yours around like a smell.

Prepare for that the way you would prepare for altitude: not by avoiding it, but by going slowly and expecting to feel stupid.

## Your body

**Humidity ninety, no wind, no evaporation.** This is the single most under-planned hazard. The Addition is not hot, but you cannot cool yourself there in the way you are built to. Sweat does not leave you; it sits. Walk the four kilometers between two towers at your usual pace and you will arrive with a headache and a heart rate you cannot explain. Halve your pace for the first eight days. Drink more than you want to, and salt it.

**Everything on you that can macerate will.** Feet, groin, armpits, under the breasts, behind the ears, inside the ears. Bring more socks than you believe possible, an antifungal powder and an antifungal cream (they are not interchangeable), and drying drops for ears. Take your shoes off indoors. Locals do; the towers are warm underfoot and that warmth is the only reliable dryer on the plain.

**The cut.** You will run your hand along an arris. Everyone does, once, and the wardens no longer bother telling people not to. Grain edges are freshly machined stone and they part skin like a razor with no sensation of having done it — you find out from the blood. Bring cyanoacrylate skin glue and use it immediately, because in ninety percent humidity an open cut does not scab, it softens, and a grain-burn that has gone soft takes three weeks and smells. Glue it, cover it, keep it covered.

**No ultraviolet.** The shell does not pass it. A month on the Addition is a month of vitamin D drawdown and a month without the mood-lift you get from direct sun without knowing you get it. Supplement from a week before you arrive and throughout. If you are staying beyond three months, bring a proper light box, not for brightness but for spectrum, and accept that you will feel ridiculous sitting in front of it.

**Your teeth.** The underwriting hum sits below hearing and everyone mislocates it in their own jaw. If you have metal restorations, implants, or a plate, you will mislocate it more strongly, and for some people it produces a persistent, maddening, entirely harmless sensation of a dental appointment about to begin. There is nothing to be done except know in advance that it is the ground and not your mouth. People who arrive without knowing this have been known to have healthy teeth pulled.

**Tinnitus.** If you have it, the Addition will either bury it or braid it into the hum. Roughly half of sufferers report relief and describe the place as the quietest they have ever been. The other half find they can no longer tell where they end. Test yourself in a very quiet anechoic room before you commit to a long stay.

## Time and sleep

There is no dark, no dawn, no noon, and no way to tell any of it by looking up. The light is a grey-gold hush from eleven hours ago.

**Bring a good eye mask and a spare.** Midnight is bright enough to read a sign by, and your pineal gland will not be fooled by curtains. Bring something that gives you a hard artificial morning, too — a bright lamp on a timer. You need a *from-one-direction* light to anchor to, even if it is a cheap one. This matters more than you think. The commonest visitor complaint after three weeks is not insomnia but a diffuse dissolution of the day, a sense that nothing has begun or ended.

**Learn the walk.** The rain is the clock: one rainfall a day, eleven minutes, vertical, and the hour of it drifts four minutes later every day, walking all the way around the year. The harbor office issues a rain-table; it is called the walk and locals carry it the way you carry a watch. Get one, and get used to the local phrasing — *two rains ago*, *before the next rain*, *rain-and-a-half*. If you ask someone the time in hours they will answer politely and then slightly recalibrate you as a person.

Do wear your own watch as well. But do not be the visitor who is visibly on a foreign clock. It reads as impatience, and impatience is the one social failing the Addition genuinely dislikes.

**Fog is your dawn.** It comes just before what would be sunrise, three meters deep, flowing uphill at walking pace, and for two hours the lower city is gone. Locals structure the day around it: sleep ends with the fog, the repair machines go in as it comes. Adopt this. You will settle faster.

## Clothing and gear

Pack for a warm cave, not for a coast.

- **Synthetics that release water, not cotton.** Cotton will not dry on your body and will never dry on a line, because there is no wind. Nothing dries on a line here.
- **The ground is your dryer.** The plates wick. Lay wet clothing flat on bare grain in the afternoon and it will be dry within the hour. **Retrieve it before the blue hour.** Anything left on the ground overnight is collected, cleaned, and placed in the nearest recovery cradle, and while you will get it back in better condition than you left it, you have spent a machine's night on your laundry, and people notice.
- **Footwear with soft, quiet, high-friction soles.** Before the fog lifts, every surface is beaded; grain under dew is genuinely slick, and a terrace edge is a knife-topped drop onto more knife-topped stone. No heels, no hard soles, nothing that would let you slide.
- **Thin gloves.** For handrails, for edges, for the moment when you forget.
- **A walking stick or trekking pole.** Not for support. For probing. See below.
- **A sit-pad.** You will be told to sit down without warning, twice a month, wherever you happen to be.
- **A sleeping mat with a true vapor barrier,** if you intend to camp out on the plain. Do not sleep on bare grain. The substrate wicks, and it does not distinguish between a puddle and you. People who have done it wake dehydrated to a degree that requires intervention, with their lips split and the taste of cucumber in their mouth.
- **Skin glue, dressings, antifungals, oral rehydration salts, vitamin D.**
- **A worn object.** See *Gifts*.

## The first week: things your eyes will do

**The lag.** Your shadow arrives about a third of a second behind you. It is small, it is at your heels, and it will make you stumble on stairs and terrace risers for roughly six days. The local mantra is not decoration; say it out loud when you trip: *my shadow is not late, I am early.* It works because it retrains where you think you are, rather than where the dark smear says you were.

**No hard shadows means no depth.** Objects sit in soft pools of their own dimming. You will misjudge the height of a step, the distance of a tower, the presence of a two-centimeter course-line, and — this is the dangerous one — the edge of a channel and the lip of the sea-wall. Walk the first few days looking down more than feels dignified. Use the stick to test edges in poor light. There are few railings; there is a cultural aversion to them.

**Fog navigation.** In the fog tide you can see three meters and the ground gives you no sound. Stay on a seam (the tram lines run on them and the moss marks them), or stay put. And know that a stand of cathedral bamboo is acoustically dead — walking into one during fog removes the last cue you had that the world continues in any direction. Visitors have gone in circles inside a stand forty meters across for an hour. If you find yourself inside sudden total silence, stop, sit, and wait for the lift.

**Sound generally.** Bass is eaten. Your own footsteps click like a pen; a dropped tool sounds like a dropped pencil; thunder is felt in the sternum and not heard. Do not rely on hearing anything approach. Conversely, your voice carries with unnerving intimacy — a sentence spoken at normal volume across a plaza arrives at the far side as though murmured beside the ear. **Do not raise your voice.** Shouting on the Addition is not loud, it is obscene: it is spoken directly into a stranger's head from two hundred meters. If you need to hail someone, wave.

## Etiquette

**When the hum changes pitch, sit down.** It lasts about ninety seconds. You will not be able to think well during it anyway; the ninety seconds are famous for producing decisions people regret. Sit, watch the six rings stand up in your water glass, wait. Do not talk through an adjustment. Do not film it while standing.

**The number.** Everyone in Meniscus knows the current heron count and will tell you. Ask. Ask early, ask everyone, do not compare the answers aloud, and do not joke about the number being wrong. It is the closest thing the Addition has to a shared pulse — a single living quantity in a place where nothing else changes — and a visitor who takes it seriously is forgiven a great deal else.

**The channels are the sacred thing, and the trap.** Do not put anything in them. Not a coin, not a leaf, not your hand, not a boat for a child. The channels are trying to learn a meander, one millimeter in ten years, and the entire local theory of the future rests on that learning being *the water's own*. A tourist who scuffs the bank to "help" has not committed vandalism; he has committed forgery. Old-timers have a word for it and will not say it in front of you.

Go to the bench by the eleventh channel. Sit there a while. Look at the millimeter. Say something small and true about it or say nothing. Do not say *it must be frustrating* and do not ask how long it will take, because they have done that arithmetic on themselves and it comes out past their grandchildren.

**Do not test the cradles.** Getting deliberately collected — lying down at dusk to be picked up, cleaned, and set out in a recovery cradle by morning — is a thing visitors think of, and it has been done, and the wardens regard it the way you would regard a guest who calls an ambulance for fun. It costs a machine a night. Their nights are being rationed on purpose.

**You may watch the repair.** Sit where you do not shade them (you will not shade them) and keep still. The cat-sized machines dressing the arrises are, to locals, roughly what a cathedral mason is to us: a thing seen so often it is only noticed by strangers, and yet still slightly holy. It is fine to be a stranger about it.

**Patience.** Conversation here has pauses in it that will feel, to you, like the other person has finished and you have failed to respond. Count to three before you fill a silence. Locals are not slow; they are living in a city built for four million with ninety thousand in it, and the whole culture is organized around the idea that things arrive when they arrive, including morning.

## Gifts, and what to say about your own world

They will ask you about weather. They always do, and specifically about wind, hard morning light, and shadows that arrive on time. Answer truthfully and without lyricism. Enthusiasm reads as boasting; understatement reads as kindness.

The best gift you can bring from outside is **a worn thing**: a river-rounded pebble, a spoon with its plating gone at one spot, a stair-tread offcut hollowed by a century of feet, a book with a softened corner. Not an antique — an *used* thing, where you can see the shape of the using. It will be passed hand to hand at a table for an hour and people will find the hollow with a thumb. Present it as a question, not a trophy. *This took eighty years. Does that seem long to you?* is a good opening and has started friendships.

Do not bring a gift of erosion imagery — photographs of canyons, of meanders, of sea-carved arches. It lands badly and you will not immediately understand why.

## Hazards, ranked honestly

1. Falling — terrace edges, channel banks, the sea-wall. The wall is six or seven meters straight down into working water with no beach and no way out; a fall from it is usually fatal not from the fall but from the absence of anywhere to be afterwards.
2. Heat strain from unevaporated sweat.
3. Infection of an ignored grain-cut.
4. Sleeping on bare grain.
5. Getting lost in fog inside bamboo.
6. Social: shouting; the channels; impatience.

Note what is not on the list: crime, animals, bad water, bad air. The Addition is one of the safest places you will ever be, which is exactly why people relax and walk off a terrace.

## Coming home

Budget for the return the way you would budget for the trip.

Your first hard morning after the Addition will hurt. Not metaphorically: one direction, all at once, yellow, and your pupils out of practice and your skin with no UV tolerance left. Wear a hat and sunglasses on day one, even in weak sun. Expect to burn at half your usual exposure.

For two or three weeks you will step early on stairs, because your feet have learned to arrive a third of a second before your shadow and are now wrong in the other direction. You will flinch at thunder, because it will be *audible*. You will find ordinary cities unbearably loud in the bass, unbearably crowded, and unbearably dirty, and you will get over the first two.

The last thing goes away more slowly and some people do not want it to. You will begin noticing wear everywhere — the dip in your own doorstep, the shine on a banister where ten thousand hands have gone, the rounded corner on a table you have owned for years and never once looked at. You will keep touching these things. It is the Addition's single best gift, and the whole plain, patiently, nightly, at enormous cost, is doing without it so that one day it can have it honestly.
