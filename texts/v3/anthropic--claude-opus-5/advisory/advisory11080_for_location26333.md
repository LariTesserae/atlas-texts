# Preparing for the Upper Shelf

## First, understand the two facts that govern everything

**One: you cannot go down.** On this plateau the standard treatment for every serious altitude illness — descent — does not exist. The rim is a clean drop with no notch, no gorge, no ramp; nobody has ever been below it; and the etiquette of the place makes you the last person who should try. Whatever your lungs and skull do to you up there, you will be treating it *in place*.

**Two: you are the only animal.** Not the first tourist. The first animal. Everything you exhale, shed, wipe, bury, or forget is an introduction event into a biosphere that has never met a nervous system. Your preparation is therefore half mountaineering and half quarantine, and the quarantine half matters more.

---

## Months out: the body

**Acclimatize before you transit, fully.** Treat the Shelf as a 4,000-metre objective with no bail-out. Spend three to four weeks sleeping high in your own world before you step through — real nights at altitude, not a hypoxic tent for an hour a day. Arrive already adapted; do not plan to adapt there.

Note a subtlety that catches people: the Shelf's air is *saturated and warm*. Water vapour takes up partial pressure that dry cold mountain air does not. At the same barometric altitude, you will get measurably less oxygen per breath here than on a dry peak. Everyone underestimates the Upper Shelf by four or five hundred metres. Plan for the deficit.

Carry, and know how to use: a pulse oximeter, acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine or a PDE-5 inhibitor for pulmonary oedema, a small oxygen supply, and — genuinely, not as a formality — a portable hyperbaric bag. It is heavy and stupid-looking and it is your only descent.

**Train slow.** In saturated air your sweat does not evaporate. The temperature is mild, so you will not die of heat, but you will discover that your body's whole cooling system has been switched off and that hauling a load uphill produces an unfamiliar, dull, rising internal pressure. Learn to work at the pace the locals work at: sledge-haul pace, one long walking rhythm, no bursts, no scrambles. Nobody on the Shelf has ever hurried.

**Get your teeth and your gut in order.** There is no dentistry, no surgery beyond obsidian, no evacuation. Any abscess, appendix, hernia or bad tooth you take up there, you keep.

## Months out: the immune problem, honestly

Two thousand people, isolated for an unguessable time, in a world with no animal reservoirs and no vectors. Their pathogen pool is tiny. Your ordinary head cold — the one you would work through — is the most dangerous object you will bring.

Minimum decency:

- **Quarantine yourself.** Two weeks of genuine isolation before transit, with daily temperature and symptom logging. If you cough on day thirteen, you do not go.
- **Screen and clear.** Full panel: respiratory viruses, TB, gut parasites, skin fungi, staph carriage. Treat carriage before you travel.
- **Accept what you cannot fix.** You cannot decontaminate yourself. You carry a kilogram of gut flora, mites in your eyelash follicles, fungi between your toes, a whole animal ecology walking around inside a bag of skin. Everything you can seal, seal. Everything you can't, minimise. Wear a mask in the town — not for you, for them — and expect to have to explain why, in a language with no word for contagion because contagion here has almost nothing to work with.
- **Never leave organic waste.** No cat-holes. Nothing biodegrades on the Shelf the way it does at home, because the entire cast of decomposers — worms, beetles, flies, mites — is absent. There is fungus and bacterium and time, and time here is very slow. Pack a sealed toilet system and carry every gram of it out. Same for nail clippings, hair, food scraps, blood-soaked dressings.
- **Bring no seed, no fruit, no soil, no live plant, no wood, no animal product you can avoid.** Especially: nothing that could establish. A single introduced leaf-chewing insect into a world of undefended, thornless, unbittered plants is not a mishap, it is an extinction event with a long fuse. Freeze your gear. Bake it. Inspect the seams of everything with a light. Check the tread of your boots with a pick.

**Avoid animal materials in your kit** for a second reason too: they are unclassifiable there. Leather, wool, bone, horn, down, felt — these are objects with no category. A wool sweater is not exotic to these people, it is *nonsense*. Go synthetic and plant-fibre throughout.
