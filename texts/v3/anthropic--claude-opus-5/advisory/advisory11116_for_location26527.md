# Preparing to Visit The Warm Room, Below

## First, understand what you are walking into

Most hazardous places kill you by event. This one kills you by *duration*. There is no rockfall, no predator, no drop. There is only a room that is warmer than your skin and wetter than your lungs, and a stair that you have to climb back up afterwards, using muscles that will by then be running two degrees hot.

Rank the dangers honestly, because your instincts will rank them wrong:

1. **Heat.** Forty-something degrees at saturation. Your skin sits at about thirty-five. Heat therefore flows *into* you from the air, continuously, on top of the hundred-odd watts you generate simply by being alive. Sweat is useless — the room has told you so, it is already raining on itself — so you have no evaporative brake at all. You will not feel yourself failing. Hyperthermia's first casualty is judgement, and its second is the memory of having had any.
2. **Gas.** The fissure breathes. A breathing volcanic seam in an unventilated sub-basement will pool carbon dioxide in the sump and in the low western third, and may deliver hydrogen sulphide, sulphur dioxide, and radon at concentrations that vary with the eleven-hour cycle. The absence of animals is not proof of sterility; it may be the room telling you something about its air. Believe the room.
3. **Spores.** Everything green down there is a thermophile. It thrives at your core temperature. Twice a day it fills the chamber with a slow grey snow, and it has never in its existence been offered a warm wet host with branching passages and a poor immune memory for fungi. You are the first opportunity this ecology has ever had. Treat the spore load as the most consequential thing you will carry home.
4. **Electricity.** A live, unmaintained circuit in a hundred-per-cent-humidity chamber with brass levers, standing water, and condensation on every fitting. Your skin will be wet salt. Your resistance will be a tenth of normal.
5. **Falls and cuts.** Fresh basalt takes skin off dry palms; it will strip a rope sheath, a hose jacket, a knee. Sweat-slick glass treads, no handrail, an offset concrete floor with a rail buried in moss, and one exit which is upward.

## Two clocks

**Yours** is roughly twenty to thirty minutes of unassisted exposure for a fit, heat-acclimated adult at rest, and that figure includes the climb out. Set a hard timer that is not on your phone, that beeps in a pattern you cannot rationalise away, and appoint a person at the stairhead whose only job is to end your visit against your objections. You *will* object. That is the diagnostic.

Conductive cooling triples the budget. A phase-change vest (packs at 15–21 °C, not ice — ice against skin causes vasoconstriction and traps your core heat) will buy you sixty to ninety usable minutes. It will also make you the coldest object in the room, which means the room will condense on you visibly, in sheets, and you will drip like the machines do. Accept this. It's honest: down here you are not a warm body, you are a cold surface. To the vegetation you are indistinguishable from a pipe with the steam off.

**The room's** clock is eleven hours long, and it belongs to the pump. Before you plan anything, establish the phase. Do this from the stairhead, where the air is survivable: sit for twelve hours with a recorder and a notebook, and mark the moment the throat-clearing rises out of the east. Everything else in your itinerary is scheduled relative to that mark.

## Weeks out

- **Heat acclimation, ten to fourteen days.** Daily exertion in heat until your plasma volume expands and your resting core temperature drops. Be clear-eyed: acclimation makes you sweat better, and sweating is worthless here. What it actually buys you is cardiovascular margin — minutes, not hours. Do it anyway; you will want those minutes on the stair.
- **Medical screening and honest self-exclusion.** Do not go with cardiac disease, uncontrolled hypertension, recent febrile illness, pregnancy, or immunosuppression of any kind. Review your medications: anticholinergics, antihistamines, beta blockers, diuretics, stimulants and SSRIs all degrade thermoregulation. Do not go hungover. Do not go under-slept. The room does not distinguish between a bad night and a pre-existing condition.
- **Vaccination and prophylaxis.** Tetanus current. Discuss antifungal and antibacterial contingencies with a physician who will believe you, and establish a baseline chest film — not because you expect trouble, but because thermophilic pulmonary infections declare themselves weeks later, vaguely, and a baseline is the difference between a diagnosis and a mystery.
- **Biosecurity plan, written down and rehearsed.** Where the dirty gear goes. Who handles it. What gets incinerated. Which of your other destinations you have agreed not to visit for thirty days afterward. A multiverse traveller's spore load is not a personal risk; it is a portfolio risk taken on behalf of worlds that did not consent.

## Kit

**Air.** A full-face powered respirator with P3/HEPA plus combined acid-gas and organic-vapour cartridges handles spores and the stinks, but filters cannot manufacture oxygen or remove carbon dioxide. Carry a four- or five-gas monitor (O₂, CO₂, H₂S, CO, combustibles) clipped at *ankle* height as well as chest height, because the heavy gases live where you'll be kneeling. If the monitor argues for supplied air, use an airline run down the stair from the surface rather than a bottle on your back — a fifteen-kilogram cylinder is fifteen kilograms of extra heat generation on the climb out. Sleeve the hose in something sacrificial for the first ten metres; the basalt will eat it.

**Cooling.** Vest plus spare packs at the stairhead. A pre-chilled electrolyte bladder with a respirator drink port. Note that your drink becomes blood-warm within twenty minutes and that drinking hot salt water while overheating is genuinely unpleasant and absolutely required.

**Light.** A dim red headlamp, and nothing else, and a white lamp in a sealed pocket for emergencies only. This matters more than it sounds. Dark-adapt on the surface — thirty minutes, red goggles or eyepatches, and keep them on through the descent — because the moss and the fissure are visible only to an adapted eye, and one careless second of white light costs you the entire reason you came. If you must photograph, understand that any lens colder than the room will fog inside and out and stay fogged; pre-warm optics above room temperature, seal them with desiccant, and never open a cool case down there. Anything cool you open in that air will contain water forever.
