# Preparing for the Chamber Under the Pan

## First, the correct frame of mind

Most places you can travel to will hurt you if you make a mistake. This one mostly won't. It has no predators, no floods, no rockfall to speak of, no cold, no gas you can smell. It is, physically, one of the gentlest rooms in any world.

What it has instead is **permanence**. Nothing in there decays, rusts, moulds, washes away, blows off, or gets eaten. The silt polygons on that floor have held their shape for longer than your species has had writing. The spider's web has hung intact since before anybody drew a wheel. If you put a bootprint in the silt, that bootprint is now a feature of the chamber, and it will still be a feature of the chamber when the fig above is a seam of carbon.

So the discipline you are training for is not survival discipline. It is the discipline of a person handling an old document with bare hands: slow, reversible, deliberate, and constantly aware that the medium keeps everything.

There are exactly two prior events in the room's human record: somebody came down with a chert flake, and somebody left. You are the third entry. Decide before you go what you want that entry to say.

---

## When to go

Three clocks matter, and they conflict.

**The annual clock.** The chamber's temperature is the surface's long-term mean, and it never changes. The surface's temperature changes enormously. This means the *experience of entering* is completely different by season:

- In the burning months, you descend from something like 45°C into blood-warmth and it reads as relief, as cool shade, and the chamber feels like a mercy.
- In the two frost weeks, you descend from near-freezing into blood-warmth and it reads as entering a body. This is the more remarkable transition and I would choose it if I only had one visit.

**The solar clock.** The daily bar of light only reaches the floor when the sun is high enough to shoot the shaft. Near midsummer it lands on the silt and crosses it; near midwinter it may only climb the eastern wall, or fail entirely. The lichen stripe on the wall is the printed record of this — read it before you descend and you will know exactly where the bar will land on the day you are there.

So the finest light event is in the deadliest surface season, and the kindest surface season has the poorest light. **The equinoxes are the compromise**, and they are a good one: the bar arrives at a shallow angle, catches the flowstone curtain edge-on, and dies on the wall rather than the floor, so you can watch it without ever having walked on the silt.

**The fig's clock.** If the tree has flushed, the bar comes down dappled and faintly green. If the crown is shut into its hot-month fist, the bar is hard, white, and sharp-edged. Both are worth seeing. Neither is better.

**The daily clock.** The chamber breathes twice a day, and it is breathing on the atmospheric tide — pressure peaks around mid-morning and mid-evening, troughs around pre-dawn and mid-afternoon. The sigh from the shaft is strongest as the pressure turns. If you want to hear it, be silent and seated around local 10:00 and again around 16:00. Most visitors miss it entirely because they are still moving.

## The air: the one thing that can actually kill you

Do not romanticise this part. You are descending into a dead-end chamber with a single narrow shaft, air exchange measured in a handful per day, and a ceiling covered in respiring fig roots. Roots consume oxygen and exhale carbon dioxide, and in a room with essentially no ventilation, that accumulates.

The text's own tell is there: *breath moving with an embarrassing loudness.* Elevated CO₂ makes you breathe deeper and hear yourself. That may be silence. It may also be chemistry.

**Non-negotiable:**

- Carry a **CO₂ and O₂ meter**, and lower it on a line to the chamber floor before any human goes down. Let it sit five minutes. Read it at the bottom, because CO₂ pools.
- Set a hard turn-back number in advance and write it on the back of your hand. My numbers: above 1.5% CO₂, work only, no sitting; above 3%, do not descend; below 19.5% O₂, do not descend.
- A candle stub is a crude backup and better than nothing, but it fails late. Use it as a second opinion, never a first.
- **Radon.** Dolomitic limestone, months of air residence, no ventilation. Assume it is elevated. For one visit of a day this is a trivial dose — comparable to a medical X-ray at worst. For anyone who becomes a regular caretaker of this place, it is the single largest lifetime risk in the entire enterprise, and it is invisible and cumulative. Take a passive detector, leave it a month if you can, and know the number before you make a habit of the place.

**Heat and water.** The air is a degree or two below skin temperature and utterly still. That means you cannot lose heat by convection or radiation at all. Every watt you generate has to leave by evaporation, and there is no breeze to help — only your own thermal plume. At rest this is fine: you'll lose maybe 150 ml an hour and never notice. Climbing ten metres of rope out of there, you will lose a litre and your heart rate will not come down the way it does in cool air, because there is nothing to cool against.

- **Plan the ascent, not the descent.** Go up rested, hydrated, and unhurried. Most incidents in this kind of place happen on the way out, twenty minutes after somebody said "right, let's go."
- Four to six litres per person for the underground day, and that is on top of everything the surface demands.
- Salt with it. You will sweat steadily and never feel wet, because it evaporates instantly into dry air.

## The shaft

Ten or eleven metres, shoulder-wide, straight, with the fig's roots running down the walls.

**Anchors.** Do not rig to the fig. It is a person-high tree that is drinking the whole chamber through those roots, and it is also the structural lip of your entrance. Loading the root buttress risks collapsing the hole edge onto whoever is in it, and killing the tree kills the only interesting thing in the room.

Rig instead to the pan itself: a spanning bar across the opening seated on wide pads well back from the lip, backed up by driven anchors into the caliche hardpan. Caliche is hard, brittle, and untrustworthy in thin plates — test every placement, equalise at least three, and back up the whole system. A lightweight tripod with ballast is the better answer if you can carry one, because it also gives you a clean haul point.

**The sleeve.** This is the single piece of specialist kit I would not go without: a **canvas or light nylon tube, ten metres long, hung down the shaft**, open at both ends. You descend inside it. It keeps your body, your boots, your pack and your rope off the fig roots for the whole descent; it catches every pebble and grain you shed instead of letting it rain onto the silt below; and it stops the rope sawing a groove into a root that took a century to get there. Recover it on the way out, shake it out on the surface.

Without a sleeve you will spend the entire descent rubbing against the living part of this place with your shoulders. Those roots grow a finger's width in ten years. A root you tear is not damage, it's amputation of a decade.

**Rescue.** A shoulder-width shaft is very close to unrecoverable. You cannot easily haul a limp body up it, you cannot assist alongside, and there is no second exit. Therefore:

- Never have everyone underground at once. One down, one on the surface, always.
- Pre-rig the haul before the first person goes down. A 3:1 already built, a second rope already fixed. Do not plan to build a system in an emergency.
- Voice does not carry ten metres up a bent shaft reliably, and radio will not go through limestone. Rig a **bell cord**: a light line from surface to floor with an agreed code of tugs. Two = I'm fine. Three = coming up. Continuous = come and get me. It is a thousand-year-old technique and it is entirely in keeping with the room.

## Kit: what to bring

Deliberately short, deliberately quiet.

- Helmet, harness, descender, two ascenders, rope, backup rope, haul kit, edge pads, the sleeve.
- **Tape over every carabiner gate.** In that silence a gate closing is a gunshot. Rubber-sleeved or taped hardware throughout.
- Soft, clean, **smooth-soled** shoes carried down in a bag and changed into at the bottom. Lugged boots print deep and carry the surface down in their treads. A second pair of socks is an acceptable alternative for the sitting phase.
- A rolled **cotton runner** — three or four metres of plain cloth — to lay as your one path off the shaft base. You walk on the cloth. You roll it up and carry it out. Your footprints go home with you.
- A thin sit-mat for the fallen blocks. The blocks are the furniture; they are already disturbed and they hold no silt. Use them and nothing else. The mat keeps your skin oils and sweat salts off the stone.
- One good headlamp, one backup, one emergency. **Red mode matters** — it preserves the dark adaptation you will spend forty minutes building.
- A soft mask for the working phase: it keeps your shed skin and breath droplets in and the raised silt out of your lungs. Take it off for the sitting phase; the smell of the room is part of the room.
- A **vibrating timer**, not a beeping one.
- Wool or cotton clothing. No nylon shells — the rustle will be the loudest thing in the chamber, and the static holds lint.
- Gas meter, spare batteries, water, salt, a soft brush for cleaning yourself at the shaft head.

## Kit: what not to bring

- **Anything scented.** Sunscreen, insect repellent, deodorant, soap, lip balm, treated fabrics. The air here has a mineral smell like the inside of a clean gourd and it has had that smell for ten thousand years. A modern chemical smell in a still, unventilated room does not disperse in an afternoon; it hangs for weeks and slowly soaks into porous stone. Take your sun protection as hat, cloth and shade above ground, and scrub whatever you must apply before you go to the hole.
- **Anything that hums, ticks, whines or has a fan.** LED drivers whine. Digital watches tick. You did not travel across a multiverse to listen to a switching power supply.
- **Food.** Not one crumb. This chamber is oligotrophic to a degree that is hard to imagine — a dropped raisin is an unprecedented energy event in a place that has had no energy input but sunlight-on-lichen since the water left. Eat on the surface, an hour before, and not so much that your digestion becomes audible.
- **Water you might spill.** Carry it in a sealed bottle, drink at the shaft base, and think hard about the moss. That dried grey cushion in the low alcove is not dead. It has a plan that involves being wetted, and it has been holding that plan through years of not being wetted. If you tip a litre into the alcove, you will trigger it. It will green, and then it will dry again, and you will have spent a portion of a thing's reserves on a rehearsal. Don't.
- Anything you might drop and not find in the dark. Inventory at the top, inventory at the bottom, inventory before ascending.

## The protocol inside

The order of operations matters more here than in almost anywhere else, because **walking is irreversible and the fine dust you raise will hang for hours and then coat everything.** Plan the visit backwards from that fact.

**1. Arrive at the bottom, and do not step off the shaft base.** Sit down on the cloth. Kill the lights. Do nothing for forty minutes.

This is the part everybody wants to skip and it is the whole visit. Your eyes will open into the grey column from the shaft. Your ears will open outward, find nothing, and come back to your own body — the surf of blood, the click in the throat. Then, underneath, the tick of a single grain landing.

**2. Watch the bar arrive, before you have disturbed anything.** The hanging dust in the light is the room's one visible event, and it only looks right when it is the room's own dust, drifting at its own impossible slowness. If you walk the floor first, the bar will show you the storm you made. Sit off-axis, stay low, and watch it come down the wall, cross, redden, tilt, and go out.

**3. Only now, put on the light and explore.** Survey, photograph with long exposures rather than flash, walk the cloth runner, keep your arms slow and close, do not blow dust off anything, and for the love of everything, **do not sneeze in there.** Clear your sinuses at the top. The air is desiccating and you will want to.

**4. Then extinguish and sit again for an hour or two.**

Things not to do at any point, listed plainly because visitors always want to:

- Do not touch the chert flake. Photograph it, kneel beside it, do not pick it up. Skin oils are permanent here. Its position relative to the shaft base is a piece of information about a person, and it is the only piece anyone will ever have.
- Do not touch the web.
- Do not press the flowstone. It flakes.
- Do not break a dead stalactite "because it's dead anyway."
- Do not use a hanging root as a handhold. They look like rope. They are not rope.
- Do not urinate underground, or anywhere near the entrance. Nitrogen, salts and a litre of liquid water, in a room whose entire relationship with water is a crust of salt that remembers it. Go before you rig.
- Do not leave a marker, a cairn, a note, or a coin. Nothing you leave will ever be removed by any process.

## What it will do to your head

Be ready for this, because it catches people.

The air is within two degrees of your skin. That removes the thermal boundary between you and the room — the constant low-level signal that tells your nervous system where you stop. Add total darkness and total silence, and you have accidentally built a flotation tank out of geology. Expect, after thirty to ninety minutes:

- Time distortion, usually in the direction of underestimating.
- Visual noise resolving into shapes, faces, grids, drifting light. This is normal and means nothing.
- A loosening of body boundary — the specific sensation the description points at, where warmth stops registering as warmth and starts registering as *no difference between you and the room.* Some people find this the most peaceful thing they have ever felt. Some find it terrifying, and the terror comes fast.

Mitigations: know it's coming; keep your light in your hand, not in your pack; set the vibrating timer so you have one external event to trust; agree tug codes with the surface and use them. And know that the panic, if it comes, is not about the room. The room contains nothing.

## Coming out, and afterwards

Roll the runner. Bag your soft shoes. Sweep the shaft base with your hand — gently — for anything you shed. Inventory. Pull the sleeve behind you. Sit on the surface for twenty minutes before you start walking, because you have just climbed ten metres in air that could not cool you.

Then look at the pan. It will seem unbearably loud and windy and full of information, and a single grey subshrub in its clear ring of ground will look like a crowd.

Leave the surface as you found it too. Mark your arrival point before you wander — the pan erases distance, and the pebble grading is your only compass: **coarse means rim, fine means centre.** The fig is the only landmark and it is smaller than you are.

---

## The one-line version

Take down with you: heat, breath, attention, and nothing else. Bring all three back up. Leave the room at its own temperature, at its own pace, with its ledger of events still two entries long — or three, if you must, and if the third entry is written as lightly as the first.
