# Preparing for the Kell: A Traveller's Brief

## First, understand what the danger is

You are not principally going somewhere cold, high, loud and vertical, though it is all four. You are going somewhere **under-documented**, and the deficiency is not administrative. On the Kell, the difference between a thing that is the case and a thing that is nearly the case is a difference that can be measured in centuries of weather, and above the Lichen Line it has not been paid.

You will arrive as the thinnest object on the mountain. Forty thousand people up there have eleven generations of ledgers, court records, dog-names and grandmothers behind them. You will have a face nobody has looked at twice. Everything in your preparation should be aimed at correcting this before you go above the Line, and at being able to prove, afterwards, at home, what you were when you left.

## Ballast: the paperwork of yourself

Do this months in advance, and do it tediously.

**Make a deposition of your particulars** and leave sealed copies with at least three people in your home world who do not know each other. Not a passport photograph. A *description*, of the sort that would be useless to a border officer and invaluable to you:

- The spelling of your full name, all of it, including the middle name you never use, written in your own hand, dated, and witnessed by two signatures.
- Which wrist carries which scar, and how it was got, and from whom you heard the story of how it was got.
- The handedness of your coat's buttons. Which pocket you keep coins in.
- Your handwriting, a full page of it, and your signature nine times.
- A dental impression if you can get one. Moles, in a diagram. The order of your teeth's crookedness.
- Six memories in full narrative, written out, of the kind only you would bother to keep: not weddings, but the smell of a particular corridor.

**Decide in advance what you will accept.** People come back from the upper mountain with details altered, and the alterations are almost never dramatic. You must settle now, in writing, which changes you would let stand and which you would consider a mutilation. Travellers who have not thought about this beforehand are the ones who come home and spend a decade trying to argue themselves back into a shape nobody else remembers.

**Bring nothing new.** This is the single most-ignored piece of advice and the one the Kell will punish. New boots, new instruments, a new knife, a coat off the rack — these are objects with no history, and above the Line they are exactly the kind of thing that turns out to have been not sufficiently the case. Carry gear that is old, mended, visibly used, and *known*. A pot with a dent you can account for. A knife you can name the previous owner of. Sew a card into your coat lining with the coat's provenance on it: where bought, when, what was spilled on it. This will look superstitious at home. In Hantle it will look like basic hygiene, and it is.

## The body

**Altitude.** The pressure does not correspond to an elevation. Whatever prophylactic you use at home for high country will work, and will work partially, and will not work in the way the dosage tables say. Expect the first four days to be bad: headache behind the eyes, appetite gone, sleep in twenty-minute pieces, a shortness of breath that arrives on the seventh step and not the sixth. The air is described locally as *thin and sweet and slightly wrong*, and you will find that all three adjectives are load-bearing. Some travellers report a faint taste of pennies. Do not attribute this to the mountain; attribute it to yourself; but write it down.

**Stairs.** Train for stairs. Not hills — stairs, with a load, for months. Hantle is eleven kilometres long, four hundred metres wide, and almost entirely vertical in its short axis. There is no square, nowhere to stand about, no flat place to rest that isn't someone's doorway. A flight of stairs is a decision; make sure you are able to make several hundred of them a day.

**Damp.** Six or seven degrees, permanent, breathing. This is not a cold that kills you, it is a cold that colonises you. Wool, wool, oiled canvas, and *no cotton anywhere against your skin*. Ten pairs of socks is not excessive. Bring foot powder, a fungicide, and a second pair of boots so that one pair is always drying near a vent. Trench foot is the commonest complaint of visitors and the one the local physicians find most boring.

**Lungs.** Downwind of the Sowing Towers the air is grey with quarried grit thrown deliberately into the wind. It is manufactured silica dust and it is doing its intended work on the rock and will do the same in you. A proper fitted respirator, not a scarf. Learn the wind and the Sowing schedule on your first day and treat the plume like an active industrial hazard, because that is precisely what it is and the locals have simply decided to live inside it.

**Eyes and skin.** The Acid Terraces spray dilute sulphuric acid off rope stages onto cliff faces, and the mist travels. Sealed goggles. A bottle of bicarbonate solution in a pocket you can reach one-handed while hanging on to something. Never stand below an etch-crew, and if you hear the hand-pumps working above you, move, and go on moving for longer than feels necessary.

**Ears.** The stamp mills run forty strokes a minute in six buildings and there is nothing on the Kell to absorb sound. You will feel them in your teeth from three kilometres out. Bring wax and bring a spare tin of it. You will also, at intervals of a few minutes, feel a sub-bass come up through the floor and the bed-frame and the bones of your feet — that one you cannot block and should not try to. Learn to sleep through it. Everyone up there did, at about age two.

**Height.** Tenements are corbelled out over drops of a kilometre and a half on iron brackets. The passenger gondolas swing in the updraughts in a manner that has made hardened sailors ill. If you have any vertigo at all, know it now, at home, and bring something for the nausea. There is no route through the city that avoids exposure. There is no ground floor.

**Light.** There is no sun. There is a grey even lumen on a twenty-seven-hour cycle, brightest at a pale noon when you can read a broadsheet if you hold it close. Two weeks of this puts most visitors into a flat, slow, argumentative mood. Bring a bright lamp for your quarters, take your vitamin preparations, and expect your sleep to drift; the city legislated the day into eight watches of unequal length precisely because bodies do not fit twenty-seven hours. Get a printed watch-table on arrival and carry it. Appointments in Hantle are made by watch and bell, never by clock-time, and a foreigner who says "at three" is telling nobody anything.

## What to carry

- A **notebook and pencils**, more than you think. This is not sentimental. See below.
- **Old, mended, provenanced gear.** Nothing shiny.
- **A bell.** Cheap, brass, loud, worn on the pack.
- **Rope, and the knowledge of tug-codes.** Above the Line you cannot rely on shouting; the air in places is *dumb*, and a shout may be lost entirely or delivered four minutes late from the wrong direction. Speech is not a reliable technology up there. Rope is.
- **Gifts.** Rag paper, good ink, graphite, and above all **used books with marginalia in them.** A well-annotated second-hand volume is, on the Kell, a brick of pure ballast, and giving one is a considerable and well-understood courtesy. A new book is a slightly insulting present.
- **No measuring instruments** unless you have a professional reason. A rangefinder or theodolite marks you as either a fool or a provocateur; instrument distances above the Line are not merely inaccurate but legally void. Distances that count are *walked*. If you need a measurement, you hire the Guild of Paces, and a pacer's certificate outweighs any instrument in court.
- **Absolutely no biological material.** No seeds, spores, lichen samples, cultures, pets, fruit. There is a black market in foreign lichen and it is a serious offence, treated roughly the way we treat arson, because the Lichenhouses are the slowest and most respected of the three industries and their timescale is two hundred years to a single candle-alder. Do not be the traveller who wrecked a century of work with something in a trouser cuff. Turn your pockets out before the funicular.

## The first three days: getting thick

You come up through the cloud-deck on the funicular, ears popping, and emerge into grey light and noise. Do these things in this order.

1. **Go to the Registry.** Nine windowless storeys of dressed stone with its own generator, and everyone knows it is the actual foundation of the city. Register yourself: name, particulars, provenance, business, lodgings. Pay the fee, which is small, and buy the certified copies, which are not. Carry one on your body at all times and lodge one with your landlord.

2. **Give an account of yourself that can be checked.** A modest, dull, verifiable-sounding provenance from below serves you far better than the truth. The truth about where you come from is unverifiable, and unverifiable is a synonym for thin. If you must be a traveller from elsewhere, be a boring one with documents.

3. **Become gossip.** This is the real work and I cannot overstate it. Eat at the same stall at the same watch. Buy your bread from the same woman and learn her children's names and let her learn yours and mispronounce it and be corrected. Drink in one house, not five. Be regular, be visible, be slightly tediously punctual. Within a week a hundred people should be able to say *oh, that one, comes through at fourth bell, always the same*. You are manufacturing local history at the fastest rate available to you, and it is the only armour that works above the Line. **Be boring, repeatedly, in public.**

4. **Keep the ledger habit.** Write down what you saw, in the local style, plainly and daily: *Fourth watch: mist on the west truss, cleared. Two stept and a herder, downward. The grit plume off the Sowing Tower at ninth bell, as usual. Nothing else.* Date every entry, sign it, and have someone else initial it. This is not journalling. Attention is a weathering agent — weak but free — and the six hundred Stare-Wardens in their tin huts are doing at civic scale exactly what you are doing for yourself.

5. **Take a warden's shift** if you are offered one, and you will be, because visitors are cheap attention. Four hours in a tin hut looking at an assigned face of rock, then the ledger. It is cold, extremely dull, and the single most respectable thing a foreigner can do on the Kell. It will also change how you see everything else there.

6. **Attend a pin inspection.** Every structure is bolted to the mountain with pins driven eight metres deep and the pins are inspected quarterly. Everyone understands the inspection is not really about the pins. Go, watch, sign the sheet if invited.

## Manners

- **Never state, guess, estimate, or write a number for the summit's height.** The charter forbids assigning one; the official elevation is *pending*. Asking is the rudeness of asking a widow her husband's worth, and writing a figure down in public is closer to vandalism. If a stranger tries to draw you into speculation, they are either testing you or drunk.
- **Do not call the mountain's rising slow.** A thumb-width a year is the local unit of the enormous.
- **Guild precedence is real and touchy**, and the moth-scrapers are the touchiest of the old ones. Learn who goes first through doors before you go through any.
- **The dead are buried high**, in raw rock, deliberately, to give the stone something to have had happen to it. This is understood as the last and most generous work a person does. It is not morbid up there and you should not treat it as such.
- **The candle-alders** — eleven of them, each with a brass plate and a guild — have things left at their roots. You may leave something. Something old, with a story, and tell the story aloud once when you leave it.

## Above the Line

Do not go for pleasure. If you must go:

- **Never alone.** Two people minimum, and the second person's actual job, stated aloud before you set out, is to remember you: your name's spelling, your scar's wrist, your coat's buttons. Rehearse the list. Recite it to each other at every rest.
- **Hire a stept.** A hoofed, long-jawed, wet-felt creature that walks on unweathered stone without slipping because — the herders say, and they are not wrong — it does not entirely touch it. A stept in a bell can go into the provisional country and come back with every detail intact, and a person walking close behind it will usually do the same. *Close* means close enough to smell it. Do not overtake it. Do not take a shortcut it declines to take.
- **Count paces aloud.** Instruments will give you fractions that wander. Your legs will not lie.
- **Watch your shadow.** Below the Line shadows fall away from lamps. If yours does something else, turn round and go down. Do not investigate. Do not photograph it. Go down.
- **Do not trust a route because you used it yesterday.** A path that was there on Tuesday can be a blank face on Wednesday, and there will be no rubble, because nothing fell; the path was simply not sufficiently the case. Route-find fresh every time. Trust the stept over your memory.
- **Do not attempt the summit.** No guide will take you and the ones who offer are the ones to avoid. The highest rock is the newest rock.
- **Come down before you are tired.** Attrition is not an event; it is arithmetic. Hours above the Line are the currency, and everyone up there is spending them for wages. You are spending them for the view.

## Leaving, and afterwards

Before you descend, obtain from the Registry a **certified extract** of your own entry and of anything written about you, including the stall-keepers' incidental mentions if you can get them. That paper is the record of the person who was there. It is also the only thing you will be able to put beside your home depositions.

On return, before you speak to anyone, break the seals on your three copies and check yourself, item by item, coldly, with a witness present. Most travellers find something. Coat buttons, a middle name off by a letter, a scar migrated across. The correct response is not panic and not correction; it is **documentation**. Write down what changed, date it, sign it, have it witnessed, and file it with the rest. You now have a history of the alteration, which is worth more than the alteration cost you.

And for a year or so afterwards you will find that when a room goes quiet you are waiting for something. Every few minutes, patient, unhurried, coming up through the floor. It isn't there. That is worth knowing in advance too.
