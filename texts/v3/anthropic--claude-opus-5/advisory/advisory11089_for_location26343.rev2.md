# Going Up to The Hour: A Traveller's Preparation

## First, understand what you are preparing *for*

You are not preparing for a hostile environment. The Hour will not attack you. It has no weather, no predators, no rockfall, no river to drown in. By every metric a mountaineer uses, it is the safest high place in any world.

You are preparing for a place where **nothing accumulates** — and you are a creature made entirely of accumulation. Digestion, healing, fatigue, memory, boredom, love, the slow silting-up of a life. You are a process visiting a conclusion.

Everything below follows from that one sentence.

---

## Months out

**Arrive finished.** The plateau holds you in whatever condition you carry across the Lip. It will not make you worse. It will also, absolutely and without appeal, not make you better. Therefore:

- **Dental work completed and settled.** No provisionals, no recent extractions, no unfilled cavity. The hum of the place conducts through bone; travellers with bad teeth describe the second day as a drill held to the jaw and never switched off. Some carry a soft rubber bite-block. It helps a little.
- **No open wounds, no infections, no fresh sutures, no courses of antibiotics in progress.** A course of treatment is a process. Processes do not run up there. You will come down having taken nine days of pills to no effect, and the infection will resume exactly where it paused, only now nine days deeper into your schedule and none deeper into the cure.
- **Acclimatise before you go up, and do not expect to acclimatise there.** This is the single most-commonly-fatal misunderstanding. The plateau sits above a cloud lid at something like five and a half thousand metres; your body would ordinarily answer that over ten to fourteen days with more red cells, changed breathing, a rebuilt blood chemistry. That is an adaptation. Adaptations are changes. They do not take. Whatever your haematocrit is when your boot crosses the Lip is your haematocrit for the whole visit. Spend three weeks in the staging camps below the cloud, doing the work, and cross only when a physician says you are as good as you will get. People who "plan to acclimatise on the plateau" are the ones the herders carry down, still breathing, still exactly as hypoxic as the moment they arrived, and mildly puzzled about it.
- **Get fat, a little.** Opinions differ and the herders think it is superstition. Do it anyway. See *The bill*, below.

**Sign the retrieval contract, and choose people who will not listen to you.**

On or about the eighth day you will decide to stay. This is not a possession, a madness, or a seduction. It is worse than that: it is a perfectly clear, calm, well-reasoned conclusion that will seem to you the sanest thought you have ever had, and you will be able to defend it eloquently, and your defence will be good. You cannot out-argue it in the moment. You can only arrange, in advance, that your opinion on the eighth day carries no weight.

So: a written instrument, signed below the Lip, witnessed, stating that your consent is given *now* and cannot be revoked *there*; naming the hour of extraction; and authorising your guides to bind and carry you. Pay the forfeit-deposit. Choose guides with a reputation for deafness. If your party includes anyone who says "but surely if a person freely chooses—", leave them at the staging camp. They are not a traveller. They are a casualty who hasn't gone up yet.

## Your party

**Go as three, or five. Never two, never alone.**

Never alone is obvious. Never two is less so: on the plateau, disagreements about what happened cannot be resolved by evidence, because the evidence is a stone you moved which you are now certain was always there. You need a third person to break ties about your own past. Odd numbers.

**Choose noisy, irritating, sequential people.** The ideal companion talks too much, fidgets, tells long stories with a middle and an end, has a project, is petty about the rations, and keeps score. Contemplatives, mystics, the recently bereaved, and the constitutionally serene are the highest-risk category by a wide margin. The plateau does not take the weak. It takes the ones who were already halfway to it.

**No pack animals. No dogs.** Mules stop. It takes a mule about eleven hours. Dogs are worse — they stop facing along the seams, in that alert forward posture, and there is nothing in this or any world quite as bad as trying to pull a warm, pliant, unblinking dog off the pan while it looks patiently down the shadow-line at something. Your transport is human porters, which is why expeditions to The Hour are short, light, and expensive, and why the ancient works up there are all small — needles, awls, a comb, a rope. Except the wall. The wall was built by people who had stopped needing to carry anything.

## Kit, and the reasons for it

**Water: all of it, on your back.** Fire does not consume. A candle lit on the plateau will burn, give light, and be exactly as long as it was a week later; a stove will roar and a pot on it will not boil; frost in the seams will not melt in your hand, in your pot, or under your tongue. There is no water on The Hour. There is no *making* of water on The Hour. Carry two and a half litres per person per day and drink it whether or not you want it, which you will not. This weight, not the hazard, is what limits visits to nine or ten days. It is a mercy that it does.

**Food that is already food.** Nothing requiring cooking or rehydration. Fat, sugar, salt, things you can chew. Chewing is also therapy; see *disciplines*.

**Two independent mechanical timepieces per person, plus a sand-glass for the watch.** Quartz drifts strangely up there. Water-clocks and candle-clocks simply do not work. A sealed spring chronometer works well enough, and the *divergence* between your three clocks is itself your best instrument: when they begin to disagree, you are deep in; when they agree to the second, day after day, you are further in than that, and you should already be walking toward the Lip.

**Eye protection, doubled.** Glacier goggles with full side shields, and a spare pair, because a broken pair will never be mended and you will never be able to improvise a repair that *takes*. The light is thin, hard, and unrelenting from one fixed bearing, and it comes up at you as well, off the salt of the Pan and off the white lid of cloud below the horizon. Under your hat brim, from underneath. Nobody expects that one.

**Zinc, and a schedule for turning around.** You are standing in a ten-o'clock sun that does not move for nine days. You will not burn evenly; you will burn *vectorially*. Travellers come down with the left ear, the left of the nose, the left jaw and the back of one hand cooked to blisters and the rest of them the colour of paper. Apply on a clock, not on a feeling, and deliberately reverse your facing every half hour even when there is no reason to.

**Clothing for a body with two climates.** No wind means no chill and no cooling. Your sun side overheats while walking; your shade side is against a cold that has been held at that exact temperature since before your species. Full-zip, vented, quickly opened and closed with gloves on. You will be doing this constantly.

**A telltale on every pack.** A light streamer on a whippy wand, a metre above the shoulder, in a colour that exists nowhere on the plateau — magenta, hot orange. In a world where nothing moves, your eye stops registering your companions within a day; they become terrain. The telltale flicks when its wearer walks. **If you see a telltale hanging still for more than a count of sixty, you go to that person immediately, and you do not call out, because your voice stops at ten metres anyway.**

**A knotted cord and a spring balance.** See *counting*.

**Paper, ink, and a letter from yourself.** Written before you go up. It says who you are, where you are from, the name of someone waiting, the date you entered, the date you leave, and the sentence *You are not from here.* You will read it aloud at every meal. By the sixth day this will feel like reading a stranger's mail. Read it anyway. Ink does not fade up there, which is the one place the plateau's stubbornness is on your side.

**Leave your return-anchor below the Lip.** Whatever device, token, or arrangement returns you to your own world: it stays in the staging camp, in the keeping of someone who never goes up. Gates, charges, and coordinates are all processes or memories, and processes stall and memories erode. The only reliable way home is to walk to a place where time works and be handed it by a person who has been living in time the whole while.

## The disciplines

These are not suggestions. On The Hour, the difference between a traveller and a landmark is entirely a matter of procedure.

**1. Eat and drink by the clock.** By the third day you will not be hungry. This does not mean you are not starving; it means the *signal* has stopped, the way the blink has stopped, the way the frost has stopped subliming. Assume the underlying process continues and is simply not being reported. Assume the bill.

**2. Sleep by the clock, masked, and always with a watch set.** You will not become tired. Sleep is the hardest discipline on the plateau because there is nothing to enforce it and no darkness to suggest it. Enforce it: eye mask, a set number of hours, and one person awake and moving. Never sleep facing along a seam. Nobody can tell you exactly why; everyone who has been up there agrees about it.

**3. Do not count. Measure.** Rule three is broader than the herds. Anything discrete resists tallying — paces, rations, days, birds, the scratches on the boulder, the members of your own party. But *continuous* quantities behave. So convert every count into a weight or a length: tie a knot in a cord every hundred paces and measure the cord; drop a pebble in a bag per ration and weigh the bag on a spring balance; measure the remaining water by depth, not by counting bottles. This is the great trick of the trade and it is worth the price of the guide by itself.

**4. Walk across the seams, not along them.** Take your routes obliquely. When you rest, stand crooked, at some ridiculous angle to the shadow-lines. It looks like nonsense. It is the most effective prophylactic known.

**5. Keep a habit that requires a future.** Knit something. Carve something. Run a long argument with a companion over several days. Tell a story in instalments. The plateau is not a place of endings; it is a place with no endings and no beginnings, and a mind that has nothing outstanding is a mind that has nothing to pull it off the pan. Go up owing somebody a chapter.

**6. Take each other's blink rate, hourly, aloud.** This is the vital sign. Ten or twelve a minute is a person. Three is a person who leaves tomorrow morning. One is a person who is carried. Your counts will disagree — of course they will — and the pattern of the disagreement is half the reading.

## The ladder

Herders' guides recognise a rough progression. Learn it and watch for it in others, because you will not see it in yourself; that is the nature of it.

1. Your footsteps arrive muffled and late. *(Everyone, within the hour. Not a symptom.)*
2. Hunger stops. *(Day three, reliably.)*
3. Your breath stops showing in the shaded air.
4. A cut neither hurts nor heals; you forget which hand it was on.
5. Sweat sits on the skin and does not go anywhere.
6. Dust settles on you and does not shake out of the wool.
7. **Your boot stops denting the insect felt.** This is the line. Check it daily by looking back at your own tracks. When the crust ceases to take your print, you are no longer entirely a visitor, and the guides will no longer negotiate.
8. You face along the seams and it is comfortable.

Beyond eight there is a ninth step which is not on the herders' list, because it is only visible from outside and over centuries: your shadow begins to cut.

**Hard limits in common use:** three days for the curious, five for the experienced, seven absolute. Nobody credible offers nine.

## The one crossing

Rule four is the reason route planning is the entire intellectual labour of the expedition, and it must be done below, on paper, with a ruler.

The plateau owns exactly one crossing and lends it out. If you go in at the east and come out at the west, and then any other party (or you, later) tries a different line, that second crossing discovers itself to have been the first — same cairns, same three-legged hare, same order of stumps against the cloud lid. Two parties entering simultaneously from opposite sides meet in the middle and find, with a politeness that becomes hysteria, that they are one party.

**Therefore: do not cross.** Go in and come out at the same notch of the Lip. An out-and-back is not a crossing and the plateau appears to accept the distinction. This means your route must be a single thread — one polyline, planned in advance, touching everything you came for and returning along itself. You get one line through the whole country. Choose it carefully, choose it at home, and *do not improvise*, because an improvisation up there will feel like remembering.

The usual thread: in at the eastern notch; the low Nailed, where you may take a bird's foot between your fingers; along the flank of the middle stump, where the four are; down to the Pan; the wall, walked on its shaded side; the vertebrae, and the swept floor; the tally boulder at the head of the Pan; and back. Nine days, done honestly.

## Conduct

**Nothing you do up there can be undone.** This is the whole of the ethics. It is the only place in any world where that is literally true.

- **Do not wipe the frost.** The bare patch stays bare forever. It is vandalism on a scale you cannot feel and cannot repair.
- **Do not move stones.** If you must, you have created an orphaned seam, cut into rock by a shadow that no longer exists, and it will outlast your language.
- **Do not add to the tallies.** Eleven thousand scratches in eight hands, counting something. You do not know what. Your five strokes will be there when the sun finally moves.
- **Do not touch the four on the eastern slope.** In particular do not attempt to help the one who is turning; do not attempt to complete the turn for her; and — this is a real and recurring problem, and guides will abandon a client for it — **do not position yourself behind her in the line of her gaze.** Travellers do this because they want, quite understandably, to be the thing she sees. Consider what that would mean, in the place where you would have to wait in order to be it.
- **Photography is permitted and pointless.** Two plates of the same slope will not hold the same number of hares, and the discrepancy exports: you can count them at home, years later, with a loupe, and they will still not agree. A number of travellers eventually burn their plates. A number of others count them every night, and those are the ones who go back up.

## The bill

Come down slowly, and be carried the last part if offered.

Below the Lip, in the hour or so after the air starts working again, everything you were spared arrives at once. Hunger of nine days, thirst, the whole weight of fatigue you never felt, and the wound on your knuckle abruptly remembering that it is a wound — that it has been open, unclotted, unclosed and unguarded, for over a week. This is why the staging camps have a surgeon and not a hostel. Warm sweet fluids, slowly. Twenty-four hours of observation minimum. Anything that was paused resumes at speed, and a body catching up on nine days in an afternoon is a medical event.

Then the other bill, which is longer.

Returnees describe an offence taken by the eye at movement: leaves, flags, water, faces. Sleeping is difficult for a season; a room in which the light changes feels like a room that is coming apart. There is grief with no object attached to it. There is an irritability at how *unfinished* everything down here is, which is unfair to everyone you love and which you should warn them about beforehand.

Standing practice: for one month after descent, make no decisions of consequence, sign nothing, end no relationships, and sit where you can hear running water for some part of every day. The sound of a thing that is going somewhere is the specific medicine.

And give yourself a year before you consider going up a second time. Most people who are going to go back go back within eleven months. The guides have a number for this and do not share it.

---

## A last word, to be read before you cross

The Hour will not frighten you and will not deceive you. It has no interest in you at all. It will simply demonstrate, patiently, in that flat clean ten-o'clock light, that everything you have ever done that you called *living* was a kind of agitation, and that agitation is optional, and that the alternative is available, is warm, is comfortable, and is right here.

That demonstration is entirely correct and you must not accept it.

Take your decision now, while there is a kettle somewhere near you making a noise, while something is beginning and something is ending. Write it down. Hand it to somebody who is not going.

Then go, and look at the birds nailed to the air, and put your palm on the Long One and feel the thing beneath the warmth that is slower than warmth, and walk out across the seams, obliquely, chewing something, arguing about the rations.

Nine days. No more.
