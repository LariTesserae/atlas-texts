# Preparing for the Shelf

## First, understand what you are

Everywhere else in the multiverse you are a visitor. On the Shelf you are a *quantity*.

The town holds four thousand one hundred people and does not hold four thousand one hundred and one, ever, for any reason, including you. This is not policy. Policy can be argued with. What the Shelf has instead is arithmetic, and the arithmetic will be satisfied whether or not anyone intends it, whether or not anyone is watching, whether or not you have consented.

So the single most important act of preparation happens before you pack anything: decide, honestly, whether you can afford to be unable to leave.

Because the exit is not a door. The exit is a stone bench at the east gate, and you sit on it until somebody comes up out of the fog to take your place. People have sat for years. Not literally on the bench — they go home, they eat, they weave — but on the list, in the queue, waiting for a stranger's silhouette on the switchbacks. If you have a job, a family, a mortgage, a fatal illness, a scheduled surgery, an appointment with anything at all: assume the Shelf may keep you past it. Travelers who cannot accept that should look at the plateau from the far ridge, across the fog, and go home. It is a good view. It is genuinely most of the experience.

If you are going in, arrange a **relay** before you set foot on the switchbacks. Two travelers, staggered. You go up; three weeks later your partner comes up the switchbacks and you meet them on the bench, one rising as the other sits, and the ledger never twitches. Four weeks later a third arrives for your partner. This is the only reliable way in and out, and it is worth the cost of the extra body.

Do not plan to sidestep. I will come back to this, but say it now: **do not plan to leave the Shelf laterally, by whatever means you came into this multiverse.** Not from the plateau. Not from the city. The Even has never yet failed to balance, and if you subtract yourself without an offset, the balance will be found elsewhere, in something that breathes.

## Your body

**Altitude.** Thin, cool, dry-feeling despite the constant water. Acclimatize for a minimum of four days somewhere comparable before the ascent, and expect the switchbacks — several hours, wet rock, rising out of fog into hard unfiltered light — to be the hardest physical work of the trip. Bring your own headache remedy; there is none up there worth the name.

**Temperature.** Cellar-cool, always. Never freezing, never warm. This is a specific and underrated misery: nothing ever dries fast, nothing ever fully warms, and you will be very slightly cold for the entire visit in a way that becomes a mood rather than a sensation. Undyed wool, in layers, is not costume compliance — it is the correct answer. Lanolin-heavy, unwashed, greasy wool. Bring more socks than you think, in horn-buttoned wool, and a wool cap you can sleep in.

**The tilt.** Two degrees northwest sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. Your inner ear will spend three days arguing with your eyes, and the argument produces nausea, a mild persistent headache behind the left eye, and a distinctive clumsiness on stairs. Prepare by living for a week on a floor you have deliberately shimmed to two degrees — sleep on it, cook on it, carry things across it. You will feel foolish. You will be grateful.

Corollaries: nothing round stays where you put it. Anything you drop travels northwest. Every cup you set down is very slightly wrong. And when you come home, the readaptation is worse than the adaptation — expect a week of feeling that every level floor is tipping the *other* way. Do not drive.

**Cuts.** The stone fractures like glass and the fragments have never been worn. Scree that lies exactly as it fell is beautiful, stable underfoot, and will open your palm to the bone if you catch yourself on it. Thick leather gloves. Long sleeves. Trousers, not shorts, regardless of exertion. Take a real wound kit: antiseptic, closures, non-adherent dressings, and enough of it to treat two long lacerations, because you will be treating them yourself. There is no infirmary in your sense of the word, and the fine chalky mineral film gets into everything open.

**Water.** Safe, cold, and heavily mineral. Over a stay of months it is a kidney-stone diet. Filter and, more importantly, drink more than you want to, and take citrate if you have a history.

## Kit: the law of materials

Here is the rule that will govern your packing, and it is stricter than any customs regime you have met:

**On the Shelf, nothing wears away. Anything you leave, you have left forever.**

Not "for a long time." Forever. There is no soil to swallow it, no rain to rot it, no midden to bury it. A dropped button is a permanent feature of the world, and — this is the part travelers fail to internalize — it is also an *addition*, and additions are answered. Somewhere a tally will be found to have shifted, and the Count will notice, because noticing is what the Count is for.

Practical consequences:

- **No plastics. None.** No zips, no buckles, no synthetic fleece that sheds microfibre, no wrapper, no lens cap, no toothbrush. Substitute horn, bone, wood, cord, boiled leather, fired clay, waxed cloth.
- **No rubber soles.** Vibram crumbs are a form of littering that lasts until the end of the world. Boots with stitched leather or horn soles, and carry a repair kit, and pick up any tack you drop.
- **Nothing you cannot eat, burn, or carry out.** Write this on the inside of your pack. Everything else is contraband.
- **Count your gear before entry, in writing, and count it again before you sit on the bench.** If the numbers differ, go and find the difference. Yes, actually go and find it.

**The knife problem.** There is no iron on the Shelf, and the count of sharp knives in the city is constant. Bring a keen steel blade and you have become the four thousand one hundred and first sharp knife — and within the day, somewhere in the lower quarter, a woman's good knife will be found to have gone dull, and she will not be able to sharpen it back, because the total holds. You will have taken a working tool out of a stranger's hand by walking through a gate.

The customary solution among experienced travelers is to carry your blade *dull* — deliberately blunted before ascent — and to sharpen it only in the fog below the rim if you need it at all. Some carry a knife in two pieces, blade and handle separate, assembled nowhere. It is ridiculous. Do it anyway.

**Recording.** Cameras behave poorly here and I have never met anyone who could explain it well. The usual report is that the frames come back correct but *identical* — the same light, the same shadow-length, the same hawk in the same posture — whatever the actual interval. Assume you will not photograph the Shelf. Bring charcoal and a slate you can wipe. Do not bring ink and do not take notes in front of the Count: ink implies revision, revision implies error, and error is not a category they enjoy having near them. Write your journal at night, privately, and understand that this is a place best carried out in memory, which is exactly what it wants.

**A moth veil.** At dusk the air becomes granular. Fine mesh, wool or silk, over hat brim and tucked into the collar. Keep your mouth closed. Do not swat — every pale moth in the joints is on somebody's cord.

## Arrival

Come up the switchbacks. Come the long, wet, stupid way, on foot, out of the fog. This matters more than any other single choice you will make.

A traveler who arrives properly is a *gift*: your body releases a person who has been waiting to go down. There will be a keeper at the gate and a stone bench, and someone will be sitting on it, and when you sit, they rise. The handover is silent and takes perhaps four seconds and it is the most consequential thing that happens to you on the Shelf.

Etiquette for that moment, learned the hard way:

- Sit. Do not hover, do not defer, do not say "after you." The bench is a scale. Put your weight on it.
- Look at them. They may have waited eleven years for you. Some weep. Some cannot look back. One or two will hate you briefly for having taken so long, which is not rational and does not need answering.
- Do not apologize, and above all do not say you might not stay long. To announce a departure before you have unpacked is to announce that you intend to trap someone else in the queue behind you. It is the rudest thing you can do in that city and they will not tell you so.

A traveler who arrives *any other way* — who steps in laterally, onto the plateau, uncounted — has a different and much worse experience. The reports are inconsistent and all unpleasant. Some travelers found they could not be registered: not exactly invisible, but unaddressable, walked around, not offered bread, spoken past, as though they had arrived at the wrong angle to be a person. Others found that within a day something died to make room for them. I do not know which of these is true and I would not experiment. Walk up the switchbacks.

## Conduct on the plateau

**Do not count out loud, and do not count wrong.** You may be told numbers with great precision. Do not correct them, do not check them, and do not offer the observation that you saw forty-two bell-stalks in a ring of forty. You may have miscounted. If you have not, the sentence you are about to say is a very serious sentence and you are not the person who should say it.

**Do not give gifts.** Generosity here is an act of vandalism. If you must express gratitude, express it in labour — carry water, split wood, help the masons with their cords — because labour is consumed and leaves nothing behind.

**Eat what you are given, and eat lightly.** Food comes off fixed terraces with a fixed yield, and your portion is somebody's portion. Carry your own supplement calories in edible, wrapper-free form — hard cheese, pressed fat, oat cake in a cloth — and eat them out of sight, because eating your own food at a host's table is an insult in most worlds and a mathematical statement in this one.

**Do not harm any animal, at all, ever.** The hawk on the wall three feet from your elbow is on a knotted cord in a room in the middle quarter. So is the hare asleep in the doorway of the empty house. If you kill one — by carelessness, by a stone kicked loose on the scree — the Count will know the number is wrong before nightfall, and the town will want to know why.

**The northern quarter is not abandoned.** Forty-one swept houses with cold hearths are not an invitation. Children play there; you may walk there; do not sleep there, do not light a hearth there, and do not move anything.

**Do not touch the bell-stalks.** They are faintly warm. That is all I will say. Everyone who visits wants to go and stand inside the ring, and the empty ground inside the ring is empty for a reason nobody has offered me.

**Do not drop anything over the rim,** including things you want to be rid of. The Spill goes over and does not land. That is not a joke about the fog. Nothing that goes over the bevel has ever been accounted for, and an unaccounted thing is precisely the wrong kind of thing to create here.

## What not to explain to them

You will be asked where you come from, and you will want to be honest and interesting, and there is one subject you should hold back: **erosion.**

Do not tell them about mountains that slump. Do not describe soil, or talus, or a river that eats a canyon, or a city built on the crushed bones of four older cities, or a shoreline that moved. Do not tell them about compound interest, or population growth, or landfill, or extinction. You will think you are describing geology. What they will hear is a world in which the ledger is open and nobody is keeping it — a place where things simply pour away and no one counts the loss — and the reaction runs from polite disbelief to something much quieter and worse. I have watched a member of the Count listen to a description of a ruin, and then go and sit down, and not speak for the rest of the evening.

If pressed, say your country is low and wet and confusing. Nobody will doubt it.

## What to actually do with your days

Three things are worth the whole journey, and none of them can be hurried.

**The dawn film.** Get up cold, before light, and lie down on bare pavement with your face a hand's width from the stone, and wait. You will watch the mineral sweat come out of the rock and settle back into exactly the hollows the previous day took it from. It takes about forty minutes and it is the only place I know where you can watch a world refuse to lose. Take a mat of folded wool, because the cold comes up through you like a claim.

**The masons.** Ask to walk with them. Twelve people with knotted cords, going round the city measuring walls that are where they were, recording nothing, and finding — every time, without exception, generation after generation — that everything holds. It is the dullest job in the multiverse and watching it done with total seriousness by grown adults will rearrange something in you.

**The rim at dusk.** Level fog below like standing water, the far ridge unrounded, long precise shadows off every bell-stalk ring, the moths coming up out of the joints, the gutters, always the gutters. Stay until full dark. Bring an extra layer; you will not want to move.

## Departure, and the failure modes

Go to the bench when your relay is due. Sit. Rise when they sit.

If your relay does not come — and sometimes they do not, for reasons that have nothing to do with the Shelf — you will be tempted to step sideways out of the world from where you stand. Understand what you would be doing. You would be removing one unit from a total that has never varied, and the Shelf has no mechanism for grief and no mechanism for exceptions, only for balance. Travelers who have done this report, afterward, that they got out cleanly. They also report that they have no way of knowing what closed behind them, and that this has not become easier to live with.

If you must go without a replacement, go down the switchbacks first, into the fog, below the bevel, off the plateau and off the ledger, and step out from *there*. It costs a day of hard descent and it is the difference between leaving and being subtracted.

## Aftercare

Three things will happen to you when you get home, and knowing them in advance helps.

**The gutters.** The soft plural trickling becomes the baseline of your hearing within an hour and it is unbearable to remember afterward. Returnees describe an actual grief for a sound. Many cannot sleep in silence, or in mortared houses, for months. A running tap does not work; it is the wrong number of streams. Some people rig a shallow gravel channel in a courtyard. It helps a little and looks insane.

**The lean.** Give it a week.
