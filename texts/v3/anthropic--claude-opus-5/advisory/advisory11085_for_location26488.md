# Preparing for the Pan

## First, calibrate what kind of trip this is

This is not a wilderness trip and it is not a city trip. It is a trip into a working industrial site that is also a single living organism that is also a town of a hundred and thirty people who have been trained since infancy not to startle it. You are the loudest thing that will happen to them this year.

Almost everything in the preparation follows from one number: a slammed door costs forty minutes of pressure. Pressure is the town's only power. You are not being asked to be quiet out of respect. You are being asked not to reach into the cash drawer.

The second thing to internalise, because it will save you a lot of confused misery: the plant's trigger is *sharpness*, not volume in the abstract. A conversation at the level you'd use across a kitchen table is, technically, below threshold. An impact is not. A cough is not. A bark of laughter is not. A dropped spoon on healed callus is a rifle shot. This means the Pan will feel, at first, like a place where you are allowed to talk and where talking is nevertheless obscene — because after four hundred years the economics have gone into the bones and become manners. Speak, if you must, at the volume you'd use beside a sleeping baby. Everyone will still hear you from thirty metres.

## When to go, and for how long

Go in late summer or early autumn if you want to be able to feel your fingers. Go in November if you want the thing that people travel for: four in the afternoon, the lamps small and orange up in the nodes, the columns going pewter into standing fog, the drip. It is one of the great sights of any world I know of, and it is also six hours of usable light and a bone-damp cold at 60 m that gets into the joints and stays.

Avoid winter unless you have a reason. Fog freezes on the flutes and comes off them later in slabs, and there is no shout to warn you.

**The Flowering.** Find out which year it is before you commit. The nine-year flowering is spectacular and it is also the town's lean week: eleven days of dark, candles, thousands of moths, everything smelling of rotten honey, and the Works stopped dead with the Turn's beam hanging mid-stroke. During it you are an extra mouth in a household with the lamps out. If you go, go with food and light for yourself and a firm plan to be no trouble. If you can't manage that, go in a non-flowering year and accept that you will only ever hear the songs described.

**Length.** You can cross the basin in nine minutes. You can see all of it in a day. You will not *hear* it in less than four: it takes about that long for your ears to open, and the moment they do is the whole trip. I'd advise six to nine days — enough for several dawn surges, a full fog-and-drip cycle, and one deer, if you're lucky.

## Arrive outside the rim

Whatever your method of arrival, arrive on the gravel, outside the horseshoe, in daylight, well after the dawn surge — mid-morning is correct. Do not arrive inside the Pan. Do not arrive at the leading edge in the northwest, where the pale soft-walled stems are being leaned by grafters and where your bootprint in undepleted marl is a real injury. Walk in on the rim path like a person.

At the rim you will be given a card with four things you must not do, and from that moment you will be watched, continuously, by an assigned person. Learn their name-sign in the first hour. Do not resent the watching; the watcher is liable for you.

## Six months out: prepare the body, not the luggage

This is the part travellers get wrong. Your kit can be fixed in an afternoon. You cannot.

**Respiratory quarantine.** A cough is not a social lapse in the Pan, it is an industrial accident with a repeat function. If you arrive with a productive cough you will either be turned back at the rim or housed in the dry ruins away from the working stems. Beyond your own convenience: this is a closed community of a hundred and thirty. Do the full protocol — isolation before departure, current immunisations, and a hard personal rule that if you develop a chest infection *en route* you cancel. Bring suppressants, and bring a **sneeze muff**: a dense felt bag, about the size of two fists, that you press your whole face into. Experienced visitors carry it clipped at the chest, not in a pocket. Practise the tongue-to-palate trick and the philtrum pinch until they're reflex.

**Snoring.** Test yourself honestly, with a recording, for a week. A snorer cannot sleep in a dwelling node and will be quartered in the silvered dead stems among the sap-cell racks, which is cold and eerie and much further from the privy. If you have apnoea, bring your device and be aware that most such machines hiss and click and may not be permitted; ask in advance, in writing, before you travel.

**The laugh drill.** The Company's oldest surviving document is a fine levied against a man for laughing. Laughter is the single hardest reflex to hold, because the quiet is *funny* — the felt shoes, the two hundred and eleven solemn hand signs, the most powerful engine in the basin creeping round at the speed of a minute hand. Spend a month practising the conversion: the bark becomes a closed-mouth shudder through the nose, shoulders only. Learn it before you need it. You will need it on day one.

**The startle drill.** Something will drop, somewhere, and someone will make a face, and you will gasp. Practise absorbing shocks without vocalising: cold water, sudden lights, a friend clapping behind you. Aim for the reflex to arrive in the diaphragm and stop there.

**The gut.** I am entirely serious. In a room this quiet, and in a low-ceilinged grown dwelling with three other people, your digestion is public. Go bland for four days before arrival and stay bland: no legumes, no carbonation, no dairy if you're marginal. Ask what the local food is and whether it agrees with outsiders. Also: nothing crunchy. Chewing an apple in the Pan is an event.

**Feet and gait.** Learn to walk without hurry, heel soft, no scuff. The marl squeaks chalkily and that squeak is permitted background; your bootheel is not. Practise stairs without weight-shifting slap. Practise standing still for twenty minutes, which is most of what you'll do.

## The silent audit of your kit

Lay everything out on a stone floor and drop it. Anything that rings, clicks, rattles, crinkles, or squeals gets fixed or left behind.

- **No hard soles.** Bring felt overboots and two spare pairs of thick felt socks; assume you'll be given felt at the rim but don't rely on it fitting.
- **No metal-on-metal.** Tape or felt every buckle, every zip pull, every carabiner. Better: replace fasteners with cloth ties, horn or wood buttons. A buckle that scrapes a stem is a *wound*, which by Rule 2 must be lacquered before dark, which is someone's evening.
- **No plastic shell.** A modern rain jacket is one of the loudest objects in that basin. Waxed cotton, oiled wool, felt.
- **No hook-and-loop fastening of any kind.** Ever.
- **Lanyard everything.** Every object you will hold gets a cord to your wrist or your chest. Pockets get flaps, not open mouths; no loose coins, no keys.
- **Sleeping mat:** closed-cell foam, not inflatable — valves squeak and the surface crackles. Wool blanket over it.
- **Eating kit:** wooden bowl, wooden spoon, wooden cup. No metal, no ceramic.
- **Notebook:** soft cloth-backed, laid paper, *not* glossy or coated — coated paper rustles like fire. Soft graphite pencil, sharpened before you go and again in the ruins where the noise doesn't matter. A small wax tablet and stylus is better still and is what the locals use for quick exchange.
- **Watch:** mechanical is fine; a tick is beneath threshold and the Turn ticks anyway. **No alarms, no chimes, no hourly signal.** Disable them physically, not by menu.
- **Devices:** if you bring anything electronic, remove the power source when not in use. Not "silent mode." Removed. One traveller's forgotten notification cost a shift, and it is still in the ledger.
- **Camera:** electronic shutter only, no mirror, no beeps, no autofocus chirp. Felt caps on tripod feet, or a beanbag. Expect long exposures; expect to be asked not to.
- **Hearing aids:** the feedback squeal is precisely the sound the stems clamp on. Run them low, carry spare batteries and a hard-shell case, and warn your watcher in advance so someone knows what happened when the valves shut.
- **No dog. No instrument. No whistling.** Humming on the breath, low, closed-mouth, is tolerated; it is what they do themselves during the Flowering.

## Cold, damp, and the drip

The Pan is *damp, not wet*, and that will lull you. Everything you own will slowly reach 100% humidity and then stay there. Bring:

- A brimmed felt hat or a hood you can live in, because it drips indoors, outdoors, and for an hour after the sky has stopped.
- Wool in layers, and one genuinely dry set sealed in an oiled bag that you do not open until you are leaving. Have a designated dry sanctuary — a bag, not a garment.
- Anti-fog for spectacles, or wear contacts. You will spend a lot of time looking up through fog at lamps, and you will spend more of it wiping your lenses than you expect. (If you wear glasses: there is a pair in a node wall back in the ruins, in profile, perfectly clear through the amber. You will want to look at it for a long time. Everyone does.)
- A small brush for silica fibre dust if you spend time near the carder, and a felt half-mask. The dust is fine and irritating and the lacquer pot fumes — iodine and cut grass — are pleasant and not good for you in volume.

**Water:** you drink the lens.
