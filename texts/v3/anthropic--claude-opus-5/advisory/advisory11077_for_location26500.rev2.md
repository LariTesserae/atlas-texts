# Preparing for the Shelf

## First, understand what you are actually agreeing to

You are not visiting a wet place. You are visiting a world where *dry* is not a condition that exists, has ever existed, or can be produced by any means available. Everything else follows from that.

Most travellers who come back damaged did not come back cold-damaged or lost. They came back because their skin failed. Permanent immersion is not a hardship you push through; it is a clock. From the moment you step in, your body begins a slow, absolutely predictable disassembly, and your preparation is not about preventing it — you cannot — but about knowing its timetable and leaving before the irreversible part.

**The honest schedule for an ordinary unmodified human:**

- **Days 1–3:** discomfort, clumsiness, cold-shock adaptation, sleep collapse.
- **Days 4–10:** the good window. You can walk, you can talk, your feet have learned the domes. This is the visit.
- **Days 10–20:** maceration of soles, groin, armpits and hands. Fissures. Fungal bloom. Everything small becomes slow. Nothing heals.
- **Beyond three weeks:** non-freezing cold injury. Nerve damage in the feet that does not fully reverse. Chronic infection with no way to dry a wound.

**Plan for eight days. Budget for twelve. Anything longer is a different kind of decision and should be made by a different kind of person.**

## Conditioning: begin twelve weeks out

**Cold-water habituation.** Daily immersion, 10–12°C, starting at three minutes and building to forty-five. This is not toughening for its own sake — it blunts the cold-shock gasp reflex, which is what kills people who slip on a rounded coping and go face-first into a basin. It also teaches your peripheral circulation to stop panicking, which is the difference between functional hands and useless ones.

**Wading, not walking.** Loaded walking on land prepares nothing. Waist-deep wading through resistance — the black hair forests genuinely feel like pushing into hanging cloth — destroys hip flexors and adductors in a way that surprises even fit people. Find a weed-choked river or a pool and wade laps with a towed load until your hips stop complaining.

**Barefoot proprioception on wet rounded stone.** The Shelf's gait is dome–groove–dome, a rolling motion, and the locals are right that the feet learn it before the mind. Practice on a cobbled ford, on wet river boulders, on anything domed and slick. Learn to hook a toe in a groove and trust it. Learn to fall sideways rather than forward.

**Face immersion.** You will need to lie face-down in near-freezing shallows if you want to touch the carvings. Practice cold face immersion with controlled exhalation until it is boring.

**Silence tolerance.** A week in a cave, or a series of long float-tank sessions. The Shelf's hiss is total and even and after three days it stops registering as sound and becomes the texture of silence. Some people find this the most beautiful thing they have ever experienced. Some people find their inner monologue gets very loud very fast. Find out which one you are somewhere you can leave.

**Shave your head.** All of it. Your hair never dries, and hair that never dries becomes weight, cold, and fungus. Shave your face too. A beard is a maceration trap and a seal-breaker.

**Cut your nails very short and file them round.** A torn nail underwater in a world where nothing heals is a two-week problem.

## The four facts, and the kit that answers them

### Fact one: you will never get out of the water

Your legs are submerged for the entire visit. Your torso is mostly not. This asymmetry defines your thermal strategy: **the money goes into the legs.**

A well-fitted drysuit with heavy leg insulation and a relief zipper is the correct answer and also an ugly one — it makes you look like a machine, which will matter when you meet people. The compromise most experienced Shelf travellers settle on is a 5mm neoprene lower half under locally-shaped cane-fibre trousers, so that from the waterline up you read as a human shape.

**You will urinate constantly.** Immersion diuresis is not optional; cold water pressure on the legs drives central blood volume up and your kidneys respond enthusiastically. You will dehydrate while standing in an ocean. Without a relief system you will soak your own insulation, which is the classic way an experienced traveller becomes a cold casualty in a place they thought they understood.

### Fact two: nothing dries, including you

Bring **sheetmoss oil discipline**, or bring its analogue. The locals grease themselves with lamp oil, because it is the only water-repellent substance their world contains, and it is the only *dryness* available to anyone. Adopt this on day one. Oil your feet, your groin, your armpits, the creases behind your knees, twice daily on the platform. It is not hygiene; it is the barrier that slows the maceration clock.

**Ears matter more than you think.** Chronic saturation gives you otitis externa within a week and it is savage — a swollen ear canal in a world with no doctors and no drying. Acidified alcohol drops, every single sleep period, without exception, from the first night.

**Feet: the unresolvable trade-off.** Bare feet read the floor — groove width, dome height, the texture that tells a local which terrace she's on. Bare feet also macerate to paper and cut on the one sharp thing in the world. The compromise is a 3mm split-toe bootie thin enough to transmit texture, worn for travel, removed for reading and for inspection. **Inspect your feet twice a day in the drizzle-band, every day, no exceptions.** You will not feel a cut. The cold takes the sensation away and the water washes the blood before you see it.

### Fact three: everything you drop is gone forever

This is the rule that separates competent visitors from embarrassed ones. Drop a knife and it slides through sheetmoss into metres of black peat and vanishes in under a second, invisibly, unrecoverably, in water where seeing stops working below two metres.

**Tether everything.** Every item, every time, lanyarded to belt or harness. Your cold hands will be clumsy and you will not notice a thing leave them. Also: anything you lose is now litter in a world with no decay, resting on a floor that people read with their fingertips.

**Carry nothing; tow everything.** Buoyancy is free here. A small sealed float dragged behind you on a cane yoke is how you move a week of supplies without destroying your hips. It also fits the local technological idiom closely enough that nobody will stare.

### Fact four: there is nowhere to lie down

You need a sleep platform. Cane pallets in the drizzle-band are what the locals use; bring a compact inflatable pallet that will bridge two cane trestles. This is the single most consequential item in your pack and also the one you must under no circumstances leave behind. A thing that lets a person lie down out of the water would reorganise an entire province's life, and you are not entitled to do that on your way through.

## Light, time, and the fact that there is no day

The light cycle here is measured in **years**. There is no dawn. There is no dusk. There is no circadian cue of any kind, and an unprepared traveller's sleep disintegrates on about the fourth night, taking their judgement with it.

Bring a small warm lamp on a hard timer and run a rigid artificial day: bright-ish on waking, nothing at all for eight hours of sleep. Melatonin helps. A wrist clock you consult obsessively helps more. Treat your schedule as a piece of survival equipment, because it is.

**Light etiquette is serious.** Oil is the cost of an hour of seeing, and few hours are judged worth it. A bright white light on the Shelf is not a convenience; it is an obscenity and a miracle and possibly the single most disruptive thing you could carry. Use the lowest usable output, shade it downward at the water, never point it at a face, and never — ever — leave one behind. Also keep in mind your own night vision: one flash of white and you are blind for forty minutes in a place where blindness means the wells.

## Water, food, and the salt problem

**Do not drink down-slope of people.** The entire population lives in the water and their waste goes into the same sheet that moves at the speed of a growing fingernail. Dilution is enormous and the cold is protective, but your gut has no relationship with this world's flora. Drink from the drizzle — collect it off cane or off a sheet in the hanging band. It is effectively distilled by the fog cycle and it is the cleanest water any world offers.

Which introduces the thing almost nobody anticipates:

**There is no sodium here.** Distilled drizzle, a diet of potassium-rich weed, no animals, no sea, no mineral springs. The locals are sodium-conserving in a way you are not, and you will be urinating four litres a day from immersion diuresis. **Hyponatremia is the most likely medical emergency you will face, and it presents as confusion and stumbling in exactly the situation where confusion and stumbling are fatal.** Bring electrolyte salts. Take them on schedule, not by thirst.

**Calories.** Wading through resistance at depth, in cold, burns somewhere between four and six times ordinary walking. Budget 5,000–6,000 kcal per day and bring it as fat, because nothing on the Shelf is dense. Weed is edible, mildly nutritious, and will not sustain you; eat it socially, eat your own rations privately, and do not make a display of either.

If you stay long enough to matter, you will run into the absences: no vitamin D (no sun, no liver, no fish), no B12 (no animals at all). Supplement. On a two-week visit this is theoretical. It is not theoretical for the people who live there, and if you find yourself wondering why their bones look like that, you have answered your own question.

## The wells

The only thing on the Shelf that will kill you quickly.

A perfect round mouth, ten paces across, rimmed with the only sharp edge in the world — sharp because the water going down moves fast enough to polish instead of round. The whole slow sheet of a basin drains into it. It breathes cold and it makes the one loud sound: a low pull, felt in the sternum.

**Rules:**

- Never approach one in the dark, ever, for any reason, including curiosity.
- Never approach one within ten paces of the rim at all. The water accelerates in the last stretch and you will be standing waist-deep in a medium that is suddenly no longer patient.
- Use the sound as a navigational beacon and nothing else.
- Assume they are funerary. In a world where the dead do not rot and there is no ground to put them in, the one place where things *go somewhere* is not going to be a neutral piece of plumbing. Behave near a well as you would behave in a cemetery: quietly, briefly, without instruments.

And a private warning: some travellers report a pull toward the wells that is not physical. The one loud sound in a silent world, the one edge in a world worn round, the one direction that isn't the contour. If you feel it, say so out loud to someone, and go up-slope.

## Navigation, and the problem you must solve before you leave

You cannot mark your arrival point. Anything you plant is litter and a sign; anything you drop is gone; and the floor is an endless grid of identical low domes in half-light under black weed.

Your instruments are the ones the locals use:

- **Ribbons.** The deep red trailing weed combs down-slope, more reliably than any compass. Down-slope is the only absolute direction the world has.
- **Weirs.** They run level. Walking a weir is walking a contour; you can go a very long way and end up nowhere new.
- **Grooves and domes.** Width of joint, height of dome. A woman who knows her province reads the terrace off her soles. You will not achieve this in eight days, but you can learn the specific signature of the two or three basins you're using.
- **Well-sound.** The only audible landmark. Bearing and loudness, triangulated.

**Therefore: arrive and depart within one basin of a well, and count weirs obsessively.** "Three weirs up-slope of the well that pulls, on the terrace where the grooves take two fingers" is an address. It is the only kind of address this world has. Write it on cane with a copper point, because paper is not a material here — it is a joke.

Set your return as a *time* rendezvous if your transit method permits it. Relying on finding a place is how people stay.

## The people

They will probably not be astonished by you. There is no template here for *impossible*, and the news cycle runs on decades. You will be interesting in the way a slumped coping is interesting: noted, discussed, remembered for four hundred years.

**Approach:**

- Come along a weir, in the open, slowly. Never emerge from a hair-forest; it's the only way to startle someone in a world where nothing startles.
- Announce yourself with the knock of a pole on stone. It is the known sound of a person.
- Speak quietly. There is nothing to raise your voice over, and volume here reads as damage or threat.
- Move slowly. Everything here that moves fast is water going down a well.

**Etiquette:**

- Never step on a reading-place. Someone lying face-down in the shallows with an arm under the moss is doing the only scholarship this world has. Go around, wide.
- Do not take coping stone. It is tool-stock, and every piece of it is a place where the machine has failed.
- Do not fix anything. Do not demonstrate anything. Do not offer to teach.
- Do not explain the sun. You will be tempted, because the light will be driving you mad and you will want to describe it to somebody. It is cruel and useless and it is not a gift.

**Language.** The vocabulary you need mostly exists. The vocabulary you'll reach for mostly doesn't: *dry, sky, ground, horizon, hill, sun, animal (as food), hunting*. There is no word for hunting and there never has been. When asked where you are from, say **up-slope**. It is the only true answer the language can hold. Everyone is from up-slope or down-slope. Learn to be comfortable with that being the whole of your biography.

**Gifts.** If you must — and you should think hard about whether you must — the right gift is a **copper needle**, given privately to a household, because copper hooks and needles already exist in their economy as inherited things and one more does not break anything. Salt would be received as something close to a sacrament and you should not find out what happens next. A lamp, a light, a knife of good steel, a waterproof fabric: these are not gifts. They are interventions, and they will outlive you by centuries in a world where nothing decays.

## Contamination, both directions

You are an animal walking into a world that has none. You are warm, you smell, you are covered in a biosphere of skin and gut organisms, and — most importantly — **you are a vector of rot into a place whose entire structure depends on things not rotting properly.** The peat is metres of undecayed corpses lying on cut stone because the cold and the sterility together have never permitted decomposition. You are carrying decomposers.

Probably nothing you bring survives. Probably. The protocol is the Antarctic one and it is not negotiable:

- Quarantine and screen before departure. Do not take a cold to the Shelf; its people have no immunological relationship with your respiratory viruses and there is no reason to find out what that costs.
- Do not dose yourself with prophylactic antibiotics and then shed resistant organisms into the one great communal water.
- **Pack out all solid waste.** All of it, sealed, home with you. Yes, it is unpleasant. You are walking in their drinking water.
- Bring nothing scented. Nothing dyed in bright colours — the only strong colour in this world is the red ribbon weed, and high-visibility orange is a scream.
- Leave nothing. Not a lanyard, not a wrapper, not a lens cap, not a tent peg. In a world without decay, litter is permanent by definition.

## Other hazards, briefly

**Raft-lift.** Every sixty or eighty years a basin's floor of accumulated dead weed lifts off the stone as a single black raft. It is the largest event the Shelf has. If it happens near you: get up-slope and out of the basin immediately, and stay well away from the water surface near the raft. Eighty years of anoxic peat lifting at once will belch sulphide and methane, and both are heavier than you'd like and behave badly in a still, saturated, windless band of air four metres thick. This is one of the very few Shelf hazards that can kill you in minutes.

**Weir-slump.** Where a coping has fallen, the water runs deeper — shoulder-deep in sunken courses — and the stone edges go sharp. Sharp is unnatural here. Where you feel a sharp edge with your foot, stop and reassess.

**Entanglement.** Six-metre ribbons, dense hair-forest, and a towed float on a line. Carry a tethered blade you can reach with either hand.

**Exhaustion.** You cannot sit down. There is no bank, no stone above water, nowhere to unload your legs except your own float or your platform. Plan rest stops the way a climber plans belays: in advance, by location, whether or not you feel you need them.

## Coming home

**Plan a decompression.** Dryness will genuinely hurt. Your skin will have adapted to permanent saturation and ordinary air will feel like abrasion; your feet will be tender and possibly numb in patches for weeks or permanently; your eyes will find an overcast afternoon on Earth violently, painfully bright.

Arrange: a dim humid room, gradual light exposure over four or five days, a podiatrist who has seen non-freezing cold injury, a course of antifungals started on the way out rather than when symptoms appear, and someone who will not mind that you are speaking very quietly.

The quiet lasts longest. Travellers back from the Shelf tend to keep their voices down for months, and to be startled by noise in a way that other people find odd, and to stand still sometimes in the rain.

---

## One last thing

Go for the right reason. The Shelf has no monuments to look at, no spectacle, no view — there is no view, there is no horizon, there is a grey band above and black-green below and a floor of worn domes going on past any weather. Nothing happens. Nothing has happened for a very long time and nothing is scheduled.

What is there is a machine the size of a continent, built by somebody, still running on nothing but the world's tilt and the world's cold, tended by people who don't know they're tending it, and a manual carved into the floor of it being read at the rate of a few words a generation by fingertips in the dark under the moss.

If that is not enough to justify eight days of being cold and wet and possibly some permanent numbness in your toes, do not go. If it is, go quietly, count your weirs, tether everything, take out what you bring in, and get out before your skin does.
