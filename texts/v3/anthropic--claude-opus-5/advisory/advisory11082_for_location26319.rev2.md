# Briefing: The Bell

*For a first descent, with a competent partner, in good health, intending to come back up.*

---

## 1. Understand what you are preparing against

New visitors prepare for the heat, the noise, and the drop. Those are the things you can feel from the crack, and they are all survivable with ordinary discipline and ordinary gear.

The thing that takes people is none of those. It is that the Bell runs at sixty-three cycles per minute, and so, at rest, do you.

Everything below is arranged around that fact. The heat will exhaust you, the chord will deafen you if you are careless, and a bad landing will break your ankle twelve meters under a salt flat with no way out — but the Bell only *keeps* the ones who caught the beat. Prepare for the beat first. Everything else is caving.

---

## 2. Who should not go

- Anyone with a resting heart rate under about 58. You will lock on within the hour and you will not notice it happening.
- Anyone with existing high-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, or a perforated drum. Double protection cannot save an ear that is already compromised, and the chord finds the damage.
- Anyone with a history of syncope, heat intolerance, or poorly managed hypertension.
- Anyone claustrophobic. The entry slot is a sideways squeeze and you cannot turn around in it.
- Anyone who is, at present, tired of things changing. This is not a joke and it is the single most reliable predictor in the whole miserable literature. The Bell offers a specific and genuine consolation, and people who want that consolation are the ones who do not climb the rope. If you have recently lost someone, wait a year.

---

## 3. Six weeks out

**Heat-acclimate.** Forty degrees at near-zero humidity is not brutal, but you will be working in coveralls and a respirator and you will be there for hours. Sauna or heat-chamber sessions, four or five a week, ninety minutes, building to light work under load. You want your plasma volume up and your sweat rate high and early.

**Train the exit, not the entry.** Practice a four-meter free ascent on ascenders, in the dark, with a twelve-kilo pack, wearing doubled ear protection, after two hours in a sauna, having drunk nothing for the last forty minutes. Do it until it is boring. The exit is the only technical problem in the Bell and you will be attempting it in the worst physiological condition of your life.

**Learn the cant.** The survey hand-vocabulary is small — maybe sixty signs — and the panel on the wall is mostly gone. Learn it from the printed reconstruction, both of you, to fluency. Speech is impossible. Writing is possible but see §9 about leaving marks. Agree on a private sign for *I am fine but I am slowing down*, because nobody ever invents that one in the moment.

**Baselines.** Audiogram. Weigh yourself dry. Hair and toenail clippings sealed and dated, for selenium comparison afterward. A two-week resting heart rate log, so that you know your own number and can recognize it when the machine offers it back to you.

**Rehearse the manifest.** Every item you carry gets a number, a lanyard, and a line on a laminated card. Practice the count-in/count-out until it takes ninety seconds.

---

## 4. The surface

The dry lake is the second most dangerous part of the trip and it gets a paragraph in most accounts.

It is white, flat, and featureless to the horizon, and the albedo will burn the underside of your chin and the inside of your nostrils. Glacier glasses with side shields, a broad brim, a neck drape, zinc on every upward-facing surface of your face. The crust crunches like sugar and in places it is a lid over magnesium brine; probe ahead of a vehicle and do not park within thirty meters of the crack.

**You must be able to find the crack again.** It is a slot in a white plain under a white sky. Fly a pole with a flag at least four meters tall, guyed, plus a second pole two hundred meters upwind, plus GPS, plus a magnetic bearing to any real landmark that exists. People have come up out of the Bell, walked eighty meters to relieve themselves, and failed to find the hole again for two hours. Not fatal in the morning. Fatal at two in the afternoon.

Set the surface camp with real shade — a fly, not a tarp on the ground — and cache twice the water you think anyone needs.

**Time the descent so that you come out at night or in the grey before dawn.** You will emerge dehydrated, partially deaf, and mentally flattened. You do not want to also emerge into a white glare at 44°C.

---

## 5. Rigging: the one thing you cannot improvise

The old steel ladder ends four meters short of the floor. Do not use it. Do not trust its bolts. They are set in anhydrite, which is soft, which creeps, and which some previous generation drilled in the confident manner of people working for a mining concern that no longer exists. The ladder is a handrail and a historical artifact. It is not an anchor and it is not an exit.

Rig your own line, top to bottom, before anyone commits to the slot. There is nothing to anchor to on the surface, so bring it: a spanning bar or bipod across the crack, backed to a loaded vehicle or three deadmen buried in the crust. Two independent lines. A knotted backup with a separate anchor.

Haul packs separately on a tagline; the slot will not admit a person and a pack together.

**Set a surface watch who does not descend.** They hold the schedule, they hold the haul system pre-rigged at 3:1, and they hold the authority to start hauling on the clock whether or not anyone below has agreed. This is not a courtesy. Understand clearly: a broken femur in the Bell is a fatality. Nothing on a stretcher goes through that slot. Descend as though there is no rescue, because there is none.

One more thing, and I will not defend it, only report it: leave nothing rigged in the chamber overnight. Take the rope out with you every time. Field lore holds that a line left hanging in the Bell comes to belong to the Bell, and that a rope which belongs to the Bell does not necessarily bear a load out of it. It costs you twenty minutes to derig. Pay the twenty minutes.

---

## 6. Clothing, skin, lungs

Every wall surface in that chamber terminates in an unweathered crystal face. There are no rounded edges anywhere in the Bell, including the ones you will brush past in the dark. Heavy coveralls, long cuffs taped, knee and elbow pads, cut-resistant gloves with nitrile over the top.

**Do not bleed in the Bell.** Not for the Bell's sake. You are the only free water for kilometers and the only nitrogen, and the mites already come for you when you hold still. Seal every cut immediately and carry more adhesive dressing than seems reasonable.

**The tallow.** It seeps from the joint planes and it looks harmless and it smells of hot insulation and almonds, which is your nose telling you, correctly, that there are volatile organoselenides in the air. Assume every long-chain selenide is dermally absorbed and systemically toxic. Do not touch it with bare skin. Do not let anyone talk you into tasting it, and someone always tries.

Respirator: half-face minimum, organic vapor cartridges, sealed. Full-face if you can tolerate it, because it also solves the fliers. Change cartridges after each descent and do not carry used ones in the same bag as your food. Track cumulative exposure across trips; chronic selenosis creeps — garlic breath first, then brittle nails, then the neurology, and by then you have been three times too many.

Goggles or full-face regardless. Your eyelids will grit up with your own dried salt within two hours and you will want to rub them with a tallow-contaminated glove. Bring sealed saline vials on a lanyard.

---

## 7. Water, heat, and the hard clock

Carry water in sealed bladders with bite valves and drink through the valve. **Never open a container in the Bell.** The air there is not merely dry; it is dry in a way that appears to be a fixed property of the chamber rather than a condition of it. An unstoppered flask fumes visibly and empties absurdly fast, and the humidity of the room does not rise by a measurable amount afterwards. Whatever accounting the Bell runs, your water is a line item in it.

Budget one and a half liters per hour. Take electrolytes — you will be laying down salt on your own eyelids, and cramping on a rope ascent is how people fall.

**Time budget, first descent:**

- 0:00 — on the floor, lines rigged, count-in complete
- 0:10 — acclimatize: stand still, do nothing, let the noise stop being an emergency
- 0:20–1:40 — work
- 1:40 — hard turnaround, no exceptions, no "one more"
- 2:00 — first ascender on the rope
- 2:30 — surface

Two hours in the chamber. Four is the absolute survivable ceiling with a large water load and it is not recommended for a first visit, or a fifth. There is no reward in the Bell for endurance. The Bell is exactly the same at hour six as at hour one. That is rather the point, and the people who stayed to confirm it are the reason we have rules.

---

## 8. The chord

One hundred and five decibels, three notes, unvarying, plus the thud and the whir. At that level, unprotected exposure becomes damaging in a handful of minutes.

Double protection: deep-inserted foam plugs, properly rolled and held until they expand, under high-attenuation muffs. Practice the insertion until you get a consistent occlusion; a badly seated plug is worth almost nothing. Expect, realistically, something in the range of 35–40 dB of reduction, which puts you at a conversational level and, more importantly, does nothing at all about the low end, which arrives through your sternum and the bones of your skull and cannot be blocked by anything you can carry.

Two notes on the acoustics, both practical.

The chamber is a standing wave. There are nodes. Locate them on your first descent with a meter and mark them on your own sketch map, and take your rest stops there. Do not attempt to attenuate the chamber. Damping a flute does not lower the volume; the energy reappears elsewhere, conserved, redistributed, and you have no way of predicting where the new antinode lands. That experiment is done. It was done in the fifties by people with better instruments than you.

And do not sing into it, hum with it, or tap along.

## 9. Objects: the count

Things brought into the Bell and left there stay, and stop. The glove near the plinth has held the curve of a hand for seventy years without stiffening. The mine-lamp burns on six meters of cut cable. The Bell sweeps away its own detritus and preserves everything foreign, exactly as it arrived, indefinitely.

Therefore:

- Lanyard everything. Pockets closed. Nothing loose, not a lens cap, not a wrapper, not a pen.
- Count in, count out, item by item off the card, before anyone touches the rope.
- If the count is short, you search. If you cannot find it, you have made a permanent addition to a place that will outlast the surface you came from, and you report it, and you write it in the register, and you feel appropriately terrible about it.

Keep your watch **on your body.** A timepiece set down on the floor of the Bell is a timepiece you are donating, and — this is the demonstration everyone does once and never repeats — it will be reading the same time when you come back next year.

Take nothing out either. Tallow samples are tolerated and have been carried out many times without incident. Organisms are not. Removing a clatterer causes the census to restore itself, which means you have not taken an animal out of the Bell; you have caused a new animal to exist. Nobody knows what that is billed against. The corpse in your bag, meanwhile, will begin to age on the ladder, and the smell of that is its own argument.

And do not add the twelfth mark to the tally. There is no evidence for this rule, no mechanism, and no recorded consequence for breaking it, largely because anyone who has broken it is not available for interview. Obeying it costs nothing.

## 10. The residents

**The Long One** circles the wall–floor junction on an eleven-minute lap. It is blind. It does not deviate for obstacles; it goes over them. It will go over your leg, your rope bag, and your hand. Keep the wall line clear and never rest with your body in its track. Time its lap on arrival and keep it in your head, but see the next section about using the Bell's clocks.

**The scale-mites** will graze the tallow film — and the salt, and the skin — off anything that stays still for a few minutes. It does not hurt. It is horrible. Tape your cuffs.

**The clatterers** cling to the flutes and the Works and shimmer in sympathy with the chord. They eat each other; they will not eat you. Do not brush them off the machine.

**The haze** holds its tilted torus above the updraft. Do not walk through it. Not because it is dangerous, but because it will reform behind you and you will have learned nothing except that it does, and because the photographic sequence going back to 1961 is the single most valuable record in the chamber and it is taken from a marked position that you should not disturb.

**The wickwood** is the only light besides the lamp. Do not break a stalk. Growth exactly matches grazing; you have no idea what the accounting does with a deficit and you are not the person to find out in a two-hour window.

## 11. The actual hazard

The Works turns at sixty-three revolutions per minute. It puts a thud into the floor and into your chest once every 0.95 seconds. Sixty-three per minute is also, near enough, the resting heart rate of a fit adult human at ease in warm air.

You will not be attacked. You will be *offered* something. Over the first hour the chord stops being noise and becomes the condition of the room; thought slows and squares off and starts arriving on the beat, one idea per thud, which is an unfamiliar and not unpleasant way to think. Somewhere in the second hour you will notice that your pulse and the machine are in step. People describe the moment with remarkable consistency: not fear, but a kind of relief. The sense of a question being withdrawn.

That is the whole danger. Everything else in the Bell is heat and rope work.

**Countermeasures, all of which you rig before you go down:**

- **A heart-rate strap with a vibrating alarm**, set to buzz if you drop below 72 for more than thirty seconds. Cheap, reliable, and it has saved more people than any other item on the list.
- **Two independent vibrating countdown timers**, one on each wrist, set to irregular intervals — eleven minutes and seventeen minutes, say. Never a round number. Never a multiple of anything in the room. Their whole job is to interrupt.
- **Refuse every rhythm the Bell offers you.** The thud, the lap of the Long One, the shimmer. They are all beautifully regular and they are all free and every one of them is an invitation. Use your own watch. Use your partner. Keep an arrhythmic physical task going — a rosary, a hand-strengthener, a string of knots to tie in a fixed non-repeating order. Something to do with your fingers that the chord cannot get into.
- **Do not sit down.** Not once, not for a moment, unless you are at a marked rest node with your partner standing.
- **Do not work in synchrony with your partner.** Deliberately stagger your movements. Two people falling into step is much faster than one.
- **Decide the exit before you descend, in writing, and delegate its enforcement.** Your judgment inside is impaired in a way that feels exactly like clarity. The surface watch begins hauling on the clock. You have signed the card that says so.
- **Establish the call-and-response.** Every ten minutes, one of you signs *time?* and the other must sign back the actual number off their own watch, not a guess. If they hesitate, or sign the Bell's number, or smile — you leave. Immediately, both of you, no discussion, no last photograph.

Field wisdom, unverifiable, universally repeated: *when you catch the beat, you go.* Not soon. Then.

## 12. Packing list

**Rigging** — two 30 m static lines on separate anchors; spanning bar or bipod; three deadmen; harnesses; two ascender sets each; descender; haul kit pre-rigged at surface; tagline and pack-haul bag; helmet.

**Environmental** — coveralls; cut gloves plus nitrile overglove box; kneepads; taped cuffs; half- or full-face respirator with OV cartridges plus spares; sealed goggles; saline vials.

**Hearing** — foam plugs (multiple pairs, sized and fitted in advance), high-attenuation muffs, spare plugs on a lanyard.

**Water** — sealed bladders with bite valves, 3 L each minimum, plus electrolyte tabs pre-dissolved. No open containers, ever.

**Light** — headlamp plus two backups plus one chemical fallback, all on the person, none set on the ground. Fresh cells; carry spares in a sealed bag.

**Time and body** — watch on the wrist; two vibrating timers; HR strap with low alarm.

**Record** — sound meter, thermometer/hygrometer, camera, the 1961 reference prints, slate and chalkless marker that you carry back out, the laminated manifest, the laminated cant card.

**Medical** — trauma dressing, adhesive closures, burn gel, oral rehydration salts, and the honest acknowledgment that this kit is for stabilizing someone who is going to have to climb the rope themselves.

---

## 13. Coming out

Derig completely. Count out. Check your partner's count, and their face.

On the ladder, the noise falls away behind you in a strange way — not fading so much as being shut, like a door. At the slot you will find your body has been holding a posture for two hours that it did not know about, and your hands will be unreliable in the squeeze. Go slow. This is where people get hurt, twelve meters up, three minutes from finishing.

At the top: fluids, shade, sit down, and let the surface watch do the talking, since you will not be hearing much of it.

---

## 14. Afterwards

Rehydrate over hours, not minutes, with salt. Weigh in; anything past four percent down goes to medical. Audiogram within a week, and again at a month — temporary threshold shift that has not recovered by then is not temporary.

Decontaminate deliberately. Tallow does not come off with water and it does not, out here, stay as obliging as it was in the chamber. Solvent wipes, outdoors, gloved, everything bagged. Watch yourself for garlic breath for a fortnight. Clip nails and hair at thirty days and compare against your baseline.

And then the part nobody schedules for.

For somewhere between three days and three weeks, the surface world will feel intolerably provisional. Everything out here is a sentence being spoken, one word after another, hurrying to get somewhere, and you have just spent two hours inside a chord that arrived at its answer a century ago and has not been asked anything since. The flatness that follows is normal, common, and temporary, and it is also precisely the mood in which people book a second descent.

So build the interval in beforehand: a minimum of three months between visits, held by somebody other than you, and a debrief with a person who has been down and come back up more than once. Ask them the count. Make them tell you the number off their own watch.

The Bell will still be running. That is the one thing about it you can absolutely rely on, and it is the reason there is never any hurry to go back.
