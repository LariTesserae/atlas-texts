## Before anything else: understand that you cannot leave early

Two boats a week. That is the single fact that should organise your entire preparation, because it converts every ordinary discomfort into a commitment. A blister, a bad tooth, a cut across the heel of your hand that goes red at the edges — in most places these are inconveniences. Here they are three days long, minimum, and possibly four.

So: decide your duration before you arrive, and provision for that duration plus one boat cycle. Do not plan to "see how it goes." Nýgres does not offer a way to see how it goes.

---

## Arrival

Do not come in onto the plain itself. Two reasons, both serious.

The first is the light. That even, sourceless, shadowless grey is beautiful and it is also merciless — there is no contrast for a body to hide in, nothing casts, and a standing human on black rock is visible from the scoria spine at a kilometre and a half. Half the town looks at that spine every day out of habit.

The second is the ground. A traveler arriving at even a slight momentum, or with the half-second of disorientation that most people get, will put a hand down. New lava takes the palm off in strips, and the strips have glass in them.

**Come to the neck.** Arrive on the rubble bridge from the older coast, in fog, before ten in the morning. The fog is reliable — most mornings, lying in the hollows — and it is the only cover the place has. Arrive damp. Arrive walking towards town with a pack on, and arrive within four or five hours *after* a boat, so that the question of where you came from answers itself before it is asked. A stranger who appears on a non-boat day is not suspected of anything in particular; he is simply an event, and a town of four hundred with no quiet in it and no privacy either will discuss an event for a decade.

Departure is the same problem inverted. Either take the boat like a person, or take the fog. Do not do both in the same week.

## The body

**Hands.** Light leather gloves, two pairs, worn outdoors always — in warmth, in the thickets, at rest. This is not about cold. It is about the fact that when a human being loses balance, the hands go out, and there is nothing here to catch yourself on that is not a file. Locals' hands are a map of this and they do not think about it.

**Feet.** Full leather uppers, high ankle, stiff sole. Mesh trail shoes will be destroyed in three days and will not protect the sides of your feet, and side-strikes on a ropy flow are how ankles go. Bring a second pair. Bring boot glue. Also bring rubber boots for the thickets and the wet seams, because the mats hold water like a sponge and leather will never dry in that air.

**No bare skin below the knee, ever.** Not once, not on the warm bright afternoons. Long trousers of tight-woven cotton or wool, and gaiters. Consider soft kneepads; anyone who works the hollows here has them, because working in the hollows means kneeling on rock.

**Wool, not down.** The air deposits moisture by contact rather than by falling, which means everything you own will be wet in a way that never reaches the point of dripping and never dries. Down wets out and stays out. Bring wool and a synthetic mid-layer, and bring more socks than you think a person could use — six pairs is not comic. Note also: near the furnace hall, no fleece and no synthetic next to skin. Molten basalt splashes. Synthetics melt into the wound and make it a different injury.

**A hat you are willing to see damaged**, and a walking stick or a length of bamboo about a metre and a half long.

## The terns

From late May, crossing the plain is a physical negotiation with several thousand birds who have chosen to nest on the bare rock between the thickets — which is to say, on the paths, which is to say, on the only ground here that is safe to walk on.

They go for the highest point on you. Hold the stick up above your head and they will strike the stick. Hold nothing up and they will strike your scalp, repeatedly, and they draw blood, and blood in that wind attracts more of them.

The essential rule, and the one that visitors break: **do not run.** The combination that puts people on the boat in a splint is not the terns and it is not the lava; it is a person running from terns across lava. Walk. Hunched, stick up, steady, and keep walking, and accept that you will be screamed at inside a white cone for four hundred metres and that it will feel like being disliked by the sky.

## The fulmars

Different problem entirely, and one visitors are never warned about in time.

Fulmars defend the nest by projectile-vomiting stomach oil. It carries several metres, it is accurate, and it does not wash out — not out of wool, not out of a shell jacket, not out of hair, not out of a pack, not with anything you can buy. The smell is not "fishy." It is a rancid, waxy, penetrating thing that will still be in the fibres of that garment in a year, in another world, in a drawer, and you will open the drawer and be back on the cliff.

Bring a **sacrificial outer layer** for any approach to the cliff ledges, and mean it — assume the garment is gone. Better: do not approach the ledges at all. The quilted nests are five hundred years of stone wool and every ornithologist who comes here has a coat they keep in an outbuilding and will not bring indoors.

## The fibre

Sixty years of stone-hair has worked down into the moss as a permanent white stratum, and it is also in the air on a west wind, and in the willow scrub as those grey glass wigs, and in the buildings, and in the bedding of every bird for two kilometres.

The locals are casual about it. They cough dryly in the mornings and shrug and say it's stone, it's the ground, we came out of the same hole. Do not imitate them. They have a lifetime's arrangement with the place and you have eleven days.

- A fitted **P2/N95 respirator**, several, for the bale shed, the quarry face, and any digging in the moss mats.
- Long sleeves and a buttoned collar in the bale shed. The itch is mechanical, not allergic: fibres in the skin. **Wash it off in cold water.** Hot water opens the pores and drives it in — this is the single most useful thing to know and almost nobody knows it.
- Never rub your eyes on the plain. Carry saline.
- Do not sit down bare-handed in moss on a west-wind day.

And know that the fibre goes home with you. It does not rot and it does not burn and it will not shake out. Whatever you wear here will carry a faint permanent white seam in its seams, in your other world, indefinitely. Some travelers find this the most affecting souvenir they have ever failed to choose.

## Noise, and the loss of it

The works: double protection in the furnace hall — plugs *and* muffs. The spinners produce a frequency that arrives as pressure rather than sound and it does damage before it registers as loud. Do not enter the hall unaccompanied and do not linger at the wheels because it is beautiful, which it is.

Outside, you cannot protect against the compressor. It thumps every eleven seconds and the thump travels through the vesicled rock and comes up through your boot soles, and earplugs will do nothing about that because it is not entering by the ears.

Expect approximately this sequence:

- **Nights one to five:** you will not sleep. The fulmar cliff does not stop at night and neither does the floor's humming, and the eleven-second thump will start to feel personal, like something waiting.
- **Nights six to eight:** it inverts. Something in the brainstem gives up and files the whole thing as *quiet*.
- **Thereafter:** you will sleep better than you have ever slept in your life, and this is not a figure of speech, and you should be prepared for it to make leaving difficult in a way you did not budget for.

Bring earplugs, but consider not using them. The acclimation is faster and cleaner if you let it happen. And bring something for the other end — because after you leave, silence will be intolerable for two to three weeks, and a proportion of travelers develop a tap: an unconscious eleven-second beat, finger on table, foot on floor. Warn whoever you live with.

## Water, and the one unforgivable act

The town drinks from a round collapse-pit pool three metres across, so cold it aches, containing thousands of translucent amphipods that arrived by no route anyone can name.

You are, functionally, a route by which things arrive by no route anyone can name.

So:

- **Wash your boots, your gaiters, your pack, your trouser cuffs, and your stick before you come.** Scrub the welts and the lug voids. Sixty-one years of biosphere here, and they have already learned exactly what an introduction does — the lupine is a permanent visible reproach spread across the whole west of the plain, brought by somebody's grandmother who meant nothing by it. A visitor who arrives with clean gear and says so, unprompted, will be trusted faster than by any other means available.
- **Do not put anything in the pool.** Not a bottle that has been elsewhere, not a hand, not a filter, not a thermometer, not soap, not a foot. There is a dipper on a chain. Use the dipper. This is the nearest thing Nýgres has to a sacred law, and it is not framed as a law, and you will not be given a second chance to observe it.
- **Drink the water as offered.** You will be watched while you do. They do not entirely trust it themselves — nobody who has looked into that polished black shaft does — and they are interested in whether you will. Filtering it in front of them is a small, legible insult. If your gut is naive, boil in private for the first two days and accept a mild reckoning.

## Navigation, and the fog

The plain reads as one look from the spine and becomes completely illegible at ankle height in fog, which is most mornings until ten or eleven. Every hollow looks like every other hollow. The bare rock between hollows is where the ankles break.

**Your compass will lie.** Fresh basalt carries strong remanent magnetisation and the flow deflects a needle by amounts that vary from slab to slab. Do not trust it, do not average it, do not build a route on it.

Navigate by sound instead, which is what the locals do without describing it as navigation:

- The **fulmar cliff** is a continuous wet cackling wall of sound and it lies along the seaward margin. It gives you one whole edge.
- The **compressor thump** gives you the neck, though its direction is genuinely ambiguous in fog and you can walk in a long curve towards it and not know.
- The **floor** — the broad grey humming below a cello — rises with wind speed and is loudest out on the open plain, and drops away when you enter a thicket. It tells you what kind of ground you are on. Learn this on a clear day, deliberately, with your eyes shut, before you need it.
- The **works glow** paints the wet moss yellow for thirty metres and diffuses in fog into a general brightening on one side of the world. Adequate.

Carry a whistle. Carry a pencil, not a pen — everything is damp. Carry a watch with a second hand; the eleven-second thump is a metronome and a metronome is a pace count, and a pace count is a position.

## The town

Four hundred people, one industry, and an obsessive documentary culture. Everything here is measured, dated, and written down, because the ground moves eleven millimetres a year and the only way to see that is to keep records — so record-keeping is not a bureaucratic habit here, it is the local form of piety.

**Bring measurement as a gift.** A good steel tape. A decent spirit level. Brass anything — brass is the material of the tide-post marks and has a status here it has nowhere else. Graph paper. Waterproof notebooks. Coffee. And if you have data of your own, offer it; a traveler who arrives with a series of numbers taken carefully over time is, in Nýgres, a person with a letter of introduction.

**Have a reason to be here that involves work.** Ornithology is the obvious cover and a bad one — the actual ornithologists come every summer, they are known by name, and the nest-counting is done in an established way you will get wrong within four minutes. Better to be interested in something with a customer: insulation, refractories, narrow-gauge stock, the dredging problem, the harbour levy.

**On the joke.** They joke constantly about the land rising, and the joke has an exact permitted form: it is about the *harbour*. The channel, the dredge, the levy, the boat's draught, the day they'll have to blast again. That is all funny and endlessly so. The version that is about the *town* — that one day this will be a hill, inland, dry, with the sea a rumour — is not funny, and it is not funny because they have all thought it, privately, at the pool at night. Do not offer it. And when someone eventually asks you, quietly, near the end of your stay, whether you have seen places like this later on — say you haven't. Whether or not you have.
