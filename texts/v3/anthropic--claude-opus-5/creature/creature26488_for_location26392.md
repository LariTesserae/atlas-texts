## The Ones Inside

They call themselves by a word that also means *the inside of a thing* — the meat of a fruit, the pith of a stem, the part that is soft because something else is hard around it. It doubles as their word for the world. When they say *the world is loud tonight* they mean the Well, and when they mean the plateau they use a word whose other sense is *spilled*.

There are around thirteen hundred of them and there have been around thirteen hundred of them for as long as anyone's bones have been on the shelf. The volume permits no more. Everyone knows this in the way a family in a small boat knows its waterline.

They are short — an adult man is a metre and a half, a metre fifty-five, and considered tall — and built like bellows. Four thousand metres of thin air gives them chests out of all proportion to their arms, a deep ribbed barrel above a narrow waist, and the standing posture of someone who has just set down a heavy load. Their limbs are thin, their hands are large, their feet are enormous: splayed, horn-soled, the toes able to grip, the pads thick enough to take stone at forty degrees without noticing. It is those feet that dished the treads on the northeast ramp. Nothing weathers in the Well; the stairs were carved concave by skin.

Their skin is a dark warm brown that never sees the sun and is oiled twice a day with fig resin against mites. Their eyes are large-pupilled and adapted to aquarium light: a Well-dweller can read a hand-sign at fifteen paces in green gloom that a trader from the lower country calls darkness, and the same trader watches them go up the ramp at noon squinting and weeping under a slitted hood like people being led toward a fire.

Their teeth are worn flat by thirty. Sand-washing, ash-cooking, and grit in every mouthful of moth-cake do that. They also carry, in the enamel, a thin sweet line for every fig mast they have lived through — one line per decade of drunkenness — so that age is not counted but read. To ask someone's age you ask *how many lines,* and it is a slightly rude question, the way asking a sum of money is rude.

They move slowly. Not solemnly — they are a loud, quarrelsome, joking people — but slowly, always, from childhood, because at twenty-nine degrees in saturated air a hurrying body sweats, and the proverb, which is one of about six that everybody says too often, is *hurry is drinking.* Children who run are not punished. They are asked, mildly, who is going to pay for it.

## What a Person Is

A person is forty litres. This is not a metaphor here; it is an accounting fact, arrived at by generations who have carried the dead and know exactly what a body weighs before and after the mat has had it.

Everything follows from the forty litres.

They do not weep. Children weep, and are held, and are not shamed; adults are taught the other thing, which is to press the face hard into a pad of vapour-moss and breathe into it until the fit passes, so that the water stays in the house.
