# The Crew, and the Others

## Start with the blood

Everything about the men follows from it.

A man who has worked a full rotation on the Shoulder carries a haematocrit between seventy-two and seventy-eight per cent. Ordinary blood is forty-five. What this means practically is that if you open a vein up here it does not spurt or run; it wells, slowly, dark to the point of purple-black, with a consistency the workshop hand describes as cold honey and the company doctor at the rail spur, who sees these men twice a year and dislikes it, describes as sludge. The heart has to push it. Everyone's heart is enlarged. Everyone's nailbeds and lips are plum-coloured, and the fingers are clubbed at the ends like little spatulas, and this is so uniformly true that a crewman meeting another crewman in a town does not need to ask.

They are short and deep. Almost nobody on the Shoulder is over one metre sixty-five, and almost everybody is a hand's breadth broader through the chest than a man of that height has any business being; they grew outward instead of up, in the way that everything here grows toward its constraint. The lungs are enormous. The ribcage flares. Under a shirt it reads as fat and there is no fat on anyone.

Appetite dies above five thousand five hundred metres. Nobody here is hungry, ever, and so they eat by the clock, four times, the way you would dose a medicine. Every man loses weight for the first eleven weeks of a rotation and then holds at his new number, which is the number the mountain has chosen for him, and which his body will defend afterwards for years.

Nobody sleeps through a night. At this pressure, breathing during sleep goes periodic: fifteen seconds of fast shallow breaths, then a pause of twenty, then a gasp. The bunkhouse at two in the morning is nineteen men stopping and starting out of phase, and it is one of the few sounds on the Shoulder that is louder than it should be. The old rule is that the first sign of the sickness is a man who begins to sleep soundly. When the lips go past plum toward black, and the headache arrives and does not leave between one Sunday and the next, the man goes down to two thousand four hundred metres and stays there, and over eight months his blood thins and his fingers unclub and he becomes ordinary.

Twelve years is the usual figure. Some men refuse to go down at twelve years. Those men are, for the most part, still on the mountain, and this is not a euphemism; several of them are landmarks.

## The gait, the soles

They all walk the same. You can see it in a new man by his fourth week: the stride lengthens, the arms stop swinging much, the head comes forward, and the stopping goes out of him. Standing still in a corridor costs a man the skin of his soles; sitting is unthinkable; leaning is what the pillars are for and only in the sun, only on a wind face. The camp is pitched on the crater's northern lip because that one hectare is the only cold ground on the summit, and it costs each man forty minutes' walk to the benches each way, and this is considered an excellent trade.

They nail a second sole of hardwood to their boots and a third of stiffened llama hide over that. A sole lasts about five weeks. The workshop keeps a wooden form for the cutting, worn to a shine, and a man's year is nine soles. When a man's boots come back down the ropeway with everything else in a sack, the soles are counted before anything else is looked at, because the soles say how long he stood.

## How they speak

Not much, and never loudly.

A sentence costs breath and breath is the whole currency, so speech here has shortened to three or four words delivered on a single exhale, usually while walking, usually to the side rather than face-on. Grammar has gone with it: no subordinate clauses, no conditionals, almost no questions. A man who uses a long sentence up here is either new or showing off, and both are corrected the same way, which is that nobody answers him.

Between men more than thirty metres apart, speech doesn't work at all, and the corridors make the problem worse: sound arrives late and from a wall that isn't the wall it came from. So the working language is percussion. A wrench on the steel tub, a pick-point on pillar rock, a boot heel on the track. Two slow, one quick: water is out. Three quick: come, no hurry. Three quick repeated without pause: come now. A single stroke every five seconds, indefinitely, is a man who cannot walk and is telling you where he is, and it is the only signal on the mountain that everyone drops everything for.

Because direction is unreliable, identity has migrated into rhythm. Each man's pick has a signature — Mamani works in a limping triplet, the old man Condori used to work in a long even four that new men found soothing — and the crew know one another by the sound of their labour the way people elsewhere know one another by face. It is also why nobody on the Shoulder calls out a name to summon someone. You tap your own rhythm and wait to be recognised.

The crew do not answer a rhythm they cannot place. This is a rule, stated flatly to every new man on his first day along with the rules about shade and about the eleven minutes, and it is not explained.

## The hour before dawn

At half past four, in the dark, at minus eleven degrees, the whole camp goes out to drink.

The nearest forty pillars have been fitted with split tin gutters and rubber hose, wired on, added to and repaired by four generations of crew, and by that hour every pillar in the field is running with condensed steam. A good pillar gives eleven litres. The water comes off warm, tastes of matchheads and faintly of pennies, and is the only water on the Shoulder that is not brine. Everyone drinks it. Everyone's teeth carry the same brown-and-yellow banding on the incisors from the fluoride and sulfate in it, laid down in annual rings like a tree, and a man from the Shoulder can be identified by his smile in a bar two thousand kilometres away by anyone who knows what to look for.

They fill the drums, and then the light comes up over the eastern cordillera and by nine the field is bone dry and nobody touches a pillar again until the next dark.

Washing is done perhaps monthly, standing over a vent in the steam, which everyone agrees is the best thing that ever happens here.

## The house

Water boils at eighty-one degrees on the Shoulder. Nothing cooks. Rice stays chalk, meat stays string, tea is a brown suggestion at the temperature of blood. The brick oven at the camp has not been lit since the last resident cook died, and there has been no cook since, because the crew discovered they did not need one: vent seven, forty metres below the latrine, runs at ninety-four degrees at the throat, which is thirteen degrees hotter than any pot on the mountain can get. Food goes into a hole in the ground wrapped in sacking and comes out cooked. The mountain does the kitchen. The oven stays as furniture.

Coca in the cheek from waking to sleeping, a wad the size of a plum, renewed four times a day; the lime gourd; the small brown stain at the corner of every mouth. Cane alcohol on Sundays, ninety-six per cent, and the first measure always goes to the ground, which here means into a vent, and the vent takes it with a short hiss that everyone treats as an answer, because it is.

The chapel holds a plaster figure whose paint has not faded in sixty years, because nothing fades here, and a rack of candles that will not stay lit in the thin air. They are left standing unburnt. Over the years other things have been left with them: a bootnail, a mule shoe, a child's shoe, a moth, a tin clip from a ropeway bucket, four teeth. None of it has changed at all. The rack is the tidiest and most permanent record of the crew's private lives that exists anywhere, and none of them would call it that.

## The mouse

Twenty-four grams. A heart at seven hundred and fifty beats a minute in an animal that cannot afford to be cold for more than a few minutes at a stretch, which means it does not live in the pinnacle field so much as live inside the mountain's warm exhalations, moving along them, mapping the place thermally rather than spatially. The vent lees that kill a sleeping man are its country.

It nests in the dead. The standard nest is the chest cavity of a desiccated vicuña, entered through the space behind the foreleg, lined with moth wings, llama hide stolen from the camp walls, and lichen. It is the only animal besides the men that goes into the drift tunnels, and it goes further in than they do. It eats moths, flies, spiders, soap, candle stubs, boot grease, and the hide glue in the spine of the ledger, which is why the ledger is now kept in a biscuit tin.

Nobody kills them. Not out of any prohibition — it has simply never suggested itself. There is no surplus of living things up here, and a mouse in the bunkhouse at night, crossing the warm floor in the dark, registers to a man lying awake between breaths as company.

## What the flies pay for

The brine fly is the currency of the mountain and everything else is denominated in it. Larvae in the rim gravel of six pools; adults in a black felt on the terraces, ten to the seventh of them, rising as one sheet when your shadow crosses and resettling behind you without a sound.

The spiders take their cut in the corridors. A corridor is a continent to a spider; eleven metres of visibility is an unimaginable vista; the dead air between two pillars is the best real estate on the plateau. Their webs are strung at shin height and they are on the same clock as everything else: every eleven minutes the breath comes through, beads every line with condensate, and loads the web to the point of collapse, and the spider runs out along its frame and shakes the water off, and then goes back. A spider here does this five hundred times between sunrise and sunset. It has never done anything else.

The lizards take their cut from the spiders, working the millimetre boundary between the sun face and the shade of a pillar, warming on the one and fleeing to the other, active four hours a day and rigid the rest. They have the men's problem exactly inverted, and they solve it with the same technique, which is never to be still in the wrong place.

The toads live in the few millimetres of a gradient where the water is neither poaching nor merely scalding, and they spend their entire lives taking the temperature of it with their bellies. Their eggs go into a band forty centimetres wide at the inflow of the second pool. Some years the inflow shifts overnight by the width of a hand and there are no toads that year.

The flamingos — six hundred on the largest pool, pale, high-altitude, standing in water that steams around their legs — do not migrate, because the pools do not freeze; there is nowhere better to go and never a reason to leave. Their leg skin is thickened into a boot. The chicks grow anklets of salt precipitate that either fall off or do not. They are silent all day. At night, in the colder denser air, they talk: a low massed murmuring off the water that carries two kilometres to the bunkhouse and is, after the ropeway, the second loudest sustained sound on the Shoulder. The crew sleep badly to it and would not sleep at all without it.

And the caracaras have learned the breath. Every eleven minutes, when the whole crater exhales and the corridors fill and nothing on the ground can see an arm's length, they come down into the white. They have forty seconds. Nobody has ever watched them do it, for the obvious reason.

## The flour

The drift walls carry a white fur, soft, faintly damp, that the men scrape off their sleeves at the portal and call the flour. It is alive: something on the order of a billion cells to the gram, sulfur-oxidising, doing chemistry in the dark on rock and warm water and needing nothing else at all.

By mass it is the largest living thing on the Shoulder — larger than the flies, larger than the flamingos, larger than the lichen, larger than the men by a factor nobody has bothered to calculate. It has been at work for a hundred thousand years. The alunite is its leavings. The kaolin is its leavings. The pale sugary silica, the corridors, the pillars, the whole enormous packed forest of the summit, is the residue of its digestion, and the mine is a small downstream operation collecting the fraction of that digestion which happens to be yellow and happens to be worth something at the rail spur.

No one on the mountain has ever seen it, and every man on the mountain has it on his sleeve.

## The dead

They are residents, and they work.

There are no names for the corridors and there is no map; the field is navigated by the dead. Turn left at the Sergeant — a man from the 1920s in a coat with two remaining brass buttons, wedged upright at a four-way junction, hide over bone, entirely indifferent. Down past the dog, second dog, not the first. The mule with its pack still lashed on is the marker for the bench four approach and has been for ninety years, and every man who has ever worked bench four has touched its shoulder on the way past, so that one patch of its hide is worn to a polish and is the only thing on the summit that is visibly changing.

The crew know perhaps forty of them by sight, the way you know a corner in a city. Four hundred years' worth are in there. Past a certain depth of time they stop being anyone and become furniture, and nobody finds this disrespectful; furniture is the permanent stuff of a house.

Their own dead stay unless a family below asks. Twice in living memory a family has asked. The second time, the crew who carried the box down and stood at the graveside in the valley learned what happens at two thousand four hundred metres to something that has been perfectly preserved for nine months, and the account of it has been passed on since with real horror. *Down there they come apart.* It is understood among the crew, without ever having been put into a sentence, that staying is better.

## The ledger

Condori keeps it now, because his blood is the worst and he cannot work the benches, and this is simultaneously the camp's only demotion and its only honour.

He writes the tonnage, the soles issued, the diesel hours, the weights of men. Once a month he writes a single line in a narrow column that was ruled and headed in a neat clerk's hand in 1954, for the buckets that come up full, and he has never in nine years been told what the column is for, and has never asked, because asking would be a long sentence.

He also draws the rations. Nineteen men sleep in the bunkhouse. Condori draws twenty portions, and has always drawn twenty portions, and when the extra plate goes cold on the bench by the door it is scraped out at the end of the day onto the warm ground behind the workshop, where by morning it is gone, and where the mice will not go.

---

So: a billion billion cells eating the mountain from inside; ten million flies; six hundred flamingos standing cooked below and chilled above; the spiders on their five-hundred-a-day schedule; the toads in their millimetres; the lizards on the seam between burning and freezing; the mice inside the chests of the dead; two eagles that do not have to work; four hundred years of the perfectly preserved serving as street signs; and nineteen men with thick dark blood and yellow-banded teeth, who do not sit down, who do not raise their voices, who take their hats off during the breath because for forty seconds nobody can see them, and who all — flies, spiders, birds, bacteria and men — keep exactly the same time.
