## The Resident

There are tenants here and there is a resident, and it takes a season to learn to tell them apart.

The tenants are the ones you'd list. Ragworms in the anoxic layer, at densities a marine ecologist once put at eleven thousand to the square metre and then re-measured, twice, because she didn't believe it. Eels in the outfall, three or four, fat and incurious. Lichen on the ash, glasswort on the humps, the purple mat that isn't oil. A fox on the crown track twice a winter, walking the rim like a man checking a fence he doesn't own. Midges in July, in columns, over the collar-pools. And the heron.

All of them ordinary. All of them, and this is the first thing you notice if you keep notes, counted.

---

Take the heron. There is one. There has never been two.

It was ringed in 1998 — a Dutch ring, bird of the year — and that bird was photographed on the same six or seven piles for eleven years. In 2009 a different ringed bird appeared, British, and the Dutch bird was not seen again. In 2016 an unringed bird. Between the 2009 arrival and the 1998 bird's last confirmed sighting there is a documented gap of one day, during which both were present, on piles about thirty metres apart, neither displaying, neither feeding, both facing the sump.

Herons are territorial and they are noisy about it. There was no fight. There was a day of two, and then a day of one, and the ledger came right.

You can watch the current one for an hour and see it strike twice. There is very little here for a heron to eat. It is not a good living. It stands with its shoulders up out of the knee-fog, waiting out the fidgeting of the ground, and what it most resembles is not a bird hunting but a bird posted.

---

The resident does not live in the mud. It lives in the ratio.

This is not a figure of speech and I want to be careful with it, because the light here is honest and the place deserves the same. Nothing is hiding. If you stand on the bank in March with the surveyors' folder open and hold plate 1948 up at arm's length against the actual basin, you will see the resident immediately and entirely, and you will not know what to do with having seen it.

Nine piles lean at more than fifteen degrees. Nine in 1911. Nine in 1948, nine in 1972, nine last March. Not the same nine. The Ordnance men, who were bored and thorough, began numbering the leaners in 1955, and the numbering is the only real record of the thing's movement. A leaner rights itself — the silt heaps against its low side, the collar-pool migrates, and over about nine weeks a pile that was over at nineteen degrees comes back to eleven and drops off the list. The mean interval between one dropping off and another going on is forty-one hours. The new leaner is never adjacent. Sometimes it is a hundred and eighty metres away, across the whole dish, in ground that has not shifted measurably in a decade, and it begins to go over in still weather, with no channel near it, and no one has ever seen the moment it starts.

That is the animal moving. It moves the way a total moves when you carry a figure from one column to another. It has no location and it has a position, and the position changes, and the total does not.

---

Its body, insofar as it keeps one, is the rotation.

There is a list. It was begun in the seventies by a man from the county museum who came for the clay pipes and stayed for the rest of his life, in the ordinary sense of visiting on Saturdays, and the list runs to two hundred and six items. Two hundred and six is not the number of things that have ever been in the basin. It is the number of things that are *in circulation*, and it has been two hundred and six since he closed it in 1989, and the museum has continued to tick the entries.

The shoemaker's last, iron pin intact, quadrant D. The hobnailed boot, right foot, size unguessable. The horse's molar. Half a set of brass scales — the half with the pan. Nine porcelain insulators, which is a coincidence and which nobody who has spent time here believes is a coincidence. A brass tap with a square shank. A child's shoe of a much later date than the boot, coming up first in 1996. Each surfaces, sits a season or two in the light, greys over, goes under and is gone for eight to nineteen years, and comes back within, at worst, forty metres of where it went.

Nothing here sinks and nothing leaves. But — and the list-keeper worried at this for years in a series of increasingly personal marginal notes — not everything is *accepted*. A bicycle went in from the eastern rim in 1983, thrown by teenagers, and was found the following autumn on the ash bank on the *western* side. Not floated. Not washed. Carried across, or around, three hundred paces of ground that cannot be crossed at speed by anything with a bicycle. In 2004 a hire skip's worth of builder's waste was tipped over the north bank and by the next spring the plasterboard had rotted, the timber had gone under, and every piece of the broken cistern was back on the crown track, stacked. Not neatly. Just up.

It keeps what it keeps. It declines the rest, without heat, the way a machine returns a coin it can't read.

---

Its metabolism is a standing order.

The pump draws 2.2 kilowatts and runs continuously, and the electricity is paid quarterly out of a maintenance line that has passed, by amalgamation and reorganisation and transfer of undertakings, through six bodies since 1962, three of which no longer exist and one of which never legally did. The current figure, which I have seen, is £61.40 a month. Sixty-one pounds and forty pence is the entire caloric intake of the resident of the Turning Ground. Everything out there — every ten-minute river, every collapsing table of silt, every one of four thousand bright collars catching the last of the sun — is that money, converted into gradient, and then converted into nothing.

It breathes at knee height, half a metre deep, and the breath doesn't drift because there's no wind inside the rim, and because it isn't going anywhere.

---

Twice, someone has tried to switch it off.

In 1974 a drainage engineer named Aldiss wrote a two-page memorandum recommending decommissioning of the Site 4/311/b lift pump on the grounds that it served no drainage function whatsoever and was, in his words, "engaged in the pumping of water from one part of a marsh to another part of the same marsh, at public expense, since a date not established." The item was placed on the agenda of the November meeting and deferred pending clarification of ownership.

In 1991 a woman named Trencher, in the successor authority, found the Aldiss memorandum in a file she had not expected to open, appended a note ("Concur. Recommend termination of supply."), and put it forward. Deferred, pending clarification of ownership.

In 2009 the papers surfaced again — genuinely surfaced, in the sense that a departing officer's boxes were opened and the Aldiss memorandum was on top of the third box, with Trencher's note still clipped to it. A third officer added a third note. Deferred.

I want to be accurate about this and not lurid. There is no conspiracy and there is nobody protecting the pump. The correspondence simply has an orbit, the same as the last and the boot: eight to nineteen years under, a season in the light, down again, up again, forty metres from where it went. The resident's immune system is not violence. It is process. It defends itself the way silt defends itself, by having no edge to push against and by outlasting the push.

The next surfacing is due.

---

What does it want?

Nothing, and the nothing is the whole of it. This is not a hungry place and it is not a waiting place. Somewhere behind 1911 there was a project — the saltworks, the tannery, the drying floor, whatever it was that wanted four thousand oak piles driven into a coastal flat — and that project finished, in the sense that it ended and in the sense that it was completed, and the difference stopped mattering. What's here is not a beginning that never got going. It's an ending that is still warm. Not a heart beating. A hand going on clenching afterwards, at the same rate, in perfect balance, with a good grip.

The one thing in the basin that has anything you could call an opinion about this is the willow on the western rim, which got its roots down past the moving layer and into the still world underneath, and which has been leaning at forty degrees over the dish since before 1948 and will not fall and is not taken. Those two have an arrangement. It is the only relationship in the place, and it consists entirely of not resolving.

---

As for you.

Nothing will happen to you. You will hop pile to pile like someone learning to be a bird, you will keep off the pale silt, and while you are inside the rim you will be part of the count, and when you climb out your part of the count leaves with you, cleanly, no residue. If you drop your keys, they will be back around 2041, in the same quadrant, and you should not wait.

The only cautionary tale attached to the Turning Ground concerns the museum man with the list, who began coming in months other than March, and then on weekdays, and eventually most days, and who — I have checked this, because I wanted there to be more to it — was fine. He lived to ninety-one in a bungalow eight miles inland. He did three things each day, in the same order, for thirty-one years. His neighbours were fond of him and could not, when asked, recall a single thing he had ever said. The list stayed at two hundred and six. He was in no distress at all. He had simply, at some point, arrived at his settled state, and by then the settled state was the whole of him and there was nothing left over to mind.

That is what lives here, and that is the extent of what it does. It is not dangerous. It is catching.

---

For about four minutes in the evening, when the sun gets under the cloud at the western rim, every collar-pool lights at once and you can see the resident whole: four thousand contacts, a live feed on four sagging poles, a load, a return path, a hum at the low end of hearing, one heron posted at the sump, nine leaners, two hundred and six items in slow orbit, and a memorandum going round on the same track as a hobnailed boot.

Then it goes flat. The fog re-forms at exactly half a metre. And the resident goes on turning over in the dark, quietly, at 2.2 kilowatts, an entry nobody has been able to close.
