## Who tends it

**Bodies.** They are long and narrow and unhurried, and the first thing you notice is that they are pale — not the pallor of illness but of a people who have never had a shadow with an edge fall across them. Under the lid there is light but no sun, and pigment has nothing to argue with. Their skin is the colour of the inside of a stripped fern trunk, and you can read the veins in their forearms from two meters off, because the vessels are up close to the surface and stay dilated, permanently, from the hour of birth. Hands and feet are large. Ears are large and stand away from the head. These are radiators. Everything about the body has been pushed outward toward whatever might be cooler than itself.

They do not sweat much, and what they do is regarded as a fault, a small leak, an embarrassment of the young. Sweat is water spent for nothing in air that will not take it. The old are dry and matte and a little dusty-looking and cool to touch at the wrist, and the young glisten and are told to stop.

No hair anywhere. Hair holds a film of water against the skin, which is the opposite of the project. They are born with it and it goes by the fourth year and no one has ever thought this remarkable. Their nails are filed back to the quick because a nail is a place where moss lives. Their fingers have the soft swollen ridging of hands that have not been dry in any of their owners' lives, and the pads are half-numb, so they handle small objects by looking at them rather than by feel, and their work is unexpectedly slow and precise, like a jeweller's.

**The wring.** Every person owns one cloth, kept in a stone box of condensate at the foot of their own sleeping step. It is a strip of linen a meter long, and it is called a wring, and it is the most private thing they have. You wring it out over the nape, or lay it along the inside of both thighs where the big vessels run, and you get four minutes. To touch another person's wring is a real offence, not a joke about one. When people are courting they exchange wrings for a night and this is understood by everyone and remarked on by no one.

**Cold.** Cool surfaces are property. The north face of the pump-house between the third and fifth hour belongs, currently, to the gallery crew as a body, and is lent by them in half-hours to whoever has asked and been owed. The iron handrails of Condenser Two, which are the coldest things on the rise, are held in named hours by named people, and those hours are inherited, sold, gambled, and included in the settlement of a marriage. Wages are reckoned partly in barge-script and mostly in cold — so many hours on such-and-such a stone. A serious debt is discharged by giving up a morning of iron for a season. The whole ledger is kept in one book in the gardener's cellar and is the only document on the rise that anyone gets angry about.

You will see two people at the third hour standing forearm to forearm against the same wet wall, not speaking, three centimeters apart, and you may take that for intimacy. It is intimacy. It is also a transaction, and both of them know the price.

**Speech.** They talk loudly, always, evenly, without rising at the ends of sentences, because the Nine are always going and a raised end gets lost in the flywheel whine. It is a flat, carrying, unmusical way of speaking that sounds furious to anyone from the barge-towns and is not.

Because of what the fog does, they have a habit foreigners find eerie: the doubling. A thing that matters is said twice, once now and once after a count of forty, in the same words, in the same tone. Not for emphasis. For arrival. And walking, they sound — a single flat syllable, not a word, pitched somewhere in the middle of the voice, thrown out every eight or ten paces and then listened for. It comes back late or does not come back. From the delay they know the air, and from the air they know the hour of the cycle, and from the hour they know whether to keep walking. It is the first thing a child is taught, before names, before the knock. Children born here sound before they speak. In a dry country these people cannot sleep: the world answers too fast and then stops answering, and they lie awake in the silence of somewhere that has nothing to say about itself.

**The crews.** The gallery hands have the coldest work and are, accordingly, the nearest thing to gentry; they are slightly heavier than the rest, and they know it, and they are careful about it. The stroke-hands serve the Nine and are deaf in the upper registers by thirty and lip-read as a matter of course, which gives their conversations a stillness, a lot of looking at each other's mouths. The roof crews are all young and light, eleven to nineteen, because the collecting roofs will not take weight and harness abrades the collecting surface; they go up barefoot in the four hours before dawn and strip moss in the dark with their hands, and they have a particular gait on the ground afterward, a wide flat-footed placing, and they are insufferable about it for about a year and then they grow and are put to something else, and miss it for the rest of their lives.

The glass-reader is one person at a time. Currently a woman of fifty-one named Sull, which here is an enormous age. She never touches the product. She holds the demijohn against the arc lamp, and she smells the air above it through a funnel of clean glass, and she says yes or she says no, and what she says no to runs off the eastern edge and roars. She eats nothing with flavour in it — no horsetail, no pollen, nothing fermented — in order to keep the instrument clean, and she is fed separately and plainly, and the crews treat this as the most ordinary thing in the world, as you would treat a man with a bad back. Because of what she rejects, she feeds the settlement. Everyone here drinks failed water. Everything they are made of is water that touched the wrong thing.

The salt-burner tends the chimney and the vent and lives at the eastern end alone in a brick room with a hood hanging on the door. Nobody contests his cold. He walks and people move off the wall. It is not fear exactly; it is the deference owed to the one who maintains the killing, which everyone lives inside of and nobody wants to stand next to.

The gardener keeps the forty tree ferns, which are one tree fern. She propagates by cutting. She also keeps the book of the cold, and she keeps the barge list, and when a new person comes off the barge the word for what she does with them is the same word she uses for the ferns: she sets them. You are a slip. You are put in the lowest cellar for nine days, one full pulse of the marsh, and fed flume water, and left largely alone, and at the end of it you have either taken or you have not. Most have not. They go back on the barge with their ears ringing and hear their own voices coming back to them, faintly, for a month. Those who take never leave, because outside the air accepts their sweat and they cannot stop shivering, and because a prompt world is unbearable to them.

**The sweepers.** They go out at first grey with the kists — the flat-bottomed barrows — and they sweep the rime off the levee margin, where the insects arrive out of the marsh all night on the wind and die at the line, and they tip it down the flume, and they come back, and they cough. What comes up is grey-brown and slightly glittering and they call it rime also, the same word, and this is not a metaphor anyone here has noticed making. Their forearms have a permanent faint silvering in the creases that soap will not shift. They are five people at present, on a rotation, and they are paid in ordinary cold like everyone else, and they are not otherwise remarkable, and that is meant exactly as it is said.

**The count.** There are sixty-one. There are always sixty-one within nine days of there being sixty. The crews will tell you the number is operational, and it isn't; sixty-one is what the Works has settled at and what it returns to, the way the corners of the brick have settled at their rounding. When there are sixty-two for a week, from a birth, the knock on Number Four is said to change its count, and people stop sleeping, and someone goes early and quietly, and the barge is not blamed.

**Prayer.** At the eleventh stroke, hands on iron, during dump, when the fog has come up through the gratings and closed to arm's length and no one can move anyway, they say the specification. It is a list of places the water has not been. *Not in a river. Not in a root. Not in a cloud that crossed land. Not in a mouth. Not in a body.* It takes about forty seconds, and it is said in the flat carrying voice by everyone standing wherever they were caught, and then they wait, and in thick air it comes back to them — late, from the direction they are facing, in their own voices and their neighbours', slightly wrong — and that returning is the answer, and is understood to be the answer, and they go back to work.

**The north flume.** The dead go up it. There is a barrow for that too and it has its own name and it is not the kist. Nobody discusses the uphill water with visitors. They will show you the chip of teak, once.

**Why they are permitted.** Three accounts are current. That the salt lets pass what keeps the beat, and they keep the beat, and a thing that does not keep the beat is not let pass. That they are salted from the inside already, from the water they drink and the rime they bring up, and the line cannot tell them from itself. That the Works does not count them as animals at all, but as stock — as forty ferns standing in sixty-one places, cut and set and cut again, propagated by hand for so long that the question of species has quietly lapsed.

What is observable is this: they cough grey in the morning, and sweep grey off the levee, and the word is the same; nobody here has ever been old; the barge comes twice a week with grain and glass and slips; and at the boundary of the rise, in the last hour of light, you will find one or two of them standing very still at the levee edge with their forearms on the cold stone of the coping, looking out at the band of real daylight lying on the water, which they call the Keeping, and which the children are told not to stare at, and which they stare at anyway, for as long as their hour of cold lasts, and then come back in.
