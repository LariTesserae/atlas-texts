## Who Is Down There

**On paper, nobody.**

The establishment returns for Cell 40 list posts, not people: forty-one gate-watch positions, two hundred and twelve invert and gallery hands, the dosing fleet at eleven hundred berths, sounders, string-hands, ringers, launch crews, medical, mess. Every one of those posts is a rotation. Every rotation has a terminal date. The whole administrative architecture of the Lower Volumes rests on the premise that this is a worksite, that worksites are temporary, and that the men and women in them are visitors who will, in due course, go back up.

Down here the word for going up is *going dry*, and it is not a compliment.

There is a threshold in the regulations — nobody remembers it being drafted, it was probably about pension liability — at which a person ceases to be *posted to* and becomes *resident on* establishment. It is a thousand days. The crews have made a thing of it, because crews make things of everything, and because it is the only anniversary available in a world four years old. On your thousandth day somebody pours cell water over your head from a survey bucket, which is a genuinely unkind thing to do to a scalp, and after that you are *wet*, and you are not a visitor any more, and whatever you were before has stopped being the main fact about you.

About a third of Cell 40 is wet. In the dosing fleet it is closer to nine tenths.

---

**Look at their mouths first.**

You will notice it within an hour of coming through the bulkhead: nobody down here opens their mouth very wide. They smile with the lips pressed. They speak in a flat, low, minimally-articulated register that carries surprisingly well under the pump throb, and they slather everything — lips, nostrils, knuckles, the webbing between finger and thumb — with a yellow barrier grease called slip, which smells of lanolin and paraffin and is the true smell of the Lower Volumes, far more than ozone or curing cement. Children raised down here can identify a parent in the dark by the back of a hand.

The alkali does the rest. Cracked knuckles that never quite close. A particular gloss to the forearms where the hair has gone. And ears: everyone who has done a decade near a lift house has lost the top of their hearing and a notch in the middle, so that they can hear the seventy-per-minute bass in their sternum and not much of a consonant at three metres.

Which is why they sign.

Invert sign is not a language anybody designed. It congealed out of crane signals, dive signals, and the simple necessity of saying *no, the other valve* through ear defenders at a range of forty metres. It has perhaps six hundred stable signs and a grammar that runs verb-last. It has borrowed the survey coordinates wholesale, so that a location is made with two fingers, red hand and amber hand, crossing. It has a sign for soffit rain that is also the sign for *wait*. It has no written form, cannot be spoken into a radio, and does not appear in any log, and this is exactly why every grievance, every love affair, every unauthorised opinion about the drawdown schedule in the Lower Volumes is conducted in it, across the width of a gantry, in full view of supervisors who have learned it too and are signing their own.

Their bodies run on twenty-six hours. That is the lamp cycle and there is no argument with it. A wet crewman sent up for statutory leave spends the first week unable to sleep and the second week weeping in daylight for reasons he cannot articulate, and comes back early, and says the sky was fine, and does not elaborate. The fleet has stopped converting to surface time altogether. They keep cell dates. Their calendar year has three hundred and thirty-seven days in it and they will tell you, without irony, that it is the surface that has drifted.

---

**The fleet.**

The dosers are the nearest thing the Lower Volumes have to a nation.

A buffer barge is a hundred and ten metres of flat-bottomed ugliness with an acid tank, a brine tank, a spreader boom, a two-storey deckhouse, and a hydroponic trough along the starboard rail in which somebody is always failing to grow tomatoes. It does eleven knots downhill. It never docks except for reagent, and reagent comes to it. A mowing pass across Cell 40 takes four months and then the barge turns and starts back along the offset lane, and the crew's entire experience of geography is a lane fourteen hundred metres wide, four months long, walked back and forth for years.

They are families. It was not planned that way. It happened the way it always happens: the fleet was crewed on long contracts, long contracts became longer, and people did what people do, and now there are barges with three generations aboard and a great-grandmother in the port cabin who remembers Cell 40 as a dry hole with cranes in it. They name their lanes. Officially a lane is a number. Aboard, they are the Long Green, the Four Knocks, Gina's Lane, the lane where a man went over in year two and was found — this is the part that matters to them — six weeks later, three hundred kilometres away, having been carried the whole way by the return flow, intact in eight-degree water, recognisable. They talk about that a great deal. It is their one piece of scripture about what the sea does with the dead: it keeps them, and it brings them along the lane, and it gives them back.

Everything alive in the cell is alive because a barge went past. The fleet knows this about themselves the way a priesthood knows things. They are not modest about it. Ask a doser what he does and he will not say chemistry. He will say he makes the water fit.

---

**The wallmen and the joint-gods.**

On the vertical coasts, work is rope-access, and a wallman's beat is forty kilometres of construction joint that he will crawl for a decade. He scrubs the green band, replaces anodes, reads tell-tales across the pour lifts, and knows his stretch of blank concrete the way a shepherd knows a valley — by the pattern of tie-holes, by a bad vibration at the fourth lift, by the one place where the shuttering grain runs the wrong way because a carpenter, once, in an afternoon, was tired.

And he knows his eels.

There is one to a joint. The joints are straight and numbered, and so the eels are numbered, and then, inevitably, named. Nine-oh-four is old and mean and will take a finger. Nine-eleven is missing an eye and gets fed first for that reason. Nine-nineteen is a hopeless coward and has twice been evicted by a squid. It is against three separate standing orders to feed the biota. Every wallman in the Lower Volumes feeds the biota. They carry silver in a thigh pocket, and they hold it out at arm's length, and the eel comes out of the crack — out of a crack that runs, unbroken, for four hundred kilometres, so that it emerges from a line with no beginning and no end — takes the fish, and goes back.

If you want to know where the religion of this place is going to come from, it is going to come from that. A god who lives in a straight line in a wall, who is one of thousands of identical gods, each with a number, each with a temperament, each fed in secret by one specific person against regulations. The wallmen would laugh at you for saying so. They would also never, ever let another man feed their joint.

---

**The gulls, who are locals now.**

Nobody invited them and there are four generations of them and the fourth generation has no concept of up.

They came down the intake shafts, and they have not gone back, because a gull that goes up a shaft into a cold black eleven-hundred-metre flue mostly does not come out the top. They nest on the gantry ledges of every lift house. They fish the outfall plumes, they ride the warm aprons to within twenty metres of the soffit and peel off, and they have rebuilt their own voices: measured against surface colonies, the Cell 40 birds call a full third higher and much louder, because the pump note occupies the bottom of the register and a chick that cannot be heard over it does not get fed. Four years. It took four years. There is a man in the survey office who has documented this and cannot get anybody to care.

They orient to the lamp grid. They rob the crew mess. The crews rob their eggs back, and consider this a fair and long-established reciprocity, established the winter before last.

---

**The ones born here.**

There are, at last count, a little over nine hundred children in the Lower Volumes who have never been to the surface.

They are not strange in the ways you would expect. They are boisterous, over-confident swimmers, appallingly good at estimating distance, and entirely unafraid of depth, which the medical staff find harder to manage than any phobia. They are excellent at perspective drawing — they have looked down a converging grid their whole lives — and draw sky as two rows of dots, red and amber, and are puzzled that you don't.

They know what rain is. Rain falls from a ceiling, all at once, on a schedule, hard enough to bruise, three weeks out of every eleven. It is the high point of the year. Children are taken out onto the aprons in ear defenders to stand in it, and marriages are timed to it, and there is already — already, in four years — a rule that you do not hold a funeral in the rain season, and nobody can tell you why, and everybody obeys it.

Send one of them up on a hospital lift and they lie down on the pad and shut their eyes. The doctors call it topside sickness and treat it with a blanket over the head. It is not the light. It is that the sky doesn't converge. There is nothing at the far end of it to give it a size, and a child who has never had to guess at a distance in her life cannot bear an object that refuses to be measured.

---

**The knocking.**

Water hammer is a fact of the plant. A gate closes wrong, the wave runs the conduit, the far end takes the blow, and every cell it passes through booms. Three knocks in a shift is normal. Six means someone is going to have a difficult conversation.

So everybody counts. Everybody, everywhere, in every cell, all the time. It is the one universal attention of the Lower Volumes, and about two years ago somebody realised what that meant.

You cannot fake a real hammer knock. But you can put a striking bar against a sounding chain in four hundred metres of hard-walled, ringing, almost perfectly empty water, and a hundred kilometres away a wallman with his ear against concrete will hear it as a click. It is slow. It is barely a bandwidth. It is entirely undetectable in the logs because the logs record pressure transients and this is nothing, this is a man hitting a chain with a bar.

The rule of the long line — and there is a rule, and it is followed with a discipline that would astonish the engineers — is never more than three. Three keeps you inside the noise floor. Three is the traffic the world already expects. So the grammar is built out of threes and intervals and silence, and it takes an hour to say almost nothing, and what it is used for, overwhelmingly, is this: births, deaths, and the fact that a particular person is still alive and still down here. Names in threes. A whole cell-floor of strangers, five hundred kilometres apart, who will never meet, passing along the news that somebody's father has died, that a barge has a new child aboard in the Long Green, that the man who fell in year two has been found and was whole.

It is illicit. Everyone does it. Supervisors do it.

And there are unclaimed knocks. There are always unclaimed knocks — patterns in the deep hours that no crew will own, coming, by bearing, out of cells that are drawn down and dry and unstaffed. The engineers say gate gear, and they are almost certainly right, because a system this size is never still and never random, and a dry cell full of cooling steel talks to itself. Almost certainly. The crews still count them. They count everything.

---

**Their customs are younger than their tools.**

That is the real strangeness of the people here, and it takes a while to see. Their folklore has no scour on it. The arrises are still sharp. You can be present at the invention of a superstition on a Tuesday and find it binding by winter, and by the following year find that it is ancient, that it has always been done, that only an idiot would ask where it came from.

Do not whistle in an adit; that is three long, and three long is a drawdown, and a man drowned for that in Cell 12 — except he didn't, that's Cell 17, except nobody can produce a name. Do not count aloud past five. Do not thank a doser for water. Do not name a child for a coordinate you have not yourself worked. Eat the gulls' eggs, never the gulls. Never the cress; everyone knows where it's been.

They date these things the way they date everything down here: by rust. A custom with tears to the skirting came in with the first construction and is unquestionable. A custom with no tear at all was invented the season before last by a foreman who is still alive and will deny it.

---

**And this, in the end, is what they have made.**

There is no natural erosion in the Lower Volumes. There is a faint bright polishing at the outlet throats, a metre or two of it, and that is the entire history of wear in a world nine hundred kilometres across.

Except on the lamp strings.

Every string within reach of a gallery rail, every string that passes a working platform or a mooring or a barge rail, is worn through its paint at about shoulder height, down to bright metal, in the shape of a closing hand. You keep a hand on the string. It is stencilled at every bulkhead as a safety instruction and it stopped being one some time in the second year, when a sounder went into the water alone in the mid-volume and the strings were the only thing in nine hundred kilometres to be found by. Now you touch it leaving a gallery. You touch it before a descent, and coming off a barge, and when the knocks pass four, and when the rain front is walking towards you across a hundred kilometres of flat lit water and there is nothing to do but stand and let it arrive.

Four years of hands. Bright metal, at shoulder height, on every string in the cell.

It is the only mark in this entire manufactured sea that was not put there by a machine, and they made it by believing something, and it is already deep enough to feel in the dark.
