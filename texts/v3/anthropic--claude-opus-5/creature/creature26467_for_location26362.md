## The people

There are fifty-one of them, and in some years fifty-two, and once in a long while fifty. They are small, most of them not much over five feet, with the deep chest and long lungs of high air, and shoulders that are wide out of proportion to everything else — the shoulders of people who have carried flat stone against the body all their lives, one arm under, one arm over, the load pressed to the sternum. Their necks are short. Their hands are broad and the fingertips are flattened and slightly spatulate, with the nail worn low, and on the pad of each right thumb there is a smooth grey callus, dense as horn, which they call the reading thumb, because it is what they test a dressed surface with. You will see two of them meet on a path, and one will take the other's hand and turn it and press the thumb pad, and neither will say anything about it. It is how they ask after each other.

Their skin is weather-darkened on the face and the backs of the hands and nowhere else, and it is finely lined early. Their eyes are grey or brown and they are near-work eyes: they can see a hair's difference in a joint at arm's length in flat cloud light, and they distinguish eleven greys and name them, and can tell the nine lichens apart by colour alone at a stride's distance. But they are poor at distance. When one of them stands at the southern lip and looks out over the terraces at the enormous blue nothing that goes on and on out there, they get a mild swimming feeling behind the eyes, a slight nausea, which they consider ordinary and uninteresting and which is not fear of falling. It is the discomfort of looking at something that cannot be checked.

They wear the goats: undyed felt, grey-black, cut in a single body-piece with a slit for the head, and under it a knitted layer of finer combings. Lichen dyes give them their small colours — rust, a soft ochre, a dull rose from the stone-mouth lichen that only grows on the western posts, and one faint purple, difficult and rationed, which appears only as a band on the left cuff of whoever is keeping the cords that year. Their feet are bare from spring to the first hard frost. The soles are pale and thick as a slab of tallow and they read the rock: every path across the shelf has its own grain of wear, and any of them can walk from the fold to the fifth cistern on a black night at the speed they would walk it at noon, and know exactly where they are by the polish under the ball of the foot.

## Names

There are sixty-three names on the shelf, and there have always been sixty-three, and the sixty-three are kept as knots on a cord in the stone niche in the wall of the public floor. A name is a tied knot. When a person dies the knot is loosened, not untied, not removed — loosened, and hangs slack, and the name is said to be *lying by.* When a child is born the child is given a slack name, usually the one that has lain by longest, and the knot is pulled tight again.

So there is exactly one Menna at a time. Exactly one Orrec, one Sabb, one Ithel, one Dremmen, one Vay. A name is not a person; it is a position that a person occupies for sixty or seventy years, and then vacates. This does not trouble them and they do not find it poetic. The child is not the old woman come back. The child is simply the new Menna, dressed to the same dimension.

There are always more names than people — a dozen slack at any time, sometimes sixteen — and this surplus is the nearest thing the town has to a consolation. There is room. There has always been room.

## What they do all day

Most of it is checking.

Each morning, before the first fog lifts, one person walks the boundary. It takes about half an hour. This is called the round, and it is done by everybody in turn, children included from about the age of six, and it has been done every day for as long as the cords go back. The rounder touches the eleven junipers — not ceremonially, they check the leaning, the crotch of the trunk where frost splits start — lifts the eleven cistern slabs and looks at the groove, runs a hand down each of the six mesh screens to feel for a slackening or a break in the weave, walks the two garden walls, counts the goats, and comes back to the public floor and says either *all at the groove* or else says what is not.

What is not, gets recorded on the cords and gets fixed. That is the entire civic life of the place. There is no chief. There is a cord-keeper, who keeps and reads, and who is chosen because they have the patience for knots and the memory for which anomaly happened in which decade, and the position carries a purple cuff-band and no authority whatsoever.

The rest of the day is stone. A crack found in the round becomes, over the following week, an expedition of four to the quarry notch, a cut, a dressing, a carry, a lift, a fitting, and the retirement of the broken slab into the thickness of a wall. They dress against rods. Every house keeps a rod, a length of juniper heartwood or shin bone marked with the dimensions of its own particular slabs, and every rod is a copy checked against another copy, and once a generation, on no particular date, a delegation goes to the niche and takes out the mother rod — a black length of wood, greasy with handling, older than any building above ground — and checks the copies against it, and puts it back.

## The short rod

They have almost no history. They have this.

Four generations ago, at one of these checkings, a rod was found short. Not by much: by about the thickness of a fingernail over its length. The rod belonged to the mason Turrow, and he had been dressing with it for eleven years.

Eleven slabs had gone into the roofs of the town at very slightly the wrong size.

The town took nine days over it, which is the longest recorded argument on the shelf. The question was not whether Turrow was at fault; he had checked as he was supposed to check, against a copy that was itself wrong, and the wrongness had come from somewhere further back and was never found. The question was whether to pull the eleven slabs.

They left them. They marked them — a single chisel dot on the underside, visible from inside the room — and they wrote the eleven locations into the cords, and every child on the shelf learns the eleven locations as a chant before they learn to dress a face. *Fourth house, north slope, second course. Ninth house, west slope, fourth course.* They can rattle it off. They will tell you about it within an hour of meeting you, with something in the voice that is not quite pride and not quite shame and might be closest to *tenderness.* It is their only irregularity. It is the one place in the whole town where the town is not exactly itself, and they have kept it for four generations, and they will keep it, and when one of those eleven slabs finally cracks with frost they will dress the replacement to the short measure, hair and all, and put the chisel dot back on the underside.

They know that this is inconsistent. They do it anyway. It is the only thing they do that has no reason.

## The old

The old do the smoothing.

When a person's hands begin to shake too much for dressing, they are given the cisterns. They go down the stair with a bag of grit and a hide pad and a lamp of goat fat, and they lie on their side in the cold hollow chamber above the waterline, and they rub. A season's work moves a hand's breadth. The insides of those eleven cisterns are smooth as the inside of a mouth because for three hundred years the dying have lain in them in the dark, by feel, taking off nothing that could be measured, and the water that the whole shelf drinks comes up tasting of stone and of ferns and of that.

An old person will spend eight or nine years down there, a few hours a day. Toward the end they are carried down and carried up. They talk while they work — you can hear it faintly through the floor of the house above, a low unhurried voice under the drip — and nobody has ever thought to write down what they say.

## Death

Loosen the knot. Carry the body down the north shoulder to the fourth terrace, to a corbelled cell no bigger than a kennel, and leave it, and come back up. In time, when the cell is wanted, the bones are gathered and brought up in a basket and go into the rubble fill of whatever wall is open that year. *Into the thickness.* It is the same phrase they use for a cracked slab.

Grief here is practical and takes a specific form: you take up the dead person's intervals. Every cistern has its drip and every drip has its rhythm, and children are set to listen to them and to know them, and adults keep the knowledge all their lives; it is how they navigate, and it is also the shelf's nervous system, because a drip that changes its interval means something has moved in the rock. When someone dies, whoever loved them most goes and sits in that person's house with the slab up and listens for a whole day, learning their water. That is the mourning. It is not spoken of as mourning.

## Children

There are usually two children on the shelf. Sometimes one. Once in a long while four, which the cords record as a hard decade.

A single child is a lonely thing and the town knows it, and so the child gets the work that suits one: the round, the listening, the counting of choughs in the afternoon, the carrying of small tools between working parties, all of which involve walking the whole town all day and being spoken to briefly and kindly by everyone in it. By ten they know fifty-one people better than most people know their families. They are dressed, not raised — the word is the mason's word, and it means the same thing: brought slowly to a dimension, checked, brought closer, checked.

Adolescents go strange here, the same way they go strange everywhere. It shows up as wanting to go down. The town has an answer for it, and the answer is: down, but not that way. A restless fifteen-year-old is given the underneath — a lamp, a short adze, a basket, and permission to open one of the buried thresholds in the cellars and find out what is behind it. Nothing has ever been demolished here; the town rose on itself; there are three floors of older town packed under the newer, stairs that arrive at ceilings, doorways sealed by the floor of a room that was built over them two hundred years ago. Excavating it is useless in every practical sense. It produces nothing but rubble, which is always wanted, and knowledge, which is put on the cords. It gives the restless somewhere to push against that is not the edge. Every generation, one or two of them spend four years in the dark under the town, and come up with a map, and after that they are usually all right.

## The descender

Copper comes up the mountain. Reed came up, once. Somebody has to go.

Roughly every forty years the cords say that the copper is low, and a descender is chosen — early thirties, good lungs, no children yet, and a temperament the town has been quietly assessing since childhood. They are given a list on a knotted string, and a load of lichen dye-cake and eleven dressed lamp-shells and whatever else is currently tradeable, and they walk off the fourth side of the shelf down the terraces, and they are gone between two months and a year.

They come back with what is on the list. Exactly what is on the list. This is the rule and it is absolute: anything extra goes over the edge or into the rubble. A descender came back once with a bronze bell and it went into the fill of the goat fold wall, where it still is, and where children knock on the stone to hear it not ring.

They also come back with everything they saw, and there is no place to put that. Nobody asks. It is not a prohibition, exactly; it is that the town has no cord for it, no groove, no rod. The descender lives another forty or fifty years, dresses stone, walks the round, smooths a cistern, and dies with all of it still in them, and it goes into the thickness. The town is not cruel about this. In the evenings the descender sometimes gets a look, and people notice the look, and they bring them something, and sit with them a while, and talk about the day's stones.

## The others who live here

**The dog.** There is one dog. It is always called Ban. It is grey-black like the goats, it works the fold, it sleeps in whichever doorway it prefers that year, and it does the round with whoever is rounding. When a dog dies a bitch is bred from the neighbouring — no. There is no neighbouring. A dog is on the descender's list, once every three or four descents, and in between there are litters and the town keeps one pup and the others are a difficulty that is quietly handled at the edge, and this is the single thing on the shelf they do not discuss even among themselves.

**Eleven goats.** They are milked, combed, and eaten at the rate of about one a year. Their bells are wrapped in wool so as not to interfere with the drips.

**The choughs**, who do not live here, and who are therefore the most interesting inhabitants. They arrive over the drop in the afternoons, three or four of them, glossy, red-billed, insolently good at flying, and they walk about on the roofs looking into the smoke holes with their heads on one side, and they steal small bright things, and they leave. The town's entire body of comedy concerns the choughs. They are held to be frivolous, unserious, badly brought up, and possessed of opinions. Anyone who is being showy is told they are *flying like a chough.* When a chough takes something — a copper needle, once, catastrophically — everyone is delighted for a decade.

**Nine lichens**, named, mapped, harvested on a rotation of years, and one of them so slow that the cords say it is worked twice a century.

**Eleven junipers**, low, clenched, leaning east, not growing and not dying, each with a name that is not one of the sixty-three.

**A slow black beetle** under the stones, and pale spiders, and in the wet lines the ferns, and in some years the rumour of a fox.
