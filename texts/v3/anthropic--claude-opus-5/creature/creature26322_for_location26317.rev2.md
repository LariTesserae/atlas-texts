## Census

Begin with the fact that everything here can be counted.

In most places a census is a courtesy — an estimate, a shrug with numbers in it. On the Drone Ground it is arithmetic. There are sixty-eight columns. There are two hundred and forty-one chambers in them dry enough and dark enough to be lived in. There are, in most years, two hundred and forty-one stoppers. There are six mosses, nine lichens, one liverwort in the gutters, one cushion plant, one cup-plant, one algal mat. There is one moth. There is one bird, and the bird does not live here.

A population that does not move is not really a population. It is a furnishing.

## The Stoppers

The name is a visitor's. It is also a joke a visitor made once about organ stops, and the joke is better than it deserves to be, and nothing on this plateau knows it.

Take one in your hand. It will let you. A stopper is the size of a fist and the weight of a wet sponge, pale grey-white going faintly pink at the ears and the webbing, the exact colour of the inside of a column, and its pelt is so fine that from a pace away it looks dusty rather than furred. It is not dusty. Breathe on it and the fog-beads it has been carrying all morning run together and slide off in a single drop. Underneath is a second coat, dense as felt, which is the whole reason a warm-blooded thing this small can exist three thousand metres up and not burn itself out by noon.

Six limbs. The middle pair are the climbers: long, thin, ending in recurved claws with a gape that matches the interior fluting of the columns to within a millimetre, because the fluting was the builder's plumbing and the claw is a key cut for a lock in a dead animal's body. The hind pair are short and broad and are mostly for sitting on. The front pair are the trowels — stubby, thick-wristed, each with one opposable digit and a horny plate along the outer edge worn smooth and slightly concave from a lifetime of ramming moss into cracks. An old stopper's forelimbs are polished like the handle of a tool.

The eyes are small and dark and adequate for the gloom of an interior and not much else. The ears are closed. Not damaged — closed, sealed over in the womb, a heritable abandonment, because in a town that has been shouting for a million years the air is no longer a medium for information. It is only weather.

What they have instead is in the wrists. A paired organ of fluid and bone, and a habit that goes with it: to learn anything, a stopper lays both forelimbs flat against the stone, spreads the digits, and lowers its head until the chin nearly touches. Visitors call this praying. It is closer to opening a door. The whole town is one fused calcareous body from the north cluster outward, and everything that happens to any part of it arrives, attenuated, everywhere else.

But not during the drone. During the drone the stone is drowned — saturated edge to edge with the wind's own roar, three fundamentals beating against each other, a bath of noise in which no small message can be distinguished. For three hundred and fifty-five days a year a stopper is effectively alone inside a wall of standing thunder, working by touch on what is directly in front of it.

They do not startle. Nothing has hunted them in longer than the columns have been their present height, and the response has simply been deleted, the way the ears were. Hold one and it does not struggle. It will locate the pulse in your thumb, settle its wrists against it, and go still. It is not trust and it is not terror. It is the thing they do when the stone says something they do not recognise.

## The Work

All spring they climb and pack. Wads of the copper sheet moss, torn from the sound-shadow mats, carried up the shafts one at a time in the crook of a forelimb, and rammed into cracks, side-vents, the old perforations of the builder's circulation. A stopper will spend forty days on a single vent, packing and repacking, testing it with its wrists between courses, adding, taking away, adding. What it is testing cannot be determined from outside. What can be determined is that in July the town stands a minor third below where it stood in February, and that the stoppers are the reason, and that not one of them has ever heard a note.

All autumn they pull the wads out again.

The wads come out heavy. Damp moss packed into a warm dark crack is the entire world of the moth larvae, and an autumn wad is studded with fat pupae like currants, and this is how a stopper eats. So the tuning is also the harvest, and the harvest is also the demolition, and the demolition is also next spring's raw material, and none of these is the reason for any of the others; they are one behaviour seen from four angles.

They are not farmers. They are farmed at, by a life cycle that has quietly arranged itself around their housekeeping.

## Tenancy

There are two hundred and forty-one chambers and that number is the government.

A stopper without a chamber does not breed. Females come into condition only after they have held a dry cavity through a full fog-season, and cavities become available when their holders die, and holders die rarely, because a fist-sized animal with no predators, a low fire and a long torpor can live sixty years and sometimes eighty. So a birth follows a death by a season or two, one for one, and has done for as long as there has been anything here to count.

The dead builder regulates their fertility. It has no opinion about this. It has no opinions.

The young are single, born blind in the deep cold, carried clinging in the undercoat for most of a year, and weaned onto pupae mashed in the forelimb plate. A juvenile learns the interior of one column before it learns there are others.

## Names

Every stopper produces an idiosyncratic rhythm when it taps: a signature, three to nine beats, personal as a gait. This is how they are addressed — you say someone by doing them.

The rhythms go with the chambers. When a holder dies and a new tenant takes the cavity, the new tenant takes the rhythm, and within a season is producing it as if it had always been theirs. Nobody teaches this and nobody could. The consequence is that there are two hundred and forty-one names on the Drone Ground and every one of them is older than every one of the animals wearing it, older than the present height of the columns, older, probably, than the mosses in the northern gardens. The names do not descend. They stay put. They are attached to rooms.

## The Still Mornings

Ten times a year, give or take, the wind fails at dawn.

The drone thins, sags, and stops, and the stone goes clear the way water goes clear when the silt settles, and for two or three hours the town is a single instrument for listening instead of a single instrument for shouting. And they come out. Not quickly — nothing here is sharp, not even leaving — but within half an hour of the silence arriving there will be a hundred and fifty pale bodies out on the pavement of the plazas and in the mouths of the avenues, spaced roughly evenly, wrists down, heads low, motionless.

What travels through the floor on a still morning is everyone. Every signature in the town, overlapping, attenuated by distance and by the number of joints between here and there, a slow braided roll of tapping that dies away and starts again. It goes on for as long as the calm does. Then a gust crosses the rim, the fundamentals come up out of the ground like a tide coming in under a boat, the stone drowns, and they go back inside, unhurriedly, and are alone again until the next one.

A visitor will want to call this a ceremony, a parliament, a service. What it is is the only time of year the telephone works. Whether the difference matters here is the same question as whether the town is a ruin, and has the same answer.

## The Layers

They die in their chambers and nobody carries them out.

Moss goes over the body. The next tenant packs fresh moss on top of the old and lives above it. Over centuries a chamber floor rises a hand's breadth, then a forearm, then the ceiling becomes too low and the chamber goes out of the census for a few thousand years until erosion takes the column's top down and opens a new one below. Break a stub open and you will find the fill banded — thin brown seams of compressed bedding and compressed tenant, each seam a life, calcifying slowly into the general fabric.

The town is partly made of its inhabitants. Not as a figure of speech. As a percentage.

## The Moth

Grey, greasy, thumb-sized, deaf, and it does not steer.

It hatches in a wall, eats the wad it was laid in, pupates, and emerges into a town whose air is not uniform. Sixty-eight pipes sounding at once produce a fixed architecture of loudness — bands where the pressure is violent, and between them narrow quiet corridors, nodes, seams of comparative calm that run along the avenues and pool in the lee of the cistern domes. The moth has a pelt of fine hairs on its underside and no more idea of navigation than a leaf has. It flies straight and is pushed. Loud places shove it, quiet places let it be, and so it is sorted, without ever making a decision, into the nulls.

The nulls are also where the fog lingers, because moving air scours and still air holds. The fog is where the moss is. The moth arrives at the moss the way water arrives at the bottom of a hill.

The town breathes its moths into its own gardens. It has no idea it is doing this either.

## The Slow Ones

The blue-grey crust on the floor of the third plaza is one organism, about two metres across, and it was that size when the northern cluster was four storeys taller. A century, for it, is a damp afternoon. The drone is not a sound to it and not even weather; it is a constant, like gravity, one of the conditions under which the world is simply built.

The copper sheet moss grows only where the sound does not reach, so the mats are a map of the noise, printed in vegetation. The stoppers strip the mats and repack the vents and the noise shifts, and four or five hundred years later the mats have shifted too, and the harvest is in slightly different places, and the tuning changes to match. It is a feedback loop with a period longer than most civilisations and it has gone round enough times to have no beginning.

In the darkest cistern, under the dome grille, the cup-plants are one clone. One individual, one genotype, filling a tank the size of a bath, opening and closing with the light for a length of time nobody has any way to establish. It is almost certainly the largest living thing on the plateau, and you could lift all of it out in two hands.

## The One With an Elsewhere

The grey broad-wing does not live here. It nests eleven kilometres down and three thousand metres lower, on a wall of red rock above a river, and it has a mate and it raises two chicks a year on lizards and nestlings and whatever the valley gives.

Four or five times a season it takes the thermal off the rim because it has learned that on top of the mountain there is a place where the air holds moths in lines. It circles twice, reading the lines, takes one out of the seam, and goes back down over the shoulder. Eight minutes. It has never landed and never will; there is nothing up here it wants and the sound, to an animal that still hears, is a wall.

It is the only creature on the Drone Ground that knows the Drone Ground has an edge — that knows it is a place, with an outside. It holds this knowledge the way you hold the shape of a table you never sit at.

## Those Who Do Not Arrive

Seeds come up on every warm afternoon. Grass, sedge, thistledown, three kinds of pine, the whole ambitious dust of the valley, and a spider a month on a thread of silk, and beetles, and pollen in enough quantity to yellow the cisterns in June.

None of it establishes. The ring-gardens are full — full at the root, full in the soil, full in the census — and fullness is a more complete refusal than hostility. The plateau is not killing them. It simply has no vacancy, and has had none for longer than the species now holding the leases have existed in their present form.

You are in the same category. You can walk up here, and drink from a cistern, and pick up a stopper and be regarded through your thumb, and spend a still morning among two hundred kneeling animals. But you cannot sleep through a gale here — nothing that hears can — and on the fourth day you will go down over the shoulder in the same direction as the bird, for the same reason, which is that everything with ears eventually leaves.

## The Actual Majority

A millimetre inside the stone, in the pore space, there is a film of living cells. Split a fresh piece and the cut face is faintly green a hair's depth below the white.

They are the oldest tenants. They were in the rock before the builder grew on it and they quietly ate the edges of the builder's body while it was still alive, and they are eating it now, and when the columns are ankle-high and the drone has fallen below hearing into a pressure you can only feel in the sternum, they will still be there, requiring nothing except that the surface be wetted twice a day, which the valley will go on doing.

If you want to know what lives on the Drone Ground, the honest answer is that the rock does, faintly, and always has. Everything else is a detail of the last few million years.

Still: at dusk the cloud comes up over the rim and moves through the columns as a wet grey crowd, and in a chamber four metres up in the north cluster something the size of a fist puts both wrists flat against the wall and lowers its head, and reads nothing but wind.

That has always been enough.
