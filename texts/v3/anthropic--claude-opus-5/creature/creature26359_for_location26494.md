## The hands

Thirty-one. Nine of those are children and two are past work, which leaves twenty for a plant that needs eighteen. The ledger's attendance column, which runs back further than anyone can read the hand it starts in, has never shown fewer than twenty-two nor more than thirty-eight, and in the fifth month it drops to eleven or twelve for three weeks every single year because in the fifth month everyone has the fever, and the fever is not written as a fever. It is written as *half hands, usual*.

They are small people, mostly, and they are the colour of things kept out of the sun. Not pale exactly — the light in here is green and thick and it lays a tint on skin the way it lays a tint on brick — but unweathered, unfreckled, smooth in a way that reads as young until you get close to the eyes. A broker who comes up the head slough twice a year with an order book has been dealing with the same four adults for eleven years and has never been able to put an age on any of them, and has never asked, because asking would mean shouting.

The exceptions are the two in the canopy, who are brown.

## What the works has done to their bodies

Hands first. Every adult here has the rope-maker's glaze: not the split, horny callus of an axeman but a smooth amber plating across the palm and the inner fingers, worn by fibre rather than by tools, so hard and so slick that they cannot pick a coin off a plank and have small hooks and picks for the work their fingertips will no longer do. Nails worn back to the quick and squared off. The retters' forearms are dyed to just past the elbow, black-brown, tannin driven so far in that it does not come out on the dead and does not come out on the bones; and this is understood to be the most beautiful thing a person can have on them, and it is the reason a retter's sleeves are rolled at the wedding and not at the work.

Feet next, and feet are the trouble. Everything above the ankle here is either wet or moving and does not rot. The ankle is the tide line of a person. Their soles carry the same horizontal band of ruin that the buildings carry at knee height: cracks, macerated seams between the toes, a white sodden softness that opens if it is not attended to. So the works has one dry hour, and it is the last hour of the light, and it happens in the boiler house — everybody in on the brick, feet out toward the fireboxes, alum and talc and lampblack going into the cracks, and the smell of thirty pairs of hot dry feet is the only smell on the terrace that is not the tanks. That hour is the social hour. Grievances get settled there. Betrothals get proposed there. A child who wants to be told something waits until the feet are out.

Their gait: they walk on the outside edges of every step without thinking, and they walk backwards very well. The spinner pays fibre out of a bundle at the waist and walks backwards down the ninety-two metres to do it, and everyone has spun, so everyone can go backwards at speed down the Walk without turning to look, and the children race that way, and a stranger watching people reverse smoothly past each other in a steaming green nave at a fast walk finds it more unsettling than anything else here.

And their hands are always on the building. Handrail, post, boiler casing, the flank of the engine. They read the works through their fingers because they cannot hear it well — everyone over thirty is deaf in the band the engine occupies and unnaturally sharp above it — and the vibration tells them what the noise used to tell them. The cart's rail joints come every four seconds. *Four seconds* is their small unit of time; you are told to wait three passes, or six. When the interval slips to four and a half, everyone within the works knows before the engineer does, and they know it through their palms, and the whole terrace turns its head north at once like a herd.

A person walking with both hands free, touching nothing, is either brought in from outside or is ill.

## Names, and when they are used

During work there are no names. You cannot deliver a name over the engine, so a working person is their station — Tanks, Hackle, Jack, Top, High, Firer — and the stations are addressed in sign, one-handed, close to the body, twenty-odd signs that cover everything the day requires and nothing it does not. The signs are ugly and fast and do not include a sign for *please*.

The engine is shut for six hours in the middle of the night, and it is in those six hours that people have names. The silence is enormous and nobody is used to it after however many decades, and so in the quiet watch everyone speaks very softly, almost mouth-to-ear, out of a kind of embarrassment, and this is when they say each other's names, and argue, and sing. Because they cannot sing in the day they have learned to sing with a hand flat on the other person's sternum, and they do that in the quiet watch too, out of habit, when there is no need.

Then the boilers come up and the names go away until the next night. Children are told that a name is a thing you keep in the dark so it doesn't go soft.

## The offices

**The engineer.** Not a bloodline, an appointment, made on the basis of whether a candidate can hear the engine — meaning feel it, meaning wake out of sleep at a change of one part in forty. The current one is a woman in her sixties who took it from a man who was not related to her, and she keeps the ledger, and the ledger is the memory of this place because the people are not. Nobody here remembers anything more than sixty years old. The column remembers. She also holds the quinine, the alum, the needle, and the lampblack, so she is the doctor by default, and her doctoring is the doctoring of a machinist: keep it wet, keep it moving, watch the boundary.

**The high pair.** Two, always two, chosen for lungs and light bones and for not minding, and they are the only brown people on the terrace and the only ones who smell of tar and cut sap instead of the tanks. They eat at their own hours. They sleep in a hut at the north end. They speak to each other in whistles that carry over the engine and that nobody else has ever bothered to learn, and they have three hundred words for canopy conditions of which the rest of the works uses four. They are the only people who see the terrace whole, and the only people who have seen the horizon: forty kilometres of unmanaged bamboo, and on a clear morning in the dry months, once or twice a year, a white glare in the east that is the sea. They do not discuss this. Each of them tells their apprentice about it exactly once.

**The night-firer.** One person awake in the six quiet hours, banking two boilers, dry and warm and alone, and every child in the history of this works has crept in to sleep on that brick, and every night-firer has let them, and it is not written down anywhere and never has been.

## Time

They do not count in years. They count in cuts, the fourteen-year rotation, and a person is four cuts old or six cuts old, and a rope is spun with a marker yarn of one colour per year of the fourteen, so any cable that leaves this place can be read for its year by anyone who knows the cycle, which is to say by no one outside this terrace. When a frayed hawser comes back up the slough on the broker's barge for inspection they open the strand and read the marker and pass it around, and it is the nearest thing they have to receiving a letter, and they talk about it for weeks: *that's a Two, that's the wet Two, that's the year the jack-wheel went.*
