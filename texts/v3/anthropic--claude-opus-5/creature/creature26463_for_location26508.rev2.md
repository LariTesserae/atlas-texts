## The Staff

Ask the question the right way and the hollow answers it, which is to say: ask it in February, at four in the morning.

Not in July, when the place is crowded and everything with wings is passing through and it is easy to mistake traffic for population. Ask in the dark, with the bowl drifted full and the fog frozen out of the air onto every surface as a coat of rime, with the pond lidded and the braids under a metre of snow, with the mill throttled back so far that the bass note has dropped almost out of hearing and only the pipe is still working, patient, under pressure, unattended. Stand there then and count the tenants.

The count is not zero. It is not large, either. But it is exactly the count that matters, because everything else you saw in July was a customer.

---

**The customers first, so they can be set aside.**

The wheatear that owned the largest boulder is in the Sahel. He held that boulder for eleven weeks, sang at it inaudibly, raised four, and left, and the distance between the stone he was shouting from and the thornscrub he is now hopping through is such that it would take you four months to walk it. He will be back on approximately the fourteenth of May. The boulder does not know him.

The snow buntings are down on some coast, in flocks of six hundred, eating tideline seed. The geese that left the droppings are a continent south. The skua is at sea, and has been for months, and will not touch land again until it wants to. The fox whose tracks crossed the moraine crest in silt does not den here — there is nothing to den in, no soil to take a spade of, no bank that isn't loose rubble that would come down on her. She dens forty minutes away over the ridge, in ground with roots holding it, and she comes through the hollow the way a person cuts through a car park.

And the bumblebee queen who works the willow for four days in June, hairy as a thumb, shivering her flight muscles up to operating temperature before every takeoff, so that you hear her warming before you see her move — she nests elsewhere too. She is a commuter with a long round trip and a short season, and the hollow is a fuel stop with two pumps.

All of these are here. None of them lives here. Residency, in a place like this, is not measured by who eats but by who is present on the night nothing is available.

---

**The hunters arrived before the food did.**

This is the first genuinely strange thing about the hollow's actual population, and it is strange in the way that a solved crime is strange: it makes no sense, and then it makes complete sense, and then it is worse.

Under the flat stones along the waterline, in the dark wet grit where the meltwater has cut its under-shelf, there are ground beetles. Small, matt black, long-legged, built like sprinters, with the reduced wings of an insect that has permanently given up on distance. By day they are pressed into the cold under-stone with their antennae folded. At night, or in the grey that passes for night, they come out and run on the snow and the ice itself, hunting, at temperatures that would stall any beetle from the valley. They are among the very first animals to occupy ground that has been out from under the ice for under a decade — earlier than the mosses, decades earlier than anything that could be called a plant community.

With them, a wolf spider. Grey-brown, fingernail-sized, no web, hunting by ambush and pounce on the open gravel floors where there is nothing at all growing. In July she drags an egg sac the size of a peppercorn under her abdomen, and she spends the warmest hours of the day hauling it onto the one dark stone in fifteen paces, angling herself so the sac takes the light, incubating it against the only warm object in the hollow, which is a rock she has selected. When the spiderlings hatch they ride on her back in a grey pile until they can hunt, and then they disperse on silk threads into a landscape that contains nothing for them to eat.

Which is the point. There is no prey base here. There is no vegetation to support a herbivore community to support a predator. These two live on *fallout* — on what the wind delivers. Aphids lifted off a valley meadow and carried up and dropped. Midges chilled out of the air. Moths, hoverflies, spiders' own ballooning young, seeds, mites, the general fine rain of exhausted small flying things that any large cold surface skims out of the atmosphere the way a weir skims a river. The glacier is a collector. The hollow is where the collection is emptied. The beetle and the spider are the staff of a lost property office, and they have been in post since the year the gravel appeared, and they do not need the ecosystem to arrive before they can start, because they are not part of it. They are eating somewhere else, at second hand, in arrears.

---

**The ice has its own animal.**

The glacier midges are not visitors. They are the hollow's proper livestock, and they do the whole of their business inside its noise.

The larvae are in the braids: red-brown, finger-joint long, in the shallow flour-laden rush within a hundred metres of the portal, where the water is at a fraction of a degree above freezing and so loaded with suspended rock that it would abrade the gills off anything from a lowland stream. They hold on. They graze the thin brown biofilm off the tops of the stable cobbles, which is the only food produced in the hollow itself rather than imported. They are essentially the only insect there. Go a kilometre downstream, where the water has warmed by two degrees and dropped half its load, and you will find six or eight other kinds; go back up to the portal and it is one genus, alone, in numbers.

The adults hatch and walk. Their wings are short and they scarcely use them, because in a place where the wind comes down off the ice every afternoon, a flying insect is an insect being removed. So they walk on the snow and on the dirty apron, black and deliberate, at a pace you can watch, meeting each other on a surface at the freezing point, mating on ice, dying on ice within a few days, and going grey and staying whole where they fall. You will find last August's individuals in the rime, intact, legs folded, indistinguishable from this morning's.

They are what the birds come for. The entire avian traffic of the hollow — the wagtail's flicking, the buntings' eleven minutes, the wheatear's summer — is underwritten by these midges and by the fallout the wind brings, and the midges are underwritten by a brown film of diatoms and bacteria on wet cobbles. Four links, and three of them are in the millimetre range.

---

**There are ten thousand towns on the apron and each one has a founding date.**

The cryoconite holes are not incidental. They are excavated. That granular black sludge at the bottom of each — the whole reason the hole exists — is a manufactured object, and the manufacturer lives inside it.

Filamentous cyanobacteria grow through the windblown dust and secrete a sticky matrix, and the matrix glues mineral grains to itself, and the filaments grow through the new layer and glue on the next, so that over years the loose dust is consolidated into a rounded granule with a layered interior, like the smallest possible stromatolite, like a peppercorn made of soot and glue. It takes a decade or more to build one. It is darker than the ice around it, so it absorbs sunlight, so it warms, so it melts down into the surface, and it keeps melting down until it is deep enough that the light reaching it balances the light hitting the ice around it, at which point it stops and holds its depth. The community drills its own shaft and then maintains the shaft at the correct depth by the simple expedient of being black.

Inside: a cylinder of clear meltwater with white walls, a few millimetres of black floor, and a full society. Bdelloid rotifers, spooling their wheel-organs, every one of them female and a member of a lineage in which no male has been recorded within the entire span of their existence as a group. Tardigrades, blunt and slow, grazing the granule surfaces with a pair of stylets, able to curl into a barrel and stop when the hole freezes solid and resume in the spring exactly where they left off, so that an individual's life of a few months may be distributed across five calendar years. Nematodes. Copepods the size of commas. Ciliates. Bacteria at a density that makes the sludge, materially, mostly bacteria.

Each hole was founded on a particular day by a particular grain of dust. Each has a distinct population — measurably distinct, hole to hole, three metres apart. Each has its own chemistry and its own water and its own history, and most of them are isolated for most of the year by the ice between them.

And each of them is under sentence. The apron they are drilled into is melting from the top at the rate of centimetres a day in summer, and moving, and cracking. Sooner or later every hole's wall is breached, or its floor is undercut, or a crevasse opens beneath it, and the whole sealed republic — granules, rotifers, tardigrades, ten years of construction — is flushed sideways into a surface stream, over the lip, down a moulin or out under the arch, and into the mill. Which grinds. Which does not distinguish between a stone and a town.

So: the hollow contains ten thousand civilisations, all of them on a conveyor, all of them facing the same direction, and the pale milk coming out of the portal is, among other things, their remains going downstream in suspension.

---

**The only architecture in the hollow is thumb-deep and green.**

Everything said about the place's unbuiltness — no bolt, no weld, no straight line — is true at human scale and false at the scale of a moss cushion.

The cushions on the seepage lines are structures. They are made of individually negligible shoots packed so densely that they behave as a single body, and the body does three things no single shoot could: it holds water by capillarity long after the seep has slackened, it damps the temperature so that the interior in sun can run six or eight degrees above the air, and it traps every particle of blowing silt that touches it. That last one is the beginning of soil, and it is the only manufacturing process in the hollow that adds rather than subtracts. The mill takes rock and makes flour. The moss takes flour and makes ground.

Squeeze a cushion and you get half a cup of water and, in it, the tenants. Springtails, grey, rebounding off the meniscus with a flick of the forked spring they keep cocked under the abdomen; some of the same tardigrade and rotifer lineages that are in the cryoconite, doing the same job in a different building; oribatid mites, slow, armoured, black, the gravediggers, such as they are; nematodes; and through it all the felt of cyanobacteria that also plates the wet stones outside, fixing nitrogen out of the air, because there is none in the rock, and without them there is no nitrogen in this hollow at all and therefore nothing green, ever.

That black felt on the wet stones is the most consequential resident of the surface. It is also the least noticeable thing in the entire bowl.

---

**And there is an elder.**

The dwarf willow, three fingers high, pencil-thick, forty-odd rings, lying flat along the gravel with its leaves turned up — that individual has been in that exact spot longer than any bird has been alive, longer than most of the moss cushions, longer than the current course of every braid in the outflow, and possibly longer than the pond has held water at its present level. It is the closest thing the hollow has to a building, in the sense that it is a made structure that persists across decades and shelters things: there is a mite fauna in its litter, a leaf-miner some years, aphids in a good July, and the bumblebee's four days in June are entirely about its catkins.

It has survived by refusing height. Everything that stands up here is either broken by wind, desiccated by wind, or left above the snowline in winter and killed by exposure in the dark. The willow stays under the snow, where it is minus four instead of minus thirty, and it spends the eight months it cannot grow simply being buried. Its strategy is not endurance. Its strategy is to be beneath the level at which the place operates.

---

**The permanent staff are under the floor.**

Everything above is recent. That is the hollow's own stated rule, and it holds: no fossils, no ruins, no bones older than this year, a ceiling on history, and the ceiling is low.

But the rule describes the surface, and the surface is the lid.

Two hundred metres away and a hundred metres down, at the bed, in permanent dark, at the pressure of the whole overburden, stones are being dragged across bedrock. Each drag breaks mineral surfaces open. A freshly fractured silicate is not an inert thing: broken bonds react with water, and the reaction yields hydrogen, and hydrogen is food. The flour is not waste. The flour is ration. Iron and sulphur come out of the ground pyrite in forms that can be respired. And the ice, when it advanced, rode over an older landscape and sealed it — soil, peat, root mat, the organic body of whatever grew here before — and that buried carbon is down there in the dark under compression, being ground and eaten by a community that has never needed light, never needed summer, and never needed to be re-founded by immigrants, because it never left.

They are chemolithotrophs and fermenters and sulphate reducers and methanogens, and they are the mill's actual workforce, and they have been employed continuously through the retreat, through the last advance, through the warm centuries and the cold ones, in a habitat whose only requirement — rock, water, pressure, dark — the glacier has never once failed to supply.

The milk that comes out of the portal carries them out by the hundred million per millilitre. That is what the pond is. Not a clean grey lens of water in a clean grey bowl of stone: an effluent stream with a population in it, a daily export of citizens from the basement into the light, where the great majority of them stop. The reason you cannot see the bottom is partly flour and partly this.

So the answer to who lives in the hollow is not the birds, which commute; nor the beetle and the spider, which are living off somewhere else's dead; nor the midges, which are the ice's one animal and last a week; nor the cryoconite republics, all of which are on the conveyor; nor the moss, which will be moved when the seep moves; nor even the willow, which is only old by local standards.

It is the ones under the floor, whom you will never see, who are older than the hollow, older than the surface, older than the retreat, who have no summer and no night and no weather, who eat the grinding itself, and who are being flushed out into your pond at a rate of tonnes of rock and billions of bodies a day and replaced from below without any interruption whatever.

Everything up here is the beginning of something, again, provisionally.

Down there is the one thing in the place that has simply been continuing — and it does not know the hollow exists, and it never comes up, and it will still be at it when the moraine has slumped and the pond has silted and the grass has joined up and the mill has gone quiet and moved on uphill, taking its staff with it, underneath, in the dark, still working.
