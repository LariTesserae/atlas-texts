# The Tenants

## Begin at the Floor, Because Everything Ends There

The most numerous animal in this world is a pale thing the length of a thumb joint, with eleven pairs of legs and no head worth the name. There are perhaps four hundred of them per square metre across two thousand square kilometres of peat, which is a number that stops meaning anything, so: imagine the floor is not a floor. Imagine it is a slow-moving fabric made of appetite, one centimetre thick, laid over everything.

They are called nothing. Call them sifters.

A sifter has no eye tissue anywhere in its lineage, not vestigial, not shut, simply never re-derived after the last ancestor that had one drowned in the dark forty million years ago. What it has instead: a comb of chemical filaments along the underside, and pressure pits, and a belly plate that reads the low notes coming up through the peat. It works the fresh fall — the day-and-a-half-old snow of dead ceiling — passing it through the comb, taking the bacteria off it, passing it out again. Everything it eats has already been eaten. Everything it excretes will be eaten again by something smaller, and again, and again, until what is left is chemically boring enough to be buried.

Its entire life happens inside a circle forty metres wide. Its entire species migrates, over centuries, following the depositional grain — the fall does not land evenly, the slow deep currents steer it into drifts and thin places, and the fabric of appetite creeps toward the drifts at roughly the speed of a growing fingernail.

Below them, a few centimetres down, is the line. The sifters taste its edge constantly and back off constantly. Below the line nothing rots, nothing is eaten, nothing moves; the record of every animal that has ever lived here lies stacked in order, uncorrupted, indexed by depth. Occasionally a tunneller cuts too deep and comes up wrong — slack-limbed, drifting off the floor, rolling, no longer sifting. Something eats it before it recovers, if it was going to recover. This has been happening at a low constant rate forever and it is not a sign of anything.

Hold that thought.

## The Rasp

Every column wears a living coat.

The grazers are flat, palm-sized, blind, with a rasping plate underneath and a foot that grips polished bark so well that current cannot take them and a drummer's strike barely can. Two million of them on a big column, arranged in bands, migrating up and down the nine hundred metres on cycles of nine or ten days, chasing the bacterial film that grows on the sap the stem sweats. They have ground the grazing stripes into the bark themselves. Those stripes are the only history anything here writes on purpose, and it isn't on purpose.

Here is the good part. A column is an island. Crossing between them means letting go, and letting go means falling, and the gap is four hundred metres of open black. So each of the eight hundred columns carries its own population, slightly its own shape, slightly its own chemistry, diverging for longer than the animals have had names, which is forever, since they have never had names. Eight hundred private nations of the same animal, none of which will ever meet as nations.

And yet none of the columns is ever empty, because of the falls. A grazer knocked loose cannot swim. It tumbles, lands somewhere on the enormous soft floor, and then does the one extraordinary thing in its repertoire: it walks, for days, toward a pitch. Toward, specifically, the pitch of the column it was born on, which it cannot find, so it settles for the nearest thing that sounds like home. The note is the address. The note has always been the address. A grazer that lands equidistant between two columns of similar pitch will stand still for a long time, and then choose, and the choice is the most significant act any grazer will ever perform.

## The Ropes

Where each column meets the floor, the felt heaps into a soft skirt full of burrows, and in the burrows live the long-bodied hunters: two metres, eel-shaped, eyeless, skinned in pressure receptors from snout to tail.

They eat the fallen.

A grazer coming off a column at four hundred metres makes a specific sound — not a splash, a stutter; a flat body tumbling end over end generates a pulse train that slows as it spreads. A rope reads that train, computes where the tumble will terminate, leaves its burrow, and is waiting at the landing site before the grazer arrives.

This is prediction. It is the only prediction in this world. Nothing else here anticipates anything; everything else waits. The ropes are, by a considerable margin, the cleverest animals in the sea, and they have used that cleverness for eleven million years to arrive slightly early at a specific patch of mud.

## The Netters

Between the columns, one every hundred metres or so through nine hundred vertical metres of nothing, the netters hang.

An adult netter is a mouth, a gut, and a thread. It begins life swimming — a brief larval fortnight, the only travelling it will ever do — then finds a patch of ceiling, bores a holdfast into the felt, and pays out twenty or fifty metres of secreted fibre and never moves again. Every day it spreads a mucus net and every day it hauls the net in and eats it, catch and all: larvae, drift, the small upward-swimming things that go to the ceiling for reasons of their own.

The thread is not only a tether. It is a tensioned line four microns thick strung between a living roof and a hanging weight, and a tensioned line conducts. Through it the netter feels the storm drumming overhead, continuously, the way you feel your own pulse: not as information, as background. And through it, very rarely, it feels something else.

When a column is failing — and they fail perhaps once a century, over a decade or two of hairline yielding in the bell — the load it was carrying redistributes through the mat above it, and the strain field shifts, and the shift arrives in the threads of every netter within a kilometre as a slow tightening that corresponds to nothing in the water.

They let go. They reel in, drop, drift, and re-anchor somewhere else.

It is an evacuation, the only one this world has ever staged, conducted by an animal with no brain to speak of, for a reason it does not have, and nothing else notices it happening. The ropes and the grazers and the sifters underneath the doomed column find out in the usual way, all at once, from directly above.

## In the Roof

The ceiling is two to four metres thick and only the top handspan is alive, and the rest — the laminated compressed dead — is mined.

Borers. Soft, segmented, a few centimetres, living in galleries inside the felt, eating old tissue that has already been dead for ninety years and is therefore safe. It is warm up there, relatively: the storm hammers the top surface and the impacts deposit their energy as heat, so the interior of the roof runs a degree and a half above the water. A borer never touches the sea. Its entire species is born in a ceiling, lives in a ceiling, and would drown.

They fall anyway. The mat sloughs from below at a constant rate, and it sloughs with the galleries in it, and so every day the roof delivers a small rain of live borers, tumbling with the snow, arriving at the floor a day and a half later and dying more or less on the way. This is not a tragedy and it is not a migration. It is a one-way export of bodies that has been running as long as the roof has, and to the sifters it is simply the richest fraction of the fall — the part with protein in it, the part worth hurrying for.

## In the Pipe

There is one more habitat, and nobody lives in it, and something does.

A drummer, hungry and buoyant and careless, sometimes strikes hard enough to open a column's sheath, and gas comes out of the wound in a column of silver that climbs nine hundred metres and joins the roof. The plant heals such wounds in a season or two. In that season or two, things get in.

Inside the lumen: no water, no light, no predators, ninety atmospheres of humid anoxic plant-gas, and a wall of soft inner fibre that is edible. It is dry, warm, still, and a nine-hundred-metre organ pipe under continuous excitation, which means it is loud past any figure that makes sense — loud enough that nothing in there can have hearing, and nothing in there does.

They are known from the dead. When a wound opens, bodies come out with the gas, and they are pale, legless, soft-shelled, and wrong, and they fall with everything else, and the sifters eat them without comment.

Whether it is one lineage that has ridden the wounds from stem to stem for a million years, or whether it happens fresh each time, is not the sort of question this world contains. But somewhere above you, inside a pillar, in a dry place, in total deafness, something is climbing.

## The Rim

A hundred metres wide, all the way around the edge, is a band where the felt thins and a grey comes through, and the rules change.

Here there is production. Green film on the shallow bottom; grazers that eat it directly without waiting for the fall; three times the density of animals and ten times the variety; colour, of a sort — faint pigments, brown and rust, that mean nothing to anything but are there. And eyes. Real ones, re-derived, simple, working: little cups of dark pigment watching for the movement of shadows in water the colour of strong tea.

To a rim animal, the deep is not dark. Darkness is not a quality it can perceive. The deep is *loud*: it is the place where the chord stands undamped and the whole sixty-kilometre bowl rings at once, and a rim animal that drifts inward past the damping shallows into the full standing sound will turn around, every time, and go back, and this behaviour is so old and so reliable that the rim population has been genetically sealed off from the black-water population for longer than the mountains have been hills.

Two seas, then, touching. One lit and small and crowded and quiet. One enormous and blind and roaring.

They have one animal in common.

## The Drummers

Three metres, four hundred kilograms, slow as a glacier's opinion, with a thickened laminated plate across the front of the skull and a cranial cavity built as a resonator.

Start with the honest fact: the strike is a feeding technique.

A drummer swims to a column, sets itself against the bark with its fore-limbs, cocks, and hits. Several hundred grazers lose their grip and tumble, and the drummer feeds in the cloud of them as they fall, taking maybe two hundred in the thirty seconds before the rest are out of reach. The ropes below get the remainder. This is the whole transaction. It is a jaw and a habit. There is no tool here, no invention, nothing that needed to be thought of.

But the column rings, and the sea hears it, and the drummer hears the sea hear it. The reflections come back off the other seven hundred and ninety-nine columns, off the rim's damped mush, off the floor's soft absorbing peat, and in the shape of that return there is a complete map. So navigation comes free with lunch. And so does announcement, because every meal is a broadcast, and a drummer cannot eat in private, and has never conceived of wanting to.

Its identity is in the strike. The head plate accumulates micro-fractures over a lifetime, thousands of them, and they change the timbre, so that no two plates sound alike and no plate sounds the same at eighty as it did at twenty. A drummer is a voice that ages audibly, in public, across sixty kilometres. Everyone hears everyone grow old. A drummer dying over a year of failing muscle broadcasts its own decline four or five times a day at a volume that reaches the far rim in thirty seconds, and the others hear the strikes go soft, and they do not come, and there is no word for what they do instead, which is: continue, in a sea that is one voice quieter.

Some things that follow from physics:

**Etiquette.** The far rim is thirty seconds away. A strike owns the sea for that long — its own returns are still arriving. To strike into another's returning echo blinds both of you. So drummers wait, and the waiting has hardened into a protocol as rigid as a grammar, and hundreds of animals scattered across a bowl the size of a city take turns without any of them having the concept of a turn.

**Names.** A drummer is known by its plate-voice and located by the column it chose, which means a name here is a chord of identity and address, and it changes when you move, and it decays as you age. There is no way to be anonymous and no way to stay the same.

**Courtship.** Two drummers converge by alternating strikes, closing over four or five days across eight or ten kilometres, and then they stop. Fasting, silent, for perhaps a week. The whole sea registers it — two voices gone out of the weather of sound — and the whole sea registers their return. A silence is the only private thing a drummer can say, and it is loud.

**Childhood.** They calve at the rim, in the tea-coloured shallows, where food is dense and the young can be left. A drummer is born with functioning eyes. For two or three years it can see: the green film, the grey overhead, the rust-coloured rim animals, the shapes of its own kind. Then the lids fuse, the cups fill in, the head plate thickens, and the mother leads it inward.

What she does then is the only teaching in this world. She works a slow spiral from the rim to the centre and back out, over eleven or twelve years, striking columns in order, and the calf swims at her flank and listens — to the note, and the return, and the note, and the return — until the eight hundred are in it. Not memorised. Installed. After that the calf can be dropped anywhere in the sixty kilometres in total blackness and know, from one strike, exactly where it is, the way you know which room of your house you have woken up in.

No adult drummer has any memory of having seen anything. All of them did.

**Loss.** A column falls about once a century. The chord loses a note. For every drummer alive at that moment the world becomes subtly, permanently wrong in one region — a hole in the map, a place where the returns come back too late and too few — and they have to re-learn that quarter of the sea by feel, in their forties, in their nineties, at whatever age it catches them.

The stump remains. It no longer rings. It thuds — a dead, splintered, unmusical knock with no sustain — and drummers still strike it, because the grazers still colonise it, and because it is a habit, and because a thud is a landmark too. It is the only unmusical sound in the world and it stays that way for three or four hundred years until the new stem climbs and takes up the load and the note comes back, slightly flat, at which point every drummer alive has to learn the region again, and none of them remember that it was ever any different.

**And then the thing that is not quite a thing.**

Drummers answer strikes. Not deliberately; a strike nearby raises the odds of a strike here, the way one dog starting raises a street. So activity propagates. And because the sea is a closed bowl with a fixed geometry and a fixed speed of sound, the propagation has a shape, and the shape is stable, and it rotates: a slow standing wave of call-and-answer that travels around the basin at something under four kilometres a year, completing a circuit in roughly two thousand years.

No drummer perceives it. A drummer lives a hundred and ten years and sees a twentieth of one rotation, which looks to it like nothing at all, which is to say it looks like the weather.

If you could take ten thousand years of strike-record and compress it into an hour, you would hear something with phrase and cadence and return, something that builds and rests and builds, something that sounds — this is the part that would be difficult to sit through — unbearably like deliberation.

It is not. Nobody is thinking it. Every note of it is an animal knocking its face against a plant to shake its dinner loose.

That is almost certainly the whole explanation.

## What Falls

When a drummer dies it takes a day and a half to reach the floor, and four hundred kilograms arriving in one place is the largest single event the benthos will ever experience. The sifters detect it from three hundred metres by scent and converge over a week; the ropes come; things come up out of the peat that are not seen at any other time and have no other function, an entire fauna that exists only for this, dormant for decades in the top laminate, waiting for a body.

The feast lasts between two and six years. Then the bones — not bone; compressed protein, but call them bones — sink past the line into the preserving dark, where they join every other drummer that has ever lived, stacked in order, a column of the dead running kilometres down through the peat, complete, sorted, legible, unread and unreadable, going all the way back to before any of them could hear.

## The Landlord

And all of it — sifters, ropes, grazers, netters, borers, the climbing thing in the pipe, the rim's small bright nations, the drummers and their two-thousand-year rotating almost-sentence — lives inside one plant.

One clone. One genome. No nervous tissue, no receptors, no capacity to register anything that happens in its own cavity. It does not know it is a roof. It does not know there is a sea under it or that the sea is full. It has never detected a single animal, has no mechanism by which it could, and has been providing the temperature, the pressure, the pillars, the food, the ceiling, the floor, and the entire acoustic character of the world for a span of time that does not convert cleanly into years.

Deaf to its own chord. The chord is played on it, not by it.

Genealogically, then, this place contains exactly one individual, and about ten to the fourteenth animals living in it, and the relationship between them has never once been mutual. It is not symbiosis. It is occupancy. The plant heals its wounds because that is what tissue does, and its healing is the closest thing to care that anything here will ever receive.

## The Held Breath

Last, and underneath everything, the tenant nobody counts.

Kilometres of peat below the anoxic line, unrotted but not inert: methanogens working at a rate so low it would take instruments to believe, converting the archive into gas, molecule by molecule, since before the animals had their present shapes. Ninety atmospheres of cold water holding all of it in solution, obediently, because that is what cold deep water does.

The sifters taste its edge and back off. The tunnellers who cut too deep come up loose-limbed and drift, and something eats them, and this has been happening at a low constant rate forever and is not a sign of anything.

The netters, occasionally, feel a tremor in the thread that corresponds to nothing overhead, and do not have a response for it, and go on spreading the net.

Nothing here has a concept for what is dissolved beneath the floor, because nothing here has concepts, and because in a world where the finished state has held for ten million years there is no selective pressure whatsoever toward imagining an exception.

When it comes up it will come all at once, the whole basin overturning in an afternoon, and it will be the single loudest thing that has ever happened in this sea, and every animal in it — the blind, the sessile, the deaf thing in the pipe, the calves at the rim with their eyes not yet sealed, the drummers in the middle of a strike thirty kilometres apart — will hear it simultaneously.

That will be the last true statement about this place, and it is a good one, better than most worlds get:

that everything in it heard the same thing at the same time, once.

Until then: the chord, the fall, the eight hundred notes and the one thud, and the long slow rotating sentence that nobody is saying.
