# The Stack-Folk

## First sight

You will walk past several of them before you see one.

At the mouth of a shadow-shaft, where the polished bore breathes its thin cold out into a stepped court, there is what you take for a plate of crust growing on the wall: pale, frilled, a metre and a half of it, standing upright. Then the frill folds down along its back with a soft dry sound like a fan closing, and it turns its head, and it has a face.

They are tall and narrow and dusted chalk-white, and they are built the way everything here is built — for one problem only. Their problem is not cold. Nothing on this world has ever had cold as a problem. Their problem is that they are warm, and the day is warmer, and there is nowhere for the heat to go except up, into a violet sky that is very nearly space.

So: long limbs, thin, with the blood run close to the surface where the air can get at it. A high deep chest for the thin air, ribbed like a basket, working slowly and continuously the way a bellows works. No hair anywhere except a crest of stiff pale filaments from the brow back over the crown, which stands up when they are working or frightened and lies flat when they are content. And along the shoulders and the upper back, folded like a moth's wings against the spine, the **fans** — two membranes of fine grey hide veined with vessels, which they raise at night and in shade and spread flat toward the zenith, and which are the single most expressive thing about them.

They powder themselves white every morning. The powder is swept sulfate from the tide-snows, ground with bone-flour, and it does two jobs: it throws the sun back off their shoulders, and it lets what heat they carry radiate straight out to the dark. An unpowdered person in open sun is a person in trouble within the hour. A person who has let their powder go patchy is a person about whom something is wrong, and everyone will know it before the second street.

They do not sweat. There is no water to spare for it. They exhale nearly dry — you can hold a cold slate in front of a stack-dweller's mouth and get a bloom the size of a coin — and they drink perhaps a cup a day, sour with dissolved metals, and they consider a visitor who sweats to be leaking, obscenely, in public.

## Cold, and manners

Cold is not a hardship here. Cold is money, comfort, hospitality, medicine, and love.

They sleep face-down on stone. A bed is a slab, polished, sunk into the coldest floor the household can afford, and the finest slabs are plumbed with copper down into a shadow-shaft so that the stone under your ribs is cold enough to make your fans tremble. In a poor gallery, a family shares one slab in shifts. In a rich one, there is a cold room kept for guests, and to be shown it is to be told you are welcome to stay for years.

When you enter a house you are offered the cold seat. You take it. Refusing is an insult of the kind that gets remembered for a generation.

Their affection-words are all cold words. *She is cold to me* is what a man says about his wife of forty years. *He has kept me cool* is gratitude. *A warm word* is a threat, and a real one. Grief is heat: the bereaved go up onto the sun-face and stand there, unpowdered, on purpose, and are brought down by their kin before it kills them, and this is a rite with a name and a fixed duration and a set of people whose job it is to count.

They move slowly. Not out of temperament — out of thermodynamics. A stack-dweller's walking pace is a long unhurried stride that eats about five kilometres in an hour and can be sustained all day; anything faster puts heat into a body that has no fast way to lose it. Running is an emergency, once, and afterward you lie on a slab shaking with your fans spread and someone fans cold air over you from a shaft-mouth with a leather flap. Children run. Children are allowed to run because they have not yet learned what it costs.

Which means violence here is very strange. A brawl overheats both parties in under a minute, and what is left is two people standing a metre apart, unable to lift their arms, hating each other. So they formalised it. A duel is a **standing**: two people go out onto the sun-face at noon, powder washed off, and stand. The first to step into shade has lost. Nobody dies, usually. The loser is helped down by the winner's family, which is part of it.

## Seeing

They have eyes — dark, heavy-lidded, slit-pupilled, good in the bruise-coloured underlight and very good in the hard white glare. But under each cheekbone there is a shallow pit of bare wet tissue, and with those they see heat.

Not vividly. Not as a picture. As a second layer over the world: the warm shape of a scraper on the far side of a wall, the cooling smear where someone sat, the four hundred sighing hot heads of an engine field like a field of lamps. And each other. Faces bloom. A stack-dweller cannot hide amusement, or fear, or desire, because all of it arrives at the throat as a soft flush of warmth that anyone in the room can read from ten metres.

So the etiquette is enormous. You do not look at a throat. You look at hands, or at the crest, or at a point beside the head, and the whole architecture of their politeness — the low doorways, the screens of woven iron mesh across the fronts of shops, the wide collars — exists to give people back the privacy their bodies won't. Liars here are not people who say false things. Liars are people who have trained their throats. There are three or four in every city and they are hired.

Laughter is silent, because laughter is waste heat. It is a flutter of the crest and a half-spread of the fans, and if you spend a season among them you will start doing it yourself, and it will take years to stop.

## Speech

The air is too thin to shout through, so they don't. Two people talk with their heads a foot apart in a low unhurried register, and the streets are hushed, and the hush is not reverence, it is physics.

Everything at distance goes through stone. Every gallery has iron rods let into the bedrock and a hammer on a chain, and the code is the second language of the species — arriving as a knocking in the floor, felt through the bare soles of feet that have plates of hard sensitive pad instead of heels. They read with their feet. They sit with one heel against a rod the way an ordinary person keeps half an ear on a radio. Children learn the code at three or four, on the floor, drumming with their knuckles, and a household will suddenly go quiet because the youngest has picked up something from the west line and is spelling it out badly, letter by letter, about a rockfall.

For close and private talk there is tapping, hand on forearm. It has its own compressed grammar, and lovers use it, and so do people conspiring in a crowded court with their faces carefully turned away from each other's throats.

They have no word for green. Visitors have tried to teach it; it doesn't take; it survives in one city as a nonsense syllable children use for something impossible, and in another as a mild obscenity of unknown derivation.

## Work

There is no leisured class, but there are professions, and each one bends the people in it into a different shape.

The **crust-shepherds** walk with the scraper herds at the speed of a minute hand, alone, for a season at a time, sleeping against the warm plated flanks of their animals when the night gets too cold even for them. They come back gentle and slightly odd, and they talk to strangers too long, and everyone forgives it.

The **wire-walkers** live on the spans. One cable, thirty kilometres of it, walked and hand-inspected for a whole working life; a hut at mid-span with a slab and a stove and a wheel the size of a dinner plate; a dialect of maybe two hundred spoken words and a great deal of tapping on cable. Their fingertips wear down to a polished bone-shine. They cannot sleep in rooms. When one of them retires into a city, the family builds them something out on the rim with a floor that moves.

The **lift-crews** go down. Cold-suits with their own little gradient engines strapped to the spine, faceplates fogged with mineral fume, descending the pipe columns into four hundred degrees and syrup air to clear a blockage at the intake. They are paid in cold — deeded shaft-time, the best slabs, a nursery place for their children — and they are deaf by forty and dead by fifty-five, and they walk through the markets with their powder deliberately thin so that everybody can see the scarring, and everybody does look, and lets them pass.

The **shadow-clerks** compute the umbra of a proposed building a hundred and fifty years forward, with tables and a brass machine and an argument. They are the lawyers of this world, and the priests of it, and they are feared in the way that people fear anyone who can take your cold away with paper.

The **bearing-grinders** are old. This is not sentiment; it is that a body cools as it ages here, and the old can bear the shafts, and the finest bearing surfaces are ground in the dark at the bottom of a cold bore over eleven years by hands that have stopped shaking because they have stopped being warm. An old grinder can sit in a cold-mouth that would kill her grandchildren and is very hard to see with the pits at all, and there is a joke about that, and nobody makes it in front of her.

## Warm ones and cold ones

A child is called *a warm one*. Children run hot, cannot dump heat well, and are therefore kept deep — the nurseries are the coldest chambers a city has, deeper than the archives, deeper than the treasury of spare bearings. Visitors always notice this and always misread it as tenderness. It is tenderness, but it is also that a warm child in the open is the brightest thing on a stack-top, and the thing that hunts heat can see it from a very long way.

Age goes the other way. The old go cold. They drift toward the shafts, tolerate what nobody else can, sit longer, radiate less, and become progressively harder to find in a dim room.
