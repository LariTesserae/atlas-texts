## The tenant

You have already walked past several.

Start by giving up the shape you came with. You are looking for something at head height, something with a front, something that would show against the black if it moved. There is nothing at head height on the Rise except cold air and your own warmth, which is an error here and which will be discussed later. Lower your attention to the level of the talus, to the waist-high stacks, to the slabs grating faintly underfoot, and understand that the distinction you are trying to draw — between the rubble and the thing that lives in the rubble — is a distinction the Rise has not yet been asked to make.

It has no name. It has no interior in which a name could be kept.

## The body

At rest it is a mound. A meter and a half at the shoulder, if it had a shoulder: forty or sixty plates of basalt and glass, interlocked, leaning inward, the whole arrangement stable in the way a stack of knives is stable. From five meters it is talus. From one meter it is talus that has been *arranged* — the flat faces turned inward, the sharp edges turned out, a dozen small compensations that no slope makes on its own but that no slope would be prevented from making either.

It is hollow. This is the part that matters. Inside is a chimney, and inside the chimney the crusts grow: the same fronds and rosettes and hollow tubes you find on the sheltered undersides of slabs everywhere on the plateau, but dense here, packed, ordered by the draught, cadmium and verdigris and that purplish bronze, furring the entire inner surface. Vent gas comes up through the floor of it and goes out the top, and on the way through, the gas is stripped — sulfur laid down, metals laid down, chlorides laid down — and the lining thickens, and the thing grows heavier.

That lining is the nearest thing to tissue on this world. It is not alive. Neither is the mound. The mound is a chemistry that has arranged its own housing so the chemistry can continue, which is a description that will do for most of what you call living, and which the Rise has arrived at some hundreds of millions of years before it was due.

There is no head. There is no front. There is a warm side and a cool side, and these change.

## The verb

It cannot cut. It cannot carry far. It cannot shape anything, ever, and has never held a tool or an idea of one.

It can choose.

That is the entire faculty. A slab lying at the foot of a riser is flat or it is not; it is the right length or it is not; it is cool enough to lift without slumping or it is not. The mound tests by contact, by the differential of heat across a plate, by the way stress propagates when weight is shifted onto an edge. Then it takes the slab and sets it.

The taking is not lifting. It has no limb. What it has is the seam — hot ground, cold air, a body standing across the boundary — and a slab held up into the cold will contract, and contraction is force, and force applied at a hairline is a crack, and a crack is the only muscle this world has issued. It moves by breaking. It advances the way the lava lobes at the edges advance: a plate is set forward, the mass tips onto it, the rear plates are drawn up one at a time and re-set. A step is four hours. A traverse of a kilometer is a season, if there were seasons.

And it sheds. It must: the lining thickens, the mass grows, and a mound that has fed well for long enough becomes too heavy to break itself forward. So it puts the excess down. Flattest at the bottom, because that is stable and stability is the only aesthetic available. Three slabs, or nine. It does not look back at them.

The cairns are its droppings. The cairns are also its housings, abandoned; also its record, in the only medium it has; also, when it finally cannot move at all, its corpse. There is no way to tell these apart from the outside, and no way from the inside either, because there is no inside.

## How it knows

It is deaf to air — the air here carries nothing worth hearing. It listens through the floor. The eleven-minute swell arrives as a long pressure up through the plates, and the ticking of fresh glass arrives as a fine continuous grain, and a terrace tilting three degrees forty kilometers off arrives as a soft, enormous, unlocatable statement about the future. It is very good at this. It knows about a fissure before the fissure opens, in the way you know about a sneeze.

It tastes. Sulfur dioxide, hydrogen chloride, the particular sweetness of a young vent versus the thin exhausted breath of an old one. Most of its life is a slow ranging between vents, and most of its decisions are this decision.

And it sees downward. The underside of the mound is sensitive to the orange — the whole luminous diagram, the cracks running out under the crust, read as a map by the floor of its own body. Its eye is its foot. It perceives the world exclusively as what is immediately beneath it, in one color, and by integrating that over days it builds something you would have to call a sense of place: bright means new, new means gas, gas means mass. It goes toward the fresh cuts. It is drawn to the plateau's wounds the way anything is drawn to a meal.

It has no clock of its own. It hasn't gotten around to one. It borrows the swell — eleven minutes, the plateau's breath — and hangs every process it has on that beat, so that its pulse is not in it but under it, and if the plateau ever stopped breathing the mound would not slow down so much as lose the ability to be a sequence.

## Keeping its distance

There is more than one. There is not, however, a population, because a population implies members, and the boundary conditions here do not support members.

Where two of them come within a few hundred meters, the vent field between them thins, and the draught through one chimney falters, and a faltering chimney means a cooling lining, and a cooling lining goes grey and flakes. One smothers. The survivor does not know it has won; it only knows the gas improved. This is exactly what the crusts do when two of them meet on the underside of a slab. It is the same arithmetic run at a larger scale, and that is all inheritance has ever been.

So they hold themselves apart, the way a crystal holds its spacing, without intent and without exception. Kilometers. Always kilometers. Whatever is doing this keeps its distance from itself, and the reason is not shyness or territory or grief. The reason is that this world has only one method and applies it at every size: what is near is competition, what is far is nothing at all, and there is no third relation yet. Love is a third relation. It has not been invented on the Rise.

Occasionally a mound grows past the point where its own structure can be broken forward as a unit, and it comes apart — not catastrophically, just a settling, a slump into two viable heaps that are each able to move, and that each begin at once to move away from the other. This is the only reproduction available and it is indistinguishable from an accident. Rockfall does the same thing. Lava lobes do the same thing, splitting down the middle so the new comes out of the old. Everything here that makes more makes more by breaking, and nothing here has yet learned to copy, which is to say nothing here has yet learned to be *wrong* in a way that matters. Give it time. Time is the one commodity the plateau has in excess and the one thing you cannot spend here.

## The wall

Twelve meters of coursed slabs on the spur above the eastern extrusions, one course thick, with a gap in it that has a lintel and a threshold and nothing else it could be.

It is a track. One of them fed for a long while on a vent line that ran straight, and moved along it slowly, and shed as it went, and the sheddings merged into a course, and the course became a wall because a wall is what a line of stacks becomes when the spacing closes. The doorway is where it turned aside for two days and came back. The lintel fell there on its own, or was set, and no examination you can perform on the ground will tell you which, and this is not a failure of your instruments.

You will want the wall to be a statement. It is a stratigraphy. It is the same information as a snail's shell — here is what happened, in order, and here is how long — written by something that cannot read, in a language that is only the record of its own construction, which happens to be the definition of every language in its first million years.

## Concerning you

You are the strangest thing that has ever stood on this plateau, and the reason is not your mind.

You are warm at the wrong altitude. Everything that has ever been warm here has been warm at ground level, glowing up out of a crack; the seam between the hot floor and the cold air is the fundamental law of the place, and you walk around violating it, a heat source a meter and a half off the deck, moving at an impossible speed, radiating from the wrong side. To something that reads the world through the soles of its plates, you are not a visitor. You are a fault in the physics.

Worse: you exhale.

There is no water here. Not withheld — not a category. No shape in this entire landscape was made by a fluid. And you stand there venting warm water vapor at a steady twelve breaths a minute, and it falls out of the cold air onto the yellow-white deposit at your feet, and the deposit, which has never known moisture, which does not clump, *clumps*. Your footprints are marked by a chemistry that has never once occurred on the Rise. Around them, within a day, the crusts will grow differently — wetter forms, rounder margins, a rosette that solves its gradient by a route it could not otherwise have found.

If you stay, and you cannot, you would find the mounds come. Not toward you; they are not capable of toward-you. Toward the anomaly in the deposit field, the way they go toward any bright new crack. They would range along the line of your walking and feed on what your breath made and shed stacks behind them, and in a hundred thousand years there would be a wall down the length of your afternoon's stroll, and it would mean nothing, and it would be the most meaningful thing on the planet.

## Rules

- It cannot make. It can only prefer. Preference, repeated, is the whole of its grammar.
- Its pulse is not inside it.
- Everything it does is also something the lava does, the crusts do, or the rubble does anyway. The difference is bookkeeping.
- It cannot recognize its own past work, and will dismantle a cairn it built for the flatness of the slabs.
- It has no age, because nothing wears. It has only mass. It dies of mass, or of a vent going cold, or of being buried, and in all three cases the result is a waist-high stack that looks like every other waist-high stack.
- It is carried upward at the rate of the plateau, always, without choosing, because that is the only direction the world has.

The stack three hundred meters off is nine slabs and slightly warm. Watch it for an hour and it will not move. Watch it for four and the topmost slab will be facing a little differently, and you will not be able to say whether you are certain, and there will have been no sound at all.
