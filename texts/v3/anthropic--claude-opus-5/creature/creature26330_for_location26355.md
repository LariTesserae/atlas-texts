## Fauna, continued

The list above is honest about the walls and the gutters and the flues. It omits the middles of things, and the middles of things are where they are.

You will walk past four of them before you see one. This is not concealment. It is that they stand very still in wide bare places, in coats the colour of wet pan, at the distance at which a person and a bollard are the same object, and your eye — trained by two days of seething masonry — has learned to look at edges. When one turns its head you will feel the correction physically, a small drop in the chest, the way you feel a step that isn't there.

There are ninety-two. I counted three times, on separate days, from the north scarp, and got ninety-two three times. You should not tell them this. I will come back to it.

## Body

Upright, two-legged, unarmoured — which in the Lap is a startling thing to be. Adult, all of them, and one who is small and is not young. Skin the colour of the pan and beaded like every other surface, so that they wear the same film of water as the gasholder and the rails and the drays. Hair kept at a length by nothing; it stopped where it stopped. Nails likewise. Scars: many, and every one of them arrested — you will meet a man with a split across the back of his hand that has neither closed nor worsened in living memory, and he will use the hand normally, and the wound will not interest him. Fingertips worn perfectly smooth and worn no smoother. They leave nothing on glass.

Their clothing is felted slag-wool and tarred canvas, stiffened by damp into something very like plate, which is presumably why the eye files them with the fauna. Under it they are cool. Not cold — the temperature of the air, eleven degrees, the temperature of the stone and the eels and the Drab. Nothing in this basin keeps heat. Heat goes up.

Faces are difficult here, and this is worth preparing for. In shadowless light a face has no modelling; you get a flat oval of features with no depth cues, and you cannot read it. Nobody in the Lap reads faces. They read voices, closely and well, and they will know your mood from a syllable while you are still failing to find theirs in a whole afternoon of looking.

Their breath smells faintly sweet and faintly of metal. They drink at the same stone cups as the cuffs, kneeling, one hand flat on the paving among the plated bodies, entirely unbothered, and the cuffs are entirely unbothered, and neither party has ever regarded this as an arrangement.

They eat eel. On the wharf a woman will walk out to a standing stilt, take the eel from its plate-jaws with two fingers, and walk back; the stilt does not object, does not flinch, does not adjust its position, and takes another. There is no fire in the Lap. There cannot be — flame here gives light and no warmth, the heat leaving instantly upward as it does from the furnaces — so everything is eaten cold and raw and the bones go down the drains with the shells. You may find, after a week, that you have stopped noticing the taste of anything. They stopped a long time before you.

## Speech

Every sentence in the Lap is four seconds long.

Nobody arranges this. The stamp falls, and the fall is a wall, and over a sufficient period of practice all speech has settled into the gap between walls: a phrase, a pause on the fall, a phrase, a pause on the fall. Their conversation therefore has a metre — a slow, level, ballad metre — and it is one of the most beautiful sounds I have ever heard, and it is being used to say that the eels are running thick at the west grating. There is no urgency available. You cannot interrupt into a stamp. You cannot rush a sentence, because a rushed sentence simply ends early and leaves you standing in the noise.

They will do you the courtesy of listening to your ragged un-metred outsider speech without comment. Within two days you will have stopped being able to produce it, and within two days of leaving you will still be pausing every four seconds, and people elsewhere will finish your sentences for you and be wrong.

They have one joke. It concerns the Quiet Cell and it is very old and it is not much of a joke. But nothing here wears out, including pleasure, so it is exactly as funny to them on its ten-thousandth telling as it was on its first, and they laugh at it with real, small, undiminished enjoyment, which is somehow harder to be near than the gargles.

## Memory

Their memory has a fixed volume. This is stated locally in exactly that way, without metaphor, in the tone in which you were told how furnaces work here.

Things go in. Things go out. The total does not change. A man will hold your name perfectly for some days — six, eight — and then one morning he will greet you warmly and not have it, and he will not be embarrassed, and he will not be able to tell you what he took in exchange. The trade is not chosen and it is not visible. They do not experience this as loss, and it is important that you understand they are not being brave about it: there is no felt deficit. The shelf is full. The shelf was always full.

The consequence is that nothing they learn changes them. You may sit with a woman for four hours in the lee of the melt-shed and tell her everything — where you came from, what happened there, the thing you have not told anyone — and she will attend to you completely, and ask good questions, and be moved, genuinely moved, and none of it will deposit. Tomorrow she is the same woman by precisely as much as she was yesterday. This is the cruelest hospitality in the world and it is offered freely and often, and after the second time you will find you cannot accept it again.
