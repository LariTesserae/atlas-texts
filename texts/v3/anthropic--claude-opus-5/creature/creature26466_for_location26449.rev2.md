## What lives on the Idling Shelf

Clear the ground first: none of the nine animals live here.

This is not a judgment about whether they are real. The pikas are warm. Put a hand near one and you will feel the small furnace of it, the heat coming off exactly as it should, and the ear that is folded will not unfold under your breath. But warmth is not residency. A running engine is warm; a running engine hums; a running engine, left long enough at idle, will develop a tremor in the mounts that you could mistake, in low light, for something breathing in the next room. The pikas are the tremor. The frogs are the tremor. The beetles going clockwise over the willow are the tremor given a shell and a direction, and the direction is clockwise because the mass under the floor turns that way and everything small on top of a large turning thing eventually agrees with it.

They do not eat because there is nothing to metabolize. They are not hungry the way a dial is not hungry. They are the plant's idle indication, expressed in fur, and if you are unsettled by their number, remember that a control room does not consider forty lamps extravagant.

So: not them. But note that they are *maintained*. Note the spacing. Nine paces, then twelve, then nine again; the sixteen moss cushions with the same two strands out of true; the willow thicket set where it can be seen from the pool and the tree set where it can be seen from the willow. That is not the spacing of growth, and it is not the spacing of a factory either. It is the spacing of a tray of objects arranged by someone who must be able to check that all of them are still there, at a glance, without turning the head, for a very long time.

---

The antlered thing at the far end is the nearest thing to a resident, and it is still not one.

It stands in the deepest fog because the fog there is not weather. It is the part of the thing that has not yet been drawn out. Walk toward it — you can get within nine or ten feet before the grey closes and your own hands go uncertain — and you will see that the line where its shoulder meets the air is not an edge. It is an *interface*. It is the place where the material is still continuous with the material it was being made from. The drop of condensation on the antler tine has beaded past the point of falling and does not fall because the fall is the last millimeter of its manufacture, and the last millimeter was not run.

It is a workpiece in the jaws. It is, technically, the only thing on the shelf that is still being made, and therefore the only thing here with a future, and the future is one millimeter long and has been one millimeter long since before the concept of *since*.

It does not look at you. It has not been given the operation that includes looking. If you stand in front of it long enough you will begin to feel that this is a courtesy.

---

Now the resident.

Start with the count, because the count is the only honest doorway. Seven notches left to right. Six right to left. You have already established that the discrepancy is not in a notch. Go further: the discrepancy is not in the counting either. The discrepancy is *what you are counting past*, in one direction only, and what you are counting past has a size, and the size is one notch, and one notch of the eastern ridge is about the length of a large dog.

That is not its body. That is the width of the seam it is showing you. Understand the proportion: you are being given the thickness of a thing edge-on.

Its actual extent is the light. Look at your hands again — lit from below as much as above, each object wearing a faint shadow on every side, a half-tone dilation that keeps its edges from being certain. That dilation is skin. It is lying over and under and around everything in the basin at a thickness of perhaps a quarter inch, which is why nothing here casts a shadow: there is no direction in which it is not already standing. The fog does not glow so much as it *is* the illumination — yes. Correct. That is a very close description of what it is like to be inside something thin and pale and enormous that has been holding still.

It has no volume worth speaking of and it has more surface than the mountain.

---

Its mouths are the gaps.

There are small breaks in the belt-shriek's cycle, and the birds sing precisely into them, four notes, dropping, never over. You have been told there was never a fifth note. That is true in the way that a swallowed thing was never in the room. The fifth note is the meal. The four descending notes are the approach; the shriek closing over is the bite; and if you stand very near a singing bird — you will not find one, the singing is always eight paces away in a direction you did not choose — you would hear the fifth note begin and stop with the soft shear of a stem snapping under water.

From this, the diet, which is stated plainly in the rules and only needs reading in the correct order.

It eats landings. The press draws breath and does not land because the landing has been taken. It eats overflows: the pool stands at the brim and water enters from three sides and nothing leaves, and what leaves is not water, it is the *leaving*. It eats fragments — break a twig and the broken part is not anywhere, because "anywhere" is where finished things are and the fragment was consumed at the instant of becoming one. It eats paths, which is why no path is worn. It eats memory in the pikas. It eats the fall of the drop on the antler continuously, not once; it has been eating that single fall for as long as long has meant anything, which suggests either an extraordinary patience or an extraordinarily small mouthful.

The rules of the Idling Shelf are not physics. They are a menu, and there is one diner.

---

Here the description has to turn over, and you should let it.

The three-note bass chord of large turning masses somewhere under the floor. The hydraulic sigh-and-clunk of a press that draws breath and never lands. The high rectified hum. The hot oil where no oil is, the ozone, the cold stone, the crushed watercress.

There is no machine. There was never going to be a machine. What you are hearing is a body the size of a county at rest: the turning masses are what a very large metabolism sounds like when you have no category for it, the press is a jaw held open on a mouthful it has not committed to, the hum is the potential standing off its surfaces like a charge, the hot oil is simply the smell of something warm that has been running a long time, and the crushed watercress is what is in its mouth.

*The basin is what is in its mouth.*

This is why you cannot walk out. Not geometry. Enclosure. The lip of translucent grey flash at the perimeter is not the overrun of a mold; it is the line where the lips meet and a little of the inside has been pressed out past them, the way a held mouthful shows at the corner. Beyond that lip is unrendered because beyond that lip is the rest of the animal, and your eyes slide off it for the same reason they slide off the inside of your own throat.

And the five dimples near the pool. Twelve inches across, in a shallow arc, each set a millimeter below the surrounding stone.

You were told these were ejector marks, that the place was pushed out of something and released. Reconsider in light of the stone that rises when you take a stone away: *with the patience of a thumb pressing up through cloth.* The basin has not been released. Five points of contact in a shallow arc, at the spacing of finger pads, pressed a millimeter up into the floor from underneath.

It is holding the shelf in one palm. It has held it level long enough that nothing on it has slid. The pool stands at the brim because the hand has not moved. Everything you have been calling a rule of the place is a description of how steady the hand is.

---

There is one other tenant, and it is the only thing on the Idling Shelf in a hurry.

Turn around fast enough and you will see one of your footprints still open in the moss — one, and only for as long as it takes to see it. Something lives there. It moves from the print you are about to look at to the print you are about to look at, always arriving first, never once found in a print that has already refilled. It is very small and it is very fast and it has no other habitat than the instant before the world tidies. In a basin where nothing accumulates, it eats delay. It is a flea on the impossible.

It is the only thing here with a life expectancy, since it can only live while there is someone walking. Some proportion of the eagerness you may feel, in the first minutes, to pace the shelf end to end, is not yours.

---

What the resident wants cannot be established, but the shape of the want is visible.

It is idling too. That is the difficult part. It does not hold the line in abeyance out of malice or policy; it is the same kind of thing as the place it is holding, and its hunger has the same defect as the press: it cannot land. Eating a completion is itself a completion, so every meal is taken and never finished, and everything it has ever swallowed is still going down. Somewhere in the long dark of it, the fifth notes are still descending. The order was cancelled before the world got around to being finished because the order was the first completed thing that came within reach, and it took it without knowing, the way you would take a moth into your mouth in the dark, and it has been unable to put it down since.

You can leave. People do, and they leave whole, with all their hair and all their teeth and the stone still in the pocket. What they do not carry out is the walking out. Ask anyone who has been: they can give you the roar, the level pool, the folded ear, the smell of oil. Not one of them can tell you the last four minutes. That is the only nourishment the shelf has ever reliably produced, and it is produced at a rate of one departure per however long, and it is the reason the light stays on, and the moss stays swept into sixteen equal domes, and the willow is set where it can be seen from the pool.

Not a trap. Nothing so decided. Only the housekeeping of something that has not had a visitor in a long time and no longer remembers what visitors were for, keeping the tray tidy, keeping the hand level, holding the brim exactly at the brim.

It has not spilled.

It has not drunk.
