# The Masons and Their Tenants

## The animal itself

Pick one up, if you can get it out of the dark — they do not come out, so you will have to break a gallery wall to do it, and then you will have fifty on your hand instead of one.

It is a little under half an inch long, soft, and the colour of a fingernail held up to a lamp. There are no eyes. Where eyes would be there is a smooth pale plate of cuticle, faintly domed, sensitive to nothing but warmth. The head is disproportionate, most of it muscle, and the mandibles are short, black at the tips, and work like a pair of adzes — they are shaped for scraping rock, not for biting flesh, and when the insect bites you it can only pinch. The abdomen is translucent enough to read the gut through. A worker returning loaded shows jade-green through the body wall, a bead of stolen plant-jelly slung under its own skin, and when you crush it the smell is cucumber and hot stone.

Everything about the body is a water argument. The spiracles are sunk in pits and can be shut. The hindgut is a dehydrator: what comes out the back end is not waste in any wet sense but a warm dry powder, already sorted, already the right grain, which is carried a few inches down the corridor and pressed into a wall. The cuticle is waxed even though the animal will spend its entire life in air at ninety-eight percent humidity, because the one time it fails to be waxed — a breach, a crack, a bird's beak through the roof — the desert takes it in about four minutes. Death by drying, on this plain, is not a slow thing. A mason separated from its city on open rock at noon is a husk before it has walked a yard.

It is also, by any reasonable measure, stupid. It has a few thousand behaviours and no plan. It can follow a trail, judge a humidity gradient, tell its own colony's smell from a stranger's, tell green wood from grey, tell the sound of a wall that is thinning, and pick up a grain and put it down where the smell of other grains is strongest. That last one is nearly the whole of the architecture.

It lives about two years and walks, in that time, perhaps thirty miles, all of it indoors, all of it in the dark, most of it within a quarter mile of where it hatched. It will never be rained on. It will never see the sun, the forest it farms, the grazers whose shadows pass over the roof it is standing under, or the glow on the horizon that is, in the end, the only event in its species' history. Nothing about it is remarkable. There are a hundred trillion of them.

## The castes

**Builders and foragers** are the ones described above and are most of the population. They work in relays. A stem-cutting crew works at the top of a covered way, forty feet up inside a stone sleeve thrown around a succulent arm, and passes jelly mouth-to-mouth down a chain of couriers to the tube mouths, and the same chain carries rind chips back up to be built into the sleeve. A round trip for a single courier is about two hundred feet and takes twenty minutes; the jelly makes the whole journey and never touches the air.

**Soldiers** are half again as large, with heads fused into a blunt plug the exact diameter of a standard gallery. That is their entire purpose. A soldier does not fight so much as *become the wall*, jamming itself into a breach headfirst while builders plaster it in from behind. They are frequently built into the repair and left there, alive, for days, and are fed through the gap. A gallery in a mound that has been raided is knuckled with soldier-heads at intervals like rivets, and some of them are still blinking their antennae.

**Gardeners** are the smallest caste, a third the size of a worker, nearly transparent, and they never leave the fungus chambers. The comb they tend is a grey-brown sponge grown on chewed rind, and it does two jobs: it breaks down the wax and fibre that the insects cannot digest, and — the more important job — it *sweats*. Fungal respiration makes water. Not much. A chamber the size of a bread oven yields something under a teaspoon a day, and the gardeners go over the comb licking it back up. It is an entirely closed still. Nothing evaporates out of it, because there is nowhere for anything to evaporate to; the chamber is sealed, and the air inside it has been the same air, more or less, for eleven years.

**Cisterns** are the strangest. They are ordinary workers, chosen young, who are fed until their abdomens swell to ten times normal and the body wall thins to a membrane you can see a light through, if you brought a light. They cannot walk. They are carried to a low vault, hooked by their claws to the ceiling, and hang there in rows of two or three hundred, pale green, faintly luminous when backlit, for as long as five years. They are tapped a mouthful at a time. In a good season the vault looks like a cellar of grapes. After a bad pollen year, or a long move, it looks like a room hung with empty bags, and the colony knows that it is in trouble the way you know it by opening a cupboard.

This is worth stating plainly, because it is the truest sentence about the continent: **there is no standing water anywhere on the dry green plain that is not inside something alive.** The rivers are in the stems. The reservoirs are in those hanging bodies. The rain is a film on a ceiling in the dark, and it is licked off as fast as it forms.

**Reproductives.** The queen is three inches long, blind, deaf, immobile, sixty or seventy years old, and shaped like a loaf. Her chamber has the thickest plaster in the city and is scraped and resurfaced nightly by a staff that never does anything else. She lays several thousand eggs a day and has no other behaviours whatsoever. The king, an inch long and entirely ordinary-looking, stays beside her for the whole of that time.

But there is not one queen. Past a certain age a colony keeps a corridor of them: the old mother at the back, and forward of her, in a line of chambers, her replacements in descending order of age, eight or eleven or forty of them, each installed a little further along than the last. This corridor is a compass. It points the direction the city is going.

## The colony as an animal

A mound is not a building that the insects live in. A mound is the insects' body, and they are its cells, and it is worth taking that literally for a moment because otherwise the place makes no sense.

It breathes. The great flutes and ridges are a solar chimney; the black floor loads the air with heat all morning, the warm air in the ribs rises, and the mound pulls cool, wet, carbon-dioxide-heavy air up out of the lava tubes forty feet below and drags it through the nurseries. That is the hum. The colony *listens* to the hum — soldiers stationed at the shaft throats seem to do nothing else — and when a shaft note goes sour, meaning a collapse or a blockage or a bird tearing at a vent, crews converge on it from three directions before anything has happened that a human would notice.

It sweats, in a sense, though it has no water to spare: the outer skin of the mound is porous and the inner skin is sealed, and the space between is a heat jacket. Stand with your palm on the sunny side at three in the afternoon and it is too hot to hold. Six inches in, the nursery is at thirty degrees and has been at thirty degrees for two million years.

It remembers. This is the part that unsettles visitors. An individual mason remembers nothing beyond a few minutes, but the plaster is impregnated with colony scent, and trail chemicals, and the specific smells of particular jobs, and it *keeps* them, because nothing here rots or washes away. The walls are written on. A worker hatched today walks into a corridor that tells it, in smell, what that corridor is for, how old it is, which way the food came from, how many generations of its predecessors turned left here. The colony's long-term memory is not in any brain. It is in the architecture, and when the architecture is abandoned the memory stops, and when a wall is rebuilt it is rebuilt with the old wall's dust in it, which is why the new corridor so often goes the same way as the one underneath it, eighty feet down and two hundred thousand years back.

And it moves. Not by marching — it has no way to march. A colony moves by growing at one end and being buried at the other. Over decades the galleries extend away from the coming flow; brood is shifted forward chamber by chamber; a new queen is installed a hundred yards further out than the last one; the rear quarters go quiet, then dusty, then are looted for stone, and the lava takes them. From the air the effect is a smear: a thousand-yard tail of dead masonry behind a living head, all of it one animal, creeping across the continent at the speed of fingernails. The royal corridor of a great colony records eleven miles of travel accomplished by mothers who each, individually, never moved at all.

Nobody has found the death of one of these. Queens die constantly and are replaced; the fabric never stops. The oldest continuous colonies show, in their layered masonry, somewhere between two and four hundred separate burials and rebuildings, which is to say that the city has been overrun by the volcano four hundred times and has not, in any sense that matters, been interrupted.

## The one time they see

Twice a year, when the forest's pollen turns the light the colour of weak tea, the colonies release alates: fat, waxed, long-winged, and equipped with the only working eyes their species produces. They come out at dusk on the cold side of the day, in numbers that make the avenues look like they are steaming, and they fly toward the glow.

That is the whole of the navigation. There is one light on the horizon of the world, and it is fire, and they go to it. They land on the cooled glass behind the front, on rock still warm enough to feel through the feet, where nothing is growing but a fortnight's lichen. They shed their wings by twisting them off against the ground. A male finds a female, and the two of them scrape out a hole the size of a pea, seal it behind them with their own bodies' last free moisture, and never see anything again.

Almost all of them fail. The rock is a griddle by nine in the morning; there is no plant within two miles; the founding pair is living on the fat of the flight and on a single mouthful of fungus carried in the female's gut, and they have to reach a lava tube before it runs out. The young ground after a flight is stippled with millions of little chimneys a knuckle high, and in a season perhaps one in forty thousand of them is still going.

But the succulent seeds are already there — the climbers and the wind put them there — and where a founding pair and a seedling find each other, the pair's first taproot-chamber becomes the plant's root-tube, and both of them live, and in two hundred years there is a mound on that spot with a corridor of mothers in it.

## The tenants

Everything else on the plain is, in the strict sense, living in the masons' house and eating their crop.

**The grazers** — three tons, shoulder-high, plated, slow, with flat shearing teeth worn into basins — are the only other things here large enough to shape the land. They never drink. The jelly gives them everything, and what comes out of them is a dry grey pellet that the masons collect within the hour. They walk the same routes for generations, and the routes are the streets: wherever a grazer habitually goes, the masons cannot build, so the city's whole street plan has been drawn by animals with no interest in it. They rub. Every buttress on the plain is polished to a soft shine at shoulder height, hundreds of acres of hand-smooth ivory, and that polishing is the single most conspicuously *civilized*-looking thing on the continent, and it is done by something with the intelligence of a cow.

**The runners** stand shoulder-high and weigh a quarter of what a grazer's calf weighs, and take them anyway. Dark, dry hides; legs like fence wire; eyes the size of plums. They do not pant — panting would kill them. They gape, and the open mouth is a radiator, and the nose is the finest piece of engineering on the plain: a scroll of turbinate bone that recaptures the moisture from every breath, so that what a runner exhales is drier than the air it took in. They hunt in the forty minutes of dusk and the forty of dawn, in silence, and they spend the rest of the day pressed against the north side of a buttress in a state very close to being switched off.

**The climbers** are the noise of the canopy: troops of eight or ten, prehensile-tailed, with a tongue like a drill that bores a clean hole through rind into the jelly core. They gossip at a soft click all day. They are also the plain's only real travellers — they move over the tops of the arcades from crown to crown, carrying seeds in their fur and out of their gut, and the forest ahead of the flow front is largely their doing. They raid the mounds when they can, tearing a roof open for the cistern vaults, and a torn vault is the worst thing that can happen to a colony short of the lava.

**The walkers** — the heavy, half-flightless birds — nest on the chimney tops for the updraft, incubate their eggs on the colony's waste heat, and eat the colony by the thousand with a hooked bill and a sticky tongue slid into the ventilation slots. They are pure parasites of the city's climate and the city's flesh, and they are the reason not one inch of foraging traffic on this continent happens in the open. At first light one of them booms: a low three-note call that the mound beneath it amplifies and that carries, over ground this flat, for thirty miles. It is the loudest sound the world makes. Then it stops, and the hum comes back, and that is the day.

**The deep.** Under all of it, in the tubes, are the masons who stopped coming up. They have lost their wax and their soldiers and most of their speed; they are soft, slow, a little larger, and blue-white. They farm the root-fans directly, pruning the white frost off the ceiling in patterns that make it grow back thicker, so that the plant above — which has never been touched by them and cannot be aware of them — puts out more root and drinks more and grows better. Around them, in the black, live the pale legged shrimp-things that graze root exudate, and the wire-legged hunters that eat the shrimp-things, and one long, slow, blind, soft-bodied predator, four feet of it, that eats deep masons and has no natural enemy and has never in its lineage encountered light.

The deep colonies and the surface colonies descend from the same mothers and no longer recognize each other. When a gallery accidentally breaks through into an occupied tube, they fight for a week, and then the survivors wall it up.

---

If you camp here, they will survey you. You will feel nothing; you will simply notice in the morning that the underside of your pack has been mapped in a thin line of grit, and that the bootprint you left on the plaster last night has been resurfaced. If you die here you will not decay, because there is no water in the world to rot you with. You will go grey and hard, standing or lying wherever you stopped. And then, within a year or two, you will be measured, cut into portable pieces, and taken down into the dark, where the moisture still in you will be resorbed, and the rest of you will be ground, mixed with spit and dust, and set into a wall that is going somewhere — slowly, blindly, in a direction chosen by nobody, one grain at a time, ahead of the fire.
