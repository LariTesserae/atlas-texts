## The Combing Ground: An Account of the Occupants

### I. Who counts

I want first to settle a rule, because on a shelf two hundred paces long the difference between a resident and a visitor is the whole of the sociology.

The rule I use is this: you live in a place if you cannot easily leave it. By that measure the choughs do not live here. They are the wealthiest creatures on the mountain and their wealth is the third dimension; they hold the void the way a landowner holds a valley. The shelf is their table. Everything else on the shelf is a tenant of a film of moss millimetres thick, and cannot get off it, and will die within a few metres of where it hatched.

So: a population of some millions, an aristocracy of four hundred, a single warm-blooded householder, and — for a period I cannot date but would guess at some hundreds of years — nine people, of whom eight were buried by their successors.

### II. The tenants of the film

The **springtails** are the population. Not countable, but weighable: a handful of moss taken from under a cushion plant yields somewhere between six and nine hundred of them, and the shelf's entire soil — the only soil for a vertical kilometre — is made of their droppings, plus the exoskeletons of insects that were born eight thousand feet below in barley and were carried up here dying. The garden is fed by farms it will never see. Its nitrogen comes from aphids torn off a crop by a thermal, cooled to death in transit, and delivered. The springtails are the shelf's digestion. They have no name that anyone here would recognise, no behaviour that is not vaulting away, and no biography whatever, and they are nonetheless the reason there is anything green.

The **jumping spiders** are the reason the arithmetic is interesting. Four hundred of them, each holding a hand's width of territory: the shelf is fully parcelled. There is no vacant land. A spider that loses its ground does not find more; it goes onto grit, and the grit at noon is sixty degrees and the grit at night is nothing at all. These are hunters by eye in a place where the eye is the only honest sense. They will track a moving finger. They know a human shadow from a chough shadow — the chough shadow makes them freeze, the human shadow makes them turn and face it, which I find hard to write about without sentiment. They are colour of the fracture-face. They have never seen a member of their own species except to fight it or to breed with it, and both encounters last under a minute, and after either one they go back to the same hand's width of moss.

The **beetle** is the shelf's philosopher, if only by accident of scale. Eleven years to maturity, black, glossy, patrolling roughly six metres of grit at the moss margin. Work out the distances: it walks perhaps two kilometres a year, twenty-odd kilometres in a life, and every one of those kilometres is laid down inside a space you could cross in five paces. An enormous journey with no displacement. It is the local form of everything here — weather that never rains, water that never descends, a garden that cannot move. Motion without going anywhere is not a failure of ambition on this shelf. It is the only available design.

The **wingless fly** is the same argument stated brutally. Its ancestors had wings and the wind took them and their wings elsewhere, off the edge, into the cold, and deleted them; so the ones that survived to be ancestors were the ones that flew badly, and their descendants have stumps. At this altitude flight is a method of being erased. There is a whole taxonomy up here of creatures that have given back the sky.

The **harvestman**, finally, whose single accomplishment is height. At dawn it stands on the points of its wire legs to hold its body three centimetres clear of the frost, which is exactly enough, and which it must do for two hours without shifting, in silence, in the dark, every morning of its life. The tallest inhabitant of the shelf. Its entire adaptation is standing very slightly above the place it lives.

### III. The householder

Under the eastern scree, where the stone is dry and the slabs are stacked at the angle of repose, lives the only warm body that stays.

Call it the sunbather. Twelve kilos or thereabouts, heavy in the haunch, orange-toothed, a fermenter — a bag of hot grass with claws. It is the only creature on the shelf that eats the garden itself, and it eats it slowly, cropping the cushion rosettes with the care of somebody trimming a hedge they intend to keep. Its burrow is old. Not old in its own lifetime: old in the way the shelter is old. There are galleries under that scree that were dug by ancestors, packed with hay of the transparent grass, and the hay at the back of them is fossilised into a mat you could cut with a knife, and if you did you would be cutting through several centuries of summers.

It comes out at noon and lies down and does not run when you approach. I want to be exact about why. It is not tame. Tameness is a thing that happens to animals that have been given reasons and then had them withdrawn. This animal has never been given a reason at all. Nothing has ever come along the shelf that wanted it, except, presumably, the people, and the people are so far back in its lineage that the information has been lost. To be looked at by it is to be looked at by something with no file on you.

It whistles when disturbed, and the whistle does not carry, and it whistles anyway.

### IV. The ones who live in the air

The choughs live in the void and commute. Six of them, or nine; the count is unreliable because they leave downward and return from below, and the void has no landmarks in which to keep a tally.

They arrive out of nothing in the mid-morning without any visible effort of climbing, which is not a trick of the eye — they have simply solved a problem that no one else on this mountain has solved. A person coming up from the valley floor spends two days on it.
