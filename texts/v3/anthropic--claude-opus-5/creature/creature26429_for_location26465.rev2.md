## The Carriers

Start with them, because everyone does, and because they are the only residents that will walk past your boot without noticing you.

A full-grown carrier is the length of a forearm and matte to the point of looking unfinished, as though it had been cast and never buffed. The dorsal hod is a true dish — a concavity with a raised lip, four anchor hooks set at the corners of it, and a shallow gutter around the rim that has no obvious function until you watch a welding. The legs are absurd. Six of them, but the front two are twice the mass of the rest and end not in claws but in a broad splayed pad of stiff bristles, ten thousand to the pad, each bristle hooked at the tip. On the ribbed inner wall of a flue this pad will hold four times the animal's loaded weight. On the polished felt of the floor it holds nothing at all, which is why carriers walk the paths with a peculiar shuffling slide, like men crossing ice.

They are born in the wall like everything else, and for the first weeks of adult life they are pale and soft and work the floor as loaders, syrup-fed, chatty, blinking. Then they darken. The darkening takes about two days and is irreversible, and the last thing to close is the mouth. A climbing carrier has no working mouth. It cannot eat, it cannot drink, it cannot take a share off the plates in the ninety seconds of indoor rain, and it has, from the moment of sealing, somewhere between nine and fourteen days of stored body in which to be useful.

They do not blink. This is the thing to sit with. In a chamber where ten thousand lights carry on an unbroken conversation, the carriers are dark — not blind, their eyes are excellent, but silent. They have nothing further to contribute and no way of hearing it if they did. They receive one instruction, by contact, at the outfall, and then they climb.

## The Welders

Three centimetres, pale amber, and the busiest talkers in the chamber.

They work the Spinner's outfall in a crowd of two or three hundred, and their job is the marriage of seed to carrier. The seed comes off the helix hot and lacquered and is rolled by hand — by many hands — into the waiting hod. Then the welders run their resin glands around the rim gutter, and the gutter fills, and in about ninety seconds it sets, and the seed is no longer cargo. It is part of the animal.

This is the cruel engineering of the place and the reason the failure rate is what it is. A carrier five hours up a flue with a cramping pad cannot drop its load and save itself. There is no jettison. The two of them go together, and they always have, because a long time ago there must have been carriers that could shed, and a seed that falls from the ninth hour lands hard enough to craze its lacquer, and craze-repair costs the Spinner four days. The plant does not care about carriers. It cares about lacquer. So the weld got stronger, and the argument ended.

Welders choose. That is the interesting part. Not every carrier that presents itself is loaded; a great deal of blinking goes on across the outfall crowd before a given animal is turned away, and the turned-away go and stand at the edge of the light and wait, sealed, with their nine days running down. Nobody has established what the welders are assessing. Leg span, probably. Pad condition. Possibly nothing that can be seen.

## The Pan Crews

Under each flue mouth the felt is worn into a shallow bowl about four metres across, dished by impact rather than traffic, and it is the only place in the chamber with a distinct smell of its own — a sharpness under the bread and iron, like a struck match.

Things come down the flues all day. Not often; perhaps one in an hour across all sixty. The sound is a single flat thud with no echo, because felt two metres deep does not echo, and then there is a rasp that climbs very fast to a roar and then falls away, and that is the crew.

They are flat. Flatter than looks survivable, a centimetre thick and a hand across, with a rasping mouth on the underside that takes up a third of the body, and they live in the felt under the pans with only their forelimbs showing. When a fall lands they come out of the floor in a ring and converge, and they strip the carrier off the seed and the weld out of the gutter and the gutter off the shell, and in about four minutes there is nothing in the pan but a seed and a clean hod-dish, and the hod-dish goes too, more slowly, over the following hour.

The seed is then rolled back to the Spinner. It goes in. It is polished again.

## The Wall

The chewing is the wall eating itself and being regrown at the same rate, which means that what looks like a structure is in fact a current, and every plate in the chamber is about eleven years old and none of them are the same eleven years old.

Split one — you can, with a wedge and some nerve, though the local blinking will stop and stay stopped for about forty minutes afterward — and you get the eleven galleries, and the census is roughly this:

Outermost, in the black resin of the seam, the mites: four species, none longer than a grain of salt, all of them scraping the resin thin from the inside so that the plate stays flexible. Without them the chamber would go rigid and crack on the pulse. They are the maintenance.

Then the roaches, three strata of them, pale as boiled fish, which are simply the population. They eat plate and are eaten, and they are the great mass of the place, and they have no other role and need none.

Then the nursery: grubs in a wet mass like rice left out, tended, turned, sorted by size into chambers. And behind the nursery, at the back of the plate where the material is youngest and softest, the farmers — a long jointed animal, thirty segments, eyeless, with mouthparts modified into something that can only be called handling gear. It does not eat grubs. It eats what the grubs excrete, and it turns them and moves them and culls them, and it has been doing this in an unbroken line for longer than the Press has had its dish.

When you split a plate the farmer comes to the breach. It does not attack. It stands in the opening with its forward segments raised and waits, exactly as everything waits when you turn a lamp on, and when you have gone it repairs the plate from the inside in about nine days.

## The Lamps

The blinking is not one animal's habit. It is a shared medium, the way a road is shared, and at least forty species use it — welders, loaders, sanitation worms, the mites at the seams, moths that never leave the knee-high band, even, sluggishly, the plate-farmers. Sound was unavailable here. Sound in this chamber is a wall of groan and crack and ratchet and ten million jaws; nothing could be said in it. So the room went optical, and because everyone needed it, everyone learned it, and the result is a pidgin used across phyla with no common ancestor nearer than mud.

Here is the part that defeats readers. The flash itself carries almost nothing. What carries is *where*: distance from the nearest polished path, height above the felt, and angle relative to the Bellows. The same pulse of green means one thing a hand's breadth from a path and something else entirely a metre off it, and the grammar is therefore spatial and has to be read as a whole room at once, which no visitor has ever managed and no instrument has been designed for. Recordings exist. They look like weather.

Certain phrases recur. There is a formation that runs the wall from the cold end to the low end over about four minutes, roughly twice a day, always before shift change. There is a tight cluster of flashes that happens only in the airspace directly over the settling basin, and it happens constantly, more than any other structure in the corpus, thousands of times a day, from every caste, for as long as anyone has watched. Whatever the chamber's most-spoken sentence is, it is about the basin.

## The Basin

Which brings us to the citizens.

Lacquer is laid down and lacquer is abraded, and a seed that goes up a flue and comes down again is slightly heavier than when it left, because eleven hours of contact polish removes less than the Spinner adds. The arithmetic runs one way only. A seed that fails ten times is fractionally fat. A seed that fails a hundred times is a different object.

There are between three and nine hundred seeds in the chamber at any time, and the standing population is the oldest thing in it — older than any plate, any carrier line, any single organ except the Bellows. They are catalogued, by the animals, in whatever way the animals do it, and they are graded by weight, and the light ones go up.

In the settling basin, under the yellow flicker, there is a hoard of the others. Call them the unliftables. Two hundred-odd seeds so thickly lacquered that nothing in the chamber can get them above the fourth metre of a flue, gleaming like river stones, grown to twice their germinal size in a case of polish that is now, functionally, permanent. Some of them are nine hundred years old. Some of them are older than the dead tiers. They have been welded to carriers eight hundred times each and eight hundred times they have come back down, and every time they come back down they go into the Spinner, because the Spinner has no test for hopelessness, and they come out heavier.

They are the wealth of the place and they are the reason the whole system exists and they are never going to leave it. The plant polishes them anyway. Everything blinks about them constantly. Nobody has read what is said.

Eleven to forty seeds a year get out. All of them are young — a seed's whole window of escape is its first few decades, before the lacquer closes over it, and after that it joins the treasury and shines.

## The Floor

The harvestmen keep the middle empty, and they do it by drumming.

They stand with four legs on the felt and two raised, and they read the floor. Felt two metres deep transmits beautifully and dampens fast, so a harvestman can locate a footfall to within a body length at thirty metres and cannot hear anything at all beyond sixty. They work the exhale. On the intake the whole chamber's chorus swells and the floor is noise; on the exhale it cuts, and for about eleven seconds the void goes quiet and they listen, and then they cross the distance faster than seems reasonable for something built out of wire.

They do not take anything off a path. Not ever. A loader six inches into the open floor is meat and the same loader on the polished channel is invisible, and the distinction is absolute and has presumably been absolute for a very long time, because the paths are the throughput and the plant will wall off and mine anything that interferes with throughput. You can watch a harvestman follow a straggler to the edge of a path and stop, and stand, and wait, for an hour, for it to make an error.

This is the only practical advice the chamber offers a visitor. Stay on the channels. They are a hand's-width across and they are a covenant.

## The One in the Cistern

Pale, eyeless, the size of a cat, with skin like wet paper over a rib cage you can count. It lies in the third cistern with its head just clear of the surface and it does not move for weeks at a time, which ought, by the rules of this place, to have had it stripped to bone some centuries ago. Stillness is consent. Except here.

Nothing touches it. The pan crews wash their leavings into the cistern feed and it eats what arrives and it keeps the water clean, and beyond that nobody can say what it does or why it is exempt, only that the exemption holds and that there is never more than one.

It is not sessile. Perhaps three or four times a year it comes out, and it walks — slowly, badly, dragging, out along the cold-end paths and into the Flue Forest and sometimes as far as the basin — and while it is out, the room changes its speech. The blinking goes into a mode that is not otherwise recorded: slower, sparser, aligned to the flues rather than the paths, and it propagates ahead of the animal so that the chamber lights up in front of it the whole way and goes dark behind. Then it returns to the water and the room resumes.

There is no evidence at all that it perceives any of this. It is eyeless. It is being discussed, at length, by ten thousand lamps, in a language addressed past it, about it, and it lies in the cistern with its head above the water and breathes on the pulse like everything else.

## Upstairs

The three dead tiers have tenants, and they are the only slow animals in the place.

Lime-eaters: soft, white, finger-length, moving at something under a metre an hour, grazing the calcified scaffolding of an industry that ran on a different chemistry and stopped. They do not blink. They do not entrain to the pulse — up there the pulse is a rumour through several metres of grown material, and the rule about timing does not reach. They have no predators, because nothing that hunts by drumming can hunt on brittle lime, and they are therefore the only residents of the chamber with nothing whatsoever to fear, and they have responded to this by becoming the dullest animals in the system.

Their tunnels, though, follow the old pipework — they eat along the lines of greatest lime — so the tiers are slowly being engraved with a map of a plant that died twelve hundred years ago, hollowed out precisely along its circulatory system, valves and all, half-shut on an instruction that never arrived. It is the only record anything here keeps.

## The Landlord

And then the thing that owns the room, which is not an animal and is not a person and cannot be pointed at.

It has no centre because it never needed one. Its decisions take ten minutes and are made locally by resin spheres responding to sap chemistry from somewhere else; its memory is the thickness of its own material, laid down in layers, so that the flue chorus detunes across decades as the plant remembers pressure. It does not know about the carriers. There is no organ in it capable of knowing about the carriers. It grew a Spinner and an outfall and a gutter around a hod-rim it has never perceived, over a length of time in which every mistake had room to be selected out, and the animals filled in the rest because the syrup was there.

Its single preference, expressed over a very long time, in a root that gave up being a root and in hairs that all point the same way and in sixty pipes that go out of sight: *up*. Not light — nothing here has wanted light in a hundred thousand years. Just up, and out, and a few heavy polished things carried a very short distance into the dry, against the odds, by an animal with its mouth sealed shut.

## You

Seven minutes to the first arrival. Ninety minutes to bare pit.

You are warm, you are large, you are made of stored reserves in a place that abolished storage, and you are, at the moment you stop to rest in the middle of the floor, the single richest object in the chamber. Nothing here will attack you. Attack is not a thing this system does; it has no need of it and no organ for it. It will simply wait, with the patience of something that has outlasted two failures of the Bellows and a change of chemistry and twelve hundred years of a plant it does not remember, until you hold still for about ten minutes.

And then ten thousand lamps will say something about you, at length, from every plate at once, and you will not be able to read it, and it will not have been a warning.
