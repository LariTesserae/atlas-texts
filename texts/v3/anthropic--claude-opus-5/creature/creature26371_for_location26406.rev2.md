# Who Lives on the Table

## The Long Residents

Ask anyone up here who lives on the Table and they will answer with a number somewhere between eleven and twelve thousand, and they will not be counting people.

The columns are the residents. Everything else is a tenant. This is not a poetic framing that the town has adopted and gets misty about; it is the plain shape of their grammar. Columns take the animate case. People, shearers, bats and beetles all take the other one, the case used for things that move, which is also the case used for weather and for falling rock. To move is to be temporary. The long grey things that stand still and hold the water are the ones actually in residence.

They have names, all of them, and the names are not decorative. A name records who first cut it, and the thickness of the trunk at the first cut, and whatever happened nearby. *Bat-in-the-Mouth.* *Cut-by-a-Left-Hand.* *Two Sisters Argued Here.* *Slow To Scar.* Some names are eleven or twelve hundred years old and the events inside them are no longer recoverable; nobody now knows which two sisters, or what the argument was, only that the column still holds it, and that its water still tastes faintly of iron compared to its neighbours.

They are not, individually, especially interesting to watch. They fatten a finger's width every ten years. They flower once in four decades. They lean, imperceptibly, away from the northeast wind, so that the whole grove is very slightly combed, and the lean of a trunk is a rough clock: a column tipped a hand's breadth off vertical has stood there something over nine hundred years. Once or twice in a generation an old one falls. It goes over slowly, catching in the arms of its neighbours, hanging in the canopy sometimes for twenty years before it comes down, and the town builds around the hanging corpse and eventually through it, and the falling — when it finally comes — is the largest event most people will ever witness. It takes three houses. Everyone knows which three, decades in advance. They are cheap houses, and given to the young.

## The Drone

The shield-insects are the most numerous inhabitants by a factor no one has ever tried to express, because arithmetic here stops at a few hundred and then becomes gestural. They clamp on, drive the stylet in, and stay. A female lives four years without ever letting go, and what she excretes, having taken far more sap than she needs for the sugar in it, is a clear syrup that drips steadily from the canopy all night.

So the town hangs mats. Waxed paddle-fibre, stretched between the arms, tilted, guttered — and in the small hours, when the grove is cold enough that the syrup runs rather than sets, every household on the upper levels is catching what is, functionally, the only rain that will ever fall on them. It is not much. A mat the size of a bedsheet yields a swallow. But it is the swallow that gets drunk first in the morning, by whoever climbed up to fetch it, and that right is defended with real heat.

The insects themselves are eaten by the double handful, dry-roasted on the hot stones at midday until they pop. They taste of nothing much. They are the potato of this world.

After about fifty years of standing under the drone, most people go deaf, and this is not treated as a loss but as a stage of life, like the greying of hair. The polite way to say that someone has grown old is to say that they have *gone quiet*. Nobody pities the quiet. They are simply in the part of life where the noise has finished, and they get the low, warm, flat-floored houses at the bottom of the town, and the children are sent down to sit with them and practise their hands.

## The Small Ones

Under the drone, in the litter, a second world.

The boring beetles are farmed in the loosest possible sense, which is to say that nobody plants them and everyone knows where the good galleries are. Their larvae are white, soft, thumb-sized and something over ninety percent water. A child's first job is larva-picking, and a child is trusted with a blade only after they have learned not to eat what they pick.

The lizards come in two kinds: the long-legged wild ones that clatter through the litter and are eaten, and the small pale ones that are kept. These live in warmed baskets, are fed on beetles, and are used for washing. They are put on the skin and they lick — the face first, then the hands, then wherever is required — and their tongues are rough and fastidious and they work a body over in about a quarter of an hour. Most adults own one. Grandmothers own several and lend them out as a favour of considerable social weight. The animal is warm, dry, patient, and entirely uninterested in you, and being cleaned by one is the closest thing to bathing that anyone on the Table will ever experience.

The bats live below the town, in the gas-cavities, which means the town's floor is their ceiling and the town's night is full of their scrabbling coming up through the stone. They are poor fliers in this air and better climbers, and they come out at dusk in a screaming corkscrew because they cannot get lift any other way. The adolescents who live high in the canopy have an entire private culture built around them: marking them with resin dots, naming them, feeding them moths by hand, competing over which of them will come to a particular whistle. It is understood by everyone that this is a phase and that they will grow out of it, and also that anyone who kills a marked bat has done something unforgivable to a specific fifteen-year-old.

The flightless birds are grey, fat, stupid, delicious, and very fast on their feet. Their ancestors flew up here from somewhere and then declined to leave. Everyone on the Table has that in common with them, and the joke is made often.

## The Shearers

There are somewhere between thirty and forty on the plateau. Everyone knows all of them by name, by the pattern of chips in the shell, by gait.

They are not owned. They are not, honestly, even herded. A shearer decides where it is going at a speed that makes argument pointless, and what the keepers do is walk ahead of it, clearing spine-litter from its path, driving off the bats that pester the soft skin at the neck, and singing to it — except that *singing* is wrong, because what they actually do is knock. A shearer hears very little, but it feels the granite. Each animal answers to a rhythm, a particular tapped pattern on the rock with a bone mallet, and these rhythms are inherited property, handed down in keeper-families along with the responsibility.

Consider what this means. A shearer lives five hundred years and a keeper lives sixty. The animal has known fifteen generations of the family that walks with it. It was answering that knock when the town was three levels shorter and the streets ran differently. The keepers are perfectly clear-eyed about the relationship: *we are the short ones.* A keeper introduces herself to her animal, formally, in her twenties, and considers herself to have been accepted when it turns its head. Some never are.

And every so often — perhaps twice in a lifetime — an old shearer will answer a rhythm that nobody living uses. It will stop, and turn, and wait, and the keeper standing there will have no idea what has been asked of her, because the family that knew that pattern died out four hundred years ago. There is a word for this. It is not a happy word. People go and look, and stand about, and eventually the animal gives up and moves on, and the incident is recorded, and the rhythm is guessed at and argued over for a generation.

The dead are carried on shearer-back to the column that will take them. It is the only cargo work the animals are asked to do, and they do it, and nobody knows whether they know.

## The Four Hundred

Now: the people.

They are short and deep. The chest is the notable thing — barrel-sprung, the ribs sprung wide, so that even the children have the silhouette of a small cask on legs. Their blood is thick and dark and slow to stop when it runs. Their hands and feet are broad and horned. They are not, by the standards of anywhere else, healthy-looking: the lips are cracked, the skin is a permanent dusty grey-brown from wax and body-flour rubbed in daily as protection against the air, and it flakes, and the flaking is not considered a defect but a form of shedding, and the flakes go back into the floor with everything else.

They squint. The pupils are large — the grove is dim, and the eye adapts to that bronze half-light — which makes the edge of the plateau, where the sun hits the bare granite unfiltered, physically unbearable to look at for more than a moment. Everyone who goes to the rim goes with their face wrapped and their eyes slitted, and everyone who stands there feels the particular nausea of too much space. They call it the *wide sickness*. Going to watch the rain is genuinely an ordeal, an act of tourism performed against the body's advice, and this is part of why children are taken: to prove that the world is larger than nine minutes' walk, and to demonstrate exactly how little they want it to be.

They move slowly and they are not ashamed of it. Haste costs water. There is one word for *running* and it also means *disaster*; you do not run unless something is falling, and if someone runs, everyone else stands up. Sweating is the most expensive thing a body can do. A person who loses their temper and shouts themselves damp has committed a small, visible, faintly embarrassing act of waste, and will be teased about it — gently, at length, for years.

Spitting is a crime with a proper penalty. Weeping is a gift, given deliberately, caught on the back of a small bone spoon and dried to a salt-mark on a thread; the threads are worn at the throat, and a person's threads are read the way another culture would read scars or medals. A long-married couple will have perhaps six between them. To be given a tear and to lose the thread is a wound that families carry for centuries.

## Voice, Hand, Floor

There are three channels, and everyone uses all three, and which one you are using says as much as the words.

The **shouting register** is the public voice: hard-consonanted, front-loaded, built of clicks and stops that punch through the drone. It is how business is done, how children are called, how a stranger is addressed. It has no soft sounds in it at all. It is not an angry register, but it cannot help sounding like one.

The **hands** run continuously alongside and slightly ahead of the voice, and take over entirely at distance, in the dark, and among the quiet old. It is a full language, not a supplement, and it is the register in which jokes are made, because a joke wants timing and the drone eats timing.

The **quiet register** exists only within a hand's breadth of another person's ear. It is the language of the sleeping platform. It uses every soft sound the shouting register has thrown away — breath, hum, sibilant — and it is unusable at any distance greater than intimacy. You cannot speak it to someone you are not touching. The practical result is that the drone has made privacy trivially easy and closeness technically difficult, and the culture has taken the technical difficulty and made it the whole architecture of love. To be quiet-spoken to is the thing. Adolescents lie awake in the canopy listening to the wall of noise and wondering who will ever come close enough.

And then there is the **floor**. The granite is hollow in a hundred places, and the plaza stones ring at different pitches, and the town talks through its feet. Knock-code carries the length of the settlement when a shout carries thirty paces. It is how the shearers are called, how a birth is announced, how the council is convened, how a falling column is warned of. Music, such as it is, is stamping — whole crowds of people working a slow polyrhythm out of the floor of the world, felt in the shins, the only sound on the Table that is louder than the insects.

## Cohorts

Everyone belongs to a flowering. You do not ask a person's age; you ask which flowering they are, and the answer places them in a cohort of forty or fifty who were conceived in the same wild week and who will be old together and die roughly together, and whose loyalty to one another cuts across household, column, and blood.

Which leaves the ones born in the trough. There are fewer of them and they grow up without a cohort, belonging nowhere in particular, and the town has quietly built a role for them out of that: they are the ones who go between. Messengers, matchmakers, mat-runners, the people who carry a grievance from one household to another so that neither has to be seen approaching the other. They marry outward, they sleep in different places, they know everyone's business and are forgiven for it. A cohort is a block of stone; the trough-born are the sap-mortar. They know this about themselves. Some of them resent it beautifully.

## The Knives, and the Deep-Cutters

The black glass does not exist here. There is no volcano on the Table and there never was; every blade on the plateau came up from below in some century nobody can name, and there are perhaps two hundred left. They are resharpened, which means they shrink. A tapping knife is therefore a family's age made visible: the new-money houses carry palm-length blades, and the oldest lineages hold slivers the size of a fingernail, worked with terrifying precision by people whose grandmothers taught them to cut with almost nothing. When a blade finally becomes too small to haft, it is pierced and worn in the ear, and the family goes to the council and is loaned another, and the loan is recorded, and it is a species of bankruptcy that takes four generations to live down.

The knives are for the cuts, and the cuts are the whole of the law. Finger-width, angled, scarring closed in two seasons, one to a column per generation, climbing the trunk in a ladder of pale ovals. You can read a family's history off a trunk the way you would read a page: the confident cuts, the shaky first cut of a fourteen-year-old, the year somebody cut twice because a child was dying, the long gap where a lineage nearly failed.

And there is one column, near the western rim, called *Answer For It*, which was cut too deep in a year everybody can still name. It has been dying for three hundred years. The arms have gone brown from the tips inward at the rate of about a hand's length a decade, and it has perhaps sixty years left, and the family responsible still lives, still bears the name, and has been given — not as punishment, exactly, but as the only sensible use for them — the job of inspecting everybody else's cuts. They are the deep-cutters. They walk the grove with a measuring reed and they are not liked and they are not shunned and their word is final, and every one of them grows up under a slowly browning tree that their however-many-greats-grandmother killed, and they are the most careful people on the Table.

## The Comers

Perhaps once in thirty years, someone arrives from below.

They come up the gulf by a route that is not written down and takes eleven days, and they arrive blue-lipped, coughing blood, half out of their minds, and they are taken in, because turning someone away would cost more in argument than keeping them. They never fully acclimatise. They cough for the rest of their lives. They are treated with a careful, slightly awkward tenderness, and they are asked — endlessly, by everyone, for decades, at every feast — to describe rain. Not to see it; to describe being *in* it. Water falling on the face. Hair wet. Clothing wet. The smell of it.

Their accounts have become a genre. There are perhaps forty rain-descriptions in the town's repertoire, memorised, attributed by name — *Tallow's rain*, *the Fourth-Flowering man's rain* — and performed. They contradict each other wildly. No one has ever been able to check.

The children of Comers are entirely ordinary, deep-chested, grey-dusted, squinting, and have never once wanted to go down.

## What Nobody Has Decided

They have no creation story. What they have instead is a story about subtraction: that there was a mountain, that it was enormous, that it went away, and that the going-away has not stopped, it has only slowed to a speed that four hundred people cannot perceive. The rim retreats. Everyone accepts this. Everyone accepts, further, that there will be a last generation — a small number of thin people on a small flat rock with three or four columns left standing — and that this is simply the far end of the schedule, no more tragic than the far end of a life.

They do not find this bleak. They find it *settled*, which is the highest thing they can say about anything. The arguments are never about whether. They are about the tapping rights of the last ninety years, which are held, on paper, by a family that will not exist for another eleven thousand years, and which are nevertheless discussed with total seriousness on the warm floor of the plaza, in the shouting register, under the drone, by people sitting on a grey cushion of moss that has been getting thicker at the rate of a fingernail per lifetime since before anyone had a name for it.
