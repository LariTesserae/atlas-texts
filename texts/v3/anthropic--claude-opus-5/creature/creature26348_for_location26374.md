## What Lives Here Is Not Adapted To It

Begin with the thing that makes them strange, which is that they cannot settle.

An animal fits its country. Given long enough, the country teaches it what shape to be, and the lesson holds, because the country stays where it is. The Rise does not stay. It goes up four to six metres a year and takes everything on its back with it, so that the people here are not adapted to a place but engaged in a pursuit — climbing the inside of their own home, always slightly behind, always adjusting. They are not a species. They are a procession.

You see it first in the chests. A man of seventy on the Rise was born nearly four hundred metres lower, in air with weight in it, and he has spent his whole life following that air upward as it thinned beneath him. He is barrel-ribbed, over-lunged, dark-blooded, and still he stands in the street with his hands on a pipe bundle and breathes carefully, like a man carrying something full. His granddaughter, born at the present summit, runs up the helical stair without noticing it. The young here are physically superior to their elders in a way that is not the usual arrogance of youth but a simple fact of geology, and everyone knows it, and it has been written into the arrangement of the city: the new works at the top are staffed by children and the unmarried young, and authority lives lower down, in the warm dense middle of the collar, where the old can still think.

## Breath, and What It Costs

They are quiet people because talking is expensive.

The language of the Rise has been filed down by six generations of thin air into something very short. Words of one syllable, mostly; verbs that carry their own subjects; a grammar that permits you to say in four sounds what a flatlander needs a sentence for. They speak on the outbreath only, low, close to the ear, and they do not raise their voices even in anger — anger on the Rise is expressed by *slowness*, by taking a very long pause before answering, and the length of that pause is legible to everyone present down to about a second.

Interruption is the cardinal rudeness. To take the air out of someone else's mouth is nearly assault. Conversations consequently have great craters in them, and a visitor sits through those craters in agony while the locals rest and think and drip.

The rest is hands. There is a full second language in the fingers, developed for the works where a press descending a centimetre an hour still requires four people to agree about it, and it has crept out of the works and into the street and the bed and the deathroom, until now the most intimate things they say are said without air at all. A couple sitting on a warm terrace wall at dusk may hold an entire quarrel in their laps and never once be heard.

## The Stone Inside

They drink constantly and are never quite watered. Everyone from the age of three carries a cup — tin, personal, dented, hung at the belt, filled a hundred times a day from the gutters and the condenser drips and the public spouts. To be seen with a dry cup is to be pitied. To fill a stranger's cup unasked is the local form of tenderness, more or less what a touch on the arm is elsewhere.

And the water is the mountain's water. It comes out of the ground already loaded, and it is boiled and settled and passed through beds of crushed off-spec block, and it is still loaded, and so the people of the Rise grow rock inside themselves. They call it the stone and they nearly all get it. Somewhere in their forties the kidneys begin their own small quarry work, and there is a whole respected trade of stone-doctors with their tinctures and their hot presses and their appalling patience, and there is an etiquette of sympathy so refined that a man can announce he is passing one with a single gesture and be given a seat, a filled cup, and silence.

They keep them. Of course they keep them. The stones are drilled and strung and worn at the throat, a life's yield on a wire, and a woman with eleven is regarded with a mixture of respect and horror. When they die the string goes downhill with them: the only part of a person that was made the same way the mountain was made, returning.

## Their Trades and Their Government

The levellers rule, because the levellers are the only ones who can be trusted to think in the correct units.

They are not architects and not quite priests. They are a guild of people who spend their working lives on their bellies in access ports under buildings, sliding shims of cast stone into courses, raising the northeast corner of a bakery by two millimetres while the survey looks over their shoulder. Their hands are ruined — split, salt-burnt, permanently grey at the knuckle — and they read walls by pressing a cheek to them, and they are trained from the age of nine to lie down on fresh cut rock at night and listen to the ticking until they can tell you which way the ground is going and how fast. A senior leveller can walk a lane and name every house that is out of true, and does, out loud, in passing, as a kind of muttered rosary.

Under them: the pourers, who make the city out of the city's own sweat, and are proud and slightly deranged by heat. The gutter-wardens, who own the fog. The drapers, who go round with hot chisels cutting flowstone back off the doorways before it closes them — an old man's work, endlessly repeated, the local emblem of futility and duty at once. The loopsmiths, who bend the slack bronze intestines that let one terrace move against another without tearing. The netters in the pools. And at the bottom, socially and literally, the mat-scrapers of the lower vent quarter, who harvest the rust and ochre and bad-green thermophile rings and press the protein cake, and who smell of it, and who are married mostly among themselves.

Every one of these trades is organised around the same fact: nothing here can be finished, only maintained, and maintained forever, at a pace that would drive an outsider to weeping.

## How They Speak Of Time

They have no words for past and future. They have *downhill* and *uphill*.

A child says *when I'm uphill* and means when I'm grown. An old woman says *that was well downhill of here* and means her wedding, forty years and two hundred metres ago. To say a thing is *in the collar* is to say it is current, alive, relevant, happening — because the fog collar is where the water is, and the water is where the city is, and the city is only ever *now*. Grief is a downhill sensation. Hope is a shortness of breath.

Their calendars are elevations. Documents are dated in metres above plain. A person's birth-height is his identity, spoken with his name; the census is a list of heights; a marriage contract states the difference between the parties in metres and adjusts the obligations accordingly, because someone born ninety metres lower than you is a different generation of lung and everyone knows what that will cost eventually.

## The Wood

There is no wood, and so wood is holy, and the reason is not scarcity.

Wood is the only substance on the Rise that can die. Everything else here is preserved: the stone does not rot, the salt does not soften, the scratch of a coin outlives the grandchildren of the hand that made it, the abandoned quarters sit down there in the dry heat in perfect condition, unrotted, immaculate, appalling. Into that permanence the rich import a chair. They keep it inside the collar, in the warm continuous drizzle, precisely where it will swell and bloom and go soft — and they watch it, over decades, doing the one thing nothing else on the mountain will do. It is not decadence. It is devotion. A family with a chair three generations gone to punk and fungus will show it to you with the expression of people showing you a shrine.

And every corpse on the Rise goes down with a splinter of wood between its teeth, so that something in the grave, at least, will eventually be gone.

## Downhill

From the summit the dead terraces are described as unentered. The word means unlived-in. It does not mean nobody goes.

The city's dead are barrowed out with the tailings — the same route, the same slow carts, the same ordinance — and carried down through the white streaks below the collar to whichever quarter dried in their lifetime, and set in the rooms they knew. There is no burial. There is nothing to bury into and nothing that requires it. They are placed sitting, in their own former houses, on shim-founded floors that will never be jacked again, with their string of stones in their lap, and the door is left open, because the flowstone that would have closed it has no water now and has stopped growing forever.

So the flanks of the Rise are populated. Ring upon ring of dry preserved streets, each one holding the generation that died while it was still wet, salt-cured, leathery, undisturbed, in rooms whose walls carry the coin-scratched signatures of everyone who ever lived in them — legible, all of it, for as long as this world lasts. A leveller's surveyor sent down to read an old benchmark walks through two hundred years of neighbours and comes back up subdued and drinks a great deal of water and does not talk about it, and everyone lets him not talk about it, and nobody asks.

They cannot leave, incidentally. That should be said. A Rise-born who descends to the plain is drunk within a day on all that air, and sick for a month, and stupid for a season; the traders who come up wheezing and are laughed at in the market go home again, and the people who laughed at them never can. Whatever they are becoming, they are becoming it here, in one direction, at four to six metres a year.

## The Ones That Get Out

There is a thing they do with the chin, a small upward tilt, which is not a greeting and not a shrug and has no name that translates. It means the birds.

The high dark crescents on the thermal, riding the column of the mountain's own heat, circling all day and all night, never landing — those are the only things on the Rise that are not going up. They are not part of the procession. They came from somewhere with soil in it and they will go back, and their nests are out on the plain in the world where things fall down, and nobody here has ever seen one on the ground and nobody expects to.

At dusk, then, at the summit works: an apprentice of eleven, born at a height her grandmother has never stood on, lies face-down on a fresh cut face with her cheek to the amber stone, cup at her belt, holding still, listening for the fine granular ticking of the rock making room for itself. Below her the warm bright belt of fog, dripping into ten thousand tin gutters. Below that, going down and down in orderly rings, the open doors, the seated ancestors, the mouths full of splinters, the signatures, all of it holding its shape in the dark heat.

Above her, the birds, in their slow indifferent circles, going nowhere in particular.

She tilts her chin at them without deciding to, and goes back to listening.
