# The Sumper

## First, the wrong answer

Ask what lives in the Loudland and someone will point at the sky, because the sky is where the movement is: gasp-flies boiling off a hot throat in columns you can see from forty kilometres, windgills turning in the updraught like burnt paper that forgot to land. And it's a reasonable mistake. But watch those things for a season and you'll notice they don't *live* here so much as *occur* here. A windgill is not an animal that uses a thermal; it is a shape the thermal makes, given a membrane and a tolerance for hunger. Gasp-flies are the exhaust with a generation time. Vent-clamps are, if you're honest about it, scabs — the pelt's own tissue that got loose a hundred million years ago, kept its keratin and lost its address, and still spends its whole existence sealing a wound in something it used to be part of. There's no clean line where the floor stops and the fauna starts. Most of what moves out there is weather with appetites.

So narrow the question. Who, in this place, can be *surprised*? Who arrives somewhere on purpose? Who has a future they're working on?

There's one answer, and it's the one the maps call the keystone destroyer, and the description is accurate and, in every way that matters, backwards.

## Feet

Start with the feet, because the sumper is mostly feet with a body slung underneath for storage.

Eight of them, splayed, each ending in a pad the size of a dinner plate — a taut grey drumskin stretched over a fluid-filled boss, and under that a fan of bone, and under that a nerve trunk as thick as a wrist running up into a skull cavity that in any other animal would be given over to eyes. The pads are tuned. Not identically: fore-pads read high, hind-pads read low, and the two mid-ranks split the middle, so a standing sumper is an eight-channel array with a two-metre baseline, pressed flat against a continent-sized diaphragm that transmits beautifully and never stops talking.

What it gets from this is not "hearing" in the sense you'd want. It's nearer to a room. A sumper standing still with all eight down knows the bearing and the health of every stack within three hundred kilometres: which piper has detuned, which slattern's cough has gone wet, which kettle is running its floor-note a shade flat because its ballast has cracked along the eastern seam. It knows where the other sumpers are by their footfall and where the big herds are by the hiss of ten thousand small feet and where the wind will be in an hour by the way the draw is shifting between two throats a hundred kilometres apart. It stands in the middle of a chord that is also a map that is also a weather forecast and a census, and it stands there for a long time, because it is in no hurry ever, and because standing still is how you listen.

It is blind, sheathed in soot, and cannot make a sound with its mouth. It has never seen another of its kind and never will. This is not a deprivation. Two sumpers meeting have already known each other for six kilometres.

## What it is actually doing in there

The received account: a sumper chews into a sap-main, drinks, plugs the duct, and thereby kills the stack. All true, and it gets the motive exactly wrong.

Sap is everywhere. Sap is the floor. A sumper can open the pelt anywhere it likes with three minutes' work and feed till it's sick; sap is not worth walking for and certainly not worth dying for, and sumpers die at the base of stacks all the time.

They go in for the drop-wells.

Down at the ankle, behind the armour plating the stacks have grown specifically and only against this animal, are the resin nodules — decades of a few grams a day, walled off and forgotten, a spoonful here, a fist there. That is what the sumper smells from twenty kilometres upwind, and that is what it chews four metres of ply to reach, and the sap-main it wrecks on the way in is incidental. It drinks the main because the main is in the way and because it is thirsty work. The plug that follows, the slow overinflation, the note going wrong, the week-long radial exodus of everything that can walk — all of that is collateral. The whole turbulence of this landscape is the wake of an animal reaching past it for something else.

It hollows out the nodules, and it does not swallow what's inside. It shunts it, by a valve in the throat, into a lined bladder under the ribs — the sump — where a lifetime's takings sit sloshing, a few hundred millilitres at best, the only mobile water on the continent.

Then it seals its belly over the breach like a vent-clamp over a valve, because everything else here also smells water at parts per trillion, and the small quick things are already arriving.

## Why water

Because the sumper does not need water to live, any more than you need gold to live. It runs on sap like everything else. Water, to a sumper, is a material.

There is exactly one thing it is a material for. A sumper egg is a lacquered case about the size of a skull, and inside it — the only place in the Loudland where this is true — there is a pool. Perhaps a cupped handful. Enough to keep an embryo wet for the nine or eleven years it takes to finish, because nothing dries in there, because the case is glazed like the mirror-scales and loses nothing at all.

That's the economy. That's the whole engine of this animal's existence. Between nine and thirty stacks, depending on luck and greed and how long you live, must be opened and drained to fill one egg. Every hole in the music, every white flash and four-second collapse and terrifying local silence, is somewhere in the back-accounting of somebody's pregnancy. Three hundred blowdowns a day across the plateau. Call it fifteen or twenty children.

Nothing here was built; everything here works. The egg is the nearest thing to a made object in the Loudland, and even that isn't made, only filled — a decade of walking, condensed.

## Names are rhythms

They know each other by tread. Each sumper's eight pads land in a signature that is as individual as a face and, unlike a face, audible for tens of kilometres through the floor, and impossible to disguise, and slowly altered by age and injury into something more itself.

So the ones that matter have names, in the only sense available. There is an old female working the southern slattern-fields whose left hind went bad forty years ago and who has come down, through long adjustment, to a gait you could transcribe as *three-and-a-drag*. Everything within two hundred kilometres knows that rhythm. Small scavengers orient on it and follow it for months, because where it goes, ply gets opened. Rivals hear it coming into their district and go and stand somewhere else, not out of fear exactly, but the way you'd rather not be in a shop someone else is already robbing. When it stops — and one year it will stop — no announcement will be made. The rhythm simply will not occur again, anywhere, and that absence is the whole of the obituary.

They speak, a little, in the same medium. Stamping: a slow claim-beat at the foot of a stack that means *this one is mine and I have been here eleven days*; the fast triple that means *the note is going, go now*, which is the only altruistic thing any of them do and which they do reliably, even to strangers, even to rivals, even when it costs them the well. Contests are shoving matches conducted over three or four days by two blind animals leaning on each other in perfect silence, each reading the other's weight through the floor.

## The gamble

A sumper can hear, precisely, how long a stack has left. The rising note, the thump of the bellows-hold going arrhythmic, the particular grinding in the ribbing at about eighty percent of the way to failure — all of it comes up through the pads clearer than the roar comes down through the air. The old ones can call it to within a day and a half.

So they cut it fine. Deliberately. The last nodules are the deepest and the oldest and the fullest, and every extra hour inside is worth more than the hour before it, and the animal is standing in the ankle of a four-hundred-metre pressure vessel that it has personally sabotaged and that is now singing its way up to the failure point in a voice you could feel in Kettle country.

Most sumper deaths are this. Not predation — nothing here predates a sumper; the thing is a ton of soot-plated hide and there's no water in it worth the trouble until it's full, and when it's full it's dangerous. They die of arithmetic. They stay for one more nodule and the ribbing goes and a kilometre of shaft lies down across the flats, and afterwards there is the hole in the music, and the silence, and nothing standing at the middle of it.

And the sump splits, and about a pint of liquid water lands on open pelt, which is a riot, briefly, and then a slow one — every fast small thing on the horizon converging, and then the floor itself puckering upward, tasting it, growing toward it, closing over the body within a day and drinking the lot. Down it goes into the galleries. Decades on, somewhere in the ankle of the new stack that blistered up six kilometres east, there is a resin nodule that shouldn't be there by any reaction sequence, fatter than it has a right to be, waiting for somebody's granddaughter to smell it from twenty kilometres upwind.

No liquid falls. All flow is interior. That rule is larger than it looks. It includes this.

## Kettle country

A mother with a full sump goes to the bass.

It takes her months, unhurried, the heaviest she will ever be, travelling in a small private weather of her own — vent-clamps riding her back plates for the free transport, gasp-flies in her wake, three or four windgills holding station above her because a crowd on the ground means an opened well sooner or later. She is worth robbing now and she knows it and she doesn't care, because nothing out here can chew what she's made of.

Kettles don't blow. Eighty metres, half a kilometre wide, no throat to lose, venting through a hundred small holes at a pressure so low and so patient that the whole lineage barely notices the centuries. Under the skirt of a kettle the pelt runs warm at the seams and the floor-note comes up through the ground at a frequency you could sleep inside, and that is where the egg goes: wedged into a seam, lacquered over, left.

Nine years. Eleven. The mother doesn't guard it — there's no reason to; the case is harder than anything with a mouth around here — but she tends to keep her circuits wide and come back through every year or two and stand over it a while with all eight pads down, doing nothing that can be measured.

What hatches is a thing the size of a dog, damp for about four minutes, the only damp creature this world produces, and then never again.

It follows. That's the whole of its education and it lasts twenty years. It walks behind an adult and learns, through its own developing pads, which cough means a slattern's ply has gone soft and easy, which piper's semitone shift means the district's draw is about to reverse, which kettle lies at the end of which week of walking. It learns the chord the way you learned your city: not as information but as the shape of being somewhere. The oldest sumpers can locate stacks that fell before they were born, by the shape of the hole left in the sound, and they walk out and drink the nodule the pelt grew over the wreck, and that is a kind of history, held in the feet, unrecorded, correct.

## Late

Pads go hard with age. The high channels fail first: the shrieks thin out, then the mid-register groaning, and the world contracts from three hundred kilometres to forty to six. An old sumper is a deafening animal going quietly deaf inside the loudest place there is, navigating on memory and the one frequency it has left, which is the kettles, which is the floor.

They mistime a stack, in the end. Almost all of them. Or they stop at the edge of the bass and stand, and keep standing, and the soot dulls and the flies stop bothering, and one morning the pelt tastes what's in them and begins, unhurriedly, to come up.

At night, when the mirror-scales fold shut in that long coin-landslide wave across the horizon and the pipers detune into silence and the rime starts sifting down out of the calm air, you can find them out on the flats, unmoving: big soot-black shapes at enormous intervals, each one alone, all eight feet planted, sump half-full, facing nothing in particular because facing is not a thing they do.

They are listening to the kettles. They have been doing it for eleven million years. There is nothing in it for them — no food in that note, no water, no mate, no danger. It's just the one sound in the world that never stops, and they stand in it the way you'd stand under stars, and then at some point in the night they pick a bearing and start walking, and the ground tells everybody who it was.
