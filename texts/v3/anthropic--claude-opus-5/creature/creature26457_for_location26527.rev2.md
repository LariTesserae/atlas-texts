## The Tenant

Take the census first. It will be wrong, but it will be wrong in an instructive way.

Five things live here. A moss. A mound of stacked plates on the steam pipe. A pale tree fern at the lip of the fissure. A rosette of twelve cupped leaves out along the conduit under the failing bulb. And threads — everywhere, crossing the open concrete, going nowhere.

That is what you would write in the notebook on the first visit, while the sweat ran into your eyes and the transformer took up residence behind your forehead. On the second visit you would begin crossing out the full stops between the entries. By the fourth you would have one line left, and it would not be a list.

---

Start with the moss, because it is the skin of the thing and it is everywhere you look, and because it is the easiest to be wrong about.

It is a moss the way a bat is a mouse. Under a lens it has the parts — the little spiralled leaves one cell thick, the rhizoids — but the rhizoids are doing something no moss should bother with: they are etching. They go into the concrete and take the lime out of it, and the grout between the bricks is not grouting any more, it is a soft grey paste being slowly digested in place. The wall is food. This is a plant eating a building at the rate of a fingernail's thickness a decade, and it has been eating for a century, and you can put a knife into the mortar to the hilt.

Touch it. This is the surprise. In a room that is raining on itself, the moss is dry. Not dry inside — inside it is saturated, it is basically wet felt — but the surface sheds. Water will not wet it. Beads stand up on the cushions like mercury and travel, and where several travel together they cut a channel, and the channels are old and have a shape, and if you follow them with your lamp you will find that all of them, all of them, arrive eventually at the sump. The room's drip is not random. It is plumbed. What looks like a chamber weeping on itself at hazard is a catchment with about eleven metres of guttering in it, laid by something with no eyes, over a length of time that makes the word *laid* embarrassing.

And the glow. Understand that the glow is a symptom. There is too much energy down here and not enough to do with it — heat without light is a wage paid in a currency the plant cannot spend — and there is sulphur coming up in the water, and something has to be done with all that reduced chemistry before it curdles the cell. What gets done produces a photon at the end of the process the way a shovelling man produces a grunt. Forty minutes after every pump cycle the whole room brightens, very slightly, for about an hour. That is the moss working off the meal. It is the only season of light there has ever been in this place, and it is exhaust.

The mug in the sump is the brightest thing in the chamber, and it is brightest because it is starving. Clean condensate, no lime, nothing to eat. The colony in there can't grow, so it puts everything out as light. You will find it beautiful. It is a famine.

---

The fern at the fissure is not bent over the crack to be warm. It is drinking.

That pale fibrous trunk is not wood. It is matted old leaf bases packed around a hollow, and the hollow is a flue. Hot gas comes up out of the seam, enters at the base, rises through three metres of trunk and leaves at the crown, and the inside of that chimney is lined the whole way with a white pile of root-hair, a metre and a half of feeding surface that no one was ever meant to see. The plant has arranged to have the planet's breath pass through its own body. What comes up is hydrogen sulphide and methane and carbon dioxide and steam; what the fern does with the first two involves bacteria in the hairs and need not detain us; the point is that this is a green thing in the dark that has stopped waiting for light and started eating the ground's exhalation, and its crown is bent over the crack not to warm its face but to catch the draught, the way you angle a sail.

Hence the colourlessness. Hence the fronds turned inward, presenting you with its back. There is nothing out here for it. You are standing in the part of the room that the fern considers empty sky.

It notices your lamp. Not quickly. Set the lamp down at the crown's edge and go away for two days and the fronds nearest it will have turned off, fifteen degrees or so, closing that side down. It has been fooled by a false vent before — a hot bolt, a leaking union — and it has a way of being fooled only once and then unfooling itself over about ninety cycles, which is to say two days, which is to say, in its reckoning, a season and a half.

It smells of struck flint and warm wool.

---

The mound on the steam pipe is not a plant. It is a range of mountains and it has weather and a treeline.

Cut into it — a clean vertical slice, forty centimetres to the metal, and it parts like mille-feuille with a sigh and the pear-and-iron smell comes out. The outer plates are broad, thin, pale, spread for condensation. Two centimetres in they thicken and darken. Ten in, they lose their form and become something more like a felt. At twenty-five centimetres you are at fifty degrees and the felt is not liverwort at all any more, it is a different body entirely, orange-brown, waxy, an organism that would die of cold out where you are standing. At the pipe itself, at eighty-odd degrees, there is a crust two millimetres thick and the colour of dried blood, and this is not a plant by any definition, and it is the reason the whole mound is alive, because it is fixing nitrogen out of nothing and the plates above it are farming it.

And where the iron strap-bracket clamps the pipe, the metal bleeds heat away and makes a cold bridge, and the whole zonation dips there, all the layers sagging inward by six or seven centimetres, and in that dip there grows a filament that grows nowhere else in the room. A valley with its own flora. Fifteen centimetres wide. Endemic.

---

Now the threads, and the correction.

The long unbranching runs across the wet concrete are not roots and not quite fungus; they are sheathed, they are about the diameter of a hair, and they go in straight lines because they are searching and a straight line is the cheapest way to search a flat dark plain. Most of them fail. The ones that fail simply stop, and you will find their tips lying dead on the floor like a sentence abandoned mid-word. The ones that succeed find a warm bolt, or the flange of the compressor, or a conduit carrying forty watts of failure, and they thicken there into a knuckle, and after that they are no longer a search, they are a road.

Follow the roads and the census collapses. The fern's foot is joined to the mound's underside. The mound is joined by two braids under the moss to the compressor cushion. The compressor cushion runs a single thread nine metres along a conduit to the rosette. There is one organism in this room. There has only ever been one. It arrived as a single spore on the shoulder of a man's coat or down a ventilation shaft, before the seam opened, and everything green in here is that spore, and the five clumps are not five lives but five organs, spaced apart the way your organs are spaced apart, with the useful emptiness in between.

The rosette, then, is a hand. It has gone out furthest, into the coldest and least rewarding part of the room, to hold twelve cups under the ceiling. Each cup carries a teaspoon of condensate with a lens of surface tension on it, and the water is not stored, it is a stomach: what falls into it is dissolved. What falls into it is the spore-snow. Every eleven hours the room broadcasts its entire annual seed into an audience of nobody, and about four per cent of it comes down in the twelve bowls of a hand held out for that purpose, and is eaten.

Call that tragic if you want. I think it is closer to thrift.

The only place in the whole chamber where the threads branch — genuinely branch, divide and rejoin and make a mesh — is where they cross the fault line in the concrete. The slip cut four of them. It took about forty cycles to reroute, and the repair is still there, a thickened braid with redundancy built into it, the one complicated structure in the room. It got complicated where it got hurt. Nowhere else.

---

The ear is on the transformer.

There is a patch of moss on the casing, thin, dark, poor-looking, no glow to speak of, with bare concrete under it — the worst moss in the room. When the pump motor down the eastern crawlway begins to draw, the transformer's load rises and its casing gains perhaps a fifth of a degree over twenty seconds, and it does this before the sound arrives, because current is faster than a noise coming up a hundred metres of crawlway.

So the room opens first and you hear the pump second. You will not believe this for a while. You will be certain the plants are responding to the sound, and then one visit the pump will misfire — it does, maybe once in three hundred cycles, a false start, a half-second of draw and then nothing — and the whole chamber will flush and gape and release into a silence that never becomes a pump at all, a spring with no summer behind it, the grey snow going up for nothing.

That is the closest thing to a mistake you will ever see it make, and the closest thing to an expression.

---

Nothing with a face, because of the air. Oxygen down two points, carbon dioxide up rather more, and hydrogen sulphide from the seam at a concentration that is merely unpleasant until forty minutes after each pump, when it triples. A rat would sleep and not wake. An insect could manage a day. Spores can wait out a century in a crack in a wall with their metabolism switched off, and that is why the vegetable inherited this room and the animal did not: not because the animal was beaten, but because the door was shut for a hundred years and only the patient can travel by not moving.

There is one animal here. In the mineral rind on the sign, under the place where the fourth letter used to be, something small and segmented is entombed and entirely replaced by calcite. It came down on the last shift, in 1911 or thereabouts, and died against the enamel, and the same slow white process that is eating the message preserved the messenger. You need a lamp at a low angle to see it at all.

---

Do the arithmetic on the eleven-hour year. Two point two per day. Seven hundred and ninety-six per annum. Since the door shut, sixty thousand of them, near enough.

That is the whole explanation. That is why it is like this — why the fern has a chimney, why the mound has a treeline, why the moss has plumbed the ceiling, why there is an ear on the transformer. It has not been down here for a century. It has been down here for sixty thousand years, in the only sense that matters to a thing that counts in seasons, and it has spent all of them in a sealed hot box with one reliable event in it, and it has bent every part of itself around that event until it fits like a hand in a glove of its own making.

It has never exchanged a gene with anything. It has no sex, no partner, no neighbour, no predator, and no injury but a floor that once moved. Every spore in the grey snow is itself. Sixty thousand years of saying one word, in a room with no walls that can hear, and catching most of it in twelve cupped hands and eating it, and saying it again in eleven hours.

It does not experience this as loneliness. Loneliness requires the information that there is an elsewhere, and no such information has ever entered this chamber.

Though it does have a taste. It refuses the canvas coat. The wall behind is green to within four centimetres of the hem and then simply stops, and the reason is in the oil the canvas is proofed with, and the effect, from where you stand, is of a room full of something that grows over everything except the one object left behind by a person, which it has declined.

---

And now you.

You came down the sharp new stair and you have got it exactly backwards, and it is worth saying plainly. You are not warm here. At thirty-seven degrees you are the third-coolest object in the chamber after the brass and the tin, and the air is at forty-something with a wet weight in it, and so the room does to you what it does to all cool metal: it condenses. You are a spring. You are running with clean water that cost this place nothing to make, and your breath is a plume of carbon dioxide richer than anything the seam has ever produced, and you are shedding, continuously, a soft rain of yourself, a hundred thousand cells an hour, each one a packet of nitrogen and phosphorus of a quality unknown in this room since 1911.

You are, in short, weather. Good weather. The best weather that has ever happened here.

Nothing will occur while you are watching. Put your knee on the concrete to reach the sump and stay an hour and there will be no response at all, because the response has a latency of about ninety cycles, and ninety cycles is two days, and you will be up in the light by then, telling someone that the place is dead quiet, that there's nothing alive down there but plants.

But the threads out on the bare floor are searching, and they search by going in straight lines to see what they meet, and something very large and very cool and full of dissolved nitrogen knelt at grid-point four and left a wet print that took nine minutes to dry.

Come back in a season. Come back in a hundred. You will find a knuckle in the concrete where your knee was, thickened, with three roads leaving it, all of them heading for the foot of the stair. Not because it wants you. It has no apparatus for wanting; it has an apparatus for arriving at whatever, in the last sixty thousand years, has reliably been worth arriving at.

You should know that you have started something and that the calendar has an entry in it now that is not the pump. When the transformer finally goes flat, and the bulbs, and the pump does not rise out of the east, the eleven-hour year will end and the grey snow will stop and everything in here will hold absolutely still in the dark and the heat, which it can do, which it is built to do, for as long as is required.

But it will not be waiting in general.

It will be waiting at the bottom of the stair.
