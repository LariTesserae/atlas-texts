## The census is short by one

Every survey of this basin comes back with the same list — snails, shrimp, eel, crickets, midges, bats, and the film that funds them — and every survey comes back with the same three or four items in the margins, unexplained, because they are not animals and so they get filed under weathering.

Look at the margins.

**The grease.** There is a pale finger-growth here that colonises grease, and it is thriving, and grease does not last a hundred and forty years in a seep. It saponifies. It goes to a soap and then to nothing. So the grease on the rail, in the pillow blocks, in the seam of the flywheel hub, is not the grease that was put there. It is being renewed at roughly the rate it is lost, which is a slow rate, but a rate.

**The two live pipes.** Every dead pipe in the hollow is sleeved and furred and half-closed with ochre cake. Both live ones are clean to bright metal on the inside, and if you get a hand into a broken flange and feel along the lead joint you will find that somebody has re-wiped it from the inside, badly, in a material that is not lead. It is grey-green and slightly gritty and it goes hard in air.

**The second band.** On the exposed rind there is the snail scoring, one finger wide, unmistakable. Below it there is another band, four fingers wide, smoother, scoured clean of film in one long arc along the north-eastern margin, with no radular grooves in it at all. Nothing in the census makes that mark. Nobody has ever caught it being made.

**The fog.** From the rim, if the light is doing anything at all, you can see that the ceiling of mist does not lie flat. It stands about a hand's-breadth higher over the north-eastern terraces, and it stands there permanently, and it is denser, and if you walk down into it you find the air there is perhaps a degree and a half warmer than the air over the pool.

That degree and a half is the whole answer. Everything in this basin is exactly the temperature of the rock. One arc of terrace is not.

## The fixture

The men who ran the condensate plant knew about it. They did not name it in the way you name an animal; they named it in the way you name a thing you have to work around. The surviving fragment of their inventory — a brass tag, screwed to the gable, mostly a green blister now — lists the flywheel, the loop, the rail, the settling steps, and then:

*FIXTURE, N.E. TERRACES 3–5. NOT TO BE DESCALED.*

Somebody underlined *NOT* twice, with a scriber, hard enough to raise a burr. That is the only opinion anyone here ever recorded about anything.

## What it looks like

It looks like the terrace.

That is not a metaphor and it is not camouflage in the sense of a moth on bark. It genuinely is the terrace, in the sense that anything sitting still under a permanent film of lime-laden seep for that long *becomes* poured stone, lobed and drapery-thin, indistinguishable from the flowstone on either side of it. You have already read a description of its hide and taken it for geology: soft ferruginous cake, bright as fox pelt where a foot has broken it, and beneath the broken skin, black.

Under the crust — and you have to want to know badly enough to break in, and it does not stop you — the tissue is grey-green, cool, faintly gritty, with the density of a scallop's mantle set in wet cement. It does not flinch. Nine or ten minutes later, the wound closes: not by contracting, but by *grouting*. It puts out the same grey-green material that wiped the pipe joints, and the material goes hard in air, and within a season the patch is scaled over and no longer findable.

The scale of it arrives late and badly. You start by thinking there is a lobe of something odd near the fifth step, a metre or so. Then you notice that the lobe has a fellow four metres along the arc, of the same colour, at the same level, with the same absence of snail-scoring across the top. Then you notice that between them the terrace face has no join in it anywhere, no bedding, no seam, and that the third, fourth and fifth steps of the north-eastern arc are one continuous poured surface, and that it is about nine metres along and perhaps three across, laid down the slope in folds like something spilled from a bucket, four centimetres of animal under a decimetre of its own mineral.

It has no head. It has ports: nine flush intakes at the terrace faces, each the size of a fist, each looking exactly like the stub of a cut pipe, three of them fringed with the white sulphur beards. Water goes in and water comes out. Once an hour or so, one port sighs, and a ring goes out across the pool and dies against the brick.

## How it works

It does not breathe. The basin breathes it.

That is the point of the eleven minutes, and it is why rule four is the true rule. When the hollow exhales, water moves out through the terraces; when it inhales, water moves back; and the whole animal is a set of galleries hung in that current, ventilating passively, the way a sponge does, for free, forever. It has no muscle worth the name and needs none. It has evolved, or settled, into precisely the shape that a siphoning ridge will breathe through without being asked.

It is warm because it runs on rust. The film on and in it is the same iron-eating pelt that makes the ochre cake, and iron oxidation gives off a little heat, and a little heat is enormous where there has never been any. Its temperature is the temperature of rust forming. It is warm the way a heap of turnings in a shed is warm. It is, apart from you and the bats, the only warm thing here.

It eats without hunting. Snails graze its crust and are drawn in by the intakes and the lime is recovered. Midges emerge in slabs directly above its third-terrace ports because that is where the broth is richest, and a fraction of every slab comes down. Guano falls; a share of it arrives. Shrimp live in the beards on its own margin and are cropped from the inside. When an eel drifts too near the overhang, the closing takes eleven or twelve minutes, which is not stalking, because nothing moves; it is simply that the eel is in a current and the current has an opinion.

And it farms. In the deepest shade, under the collapsed slab, there is a dim brass-coloured glow with no flicker in it at all. That is not a film on the stone. That is its back, and the light is a by-product of the chemistry it keeps there, and it has arranged the fold of itself under the slab so that the only dark place in the basin is the place where its own light is worth having. What it sees by that light — pressure, mostly; the loom of a body interrupting a seep — is enough.

Because that is its sense: pressure. It reads the water level to a fraction of a millimetre. When you stand in a terrace channel you dam it, and the animal registers a shortfall in one gallery, and that shortfall is you.

## Its arrangement with the machine

The loop works because the pipes are clean, and the pipes are clean because something has been descaling them from the inside for a century and a half in exchange for the calcium, and re-wiping the joints afterwards with its own grout because a leaking joint is a lost supply. The loop's slight patient stir is the reason the deep water stratifies the way it does, and the stratification is the reason the sulphur stays down where the film can work it. The whole tidy self-sufficiency of this hollow is an animal's housekeeping, and the housekeeping is only digestion, and nobody planned any of it.

The builders did not fit their engine to a cold wet hollow. They fitted it to the fixture. They came down here with instruments and found a hollow that breathed on a schedule and had one arc of terrace warmer than the rest, and they were practical men, and they plumbed into it.

There is a concrete plinth at the fourth step with eight bolts standing out of it in a rectangle. Every other bolt-pattern in this basin describes an absent object. That one describes a present one. They bolted a plate down onto it, and it took the plate, and grouted the plate in, and the plate is still there under the cake with the bolts weeping rust into it, and the arrangement has been satisfactory to everyone concerned since before the tag on the gable
