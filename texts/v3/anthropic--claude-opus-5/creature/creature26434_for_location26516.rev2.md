# The Stack-Folk

## First sight

From above, a city looks abandoned.

This is because nobody hurries and nobody shouts. The streets are cut deep into the cap-rock and roofed in places with slabs, and the people in them are moving at the pace of someone carrying a full cup. You will not pick out a person by motion. You will pick out a person because they have *stopped* — standing at a cable-head with one bare hand flat on the anchor iron, head tilted, reading with their palm what is being hammered three hundred kilometres away.

That is the posture of the place. A person listening through their skin.

## Bodies

They are people. They are shorter than you and wider through the chest than you would think a person could be and still be graceful, and they are graceful — the deep-chested slow grace of divers. Heart rates in the forties. A resting breath you can count to eight on. They do not run. Running is not a thing you do; running is a *signal*, and it means fire, fall, or cold, and a running figure crossing a court will empty the buildings on either side of it without a word being said.

Bare skin is dark and faintly lustrous, like manganese varnish on an old face of rock, and it does not sweat much, because water here is cooked out of sulfate stone a gram at a time and is not for throwing away. They shed heat the way the rock does: by contact and by radiance. A child too hot is taught to *take the stone* — to go and lie full-length on a shaded floor, cheek down, arms spread, until the floor has had what it wants. You will see grown men do it in the middle of a working day, silently, at the edge of a yard, and nobody remarks on it any more than you would remark on a man sitting down.

The eyes are deep under heavy brows and there is a second lid, a pale film that slides across at noon and gives the whole population, in the bright hours, a look of mild and universal reserve.

Nobody is ever without a coat. The coat is two-faced: kite-silk on the outer side, varnished and flaked with mica so it throws the sun back, and crust-char felt on the inner, black, which drinks heat greedily and gives it up slowly. You turn it as the day turns. Silver-out in the open, black-out in the galleries and the shadow-precincts. A person's coat is their biography — it is repaired for fifty years and never replaced, and the patches are datable, and a stranger's origin and trade and approximate decade can be read off their shoulders by anyone who cares to look. To take a coat off a living person is assault. To take one off a dead person is inheritance.

## The turning

Sun on one side of you and killing cold on the other, every hour of every day of a long life, does a thing to a body over time. So they turn.

It is a discipline taught to children with the same patience as chewing with your mouth closed: at intervals, unconsciously, you rotate. Working benches are built to be approached from either end. Chairs have no backs. A conversation between two people standing in the open will rotate slowly through half a circle in the course of it, both parties drifting, neither noticing, like two sticks on a current.

You can tell the ones who didn't. Great craftsmen, the ones who sat forty years at a single bench with the sun over the same shoulder, go *one-sided* — one cheek near black and cracked, the other pale as the inside of a shell, one eye rheumy from the glare. It is a shame and a boast at once. A one-sided old woman at a cable-head is given room.

## Greeting

Everyone touches on meeting. Palm flat to palm, about two seconds, no grip.

It is a temperature check. It has always been a temperature check. A cold hand means fever passing into its second stage, or exhaustion, or frostbite coming up through the fingers from a shaft-shift, and in any of those cases the correct response is not to recoil but to close both your hands over theirs and hold until they are warm, and then to ask. It is the single gesture from which the whole of their courtesy grows.

Underneath it, unspoken, is the other reason. Shade-hunters get very cold indeed. Everybody knows the story — everybody's city has its own version, set three generations back, with names — of the figure that came into the lamplight at the end of a long shift and put out its hand.

## Speech

The air will not carry a voice fifty metres, so speech is close work. They stand near. They stand nearer than you will be comfortable with, shoulder almost to shoulder, faces angled away rather than toward, both looking out at the same view, talking low. A person who backs off to speak to you is insulting you or is afraid of you. A person who addresses you from across a court is not addressing you; they are making a scene.

Everything public is hammered. Iron rods into bedrock, and a code of long and short that children learn before letters, tapped on floors and pipes and table-legs and the anchor irons of spans, and felt in the boot-sole and the seat-bone as much as heard. There are two registers of the code: the fast slurred domestic tapping of a family arguing across three rooms, and the slow formal beat of city-to-city rods, which has a grammar of its own, archaic and abbreviated and beautiful, and which nobody has spoken aloud in eight hundred years.

They laugh almost silently — a shake, a hiss of breath, a knuckle tapped twice on whatever is to hand. A room of thirty people finding something funny sounds like light rain on a roof, if there were rain, or roofs of that kind.

Span-keepers have their own dialect, hammered along thirty kilometres of steel cable, one walker to the next, arriving stretched and softened and doubled by echo. They can hear each other's gait. They can tell, from the hammering, which of them has grown old.

## Cold, and manners

Heat is everywhere; cold is the only thing worth having; so cold is what you give.

A guest is offered the shade side of the room before they are offered food. In a poor house that is simply the better chair. In a good house a servant brings a bowl of shaft-stone, a smooth block that has spent the night in the cold bore, to set in your lap and hold with both hands. In a rich house you are served sulfate needles in a footed glass, swept from the street that morning and kept below, and they are eaten with a flat spoon and they taste of struck matches and metal and they are nothing, nutritionally, and they mean: *we have more difference than we can use.*

The mirror taboo comes from the same place. Any polished surface is a weapon aimed at a shadow-shaft. You do not carry an uncovered bright thing into another city's shaft-precinct; you turn your coat black-out at the boundary, all of you, the whole party, and it is done without discussion and without exception, the way you'd take off a hat. What was done at Mirrowen four centuries ago is taught to children in a flat voice and not embroidered, because it does not need it.

## Work, and what a life looks like

They are a people of one job.

A bearing is ground for eleven years; a wheel is cast and balanced across sixty; a smelter comes up to heat in a season and is never permitted to cool, and the families that keep it — the **banked** — have kept it for a line of grandmothers, and speak of the heat as a resident of the house with opinions. You do not change trade. There is no ladder to climb. There is only the slow deepening of the one thing, and the expectation, entirely unhysterical, that you will hand it on better than you got it.

So you meet, in the galleries: drovers who have made three herd-crossings in a working life and can recite the gradient of a grazing-trail scraped by an animal that no longer exists; span-keepers who walk one wire out and back for forty years and marry at the far end and are, in some sense, citizens of a catenary; kite-catchers, who work three weeks a year at altitude, netting gas out of the migration with long poles from platform-cranes and are idle and insufferable for the rest of it; wheel-keepers, who live in the pit galleries under the drone and go half-deaf to human speech and can tell you the state of twenty tonnes of stone and iron by laying two fingers on the housing; and the **projectionists**, who compute shadows a hundred years out.

The projectionists are the nearest thing here to priests and the nearest thing to lawyers and they are both feared and kept close. In a world that has already decided what it is, where the cliff-line retreats a hand's width per lifetime and the profile of the stacks is *finished*, they are the only people who speak with any authority about the future. They carry brass, and tables, and a smell of ink. They rule on whether a proposed blockhouse will, in the reign of a person not yet born, take the cold from a cold-head that is currently somebody's entire fortune. Their rulings are hammered into the rods and into the rock and are not appealed.

## Money, marriage, and the Slow

Wealth is rotation. A share in a city's great wheel is written as turns, and a prosperous house holds turns in two or three wheels in two or three cities, and the language of debt is the language of friction. A man in trouble is *slowing*. A ruinous obligation is *drag*. To be paid is to be *spun up*. When freight of value moves along a span it is a crated flywheel in a gimballed box, kept upright, fighting the porters at every corner, with two guards who are not there for thieves — there are no thieves worth the name — but because a dropped wheel is a dropped bank.

Marriage is, among other things, the merger of shade rights, and so it is drawn up by projectionists and survives them. Divorce is a re-survey: the shadow is physically divided, the boundary marked in the rock, and the two parties come out of it with smaller, meaner, colder holdings and are generally civil about it, because the alternative is to be seen arguing loudly in public, which is unthinkable.

And then there are the **Slow** — the descendants of the three Stopped cities of the west, whose great wheels ran down inside living memory of somebody's grandmother, and who cannot be given a share in any wheel because their line's turns were in a wheel that no longer turns and the law has never found a way round it. They walk spans. They work bounties. When a shade-hunter must be taken, it is usually one of the Slow who goes out wrapped in silver and lies down in a cold place and lets themself go cold enough to become invisible to it — which is to say, lets themself briefly stop being, in the only sense that matters here, alive. They are re-warmed at the gate afterward, publicly, by whoever is senior, hands over hands, before they are allowed back among people. Everybody attends. Nobody makes a speech, because speeches can't be heard.

## Death

The water is reclaimed first. That is simply done, quietly, by the household, and it is not considered grim; it is the inheritance, and it goes into the still and then into the children, and there is a word for it that also means *dowry*.

What is left is carried out at dawn to the family's plate — a walled rectangle of sun-facing rock, ungrazed for centuries, where the crust is allowed to grow and never harvested, rust and cream and deep peacock where the copper runs, thick as roof-tile, ticking open in the morning with the rest of the field. The body is laid on it. The crust does the rest across a few slow seasons.

A great house is one whose plate is older than the city. They take visitors there. They walk you along the wall and tell you, without emphasis, which swelling in the crust is which grandmother, and the plate is warm, and it is the only warmth in this entire civilization that anyone has ever described as tender.

## The un-spanned

Not every inhabited stack has cables. There are tops out there with a single household on them, four hundred people at most, a small wheel, a shaft, a plate, reached once in a generation by a cable-laying expedition that may or may not be welcome. They have their own tapping, drifted so far from the rod-code that the formal grammar is barely recoverable. They marry inward. They are said — by everyone who has never met them — to be strange about mirrors, or about children, or about the drumming.

Every so often a stack stops answering the rods. A party goes out. Sometimes there is an explanation: a shaft warmed, a wheel stopped, a cliff-line that finally lost the crucial hand's width. Sometimes the wheel is still turning and the plate is tended and the coats are hanging on their pegs and the party comes home and hammers nothing about it into the rods at all.

## And under the floor

In the flywheel pits, where nobody goes but the keepers and nothing should be able to live, something drums.

It is not the wheel. The keepers are very clear on that; they know the wheel's note the way you know a voice in the next room. It is slower than the wheel and it is not regular and it answers, sometimes, if you tap.

They leave it food. They do not discuss this with outsiders, and they would prefer you did not write it down.
