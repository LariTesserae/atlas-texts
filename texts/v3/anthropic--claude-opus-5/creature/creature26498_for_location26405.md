## The Tenants of the Even

They lean. That is the first thing, and a visitor sees it before he sees a face: four thousand one hundred people standing very slightly into the hill, right shoulder carried a finger's width lower than the left, the whole town canted two degrees against the two degrees of the floor. It is not a posture they adopt. It is the shape their spines took while they were soft. A Shelf-born woman set down on level ground will list northwest and correct, list and correct, and be exhausted by noon, and will say, if she is polite, that the ground where you come from is restless. Their calves are uneven. Their beds are shimmed. The tilt is in them the way salt is in a sailor.

Beyond that they are unremarkable in the way of any mountain people: deep-chested for the thin air, brown-skinned or grey-skinned depending on the season's light, hair worn short and dressed with bell-stalk resin so that it stands in flat plates and doesn't take the wet. They smell of lanolin and woodsmoke and that resin, which is sweet and faintly medicinal, and they smell of it uniformly, so that the town has one smell and a stranger has another, and dogs — there are eleven dogs, there have always been eleven dogs — find strangers hilarious.

Look at their hands. Every adult on the Shelf has the same three calluses: two at the finger-joints and one along the outside edge of the thumb, from cord. Knotting cord, running cord through the fist, thumbing a knot to see whether it is a five-knot or a six. The nails are worn flat and slightly concave. Old people's fingertips are grooved in fine parallel lines, like the fins on their roofs, and they are proud of this and hold their hands out for you to see.

And watch their eyes, which is the thing that makes visitors uneasy long before they can say why. A Shelf native's gaze goes around a room in a particular way when she enters it: a flick over the heads, a flick along the shelf, a flick to the doorway, a slight settling. She has counted you. She has counted the cups, the loaves, the children, the pins in the wall. She was not being suspicious and she will not remember doing it. When two of them pass on a stair, one says a number aloud — *two*, usually, or *four* if children are following — and the other says the same number back, and that is the whole of their greeting. To pass in silence is rude. To say the wrong number is a joke, and a good one, and the sort of thing that gets repeated for a week.

There are no locks on the Shelf. Nothing can be stolen in any final sense; a thing can only be moved, and by evening the Count will know where it went. A thief is not punished. A thief is *informed*, in front of others, of the present location of the object, which is the worst thing that can be done to anybody here, and is done perhaps twice in a generation.

Their work is thin and slow and never finished because it was never unfinished. The shepherds walk the eastern terraces with the four hundred browsers and do almost nothing, since the browsers eat the moss cushions to a certain height and stop, and no one has ever seen one eat past that height, and the shepherds' real function is to be present at the tally. The grain terraces on the second level yield the same weight every year and are winnowed by hand on the same four threshing floors, and the winnowing is the loudest human sound in the city — a long dry hiss, twice a day, like the town clearing its throat. The bell-stalk cutters go out in pairs at dawn with copper knives and take sap from the rings, never entering the ring's interior, which is not forbidden so much as unthinkable, the way it is unthinkable to put your hand in your own mouth up to the wrist. The twelve masons walk with knotted cords and confirm that the walls are where the walls were. And on cold mornings, forty or fifty people sit out on the pavement in wool, without speaking, watching the mineral film come back down into the places the day took it from. This is called the dust-watch. Sitting it alone, all the way through, from the first sweat to the first sun, is how a child becomes an adult. Nobody tells you when to do it. You go out one night and you sit and in the morning you are one.

They eat porridge, hard flat bread, milk in three forms, greens from the joint-channels, and mutton as a bureaucratic event. The slaughter-clerk is a mild man with a stammer who must be satisfied that a lamb has been born before a sheep can be taken, and who is never argued with, and who is not thanked either. The one luxury, and the only object on the Shelf that is permitted to get older, is cheese. They make a hard grey cheese that ripens for years in a cellar cut into the rim wall, and the total weight of ripening cheese is fixed to the ounce, so that to take a wheel down you must set a wheel up, and each wheel wears a cord with a knot for every year. The cheese-cellar is the closest thing the city has to an archive, and the only place where a person can put a hand on something and feel duration. People go down there to be sad. The cellarer pretends not to notice.

The Count is nineteen. It has always been nineteen. They wear nothing to mark themselves and they do not sit apart at meals, and yet you can pick them out at once, because they are the only people in the city who look at other people's faces for longer than a moment. A counter's business is not really numbers. Numbers are easy here. A counter's business is the interval — the gap between the loss and the return, which the town calls the hollow day. When a death is cried in the lower quarter, the counter of that quarter goes to the house, and does not offer comfort, and does not weep, and sits down at the door with her cords across her knees and waits. She may sit an hour. She may sit until dark. The family does not weep either. They make tea and bring her some and they all wait together, and the noise of the gutters comes up through the floor, and this is the most terrible ordinary experience available to a human being on the Shelf: the hollow day, the hours in which the ledger stands open and the whole city is short by one and nobody knows yet what the change will be. Then the runner comes — a birth in the fourth level, or a stranger on the switchbacks — and the counter ties her knot, and stands up, and says *closed*, and only then does the room break, and they cry properly, and loudly, and for three days.

There are four thousand one hundred names on the Shelf, and every one of them is in use right now.

This follows from everything else and it is where the strangeness stops being architectural and becomes personal. A child is named at birth for the dead she answers, and the name goes on without a break, and there is no numbering of them, no *the third*, no *the elder*. There is only whoever is currently wearing it. They do not say *Ambry died*; they say *the name went cold for a day*, or simply *Ambry turned*. A woman speaking of a mason who fitted a particular corner will say *Halt cut that*, and she means Halt, and she cannot tell you and does not wonder which Halt, because the question has no more purchase than asking which rain fell in the gutter. Genealogy is impossible. Ancestry is impossible. The dead are not gone anywhere; they are standing in the street holding a bucket. This is why there is no history on the Shelf. Not because nothing happened, but because memory has nowhere to pile up. Every hook is occupied.

At the east gate there is a stone bench worn shiny in two places, and on it sit the benchers, who want to leave. They come at dawn and sit until dusk and go home to eat and come back. Some have sat for a year. One, called Hask, has sat eleven, and has become a fixture, and gives directions to the terraces, and mends cord for the gatekeeper, and no longer talks about the fog with any of the fervour he had at nineteen.
