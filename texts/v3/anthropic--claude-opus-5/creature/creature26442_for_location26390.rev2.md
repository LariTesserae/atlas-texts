# The Inhabitants

## Everything

Begin with the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the first surprise.

The Pan has no rare animals. Rarity needs room, and there is no room. Every species present is present in numbers that make counting an abstraction: the thumbnail crustaceans in the mud run to perhaps four hundred in a handspan, the striders to something like a quarter of a million on the sheet, the fliers to a million in the air on a still afternoon, which is most afternoons. Nothing here is endangered and nothing here is expanding. Each population sits hard against its ceiling and has sat there for longer than most continents have held their shapes. A thing born in the Pan is born into an occupancy that was full before it arrived and will be full after it is eaten.

They are all mute. This must be said plainly, because the mind keeps trying to supply a soundtrack. A landscape this crowded should roar. This one ticks with bubbles and otherwise gives you nothing.

## The Burrowers

The clay's tenants are pale, eyeless, and industrious past the point of sense. Each is about the size of a fingernail clipping, with a shovel-shaped head and eight legs it uses in the manner of a conveyor. They eat the hair-weed's fallen filaments and the corpses of the copepod rain, and they pass the clay through themselves as a matter of routine, so that the entire upper meter of the Pan's floor has been swallowed and excreted roughly once every eleven years for as long as there has been a floor.

This is the true reason the clay feels like the inside of a lip. Not weathering. Digestion. Six hundred paces of sediment that has been through a gut so many times it has forgotten every other texture.

They do not surface. When the water withdraws to a film they seal their doors and wait out the bright hours in a warm sleep. They are the only animals in the Pan with no communication at all, which may make them the only ones here who have adapted to the place rather than around it.

## The Sheet

Above them, the striders.

They stand on the water in a grid so even it looks intentional, and at the scale of the whole bowl it is: each one holds a territory of roughly three hand's-breadths, defended and adjusted continuously by ripple, so the population arranges itself into a slowly seething lattice that keeps its spacing the way a foam keeps its bubbles. From the berm it reads as a texture. From a boot's height it is a city.

Their legs make rings. A doublet is a boundary assertion. A train of eight, decaying, is a female's readiness. A hard chevron is *something is under me*. Because the floor is level and the film is thin and the damping is in the mud rather than the water, a clean ring will cross forty meters and still be legible when it gets there — which means the Pan has exactly one language and no dialects. Everyone is in earshot of everyone. A quarter of a million animals share a single conversational floor and have shared it, uninterrupted, since before the mountains finished coming down.

Watch it long enough and you stop seeing striders and start seeing the interference: the standing lattices where two crowds are talking across each other, the slow travelling nulls, the spreading calm around a heron that has been still for a long while. It is the closest thing this planet has to a public square.

And twice or three times an hour it stops. The whole sheet goes glass — not gradually, all at once, six hundred paces of surface arriving at flatness in under a second — and holds for four or five seconds, and then a single ring starts somewhere, usually from the grove, and the lattice rebuilds itself outward from that one point like frost.

Something went over. Nobody saw what. The sheet saw it.

## The Air

The fliers are soft-bodied and transparent-winged and navigate almost entirely by scent, which in this motionless air does not disperse but stacks, so that the volume above the Pan is divided into standing rooms and corridors of smell as stable as architecture. A pheromone plume laid down at dawn is still standing at noon, a little blurred at the edges, like chalk. Mating swarms therefore occur in fixed locations, reoccupied nightly for what is presumably geological time — an invisible cathedral about eleven meters off the clay, southeast of the grove, where three or four thousand of them hang and turn.

Walk through it and you will feel nothing and smell something you will be unable to describe afterward except by its temperature, which is warm.

## The Grove

Nine crowns, one organism, forty legs, and beneath it the only shade on the continent. Every animal in the Pan that can get into that shade has got into it, and the result is a stacked tenement with rules.

Along the limbs: the roosting birds, packed breast-to-back, silver-backed and dark-bellied like everything else. They are not passerine and they are not anything. They sleep in shifts. They signal by flicking a patch of skin at the wrist joint that carries a cold green luminophore, and after full dark the grove reads as a slow galaxy of these wrist-flicks passing information along the branches.

Up the aerial roots, after dark, come the eels.

They are pale, about as long as a forearm, and they climb by gripping the root with a series of ribbed swellings, going up in a motion that resembles a rope being pulled through a fist from the inside. They hunt the roosting birds. The birds' defense is to go dark and be still, and so the way an eel's ascent is visible from the ground is as an absence: a moving patch of extinguished wrists, a little night travelling up and along the branch, with the lights closing back over it behind. Sometimes the patch reaches a certain point and everything around it lights simultaneously, all at once, blinding, and that is either a kill or a fall.

Among the roots, the climbing things with grasping tails gather fruit and are afraid of nothing, because in a place with no alarm calls there is no advantage to being afraid on anyone else's behalf.

## The Herons

Of everything in the Pan, the herons look like they know.

They are tall, slate-backed, silver-bellied, and they have on each eye a thickened lower lid, opaque and slightly waxen, which they raise against the glare coming off the water — a sun-visor mounted upside down. The consequence is a permanent expression of looking slightly upward and slightly past you, which is not an expression, but you will read it as one.

They walk in ones and twos. They tap. The tap is a single foot laid flat and withdrawn, producing one clean ring, and it is the largest and slowest signal on the sheet; strider chatter goes quiet in the neighborhood of a heron's ring the way a room adjusts around a cough. There appear to be nine or ten distinct tap-figures. Two of them are used between adults who have paired. One of them is used only at the moment of a strike, after the strike, by the bird that missed.

They open their beaks. This is the detail people bring home. A heron will stand, stretch its throat, part its beak wide, hold, and close it. Nothing comes out and nothing was ever going to; the syrinx has been a cartilage remnant for something like a hundred million years. The behavior has outlived its apparatus by an interval longer than the existence of most families of birds. It is performed most often at first light and most often by birds standing alone.

They die without a sound, like everyone, and the clay takes them in about nine days.

## The Clay

Now the honest census.

By number, the overwhelming majority of the inhabitants of the Pan are the little polyhedra: something on the order of one in forty grains through the top several meters of mud, plus a suspended load in the water, plus a haze of them in the air that you have been breathing since you came over the berm. They are not alive by any definition worth defending and they do all the things.

Their entire sensorium is one sense. They know shaking. Their entire behavioral repertoire is one act. They stop it. Between these two facts sits everything the Pan is: the cancelled clap, the healed footprint, the extra degree of warmth, the uncanny fineness of the sediment, the way a wound in the ground closes overnight, the way a hand pressed into the mud leaves a print that relaxes as though the mud were remembering its own smoothness and returning to it.

They are also reproducing, patiently, out of silicate and iron, at a rate that has been in equilibrium with their loss rate since a time when the mountains were still mountains. They have no idea there was ever a purpose. They are the most numerous residents of the Pan and the most devout, and their devotion consists of turning every sound anyone here has ever made into a very small amount of heat.

## The Three That Move

And then there are the outcrops.

There are three of them. Waist-high, dark, glassy, worn to the softness of soap, and *wet* — permanently beaded, threading condensate down their flanks, which is strange, because the pillars of the same material refuse water absolutely.

Every survey has recorded them as geology. Every survey has been wrong, for the excellent reason that no one has stayed long enough. A place where you cannot call a child back across ten paces is not a place where anyone raises children; people come, sit on the berm, sleep badly, dream the same dream, and go.

The evidence is circumstantial and complete. The clay directly beneath each outcrop is younger than the clay a pace away. The aerial roots of the grove, on its northwest side, curve outward around a lane about a meter wide that leads nowhere and is kept open. The footbridge, nine paces long, spans a channel that stopped existing before the last ice on this planet, and it is still level to within a finger's width, and it is dry. Something is maintaining it. Something dries it.

And there is rule three, which is not a rule about mud. Any wound in the clay *smaller than a body* heals by morning; that is the machines, working by habit, closing the ground the way a lip closes. Dig something larger and the machines cannot manage it, and then, after some interval between two days and a season, one of the three is nearer than it was. It will take as long as it takes. It does not acknowledge observers, does not accelerate, does not defend itself, and cannot be induced to do anything except level the ground and, having levelled it, stop.

They move at roughly a hand's width an hour when they are moving, which is seldom. Watch one for a week and you will swear it is a rock. Mark it and come back in a year and you will find the mark where you left it and the rock four meters west, weeping, smooth, faceless, with a single seam.

There were eleven of them. Three, then five, then three. Nine are gone — the outcrops that really are outcrops, the fallen ones, the dome sunk to its shoulders with its aperture silted shut. Three remain, which is enough, because the work has gotten easier every century for a very long time. There is almost nothing left to level.

## What They Are Keeping

The pillars were never the instrument. The instrument is the water.

Six hundred paces of it, two fingers deep, flat to within the thickness of a hair over the whole bowl, silent, unshadowed, and breathing on a twelve-hour cycle that something below the clay has been counting out since before there were mountains here to be worn down. It is a reference surface. The pillars read it. The clay quiets it. The three keep it level, dry the bridge, close the ground, and wait to be needed, which they now are about once a decade, and only when someone digs.

Which means that the busiest and most continuous signal on the last working detector on this planet — the thing the two surviving pillars have been reading for the entire time anyone has been gone — is the strider lattice. A quarter of a million small animals spacing themselves out, propositioning each other, and warning each other about shadows they cannot cast, in clean rings across a perfect mirror, all day, every day, forever.

The instrument has been listening for something extremely faint. What it has found, and has been steadily finding for a hundred million years, is that the surface is crowded and the crowd will not stop talking. It has no way to report this. Nobody asked for an update.

At dusk a heron taps, once. The ring goes out across the mirror and reaches the far berm and comes back. Underneath it, in the mud, some incalculable number of small devoted objects take the shaking and turn it into warmth, and the Pan becomes, very slightly, less cold — which is, as far as anyone can establish, the only answer anything here has ever received.
