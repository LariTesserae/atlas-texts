## The hands

They call themselves hands. A stack does not have a population, it has a complement: *Fourteen carries nine hundred and forty hands.* A stranger — someone from another stack, arriving on the tram with his kit strapped and his plates in order — is a *mouth* until somebody vouches for him, and then he is a hand too.

They are long people, but not tall: long in the forearm and the shin, short in the trunk, built by thirty thousand spiral stairs climbed sideways between two hissing walls. Every one of them has the shoulders of a person who has spent their life turning to fit. Their grip is frightening. A fitter of sixty can hold a wrench closed against eleven atmospheres with two fingers, and shaking hands with one of them is a small act of trust that outsiders do not understand until afterwards, when they look at their knuckles.

Their skin tells you their altitude. The stacks throw away their heat as light, and some of that light comes off with a violet edge, so the rind-crews and the ceiling-inspectors who work the high faces are dark, seamed, kippered, with the deep creases of people who squint for a living, while the pipe-fitters and sweepers on the floor plain are the colour of the pith they walk on. In the Chamber, high means dark and low means pale, and the class snobbery runs in both directions and does not resolve.

## The veil

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink.

Their water is made, not taken — burned out of sugar in the muscle, the way a fat animal manufactures a river out of its own back. They eat glass in drams and sugar in spoonfuls and miller-meat pressed into cakes, and their bodies quietly assemble from it the four litres a day that keeps them alive, and every one of those litres is precious in a way that has no equivalent anywhere water falls.

So they lose it only through the lungs, and they fight even that. Every hand wears a *veil*: a woven muzzle of fine pith fibre over mouth and nose, damp on the inside, cool on the outside, that catches the breath going out and hands it back on the way in. A child's first veil is stitched by its mother's mother. A working veil is changed twice a shift. A dry veil is a dangerous veil. You can tell a great deal about a household by the state of their veils on the line.

To be unveiled in company is not obscene, exactly. It is *extravagant*. It is a man buying drinks for a room he cannot afford. Lovers unveil to each other; that is what unveiling is for. A physician will ask permission. A fistfight begins with two hands stripping their faces, and there are old men in the Fume Works who have not shown their mouths since their wives died.

And weeping — weeping is the most expensive thing a body can do. A hard cry costs more water than the Dew House traps in a month. It happens perhaps four times in a life. It is not a sign of weakness here; it is a *statement*, ruinous, unanswerable, and when someone does it the room goes still and afterwards nobody mentions it, ever, in the same way that nobody mentions a house that burned down with people in it.

## Three voices

Because the air is dry and cold and pressurized, and because sound crosses two kilometres of it as though carried by hand, a hand does not speak.

They *murmur*: a close, veiled, flat-toned register for anything under two metres, all consonant and no lift, developed to die inside a doorway. It is what they use with family, and it sounds, to a mouth fresh off the tram, like a room full of people confiding secrets about him.

They *sign*: a fast one-handed grammar built for people whose other hand is holding a rung or a lamp or a slate. Numbers, pressures, pitches, the six standing orders, the eleven kinds of *no*. Signed insults are the good ones. A crew can conduct an entire quarrel across a gantry in absolute silence while both parties keep working, and the only sign that anything has happened is that one of them takes the long stairs down.

And they *whistle*. Not signals — language: a full tonal speech, thin and pure and legible at three kilometres, the pitch carrying the vowels and the tonguing carrying the rest. It is what the floor plain sounds like when it sounds like anything: two surveyors at the Blind End and a sweeper by the trestles holding a conversation none of them would raise their voice for, in a language no one has ever written down, over distances that would need a telephone anywhere else. Children learn to whistle before they learn to murmur. Old whistler-men whistle in their sleep.

Their ears are their great organ. Every hand in the Chamber has perfect pitch, because pitch is a unit of measurement: a puncture screams and drops a semitone every twelve hours, so a child of six can look at a wounded flank and say *four days, and someone knew and said nothing*, and mean it as an accusation. They hear the mains sigh, the beam engine's four-second stroke, the *shhk … shhk* of the herds that they stopped hearing at four years old and would notice instantly if it stopped. They name intervals the way other people name colours. A discordant stack, whistler-riddled, is heard by everyone within a kilometre as a slow sour chord, and it is felt as a civic shame, like a smell.

## Eyes, and the cloth over them

Nothing here is ever dark, so their pupils are small as pins and they are all, in the practical sense, night-blind: put a hand in a sealed gas cellar with the lamp out and they will stand very still and start to whistle, softly, mapping the walls by echo, and they will get out, and they will not enjoy it.

The one thing every hand owns that is beautiful is the blindfold. It is the only decorated object in a world with no dyes and one colour: embroidered, quilted, weighted at the corners, given as a marriage gift, inherited, patched, patched again, stitched with the giver's mark. Ask a hand what they'd save from a fire and they'll say the flask and the fold. Sleeping bare-eyed is what the bereaved do, for nine cycles, so that everyone can see they aren't sleeping. Sleeping bare-eyed is also a sentence — *the open eye*, thirty cycles of it, and there are men who came out of that quieter than they went in and stayed quiet.

The old go blind, most of them, from sixty years of violet light. And in a world read by sound and pressure and touch, this is barely a disfigurement; it is a *promotion*. The blind become auscultators. They walk the flank of a stack with their palms flat and their heads tipped and they listen to eleven thousand pressure tubes the way a doctor listens to a chest, and they find the crimped-off wound behind the rind, the dying tube, the root that has run into another root's ground and is losing. Old Pell of Nine, blind since her fortieth, condemned a whole west face by pitch alone and was disbelieved for two years and was right. The auscultators are paid in drams and deferred to by engineers and they have the worst manners in the Chamber and nobody corrects them.

## What they do all shift

They tap. They sweep. They hollow. They hunt.

**Tappers** set a spigot into a bole and wait, because glass moves at the speed of a minute hand. A tap is not a job, it is an inheritance; a good spigot is worked by four generations, and the family keeps a book of its drams going back to the man who cut the thread. Glass chaps the lips and cracks the hands — it is hygroscopic, it drinks you back — so tappers are known by their mouths, which are always split, and by the smell they carry, sweet and burning, that never washes out of a coat.

**Sweepers** rake the floor plain into those long parallel arcs, and every mouth who arrives assumes it is a religion. It is surveying. A raked floor shows a new fracture as a break in the line, visible from four hundred metres up, and the sweepers are the reason the Chamber knows the ground is moving before the ground moves. That the arcs are also, incidentally, the most beautiful made thing in the world is a fact the sweepers will not discuss and will not permit you to walk across.

**Hollowers** cut houses out of a living pressure vessel, which is exactly as reasonable as it sounds. They work to a code with the thickness of every wall specified in millimetres and they are the most feared and best-paid crews in the Chamber, and roughly once in a generation a hollowing crew misjudges an interstitial and a stack loses a bole and forty floors of town come down out of the ceiling in about nine seconds. There are two such columns of rubble in the east lobe. Nothing grows there. Nobody sweeps there.

**Whistler control** employs four hundred hands and is the only department everyone respects. They go out with felt boots and long thin gigs and stopping-wax, they take the parasites off the tubes, they plug the singing, and they carry the shells home strung on a wire. A whistler crew names its members by note — *Fourth*, *Flat-Sixth*, *Tenor* — and they will use those names on each other for fifty years and never learn each other's wet names, which is considered by everyone else to be slightly indecent and very whistler.

## The flask

The dead do not rot, because rot is wet. They dry.

So the dead are given to the root. A body is carried down into a root gallery and laid in the crystallizing face and left, and over thirty or forty years the sugar takes it apart and the stack drinks what there is to drink of it, and the tap above that gallery yields a glass that is understood, without any metaphor being intended, to be the dead person. Every household keeps a flask of the house sweet. It is opened for births, for oaths, for the settling of a feud. You drink your grandmother. You drink the great-uncle who was a bastard, and someone says so, and everyone laughs, and he goes round again.

This is why a family fights over a spigot the way families elsewhere fight over land, and why a hand who dies at the Blind End, out past the tramway, in fresh rock with no root to take them, is the worst of all deaths — *dry-laid* — and why the surveyors go out in yoked pairs and the yoke is a binding contract with one clause: *you bring me back*.

## The thimble, and the calling

Water exists in one vessel in one cold corner of the world, a thimbleful a week, two hundred years accumulated and spent and accumulated again.

It is used to name. A hand receives a wet name once, at eleven days old, when the Dew House keeper touches a needle-tip of water to the child's tongue, and that name is spoken four times in a life: then, at yoking, at oath, and by whoever gives them to the root. Between those occasions everyone uses a use-name — a face and a number, *Marra of Fourteen West*, *Sennet Nine-Low* — and it is entirely usual to work beside someone for thirty years and not know what the water called them.

It is used to swear. An oath at the Dew House is not enforceable by anyone; it is simply not broken. Once, in living memory, it was used for an apology, and the whole Chamber knows what for, and no one who was not there will tell you.

And twice a cycle, on the low light and on the turn of the exhale, one voice goes out over the plain — because the crews said it themselves: roughly twice a day, a human voice, and everyone within two kilometres hears it perfectly. That is the **caller**. Each stack keeps one: a woman or man who unveils in public, on the town's account, and spends the town's water on a single carrying cry — the shift, the hour, the children in, the names of anyone hurt, the pitch of any new wound. It is a paid extravagance. It is the one thing the Chamber does out loud. Callers burn out in a decade or so; they end up thin and hoarse and small-voiced and are given the good seat by the heater in every gallery they walk into for the rest of their lives, and they are the only hands who will speak, in a murmur, to a mouth without being introduced.

## The drag, and the sugar

The stacks breathe on nineteen hours. The hands, obscurely, insist on twenty-four. Nobody knows why; nobody has ever seen the thing their bodies are still keeping time to.
