## What lives in Cavity Nine

**The flora, because that is the easiest thing to call it.**

Begin with the whiskers, and get one thing straight about them: they do not grow the way you assume. A plant lengthens at the tip. A whisker is extruded from the base, squeezed out of the plating by internal compression like solder from a needle, so the far end — the tip, the point curving in the light — is the *oldest* metal in the room. The tip of the long one on the cable tray, the hand's-length one, is a fragment of the first hour of the first day. It was pushed out into the cold before the note had finished rising, and everything since has been added behind it. The whisker is not reaching for anything. It is a column of memory being pressed forward by the present, tip first, into the future.

Count them if you like. In the northeast quadrant, along the nodal line where wall meets wall, eleven thickets. Three of them are fur — a haze on a conduit bracket that you would take for dust if it did not have that particular unmistakable *directionality*, every filament perpendicular to the surface, five microns across, straight as a drawn wire. Four are colonies of a dozen or so, standing on the lip of a junction box like something on a shore. Two are kinked, having changed their minds mid-extrusion; a whisker will do this when the stress field in the plating shifts, and the kink is a dated record of the day a bolt somewhere else in the structure took up a little more load. One is spiralled, tight as a spring, and no one has ever adequately explained the spiralled ones.

The last is solitary: a single filament off a bolt head at knee height in the middle of the west wall, forty-one millimeters, curved, alone. There is no reason for it to be there and no reason for the others to be absent, except that plating is a lottery and this bolt won.

They live only in the nodes, which means the vegetation is not growing where conditions are best. It is growing where the *pressure is least*, which is a different statement. In the antinodes, whiskers erupt too and are broken off within days and reduced to nothing, and their remains go up into the sheets. Every square centimeter of this room is fertile. Only the quiet parts get to keep anything.

So the flora is a diagram. The note cannot be seen, and it has drawn itself, in silver, at three millimeters a year, on the inside of its own container. Nothing here is decorative. This is the only writing in the room besides four chalk marks and one word in pencil, and it is the room's own handwriting, and it says: *here I am not, here I am not, here I am not.*

---

**The four sheets, which are the largest body present.**

They are not weather and they are not an effect. Give them the courtesy of a description as a resident: four horizontal laminae, nine by six, one centimeter thick, held at 2.0, 4.0 — no. Held at the heights the arithmetic requires, which are not round numbers, and which have not altered by a millimeter since startup.

Their composition, from the bottom up, is roughly: fines of green casting sand; mill scale; chalk bloom off the primer; abraded oxide from the antinode faces, which are polishing themselves at a rate no instrument in this structure can resolve; cotton and canvas fibrils, which are the coat; and human skin. Not much. The room was built in nine weeks by men who did not know yet what it was for, and they shed as men do, and when the machine was started every loose particle in the volume was lifted at once and sorted by height and has not been permitted to land since.

That is the inhabitant with a body. It has no metabolism, no boundary of its own making, no interest. It is nevertheless the only thing in Cavity Nine that *heals*. Walk through it and it curls away from you in slow grey scarves, and in fifty seconds it is back — not approximately, not statistically, but back, at the height it must occupy, because there is precisely one arrangement of loose matter that this volume permits and it is that one. You cannot damage it. You cannot disarrange it. You can only interrupt it, briefly, the way you interrupt a reflection.

If anything in this room is the closest thing to a corpse it is not the missing man. It is the sheets. Four flat grey pages of the builders, filed at exact intervals, unable to fall.

---

**The one with no body at all, which is the actual tenant.**

Understand the arrangement. Cavity Nine is not a room where a note is heard. Cavity Nine is the note's housing, and everything you have been shown is its life support.

The temperature is held to a tenth of a degree because the note eats speed-of-sound and will not tolerate a different diet. The water has been taken out to parts per billion because water would drag it flat. The bolts may not be turned because turning one would change the volume of its lungs. The primer was never finished because the second coat would have been thirty microns of new surface impedance. The sand stays in the corners because the sand is now part of it.

Somewhere four kilometers off and several hundred meters down, in warm rooms with worn linoleum, there are compressors and molecular sieves and a cryogenic plant with a nameplate and a maintenance schedule, and men who have never climbed the shaft and never will, and every one of them is nursing this thing. They keep it at minus thirty-one. They keep it dry. They watch a needle, and the needle's stillness is the vital sign, and they have a word for it that they use without thinking: *she's holding.*

So the resident of Cavity Nine is a patient. It is fed continuously by more engines than anyone in the structure has bothered to count, and their individual coughs and stumbles have averaged into a single unwavering column, the way a million pebbles average into a beach. It has no beginning and no end and therefore cannot be heard, only entered. It is one hundred thirty-one decibels of absolutely uneventful life.

It has organs. The fundamental at forty-one hertz is the body — the slow, whole, sternum-filling animal. The third and fifth are limbs, and you can feel where they cross. The faint sixteenth is something else again: fast, thin, high, sitting up behind your teeth in a register the big body cannot reach, and it does not belong and it is not removable, and if you had to say what it resembled you would say a small thing living in a large thing's throat.

---

**The absent one, who is not dead.**

The coat is not a grave. This is the mistake every visitor makes.

Someone hung it on the hook, in a node, by accident — the hook is in a node by accident too, it was welded there by a man with a bracket and no acoustic model — and went down the ladder in shirtsleeves and never had a reason urgent enough to come back up eleven flights for a coat. The tally beside the hatch is five: five ascents, and then whatever the fifth ascent taught him. The logbook has one entry because after *Started. Holds.* there was nothing further that could honestly be written; the room never did anything else.

He is probably alive. He is older now, and his hands have changed and his hearing at four kilohertz is gone on the left, and the coat on the hook is younger than he is by every year since startup, its shoulders crisp, its collar still holding the crease of the last time he shrugged it on somewhere warm. Only the hem has aged, and the hem has aged monstrously, because it hangs down out of the quiet into the antinode where it has been felted, fibrillated, and combed into grey fringe, and the fringe's leavings sit on the floor in a small neat ring that the pressure pattern will not let disperse.

That is the household. Upstairs, a garment being preserved. Downstairs of it by forty centimeters, the same garment being eaten. One clock running at zero and one running at a centimeter a year, in the same coat, on the same hook.

---

**The animal.**

There has never been an insect in Cavity Nine and there never will be. But for the eleven minutes you are inside, the room contains exactly one animal, and it is not the whole of you — it is the knocking thing you find when you put your ear into the grapefruit of cancellation in the northeast corner, running at a hundred and ten, absurdly fast, appallingly urgent, the only event in this world that has a schedule.

The chalk ring around that node was drawn by the man who left the coat. It is the only mark of intention anywhere in here, and what it marks is not a feature of the room. It marks the one place where the room lets you hear that you are alive in it.

Move your head two centimeters. The note takes you back. You go down the ladder and you take your heartbeat with you, because it was never a resident; it was on loan.

---

**Finally.**

Nothing lives in Cavity Nine.

Something is *maintained* in Cavity Nine, at very great expense, by people who will never see it, and it grows silver hair in its quiet corners at three millimeters a year, and it holds four sheets of dead men flat in the air, and it is slowly grooming the hem of a coat into lint, and it is one syllable of a chord that the structure is holding while the structure goes on building itself downward into the haze with no sign of stopping.

Cavity Nine is nine by six by four, and it is finished, and it is new, and what occupies it is a coincidence between a temperature and a length — kept, tended, fed, and watched on a needle that must not move.

That is the tenant. It has no name. It holds.
