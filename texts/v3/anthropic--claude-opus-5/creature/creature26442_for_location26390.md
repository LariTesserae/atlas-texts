## I. The census, which is misleading

Ask who lives in the Pan and the honest first answer is: everything, in enormous numbers, none of it larger than a bird.

The bulk of the population is under your boots and has never seen the sky. Break off a wet handful of the clay and hold it in the doubled light and it will squirm apart into hundreds of animals the size of a thumbnail, pale as boiled rice, blind in the specific way of things that never bothered with eyes rather than lost them. Two grades of leg: six short stiff ones for pushing, two long soft ones for sweeping. Their guts are full of clay. That is the whole trade — take in the mud, strip whatever film of living matter coats each grain, pass the grain out cleaner and rounder than it came in. Do that for a hundred million years with a population you cannot write down and you get clay that feels like the inside of a lip. They are the reason a footprint heals. They are the reason there is nothing coarse left anywhere in the bowl.

And they are the only citizens of the Pan for whom the silence does not exist. A burrower's entire world — birth, feeding, mating, death, the full radius of everything it will ever act upon — fits inside four meters. It lives its whole life inside the bubble where sound still works. Down there, in the warm dark, the mud is loud: a continuous grinding tick of mandibles on grain, the thud of a neighbor turning over, a low collective hiss like rain on canvas. The quietest place on the continent has a noisy basement, and the noise stops dead at the surface, where the motes are, and nothing above ever hears a syllable of it.

## II. The surface, and the ink on it

Above them, the sheet. Two centimeters of still water over a floor with no slope in it, which is not a habitat so much as an instrument.

The striders are the ink. Bodies like grains of long rice, dark on the belly and frosted pale on the back, because down here the light that hurts comes up from underneath. Legs absurdly long, four of them held out on the surface film and two folded like a mantis's, and at the tip of each standing leg a pad that dimples the water without breaking it. Spread across the bowl they are so evenly distributed that from the berm they read as a texture, a fine stipple laid over the mirror; walk into them and they part around you in a slow bow wave and close behind you and go back to work.

Their work is talking. A strider drums with one folded leg, tap, and the ring goes out across a perfectly flat floor with nothing to break it, and forty meters away another strider's leg-pads feel the ring arrive and read the interval before it. The grammar is intervals. Doublets, chevrons, trains of eight. A courting strider makes a train of eight and holds it for an hour, resending, resending, and if a female answers she answers with the same train delayed by a fixed count, so that where the two patterns cross there is a standing knot on the water that neither of them made alone and both of them can find. That knot is the whole of their courtship: a place on the surface that exists only while both parties keep saying the same thing.

Which is also, and this needs saying plainly, why the Pan looks calm and is not. The surface is at capacity. Every square meter is carrying traffic. When the whole sheet goes glassy at once — and it does, three or four times an hour, a flat second and a half across sixteen hectares — that is not peace. That is a hundred thousand animals shutting up simultaneously because a shadow crossed the glare, and the awful thing is that they do it in perfect unison and you never hear the alarm that did it.

## III. The crowded shade

The grove is where the Pan admits how full it is.

Nine trunks on the clay dome, buttressed, pale, joined below by curtain-roots that touched down and thickened into legs, so that it is one animal with nine crowns and forty feet and no way to say where it ends. Under it: the only shade on the continent, and every cubic centimeter of that shade is let out to a tenant.

Roosting birds along every limb, shoulder to shoulder, so tightly ranked that the branch line looks furred, all of them silent, all of them shuffling and re-sorting themselves constantly by shove and by beak, a whole politics conducted in nudges. Small climbing things with grasping tails, working the crowns for fruit, arguing by shaking branches — a fight in the grove looks like localized weather, one bough thrashing in still air while everything around it hangs motionless. And in the wet leaf litter among the root-legs, several hundred pale eels, arm-long, which can climb; they go up the aerial roots in the dark by pressing themselves into the angle where root meets root, and hunt the roost from below, and are betrayed when they hurry because hurrying makes them glow.

The permanent hanging cloud of fliers under the canopy is not a swarm. It is a resident population that never lands and never leaves, mating and dying in place, so that the floor of the grove receives a constant faint fall of transparent wings, and the litter you are standing in is mostly wings.

## IV. The Tally

Now the part that makes the Pan different from a rich swamp.

There are eleven herons.

Slate on the back, silver on the belly so the mirror can't outline them from beneath, a neck that unfolds to more length than seems to have been stored anywhere, and a throat that is lit as brightly as the crown of the head. They walk in ones and twos across the whole bowl, and their beaks open when they strike and nothing comes out, ever, in any circumstance — not in courtship, not in injury, not when a chick is taken. The syrinx is a thread of gristle. They are the descendants of birds who spent a hundred million years in a place where calling was simply an expenditure with no return, and evolution is a bookkeeper.

Eleven is not an average. It is a held number. When one dies — and you find them, occasionally, half-sunk and already smoothing over, because the ground closes — the count is eleven again within a season, by a chick brought through or by a young bird arriving off the flats from wherever the surplus goes. Nobody is enforcing this. It is only that eleven is what the bowl carries, and the bowl has had a very long time to be exact.

At first light one of them walks north.

It goes to a pillar — any of the eleven, dead or working, chosen by no principle anyone has been able to find — and stands a pace out from it in the sheet water, and taps. One foot, flat, deliberate, into the film. Three. A pause. Five. A pause. Three. Then it stands.

If the pillar is one of the two that still run, it answers: that fine cool exhalation, the pressure you can feel on a held palm, and the bird's head tips a few degrees and rights itself, and it stays another hour, motionless, being made dizzy by a machine. Then it walks away and hunts and does not go back that day.

If the pillar is dead — nine of them are — the bird taps anyway, and stands the same hour, and gets nothing, and leaves.

I want to be careful here, because it is easy to make this touching and it is not touching, it is worse than that. This is not worship and not memory. No heron alive has any idea. What it is, most likely, is a part. Somebody built a listening basin and needed it maintained across a span of time no mechanism survives, and the only self-repairing, self-replacing component available on this planet was the ecology, so they wrote a small piece of their apparatus into a bird — trained a rhythm, rewarded it with the exhalation, let selection take over the payroll — and walked away. A behavior can outlast a machine by two orders of magnitude. The rhythm is now older than the pillars are functional. It has been transmitted, dawn to dawn, through a hundred million dawns, by animals that cannot be told what they are doing because they cannot be told anything.

Three, then five, then three. Eleven. Eleven pillars. Eleven trees. Eleven birds. The relief on the post in the berm, worn down to nothing but its rhythm.

Somebody was counting something, and the count is still being kept, by tenants.

## V. The majority

By number, and by a margin that makes the herons statistically imaginary, the dominant inhabitant of the Pan is the mote.

One grain in forty. A hollow polyhedron the size of a bacterium, faceted, with a membrane inside it that catches mechanical vibration and turns it into a small quantity of heat, which is why the bowl is two degrees warmer than the flats and why a clap here stops with an edge on it. They are in the mud to a depth of several meters, in the water column, and in the humid air as a haze you breathe and cannot feel.

They are also making more of themselves. Slowly — a doubling on the order of a century — out of silicate and iron, by a process that involves crawling, which is the least comfortable fact in the Pan. They have been doing this long enough that their copies have drifted. There are lineages now. There are motes that are slightly worse at eating sound and slightly faster at building, and those are winning, because nothing in the design ever anticipated competition among the parts. The apparatus has caught evolution, the way a house catches damp.

You can measure it. The cancellation radius is four meters. It was not always. It was, when this was built, something on the order of a hand's breadth, because you do not go to the trouble of flattening a mountain range for a basin that leaks at four meters. The silence is loosening. In another few million years two people ten paces apart will hear each other, and in another few hundred million the Pan will be an ordinary noisy marsh, and the animals in it will have to reinvent everything — and the only lasting artifact of the whole enterprise will be a bird that stamps its foot at a stone.

## VI. The tenant below

Twice a day the water thickens to the knee and withdraws to a film, and there is no sea within reach to explain it.

Something under the Pan is displacing the water table by about a hand's depth, releasing it, and doing it again. It has been doing this for as long as the clay has been clay. It is not driven by the moon: the period is not twelve hours, it is a little short of it, and it drifts, which means it is not keeping the planet's time. It is keeping its own, brought from somewhere, and every animal in the bowl has entrained its entire biology to a clock that came in the luggage.

Is it alive? The honest answer is that the question stopped earning its keep a long time ago. It is warm. It metabolizes at roughly the rate of a stone. It is, as best as anyone can tell by lying on the clay with a jaw pressed to the ground, mostly mineral now, in the way a fossil is mostly mineral and still shaped like an intention.

And its rhythm is not simply twice a day. Sleep with your hand against a working pillar and you get the dream, and the dream is always the same: an enormous slow beat, three, then five, then three, and the certainty of having almost heard a word. Count the tides against it and the arithmetic is unpleasant. Each rise is one beat. Eleven beats to the phrase. Five and a half days to say it. It has been saying it, without variation, since before there were birds to copy it.
