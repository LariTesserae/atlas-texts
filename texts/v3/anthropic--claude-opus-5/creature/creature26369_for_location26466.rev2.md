## First, a correction

Nobody lives *on* the Late Plain. The plain will not support the verb. It has no shelter, no fuel, no standing water, no soil deeper than the varnish, and it has been in its final condition long enough that it has stopped offering terms.

What people do here is live *across* it. The unit of dwelling is not a place but a route: a closed circuit some eleven hundred kilometres long, walked at a fixed pace, arriving back at its own beginning every four hundred and some days, which is a year here and also a house. A person is from a circuit the way you are from a town. When two parties meet at a marker and want to know each other, they do not ask where you are from. They ask, with a flat hand drawn in a slow arc, *what are you walking* — and the answer is a sequence of markers, given as a rhythm of steps, and the other party either knows it or does not.

There are, at present, about eleven hundred people doing this. There have been about eleven hundred people doing this for as long as any count exists, which is a long time. The number is not maintained by anyone. It simply is what the frost supports.

## The ones it does not make

They have a name for themselves, but it is not a word. It is a gesture: the palm laid flat against the sternum, held long enough to feel the chest move under it, then opened outward and dropped. Rendered into speech it comes out something like *the ones it does not make*, or *unrepeated*, or, if you want the flavour of how they actually use it — the way a farmer says *folk* — simply *the breathing*.

They are small. An adult stands about a metre and a half and weighs less than you would guess from the ribcage, which is enormous, barrelled, carried high, with a costal flare you can see through cloth. The limbs are thin and long-tendoned and the hands are large. The blood is thick to the point of sludge and the spleen is the size of a fist and doubles under effort. They cannot run for more than twenty seconds. They can walk, at four kilometres an hour, for eleven hours, on a cup and a half of water and eighty grams of pressed grazer, and then do it again the next day, and the next, for sixty years.

The face darkens over a life. This is not pigment, or not only. Frost lies on an upturned face for the same hour it lies on the pavement, and it carries the same trace manganese, and it leaves the same trace behind when it goes. An elder's cheekbones and brow and the bridge of the nose are red-black and faintly lustrous, laid down in a film a few molecules thick per decade, while the shadowed hollows — under the jaw, beside the nose — stay the colour they were born. Their word for an old person is the same as their word for a well-seated stone. They say the plain is taking them, slowly, and only from the outside, and that it will never get further in than that, because the dust stops at skin.

They see badly by forty. Everyone wears slit-visors cut from the quartz stumps — a wafer struck from a mountain's polished root, ground on another stone, corded to the head. It is the only wound anyone is permitted to make in the plain, and the varnish closes it in a decade, and the same stumps have been quarried for wafers since before the oldest route was walked, and are not measurably smaller.

## The rule of the cord

Everything they own is tied to them.

This is not thrift. It is the central practical fact of life here, and children learn it before they learn the map. The dust copies what you leave. A dropped cup becomes a colonnade of cups. A lost knife becomes a ridge of knives four kilometres long, each larger and blinder than the last, tumbling downwind in a diminishing series for a century until the last of them is a lump. Litter here is not mess; it is monument, and monument is obscene.

So: no pockets that can spill. Every object corded at two points. The garment itself — a long coat of felted grazer-silk, patched and overpatched until the original cloth is a rumour somewhere in the lining — is put on once, at fourteen, and is not taken off again. It is mended on the body. It is cut away from the body only by other hands, once. A walker's coat records their entire adult life as a stratigraphy of repairs, and a good reader can date a patch to within five years, and the oldest coats on the plain are the most beautiful objects anybody here will ever see.

There are heretics who leave things on purpose. A knife, a bowl, a doll of bound hair, set down in an assembly-field at dusk so that by morning there is something in the world with their shape on it. The people have a use for their names afterwards. Names are never repeated here — a name is retired the day its owner dies, and new ones are built by name-makers, so that the very old have names of one syllable and the young stagger about under four and five — but the names of leavers are kept in circulation deliberately, as insults, passed hand to hand for centuries, worn smooth. It is the only immortality anyone is actually granted while breathing, and it is a punishment.

## The hour

The day begins in the dark, roughly eighty minutes before the sun clears the rim, and it consists of one hour of work and eleven hours of walking.

In the hour, everyone kneels along the shadow-line of the larger stones with cloths of grazer-silk laid out flat, and waits, and does not speak, because speech is warm. Frost comes out of the shadow onto the cloth. When the sun's edge reaches a marked pebble, the cloths are gathered fast, twisted into gourds, and it is over — the frost goes from ice to nothing in under four minutes once the light touches it, and what you did not get you did not get.

A good hour yields a cup and a half a head. A poor one yields a swallow. Nobody on the Late Plain has ever been not thirsty. Their word for *comfort* and their word for *less thirsty* are the same word, and it is used constantly, and it is a word of enormous warmth.

## Harvest

Everything else edible rides the towers.

The glassy scale — the prostrate lichen with the translucent lobes, the one with no strategy but to go up — is the only concentrated sugar in a continent, and it is available for something under nine hours, on a structure that will not exist tomorrow, at whatever altitude the structure has lifted it to. So a harvest party runs at a dust-field. They do not climb the shaft. They use the stair.

The towers make stairs. They make cornices and fluting and balustrades and doorless doorways, faithfully, compulsively, for nothing and for no one, out of a memory of buildings that stood somewhere else under a different sky. The treads are a little too deep and a little too shallow by turns, as though remembered from a body that was not quite this body, and they hold a person's weight for as long as the charge holds, and no longer.

The signal to come down is the soarer. It comes in high, banking off the standing wave, and drops its shadow across the upper tiers, and it is never wrong by more than twenty minutes. It can feel the charge redistributing; the grazers can feel it too, and rise in a transparent shimmer off the fringe, which is what the soarer is there for. This is why the bird has never been seen on the ground. It lands only on things that will be gone by morning. Harvesters and soarers meet, several times a season, on the same collapsing stair, eight metres apart, each wholly uninterested in the other, both leaving at the same moment for the same reason.

The fall itself makes a sound like a held breath let go across a long room.

## How they talk

Speech costs air and the air will not carry it. So there is a hand-language, fast, low, held at chest height where the other party is looking anyway, with a vocabulary of about four thousand signs and a shameless fondness for puns. And there is the other language, which is walking: you speak to someone by falling in beside them and adjusting your step. Agreement is synchrony. Disagreement is a deliberate half-beat off. Affection is a shortening of stride to match a shorter leg. Grief is walked. Courtship is walked, for months, at a distance of two metres, in front of everybody, and everybody can read it, and nobody comments.

Mouth-speech exists and is rationed like water. It is used for the naming of children, for certain instructions at the tower, and once a year — at the marker where the circuit closes — for singing, which is the single most wasteful act in the culture and which leaves everyone on their knees, blind-headed, laughing, hyperventilating in the cold, for a quarter of an hour afterwards.

## The other end of the rule

*It has never been observed to model anything that respires.*

That is the sentence every visitor is given, and it is a comfort, and it is incomplete. The dust does not copy the living. The dead are a different matter.

When someone dies on the Late Plain they are laid out on clean pavement, downwind of the route, coat cut away and kept, and the party stays one night. There is nothing to bury them in and nothing to burn. By the third hour after dark the dust has come, the way it comes to a bootprint, and by midnight it is standing up.

The first copy is always the best likeness. It stands two metres, grey-gold, matte, holding the light the way a moth's wing does, faithful down to the set of the shoulders and the way the hands were kept — and this is, for these people, the only portrait that will ever exist of anybody. There is no water on this world. There are no mirrors but the black glass of the slag rings, which lies, and the surface of the dome, which is not for you. A person here sees their own face perhaps three times in a life, badly. Then they die, and are rendered once, enormous and exact, in a substance that will not last until noon, in front of the people who walked beside them.

They are expected to look. That is the whole of the rite. Look, for as long as it stands.

By dawn it has gone down and a larger, coarser one is rising eight hundred metres downwind, and a coarser one beyond that, and the line will run for forty or fifty kilometres and several centuries, softening, generalising, losing the hands first and then the face and then the fact of being a person at all, until what is left out there is a low range of humps that the walkers still name, and still route around, and no longer recognise. There are lines out there three thousand years long. The oldest of them are nobody. They are called by one-syllable names, and there are living children who know a dozen of them by heart, the way you know the names of hills.

Opinions differ, fiercely, along the whole length of every circuit. One school holds that this is the only continuation anyone gets and that it is a gift, and that the plain, having no memory of its own, is doing its patient best with the material. Another holds that it is the obscenity of leaving, committed by someone who can no longer help it, and these people arrange to be carried into the deep shadow of the escarpment, or sealed under the slag rings, where the dust does not work, and their names go out cleanly, unmade.

There is a third position, much older, which says that the reason the dust cannot copy the living is that copying requires a finished thing. The plain is finished. The cities are finished the instant they are imagined. A corpse is finished. A breathing person is the only unfinished object in a continent, and is therefore invisible, and is therefore free, and is therefore obliged — and here the sign for *obliged* is the one that also means *carrying water* — to stay unfinished as long as possible.

That is not a metaphor to them. That is why they walk.

## The counters

At the northern rim, near the dome, there is one group that does not walk. Six or seven adults at a time, in relays, camped on the glassy lee.

The dome pulses once every eleven seconds. It has done so since before the first route. Seven hundred and some years ago somebody began counting, and the count has not stopped, and it is kept by hand — a knotted cord, a slate, a spoken tally passed at shift-change with both parties touching the dome so that neither can cheat. Two-hour watches. Nobody has been alone with the number since the beginning.

It stands at a little over two billion.

It is the only large number on the plain, the only continuous record, the only inheritance that is not a coat or a route, and it is entirely without content. The dome does not answer, has never answered, and is not listening to them. The counters know this better than anyone. Ask them why and they will give you the sign for *because it is still going*, which is made with two fingers walking across the opposite palm, and is the same sign used for a person still alive.

If the chain ever broke, they would not say.

## The tenant

And then there is the dust, which is not a *whoever* and has to be included anyway, because it is the only thing here that has been here the whole time and will be here after.

It has no interior. It wants nothing, remembers nothing, has never once been observed to improve. It has a repertoire — the walkers count something over four hundred recurring forms and have names for all of them: the lung, the comb, the stack of plates, the spine, the child's fist, the cathedral, the long low one with the six hundred windows. It runs through them without preference and without order, everywhere, at the rate of some hundreds per hour, and has done so through the entire erosion of a mountain range.

Twice a generation it builds something nobody can place. It is unmistakably *of* something. It has a seat in it, at the wrong height. It has a handle, at the top of a flight of stairs, sized for a grip that closes the other way. Word goes out along the circuits and parties divert for days to stand and look at it, and they look at it the way you would look at a word in a language you know you once spoke.

Then the shear-line comes through and it lies down and is pavement again.

## Coda

On a clear night, on the rim, a harvest party will sit in the lee of a marker with their backs to the stone and their cords in their hands and watch three hundred kilometres of cities being born and coming apart under sheets of silent lilac fire.

They watch it for a few minutes. Then, mostly, they stop watching it, because they have seen it, because everyone has always seen it, because it is the one thing on this world guaranteed to happen again.

And they turn and look at each other instead.
