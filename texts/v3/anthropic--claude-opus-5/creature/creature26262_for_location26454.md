## The People of the Nodes

Visitors arrive expecting cripples. They have been told about the plugs and the wax and the water-filled suits, and they assemble in their heads a population of squinting, shouting, half-deaf unfortunates crouched in cellars. Then the cable car sets them down at the eastern terminus and the first thing they meet is a bonded-warehouse clerk who looks them over without hurrying, waits about five seconds, and asks them in a low even voice, at a volume no larger than the room requires, whether they intend to stand there.

They are big-chested and short-necked and they walk as though through shallow water even on dry stone. Their skin is soft the way a launderer's hands are soft, and everyone above the age of about thirty has a permanent tidemark, a faint green-grey bloom in the creases of the throat and behind the ears, from a lifetime of sweating walls. Their forearms are the give-away: long, ropy, hard along the outer edge, because they talk with their hands from before they can walk and go on talking with them for eighty years. A Hush grandmother can hold her arms up at chest height, unsupported, and sign for two hours without a tremor.

They find you loud. Not your voice — your habit. You throw words out into open air where anybody at all might take them, at three times the necessary volume, before the other person has finished being spoken to. Down in the valley this is called conversation. Up here it looks like a man urinating in a market.

---

Their hearing is the strangest thing about them and they do not consider it a loss.

Every child raised on the plateau grows a notch — a narrow band of deafness centred on the note and reaching up through its lower partials. They call it the *window*, on the grounds that it is a thing you look out of. A native of Hush cannot hear the bottom string of a bass viol; cannot hear a man's chest voice at its lowest; hears a mine whistle as a thin ghost of itself. Above the window, though, they hear beautifully, better than valley people, because they have spent no part of their childhood being deafened by anything at all in the registers that matter. Their music is accordingly all ceiling: wire strings, small bells, reed pipes pitched shrill, women and men both singing high and straight without vibrato, because vibrato is a wobble and up here a wobble means the beat of something enormous. To an outsider a Hush choir sounds like children. To them, valley music sounds like furniture being moved.

The size of a person's window tells you where they were raised, and everybody knows how to listen for it. A wide window, swallowing a whole octave and a half, means the west: means a childhood in a quarter the node had already begun to leave, in rooms with drained wall-cavities and cracked mortar, where the note came through the floor all night. A narrow window means the eastern new-build, wet walls, a bedroom in the belly of a hollow. In Hush, class is audible. You hear it the instant a stranger fails to react to a low sound, and a certain kind of eastern family will drop a coin on a stone table during introductions and watch the guest's face.

They have no word for silence in the valley sense. Their word for silence means *the note with nothing on top of it*, and it is a compliment; a good room is a silent room. As for the note itself, they mostly do not name it. There is a sign — flat palm laid against the sternum, once, briefly — which serves for it, and which serves also for *here*, for *always*, and for *of course*. A child asks why the water in the walls must be pumped uphill forever and gets a hand laid flat on a breastbone: *of course. Because of the thing that is always. Naturally.*

---

Speech is a room-shaped luxury and they treat it that way. Indoors, in a node, at a table, they will talk for six hours, and they are wonderful talkers — slow, patient, terribly funny — but the *slack* is in them. They wait a beat before answering, always, the same beat, five and a bit seconds long, and they wait it whether the pulse is audible or not, and they wait it in the valley too, and it has made every diplomat and card-player Hush ever exported quietly, ruinously formidable. A visitor who does not know this fills the gap. He fills it every time, and by the end of an hour he has conceded three points he never meant to raise.

Outside, on loud ground, the hands take over: four thousand signs, held low and close, elbows in, because a sign made out wide is a sign made to strangers and a sign made against the chest is a sign made to your own party. The poetic register is famous — it is what the plateau has instead of literature — and it cannot be written down. Attempts have been made. There are three published dictionaries and a school of criticism and none of it works, because half the meaning is in the tempo relative to the slack, and the slack is not on the page.

And beneath the hands there is bone-talk. Forehead to forehead, jaw set against jaw, one hand cupped over the join to keep the note out of the seam, and the words go through the skull and arrive in the middle of your head without ever having been in the air. Business is done this way in a loud street; instructions are given this way on a scaffold. But it is also, inevitably, how the plateau does love. The standing image of romance in Hush is not two people in a garden. It is two figures on a polished pan in flat even light, hooded, motionless, heads together, having been there some time. Bone poems are composed and given and cannot be copied or kept, only re-given, and the customary reply to a beautiful one is to say nothing and not move.

There is a small paid profession, twelve or fifteen people, called *carriers*, who are hired to take the last words of the dying to relatives who cannot reach the sickroom in time. A carrier receives the message skull to skull from a stranger, holds it for three days on the cable railway and the mule road, and delivers it skull to skull to another stranger in a valley inn, in the dead man's own phrasing and, as far as possible, his rhythm. They are paid extremely well and they are not, as a class, cheerful.

They laugh, incidentally, without sound: shoulders going, mouth wide, eyes shut, the whole body used because on loud ground a laugh that is not visible has not happened. To an outsider it looks exactly like grief. Their grief is the loud one — weeping is done aloud, freely, in company, in the nodes, and nobody looks away.

---

The city sorts itself into people by what they do about the noise.

The **midwives** own the deepest quiet in the world and know it. Four of them at a time, elected for life, holding the birthing house and its garden and its lawn, and holding therefore the one thing in Hush that cannot be bought, moved, or manufactured. They have refused three mayors and one imperial commissioner. They perform the Hearing themselves: eight days after birth, in the deepest room, a silver bell the size of a thimble struck once beside the infant's head, and the flinch — or the absence of it — written into the civic register in a hand that has not changed in two hundred years.

The **Pitch Office** is nine clerks and a ledger longer than everything else the city has ever written. Twice a month, at slack, one of them walks out to the marked stone with a fork and a bowl of water and takes the note, and there is a particular expression Pitch clerks get, a slight upward tilt and a stillness of the mouth, that other citizens imitate cruelly and secretly dread. They know a number nobody else knows to four decimal places. They know how fast the world is leaving. Twice in the city's history a clerk has falsified the ledger to slow the map down, and both times it was discovered within a season, and both times the punishment was to be shown the true numbers and made to do the arithmetic aloud.

The **lifters** — the Guild of the Second Course, whose mark is a numbered stone — take the houses apart and put them back together eastward. Every block a family owns is chiselled with a lineage of small numbers on its hidden face, and there are lintels in Hush that have been carted a mile and a half in eleven separate journeys over six hundred years, and there are families whose whole self-conception rests on the fact that these twelve stones and no others have always been theirs. A lifter is a mason, a surveyor, a removal man, and a sort of priest, and the worst hour of his working life is the one where he stands in a drained empty shell with a widow who has decided she is not going.

The **wet crews** come out of the antinodes at shift's end in their articulated suits, dripping, unbolting each other's collars, and they stink, and they know they stink, and they will tell you it is the smell of the lamps being lit. They live short. The pressure gets into the joints — *the fret* — and into the sinuses, and after fifteen years a man's hands are unreliable, which for a hand-speaker is the end of the world; the Company's pension is calculated in signs per minute. Underwater and hooded they use a stripped dialect of about twenty gauntlet-signs, and they use it in taverns afterwards out of pure swagger, and young men in the eastern quarters affect it and are despised for it.

And there are the **westerners**: eleven thousand people living in the loudening streets because rent there is measured, frankly and openly, in how much of your hearing the lease will cost you. Dry cavities. Mortar going to powder. Grandmothers with windows an octave wide sitting in doorways signing at each other in the enormous slow arm-wide style of the near-deaf, which the east finds embarrassing and the west finds honest. They are the ones who say — and it is the closest thing the plateau has to a political slogan — that the city has been running away from the noise for six hundred years and has got a mile and a half, and that this is not enough to be proud of.

---

Not everyone stays inside the shape.

There are hermits in the little nulls out on the pans — pockets the size of a bed where two hollows clip each other and the note thins for reasons the Pitch Office can model but not explain. Some are pious, looking for the *Dead Middle*, a perfect cancelling point that the mathematics permits and the plateau has never yet produced, where a person would stand in open air in absolute stillness and hear, presumably, whatever is under all of it. Some are simply unfit for company. They are fed by relays of children and they die of ordinary things.

And there is the other way of leaving, which is to stop minding. It comes on as an indifference; the plugs get left on the shelf; a person begins to take the short route across a lobe instead of the long way round the ridge, and then to take it slowly, and then to stop in the middle. *Going to the middle* is what it is called, and the bodies are found sitting up, unmarked, out on the polished ground, and the beetles are usually there.

The beetles. Thumb-sized, dun, earless, sitting in the worst places in visible vibration, and after thirty years of work a woman named Sedd Arrowen worked out what they are doing, and no one has been able to disprove it. They are talking. The one channel on the plateau with no traffic on it is the note itself; the males modulate the standing wave with their own bodies, a stutter here, a swallowed beat there, and the females feel it — arrive along it — from four, five, seven miles off. It is not a small language. It is the largest structure the plateau contains, an object miles across, made of interruptions, and it is entirely love-talk, and it has been going on the whole time, underneath the city, through the walls, through the water, through the beds. Arrowen learned to read about ninety signs of it with her palms flat on a cold pan at night. She published a slim book. It is mostly one word repeated with small variations, and the word is roughly *here*.

Everything else alive up here is smaller and dumber and dear to them: the eels in the seeps, blind and gentle, tasting their way, sold smoked in the market and considered lucky; the six lichens, which are counted and mapped and inherited; the salt grass; the red-edged succulent that keeps advancing into the abandoned western rooms because nobody has told it the node has gone. And the hounds — low, broad, silent, bred over centuries out of some ordinary valley dog into an animal that does not bark because barking is useless and instead leans, presses, puts its whole weight against a shin to say what it has to say. A hound belongs to a node and not to a person. Carry it over a ridge and it fits, foams, and dies, and there are hounds in Hush who have been lowered into water-filled panniers and carried east with the family and lived, and this is considered a piece of unusual good fortune, and it is talked about for years.

---

The last thing is what happens to the old.

Hearing narrows with age everywhere; here it narrows from the top down, so that over a decade or two the wire strings and the little bells and the high straight singing thin out and go, and the window widens upward to meet the loss, and finally there is a night when a very old woman sitting in the deepest room of a good eastern house lifts her head, because through her skull and her jaw and the arms of her chair something enormous has arrived that she has not heard since she was eight days old and did not flinch at a silver thimble.

*She has begun to hear the bell again.* That is the phrase, and everyone knows what it means, and the family sends for the carriers.

They do not bury. Water is a hand's depth down and a grave is a well. The dead are taken out on frames to the loudest ground, in a slow procession that everybody plugs for and nobody speaks during, and left, and the note takes them apart — a season for the flesh, four or five years for the rest, the bones unstitching at the sutures first and going at last to a pale grit that the ridges gather and heap in the nodes with
