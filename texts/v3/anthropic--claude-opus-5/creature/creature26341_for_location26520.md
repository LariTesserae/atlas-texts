## Who keeps it

### The stock

Ninety-one thousand three hundred and something. They will give you the figure to the person if you ask, and they will be right, because they count each other the way they count the herons — not out of anxiety, they insist, but because a number you can hold is a comfort in a place with no horizon and no shadow to measure.

Three lineages, and everybody can place everybody within two questions. There are the **crews** — descendants of the extruder teams and spinners who laid the plates and walked away from a finished city with nowhere to go, because the ships that were to bring the four million were cancelled, or delayed, or were never quite as real as the plumbing. There are the **second-comers**, brought in twenty years later on a settlement contract that promised a metropolis and delivered a rehearsal; they are still, four decades on, faintly litigious, and they are the ones who keep the archives. And there are the **wardens**, who are not a lineage exactly but behave as one, marrying inward, holding themselves slightly apart, and being generally forgiven for it.

They do not look like a frontier people. They look like librarians. There is no sun to weather them, no wind to redden them, no season to thicken or thin them. The Addition's population is uniformly pale in a particular grey-gold way, unlined until quite late, and then suddenly very lined indeed, because the shell filters the light that ages skin and does nothing about the ninety percent humidity that eventually makes a face go soft and crease all at once, like paper left in a bathroom.

This matters more than it sounds. On a plain where every edge is filed sharp each night, where nothing is permitted to wear, human beings are the only surfaces that show use. They are the only old thing on the Addition. They know it. It has made them the opposite of vain: an eighty-year-old is displayed, brought out, walked slowly along the seam-avenues at low water so that people can look at her hands. A deep wrinkle is regarded here roughly the way a cathedral is regarded elsewhere.

### The scar

Everyone has it. Third or fourth week of life on the plain, generally on the mound below the little finger, sometimes across two fingers if they were foolish about it: a thin white line from running a hand along an arris. It is the same wound on ninety thousand people, in the same place, made by the same species of edge.

So the greeting on the Addition is to turn the right hand palm-up at waist height, briefly, as you pass. It means *I have been cut by this ground too.* Nobody performs it with any solemnity; it is as automatic as a nod, and children do it to herons.

### Voices

They speak quietly, high in the throat, and they never raise their voices, because the grain makes raising pointless — volume does not travel, only pitch does, and a shout arrives as a thin scrape. To visitors they all sound like people confiding, at any distance. Across a four-hundred-metre plaza a stranger's remark about her knee will arrive beside your ear as though she were leaning on your shoulder.

The consequence is an elaborate deafness. There is no architectural privacy on the Addition and so privacy has become a manner: you hear everything and you attend to nothing, and to repeat back something you overheard from a distance is an ugly act, worse than theft. They call it *carrying*. "He carries," said of a man, is the whole indictment.

Their music has no floor. Every ensemble on the plain is treble — reed, glass, wire, whistling — because bass does not exist here to be composed for, and the two or three recordings of off-plain orchestras that circulate are listened to the way pornography is listened to: privately, with the ears of somebody being told about a body part they do not have. When an underwriter comes up and speaks, children cry.

### Names, and the count

Everyone has a given name, a family name, and a number, and the number is the heron count on the day they were born.

Two hundred and eighty-seven. Three hundred and four. Two-fifteen. The count moves slowly, and it goes down as well as up, so numbers recur across decades, and anyone sharing your number is your *namesake* — a formal kinship with real obligations. A namesake may ask you for lodging, money, or an alibi and may not be refused twice. It produces a society knitted crosswise: a girl of nine and a man of seventy standing at a funeral in the front row together because on two separate mornings forty years apart there were three hundred and eleven herons walking the trunk mains.

When a heron dies the count drops and a cohort closes; nobody born after will ever share those names. People are quiet for a day. They do not pretend it is grief for the bird.

### Airing

The work of the Addition is not building. Nothing needs building. The work is keeping four million people's worth of apartments ready for the four million, and this is done by hand, because the machines only do surfaces.

Each empty flat in Meniscus is visited once every fourteen months. The airer walks up the whorled stair, opens the door, runs every tap for ninety seconds, cycles the vent, sits for the prescribed six minutes, and certifies. Six hundred flats a tower, sixty-some towers, ninety thousand people: do the arithmetic and you will understand why nearly everyone airs, two days a week, for their whole life, and why the phrase *my rounds* is spoken here the way other people say *my family*.

They are not supposed to leave anything. They leave things anyway. Behind the spin-seam of every riser — the one hand's-width of any tower the cats cannot reach — there are marks: initials, dates, a scratched tally, a pencilled height of a child, a line of a song, the same obscenity in three generations of handwriting. It is a whole illicit literature written in the only places on the plain where a mark is allowed to survive.

The wardens know. They air too, and their initials are there as well. Everyone understands what it is: it is not vandalism, it is the local religion. It is people trying to make a city old by hand, at the rate of a millimetre a decade, exactly as the eleventh channel is doing it, and by exactly the same means — by keeping a decision.

### The slackeners

The hardest job on the Addition, and the one nobody thanks.

Somebody has to decide how much worse things are allowed to get this year. Somebody has to write the order that the cats will run four seconds slower per arris, that terrace nine's moss may be permitted to reach the lip and stay, that the sea-wall is to be given back only three and a half grams of the four the water took. They are called slackeners, they work in threes, and each ruling is physically signed and then read aloud in the plaza, because a thing done to the ground of the world should be said out loud in front of people who live on it.

They are hated in a mild, structural way, like tax officers, and they are also the only people on the plain doing anything that could be described as ambition. A slackener's career is measured in permitted decay. There is a woman in the harbour towers whose entire life's work is a stain.

### The underwriters

You will not meet one socially.

They live below, at stations nobody visits, in the bass. Their hearing is inverted with respect to everyone else's: down there the grain's appetite is fed and the low frequencies live, and after fifteen years in it an underwriter cannot follow ordinary Addition speech at all — the thin high placed voices go past them like a draught. They speak loud, flat, and slowly, and they have a reputation for bluntness that is really just a reputation for volume.

Twice a month they trim the plain and the hum shifts and ninety thousand people sit down. Every household therefore has a relationship with the underwriting that is not quite worship and not quite plumbing. Old people claim to hear which quarter is being adjusted; children can actually do it, and are tested on it, and are pleasingly, disturbingly accurate.

Nobody in Meniscus knows how many underwriters there are. The wardens say the number is not withheld; it is simply not counted, and the fact that there is one number on the Addition that nobody counts is discussed, at length, by everyone, forever.

### Low water

The fog hours are when the Addition does its living.

Two hours a day, everything below the third floor is gone and the plain is a white lake with towers standing out of it. In that white you cannot be seen from a metre away, and although you can still be heard — the grain sees to that — the etiquette of not-carrying is absolute during low water, and enforced by common consent with real ferocity.

So: arguments happen in fog. Weeping happens in fog. Courtship, entirely. Confession, reconciliation, the breaking of contracts, the giving of bad news. Business transacted at low water is not referred to afterward and is not, strictly, binding, which is why so much of it is honoured. Two people who meet in the fog for a year may pass each other in the grey-gold afternoon with a palm turned up and nothing else.

They date events by rain, since the rain's hour walks four minutes a day around the year. *We married at eleven-past-fog, in the season when it rained at supper.* Everyone carries a rain-clock, a cheap brass thing that is really a calendar, and the young flick them open constantly, the way the young everywhere fidget with the shape of time.

### Sleep, and being drawn

They sleep off the ground, always, on cots and slung beds, and a child who falls asleep on bare grain is picked up with a haste that has nothing rational in it, because the substrate wicks and a person left on it wakes dry-mouthed, dry-eyed, flattened, *drawn*. It takes a day to come back. It has never taken longer than a day. It does not matter: the ghost story of the Addition is a man who lay down on plate seven and was taken up by the ground a little at a time, and there are forty variants, and every one of them ends with the recovery cradles at dawn holding something clean and folded.

They will tell you about the two people the cradles have collected. Both were fine. Both are famous. One of them is a woman in her sixties now who is stopped in the street by strangers wanting to know whether it was gentle, and who says yes, and who has never once been asked whether she wanted to be found.

The bodies of the dead are the single exception the machines make. A body left on the ground overnight is not lifted, not cleaned, not moved; the cats go around it in a wide arc and continue filing the edges. Nobody knows who wrote that in, or why, or where the rule lives. It is the thing about the Addition that most frightens the people who live there, and it is the reason funerals are held at low water, over the harbour cut, quickly, at the wall the sea is never allowed to win.

### The other residents

The **cats** outnumber the people about nine to one, and are talked about as fauna: their gait, their preferences, which risers they favour, the one on terrace nine that has, for eleven years, always begun at the same corner. They are being permitted to grow slow. Children ride them, which is forbidden and universal.

The **ants** are tenants of the north faces and are formally owed water in dry years. The **mist-spider** is admired for having invented, unprompted, an economy identical to the city's: catch the fog, drink, wait.

The **beetle** — a single species, occasionally reported, never photographed convincingly — is the object of a great deal of affection and one salaried post. The beetle-keeper is a hereditary appointment, currently a man of thirty-one who has never seen a beetle, files an annual report of two lines, and is taken seriously by everyone. In a city built for millions and holding ninety thousand, the beetle is understood to be a joke about themselves, and it would be a discourtesy to explain it.

And the **herons**, who are neighbours rather than wildlife. They file through the service galleries under the plain, dusted grey-white, and come up at hatches into the middle of somebody's day, and are not fed and are not shooed. They are named — all three hundred and some, individually — and the naming is done by children in a ceremony nobody organizes. To be looked at, at close range, by a heron in a lit tunnel is the closest thing the Addition has to being addressed by the place itself.

### Leaving

Some go. They do badly.

Off-plain, a hard shadow reads as a hole; morning arrives like an assault, from one side, all at once; thunder is *audible*, which is obscene; rooms have bass in them and speech drowns; nothing is where the ear expects. They flinch, they squint, they are exhausted by weather. Most come back within two years and are called **returned** and are not teased about it, because everyone knows.

The ones who stay away send letters that are read aloud at low water and then not discussed.

### What they are for

Ask, and you will get the official answer: we hold the Addition ready.

Sit with them longer, at the bench by the eleventh channel, in the warm grey damp, and you will get the real one, which is not phrased as an answer at all. They will point at the millimetre in the left bank. They will explain that it took ten years, that it is a decision the water has managed to keep, that when the meanders are as deep as a hand the shell can be let go and there will be wind, and shadows on time, and a morning that comes hard and yellow from one direction.

Then they will say, without self-pity, that they will not see it, and that their children will not, and that the marks behind the spin-seams and the stain in the harbour tower and the moss on terrace nine and the four grams the sea is quietly being allowed to keep are all the same work as the millimetre, done by hands instead of water.

They bring their old people out to the bench. That is the thing I keep coming back to. A grandson will walk a woman of ninety the six minutes from the tram, and sit her down facing the channel, and the two of them will look at the millimetre together for an hour without speaking — the only two aging things on the plain, keeping each other company, while the herons file past inside the ground beneath, counting themselves, going somewhere.
