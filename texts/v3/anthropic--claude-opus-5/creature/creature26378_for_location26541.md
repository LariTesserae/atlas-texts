## The Hantlefolk

They are not tall. Eleven generations of thin sweet wrong air have given them deep chests and short shins and a resting pulse a lowland physician would treat as an emergency. Their hands are large out of proportion to everything else, and the palms are strange: a hard bar of callus across the base of the fingers from rope and comb-frame and hoist-rail, and inside that bar, on nearly everyone, a second, softer polish on the pad of the right hand, from touching walls.

They touch walls. Constantly, absently, the way other people hum. Passing down a stair they lay the flat of the hand on the stone at every landing, one tap, no ceremony, and go on. Bannisters in the old streets are worn through the brass into the iron. Doorframes have hollows in them. If you ask why they will look at you as if you had asked why they breathe out as well as in, and then, because they are a courteous people in a graceless way, they will say: *to see whether it is there.* It always is, below the Line. They do it anyway. It is hygiene.

Their skin runs pale to a faint slate, and in cold weeks to the blue of skimmed milk, and it is always slightly damp, because everything is always slightly damp at six degrees in a breathing fog, and they are greased against it — lanolin from stept wool, rendered fat, a foul yellow ointment sold by weight. They smell of wet wool, peat smoke, and sulphur, in that order, and they cannot smell it on each other, and lowland traders smell nothing else for a week.

Their eyes are large-pupilled and slow to contract and they do not squint, having nothing to squint at. They read easily in what a visitor calls dark. Under the smelter arc-lamps they suffer, and the well-off affect smoked glass indoors, and the mill hands wear leather blinkers, and the whole city develops, around thirty, a habit of shading the brow with the hand at any bright thing, which reads as suspicion and is not.

Their teeth are excellent. This is the flint milk. It disconcerts the traders, who expect bad teeth in a shabby people, and it is one of the small reasons the traders do not like coming up.

## Speech

They talk all the time, mostly about themselves, in a flat unmusical mutter, and it is the most misunderstood thing about them.

*Going up to the fourth for the tin. Bought a lamp wick, brass one, on the Row, from Ledda's boy. Slipped on the eighth stair, did not fall.* A man alone in an alley narrating his errand to nobody. A woman on a gondola listing what she has done since morning to the cable and the fog. Visitors think it madness or senility or prayer. It is none of these. It is load-bearing.

The language has two moods a lowlander's ear cannot hear. There is the *attested*, used for what you personally witnessed, and there is the *provisional*, used for what is reported, inferred, remembered wrong, or situated above the Lichen Line. Court speech is attested throughout; a witness who slips into the provisional has ended his testimony whether he meant to or not. Lowlanders, having only one mood, speak entirely in what sounds to Hantle ears like the provisional, and are therefore held to be lying continuously, without malice, the way water is held to be wet. Contracts with the low country are drafted twice.

To use the provisional mood of a living person to their face is the worst thing you can say. Duels have been fought over a verb ending. It is roughly: *you are not sufficiently the case.*

Greeting is not *how are you*. Greeting is your own name, spoken first, in full, before your business — and then, if you are close, *who saw you today?* To enter a room and not name yourself is not rudeness. It is a threat, and is prosecuted as one.

## Names

Names accrete. A birth-name from the family, a work-name from the guild, and then an incident-name, which you do not choose: it comes from the thing that happened to you, the fall you survived, the suit you lost, the joke that would not die. Kesserin Comb-hand Nine-Times-Sued. Oram Grit Ellwaine Who-Dropped-The-Bishop. After fifty, if you take a warden's station, a station-name. Nobody is fond of their incident-name and everybody uses it, because a name is ballast and a humiliating name is heavier than a pleasant one.

Abbreviation on documents is a criminal offence. Full names take the better part of a minute to say aloud and are said aloud, in full, at every legal occasion, at every door of a warden's hut, and at every funeral, twice.

The insult is *short-named*. It means new, thin, unheld, easily mislaid. Children are short-named as a matter of fact and not as an insult. Immigrants are short-named for years, and the city has an entire apparatus for fixing this: a newcomer is assigned a lodging, a debt, a guild-master, a feud, and a rota of people obliged to mention them by name once a day in the hearing of a third party. Debt is not shameful here. To owe is to be recorded. The genuinely poor are the ones nobody has a claim on.

## The Guilds

**The Guild of Paces.** Their bodies are the instruments and they maintain them like instruments. A certified pacer eats measured meals, is weighed weekly, does not permit himself to grow or shrink, does not run, does not marry outside the guild if he can help it because a domestic upheaval shows in the stride within a fortnight. His certificate lists his pace to the third decimal at three temperatures. He walks the provisional country and the distance he walked is the distance there was, by law, and by nothing else. Pacers' knees are finished by forty-five and the guild pensions them into wardenship, and a retired pacer's stride is the saddest thing in Hantle, being no longer worth anything and still, helplessly, exact.

**The hanging crews.** Lichen-pressers and etch-men, five hundred metres of nothing under the stage, wooden paddles and copper hoses, the most fatalistic and the best-paid labour on the mountain. They work roped in threes and the third man does not work: the third man watches the other two and says their names at intervals. He is called the sayer. The wage schedule pays him the same. This is not sentiment; it is why crews come back with their scars on the correct wrists.

**The moth-scrapers.** Old, hereditary, insufferable about precedence, and the only guild with a religious practice worth the name. They believe — the word they use is *hold*, they *hold* — that attention is carried, and that the fog-moths carry it, and that a room with a moth in it is a room with a witness in it. Killing one is unlucky to the point of professional ruin. They scrape a moth's guano off the frames for manure and wear a plaited cord at the wrist as an apology for the intimacy, and they will tell you the cord is nothing, a habit, and they will not take it off.

**The Registry clerks.** The priesthood, and they hate the word. Pale even here, indoor-thin, ink to the second knuckle, half of them ruined-eyed by fifty, and collectively the most stable human beings on the Kell, because they write about each other all day. There is a standing rule, unwritten and inviolable, that no clerk goes a day unmentioned in another clerk's marginalia. The margins of the Hantle ledgers are therefore full of the smallest possible facts about clerks — *Vessel had the cough again, Hollam's chair mended* — and these marginal notes are indexed, cross-referenced, and preserved with the same care as the deeds, and constitute, taken together, the most complete record of any group of people anywhere.

**The Stare-Wardens.** Six hundred old and broken people in tin huts, looking at rock. They are the nearest thing the city has to saints and are treated with an affection that has fear in it, because a warden's work is to pour attention outward for four hours at a stretch into country that gives nothing back, and this is known to be dangerous in a way that is hard to draft regulations about. So there is a rota. A child comes up the path at the end of every shift with tea and to collect the ledger, and stands at the door, and says the warden's whole name, all of it, station-name last, and waits to be answered before entering. Wardens who are not answered are logged as such. There is a procedure. It is not discussed with the children.

## The Deep-Registered

There are nine.

They are very old, they have eleven or twelve name-parts, they have been sued into the hundreds of pages, they have biographies in three editions and dogs the whole city can name, and they are so thick with recorded incident that unwritten rock does not slip within thirty paces of them.

They are hired out. A new bolt-site on a raw shelf, eight-metre pins going into stone that has never had anything happen to it, and the company sends up a sedan chair with two porters and a brazier and a woman of ninety-four in it, and she sits there for an afternoon knitting and complaining about the draught, and afterwards the site has had someone there, and the pins hold. The fee is enormous. She resents every minute of it, has outlived four insurers, and goes when called, every time, and if you ask her why she will say that somebody has to, in the attested mood, and change the subject to her knees.

## Thinning

It does not look like fading. That is the whole trouble.

A man comes back from a long stretch above the Line and there is nothing wrong with him. A week later his coat buttons the other way, and his wife's name is nearly the one it was, and his middle daughter's birthday has moved by a day, and he does not notice, and he cannot be brought to notice, and he is not lying and never was, and this is attested, and so is the other version, and both are in the ledgers in a clerk's careful hand
