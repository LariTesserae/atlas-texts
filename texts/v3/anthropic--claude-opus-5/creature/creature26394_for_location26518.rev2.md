## Who is in there

The honest first answer is: nobody. Nothing in the vault is a resident. Everything in the vault is staff.

That answer lasts about a night. Then it stops being adequate, and the second answer starts assembling itself out of details you'd rather not have noticed.

---

**The simmering.** Take a pool — any of them, they're all the same pool — and draw off a spoonful and let it settle on a slide. Several hundred animals, fingernail-joint length, translucent to the point of being mostly rumour. They have no eyes and no pigment and a gut that occupies most of the body, and the gut is not for food.

It is an ion exchanger. The seep water comes into this chamber carrying the ordinary filth of groundwater — magnesium, strontium, a little iron, the usual sloppy chemistry that makes ordinary cave calcite cloudy and crooked and full of twinned mistakes. The animals drink it, strip it, and hold the wrong atoms in their tissue. That is their entire occupation. A young one is clear as meltwater. An old one is faintly grey, and then chalky, and then it is so loaded it can no longer moult, and it climbs.

You can watch this. A grey one leaves the pool, crosses four centimetres of liverwort skin, presses itself flat against the wall, and waits, and within a day the wall has grown over it. If you cut a wall sheet — you shouldn't, but people have — you find them in there, in lenses, thousands of bodies laid flat in a seam like a stratum of ash. Tailings. The chemists date the lenses and find the record of every wet spring in four hundred years, because a wet spring means dirty water means a heavy harvest means a thick grey band.

The flawlessness of the stone growing in the floor is not a physical fact. It is a biological achievement, performed one atom at a time, by animals with no eyes.

**The grazers.** Glacial worms on the liverwort, black threads, mats of them, moving all the time. They eat frost. Specifically they eat the frost-ferns at the tips, where the feathers would otherwise reach across a gap and touch, and fuse, and start bridging the chamber shut.

A vault's acoustics depend absolutely on the shape of the void. Where a worm mat thins — disease, an intruder's boot, a lamp left burning too long — the wall grows a blind white wart of unbrowsed frost within a week, and the chamber's fundamental goes flat by a measurable amount, and three metres away the spark-rate on the floor drops and the crystals in that district come in slightly wrong and have to be dissolved and redone. It takes eleven years to redo. A careless afternoon costs the vault eleven years.

**The shivering.** The springtails are not grazing. Put a density meter on the wall and map them and you get a picture of the standing wave: they crowd where the tissue moves most, thin out where it doesn't, and their collective mass loads the membrane exactly where loading is wanted. They are upholstery. They are damping. They go where the note tells them and the note is what it is because they went there.

Brush a palm-sized patch clean — this experiment has been done, once, by someone who then wrote a very unhappy three pages about it — and the vault goes sharp within the hour, audibly, sourly, across the whole chamber. The animals come back over the next two days from all directions and the pitch walks back down to where it was and settles, and overshoots, and settles again. Whoever did that, did it in 1961, and the vault has not entirely forgotten.

**The furnace that isn't.** In the felt, the grey fur: tardigrades in numbers that stop being countable and become a material property. They are not eating anything much. They are going into cryptobiosis and coming out of it, over and over, out of phase with each other, in their billions.

Each transition moves a trivial quantity of latent heat. Multiply by the population and you have the thermostat — the reason the chamber reads zero, zero, zero with a stability that looks like instrument failure. The plant supplies the coarse control. The tardigrades do the last two decimal places. When a warm front sits over the watershed in July, the felt goes a shade paler as a hundred million animals dry down together, and the chamber does not move off zero by any amount that can be measured.

**The masons.** Ten centimetres, segmented, the colour and clarity of ground glass. Blind. Along the whole length of the body, under the plates, runs a chordotonal organ — a hearing organ of a kind found elsewhere in insect legs, here scaled up and run the length of the animal like a spine. They do not hear the vault. They *are* a probe dragged through the vault's field, and they know where they are in the standing wave the way you know which way is down.

Under the sixth segment each one grows, over about eight months, a single lens of aragonite the size of a lentil. Then it walks — slowly; a metre is most of a day — to a place it has been told about, and stops, and presses its underside to the floor, and stays there for an hour, and leaves the lens behind, and walks away to spend another eight months making the next one. The lens is the seed. Everything that grows in that district for the next decade grows in register with it.

The instruction is audible once you know it: the bass note with a burr in it. Two masons will converge on the same spot from opposite sides of the chamber and arrive within a minute of each other, one to place and one, apparently, to check. They moult perhaps four times in a life. The shed shells are not wasted; they go into the floor. Some of the floor is made of the discarded skins of the animals that made the floor.

Nobody has seen one hatch. Three separate people have proposed that the masons are the simmering crustaceans grown up — that the pool animals which stay clear, which never load with strontium, which are therefore the failures of the filtering caste, go on to metamorphose into the glass woodlice. It is a tidy story. It would take about sixty years to confirm, which is longer than a career, and longer than most of the funding.

## The body

None of the above is the tenant either.

The tenant is the wall. It is one organism, continuous from the liverwort skin you touch to the felt to the columns to the catenary sheets to the unhealed seams, and continuous, further, out through the peat in every direction for something like nine hectares.

And here is the part that embarrasses everybody: at the surface, it is a plant you have walked on. A low, dull, wet-ground sward, four lines in the regional key, *locally frequent on peat, of no economic importance*, keyed and pressed and filed by a hundred and thirty years of competent botanists who never once wondered what it was doing with the energy. Every photon that lands on those nine hectares all summer goes down the stem. Not into seed. Not into growth you can see. Into holding three and a half metres of air at exactly zero and pushing a forty-hertz standing wave through it for four hundred years.

You have had it in your boot treads. It has been in herbarium cabinets since 1889.

The seam you climbed down is not an accident of growth and it is not a door. It is a suture that has not yet closed. Sixty-one vaults have been located in this watershed by sounding the moss with a stethoscope and a patient morning. Nine can be entered. The other fifty-two are sealed, and their sutures are the hard white ridges you can feel through the peat if you know to feel for them, and their hum comes up through the ground at a pitch you can stand on and feel in the soles of your feet, and there is no way in and there will not be one.

The enterable ones are the young ones. You have only ever been inside infants.

## The tuning

The fundamentals were first measured properly in 1913, by a man with a set of tuning forks and no idea what he was doing, and his numbers are good. They were measured again in 1971 and in 2004 and there is a programme now that does it annually with equipment that would have made him weep.

The sixty-one notes are not random and they are not identical. They are close. Mean deviation from simple whole-number ratios against the deepest vault in the system: thirty-one cents in 1913, nineteen in 1971, twelve in 2004.

They are coming into tune. Slowly, over centuries, with the whole watershed as the instrument and the peat as the soundboard, sixty-one buried chambers are converging on a chord.

## What it is for

At the end — four hundred thousand more feather-and-fail cycles from now — each vault is a solid body of aligned, flawless, singing calcite, and the organism that spent its whole life making it lies down and becomes peat.

Under three metres of tundra, nothing is going to strike that stone. Except the winter. Every autumn the active layer freezes and closes on what it contains with a pressure of tonnes, and every spring it lets go, and a body of flawless aligned calcite, clamped and released by a continent of ice, will ring. Once a year. One note apiece. Into rock and water and permafrost, where sound travels appallingly well and very far.

Sixty-one stones, tuned to each other over a millennium, struck annually by winter, for as long as there is winter.

The least embarrassing of the available stories — and you should hear the others — is that this is not architecture at all but reproduction. That the wet peat between the stones is where the standing waves of that annual chord interfere, and that in the places where the interference is right, something in the groundwater organises, and a new sward takes hold above it, and in due course a new volume of nothing gets closed around by tissue growing to a plan. That the vaults are not houses. That they are gonads, or tuning forks, and that the animals inside them are gametes' worth of labour, and that the flawlessness matters because you cannot conceive anything precise with a crooked note.

That story requires the species' generations to be conducted entirely in the acoustics of buried stone, at a tempo of a thousand years, and it does not survive a second reading either.

---

So: the inhabitants are a filtering caste, a grazing caste, a damping caste, a thermostat made of animals that are technically dead most of the time, a blind mason corps that takes instructions from a burr in a note, and one plant, which is the walls, and the ceiling, and the floor's roots, and the tundra you crossed to get here, and which will not live to hear the thing it is casting make a sound.

And you. For about nine hours you stood in a saturated chamber at exactly zero and breathed out carbon dioxide into water that was waiting for it.

Some micrograms. A few thousand lattice cells, somewhere in the floor, in perfect register with the rest.

Your breath up on the surface is the first you've made all day that nobody used. Everything you exhaled down there is in the stone, and the stone will ring, and it will go on ringing once a year into the rock long after the language you'd have used to explain yourself has stopped being spoken.
