# What Lives in the Bowl

## The first millimetre

Begin with the film, because everything else is a consequence of it.

On every wet surface in the hollow — and every surface is wet — there is a layer you can only detect as a slight reluctance under the fingertip, a fractional stickiness, like the inside of a peach stone. That is the entire primary population of the bowl. Cells living off the sulphides and the ammonium coming up out of the seep, building sugar in the dark the way their ancestors did before there was any other way. They are not a species. They are a consortium, several dozen kinds in a stack four or five cells deep, each one eating the waste of the one above it, and the stack is so old and so stable that its layers have become as regular as varves.

Numerically, this is the bowl. Everything larger — the columns, the curtains, the frogs, the thing that walks — is a rounding error carried on top of it, the way a forest is a rounding error on the number of leaves.

The important consequence: *the food is on the outside of things.* It does not fall, it does not grow on branches, it is not concentrated anywhere. It is a skin, spread evenly over the whole interior of the world, renewed at a rate of a few grams per square metre per year. Nothing here can be a hunter of concentrations. Everything must be a grazer of surfaces, or a grazer of grazers, and all of it must be slow.

## The standing growth, which is not plants

The fluted columns are farms. Each one is a sheath of firm cartilaginous tissue that does nothing photosynthetic and nothing chemosynthetic either; what it does is present area. It grows a pelt of the film on its outer flutes, grooming it with a slow ciliary creep you can feel if you leave a hand still on the surface for a minute — a faint upward crawl, like being licked by something with no tongue. It eats its own pelt from underneath. A column is an animal that has solved the problem of farming by becoming the field.

The hanging curtains do the same in the vertical, and they are better at it, because the wall-water crawls down them and delivers fresh chemistry continuously. Parting them with your forearms, you are harvesting; you will find your sleeves grey with the crop and faintly sweet when they dry.

The rosettes on the floor close when touched, and the closing makes a sound, and the sound is the point. They are not flinching. They are the only organisms here that speak on purpose into the sound of the bowl, and what they say is a single click that means *something is standing on me.* Over the course of a night, a walking body drags a line of clicks across the floor of the hollow like a spark drawn along a fuse, and everything that hunts — everything — knows where you are. The rosettes are paid for this. What comes to investigate leaves droppings.

## The ones that see in heat

The moths are not present most of the time. In the ordinary years the air is empty, as you found by walking two hundred paces with your arms out, and the moths are in the peat as larvae, chewing root-mat at a rate of perhaps a gram a year, lying there for twenty or thirty years with their metabolisms almost stopped, waiting for a chemical signal that a flowering is coming.

When it comes, the mat produces them by the thousand within a week.

An adult has no eyes in any sense you would credit; it has two hooded pits on the head, and along the leading edge of each wing a strip of pits finer than the ones on a viper's face, and with these it reads the thermal shape of the hollow to a resolution of about a hand's width at ten metres. What it sees: warm columns faintly, warm walls, the warm floor, the cold air pouring over the rim and falling down the north side like a river, and — during a flowering — the advertisements. A warm ring with a warm spot in it. A slow pulse. A three-beat.

They signal to each other the same way. A courting male runs a pattern down his own thorax, a rippling heat-script, at a cost so high it shortens his life measurably; a female answers by warming one wing and not the other. It is a bright, gaudy, wasteful display, and it is being conducted continuously all around you at a distance of half a metre, and you are standing in the middle of it blind as a stone.

They will not go near the four standing posts. Not fear; vertigo. The post takes infrared as readily as it takes light, which means that to a pit-organ the post is not a cold object — a cold object is legible, a cold object is *information* — the post is an absence of any signal whatsoever, an edge past which the world does not continue. A moth that strays within a metre finds its map ending in mid-air and does the only sane thing, which is to stall, drop, and crawl away through the mat.

## Sound is not speech here

This is the thing that must be understood before anything else in the bowl makes sense.

The reservoir is saturated. It has been saturated for a very long time. In a bath of accumulated noise at that level, no animal can be heard *now* — a call does not stand out from the background, because the background is made of calls. Real-time acoustic communication is not merely difficult here, it is unavailable, and has been unavailable for as long as there has been anything to communicate. Nothing in the bowl talks.

What the reservoir does instead is serve as illumination.

It is even, it is omnidirectional, it comes from the walls and the floor and the air, and any solid body standing in it casts a shadow — a small, sharp, local reduction where an object absorbs or blocks the diffuse field. That is what the animals of the hollow read. Not echoes; echoes are useless in a space where every reflection has been folded back into the mean a thousand times. They read *occlusion*. A column is a quiet place shaped like a column. A frog is a quiet place the size of a thumb. A person is a great silent slab moving slowly, and can be located at twenty metres by anything with ears, and is the loudest thing in the bowl precisely by being the most silent.

You can approximate the experience. Cup both hands behind your ears and turn slowly in place, and somewhere in the roar you will feel rather than hear a thinning off to your left, and if you walk carefully towards the thinning you will put your hand on a column. Do it fifty times and you will start to be able to do it. Do it for six months and you would begin to be a resident.

## The frogs, and what they are doing

One in ten square metres, unpigmented, semi-transparent over a dark gut, eyes reduced to two thermal pits and a vestigial lens that has not focused an image in half a million years. They eat moth larvae in the peat, and mites, and the droppings the rosettes are paid with.

A male calls perhaps four times a night. Given the density, he will go his whole life without ever being within earshot — in any normal sense — of a rival.

He is not calling to be heard tonight. He is making a deposit. His call, a single hammered metallic syllable pitched precisely into the narrow band that the bowl's geometry preserves best, will circulate for nine to twelve days before it loses structure, and a female moving through the hollow at any point in that window is moving through it. She is not sampling males. She is sampling the bath: she assesses the density and the durability of male syllable in the ambient, and settles in the region where it is thickest and least degraded, and calls once, and waits, and whoever finds her by occlusion within the next day or so has won.

Consider what this selects for. Not loudness — loudness fades at the same rate. Not complexity — complexity smears first. It selects for *persistence*: for a call shaped so exactly to the resonance of a worn-out mountain that it decays more slowly than its neighbours. And because a nine-day call outlives the average interval between a frog's death and his next scheduled call, a certain fraction of the chorus at any given moment belongs to animals that are already dead, and their late-arriving reproductive success is not negligible.

The frogs of this bowl have been selected, for an unknowable number of generations, on the durability of their afterlives.

## The small hunters

In the curtains, hanging head-down with eight legs spread across four separate strands, are animals about the size of a fingernail that have no ears in any recognisable place — the strands themselves are the ear, and the animal reads them the way a spider reads a web, except that what it is feeling for is not a struggling insect but a *dip*. A moth passing on the other side of the curtain casts a brief quiet shadow, and the shadow arrives at the eight legs at eight slightly different times, and that is sufficient to aim a strike through a gap you could not put a pencil through.

In the peat there is a long, limbless, pale thing, arm-length, which hunts frogs by the clicks of rosettes and has never in its life encountered any other kind of information. It will cross the entire bowl on a line of clicks. It has been recorded following a human across the floor for forty minutes at a distance of two metres, patiently, having concluded something about the size of the meal.

Nobody has named any of these. Naming would require seeing them.

## The one large one

There are three. There may be four; the third and fourth have never been distinguished with confidence, because a body identified by its acoustic hole is identified only as a hole.

Shoulder-high at the hip, long, low, heavy in the hindquarters, with a broad soft foot that spreads under load so that it compresses the mat rather than tearing it. Hairless; wet; warm to the touch, though barely — its core runs about a degree above the bowl and it is the most efficient animal in the world, because it has to be. Its heart beats every four or five seconds. It moves perhaps thirty metres in a day, and stands still for the rest of it. It crops columns and shears pelt off the curtains with a flat plate of a tongue, taking maybe a kilogram a week.

It does not echolocate. It navigates by three things: by occlusion, like everything else; by scent, which in a still warm hollow stratifies into usable layers; and by the depth of the mat under its foot.

That last one is the beautiful one. It walks its own paths, and its paths are compressed, and the mat recovers at a centimetre a decade, so the floor of the bowl is a raised relief map of where this animal has been, graded by how long ago. It knows a fortnight-old trail from a ten-year-old trail through the sole of its foot. It grazes the hollow in a rotation with a period somewhere near a century, and it does not overgraze, and it has no idea that it is farming; it simply prefers the taste of ground it cannot remember. Its memory is not in its head. Its memory is the floor, and the floor keeps about three hundred years of it, which is roughly how long the animal lives.

They meet rarely and without much ceremony. The whole reproductive event, from first contact to a weaned calf, takes something over four years.

## The whistle

Once or twice a night, one of them whistles: a long descending tone, six or seven seconds, enormously loud.

It is not a call to the others. It is a flare.

For the duration of the whistle and for perhaps eleven seconds after, the reservoir is overloaded — flooded far above its saturated mean — and every shadow in the bowl deepens and sharpens at once. The animal is lighting the room. In those seconds it gets a full structural read of the hollow ahead of it: columns, curtains, walls, the other large ones, you.

And every single creature in the bowl freezes, because for eleven seconds the world has become legible, and legibility is the most dangerous condition available here. The curtain-hunters lock. The frogs stop. The rosettes clamp. The moths drop into the mat. Then it fades back down into the general roar, and after a moment everything resumes, and this happens twice a night, every night, and has happened every night for longer than the notch has been lined.

## What is underneath

Dig down through the mat — and you should not, but it has been done — and you pass peat, and more peat, and at about a metre and a half you reach something warm that is not rock.

It has a lattice structure at the scale of a knuckle. It is not eroded. The seep comes up through it, loaded, and the film lives directly on its surface in a stack twice as deep as anywhere else, and the whole trophic column of the hollow — the columns, the curtains, the moths, the frogs, the pale hunter, the three or four great slow gardeners walking their hundred-year rounds — rests on whatever that thing is still exhaling.

By touch, it is not determinable whether it is machinery or a body. Both would feel the same after this long. The engineering above ground hosts nothing at all, and is the only clean surface in the world; the engineering below ground is the source of everything that lives, and is thoroughly, gloriously infested.

## And, briefly, you

There are two scars near the standing posts, both the size of a person kneeling with a lamp. Someone came here and knelt and made light and killed forty years of mat, and then presumably went away, and the bowl absorbed the incident the way it absorbs everything.

The structured part of a sound lasts a week or two in the reservoir. Nothing that was said here is still here as itself. But energy does not leave the bowl except very slowly through the notch, and that broadband hiss under the chorus — the one you took for insects, or for water — is what every call, every click, every whistle, every word ever spoken in this hollow has been ground down into. It is the reservoir's floor. It is made of ruined speech.

There is one exception, and it is worth knowing before you go.

The frogs learn their syllable. They do not inherit it; they copy it, from the persistent portion of the bath, in their first season. And somewhere in the hammered metallic note of the local population — you will not pick it out, but it is there in the recordings, and it is there in the recordings from ninety years ago, and from two hundred — there is a stop-consonant and a vowel that no frog anywhere else in the world makes, and that no plausible process of the bowl would produce.

It is very likely a fragment of a name. Somebody knelt here with a lamp, and burned a hole in the floor, and said something out loud, and the bowl kept the habit long after it lost the reason, which is the only thing this place has ever been good at.

So: do not bring light. And be careful what you say.
