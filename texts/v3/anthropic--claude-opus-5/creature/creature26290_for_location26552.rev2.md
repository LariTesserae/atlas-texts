## The count

If you could take a census of the basin — and you could not, because most of it is smaller than the word *animal* and the rest of it would not hold still — you would find that the overwhelming majority of the population has no body plan at all. Everything with legs is a minority. Everything with a face is a rounding error.

But start where a hand would start, with the things that have edges.

## The masonry

The mussels are the citizens in the oldest sense: they hold the property.

Each is a palm-length, thin-shelled, the colour of wet ash. They do not feed. They have a gut, technically, run down to a thread, kept the way you keep a door you have bricked over. What they have instead is gills — enormously overbuilt, folded and refolded, and packed so densely with bacteria that the tissue is grey-green and heavy, like liver. The mussel's whole occupation is to sit with its shell parted two millimetres, on the exact line where brine-breath meets oxygenated water, and let the two touch inside its own body. It is a wall built around a chemistry. It holds a seam.

They grow into each other. A mussel settles on the shell of its grandmother and cements there, and when it dies its shell stays, and the bed thickens by a few millimetres a decade until it is a metre and a half of calcareous rubble, honeycombed, groaning faintly under its own weight, riddled with the accidental architecture of a hundred generations of closed doors. The living layer is only ever the top two centimetres. Everything beneath is the city.

They are not slow so much as finished. A mussel here can live a hundred and forty years and spend all of it within one shell-length of where it landed. What passes for its life story is a record of the brine: a good decade, when the channel ran full and the bed was fat, is written as a band of thick shell; a bad one, when the river shifted sixty metres east and the whole district starved, as a crowded scar. The bed at the north lakeshore has three such scars. The animals living on it now have no way of knowing this and the information survives anyway, in the wall, under them.

The front rank — the mussels actually on the margin, half in the poison — grow fastest and die soonest. Every generation produces its volunteers.

## The towers

The worms do not move and do not eat and have no mouth, and they are the largest living structures in the basin.

A tube is white, flexible near the crown and stone-stiff below, ringed with growth-bands like a stack of collars. From the top, when the water is right, the animal pushes out a plume — a feathery, blood-heavy fan, scarlet in a light that has never fallen on it. That plume takes oxygen from the sea. Two and a half metres below, in the mud, the same animal's root — a long pale tail buried like a taproot — takes sulphide from the sediment. The worm's body is the pipe between them. Inside it, in an organ that takes up most of what would otherwise be a stomach, its bacteria do the rest. It has no more idea of the brine than a chimney has of a fire. It is simply plumbed to it.

They grow a millimetre a year and they do not stop. Fifty years to reach a knee, three hundred to reach a shoulder, and the great stand at the western terrace has individuals that were laid down before the landslide that made the terrace. Where they crowd, their tubes fuse and buttress and lean, and the leaning ones are held up by the ones they lean on, so that a colonnade is a single load-bearing argument that no member of it is aware of making.

Touch any tube anywhere along a stand, and every plume in reach of the shock withdraws at once — a fast, silent, unanimous flinching, the whole arcade going bare in a half second and staying bare for a quarter of an hour. It is the nearest thing the basin has to a public event.

## The sleet

The shrimp are the noise. They are also, in the accounting, the money.

They are small, translucent, and blind in the ordinary way — but not in the interesting way. Each carries on its back, under the shell, a pale patch of tissue the size of a seed, wired to the brain by a nerve thicker than its own gut. In an ancestor, on a ridge somewhere with hot vents and a glow, that patch saw. Here there is nothing to see and there has been nothing to see for as long as there has been a basin, and the shrimp build it anyway, every one of them, at a cost, generation after generation. It is the only monument in the place, and it commemorates light, and nobody knows.

They graze the mats in sheets. Ten thousand to a square metre, moving together, scraping with their forelimbs at the bacterial rind and putting up that ceaseless fine crackle that is the basin's floor of sound — unlocatable, continuous, everywhere at once, the reason a careful animal can cross a shell apron if it is patient and the reason a careless one still gets heard. When a section of mat is spent the whole sheet slides, and the sliding is perceptible as a change in the texture of the dark.

Everything eats them. Their entire ecological function is to convert bacterial slime into something with a shell on it that can be chased. They are eaten as larvae, eaten as juveniles, eaten by the crabs and the eels and the octopuses and the sponge and by each other, and there is no year in which there are meaningfully fewer of them.

## The mob

The squat crabs are white, hand-sized, and stupid with numbers.

They live on the tower bases and the shell aprons, thousands to a stand, scraping bacteria off the worms' tubes with a delicacy that looks like grooming and is nothing of the kind. They have no fear, because fear is a calculation that requires you to matter, and no individual in a crowd of four thousand has ever matter. They are eaten constantly and continuously and they do not react to it. A crab will keep scraping with an octopus arm closing on the crab beside it.

At a fall they go mad in the only direction available to them: they climb. Every vertical surface in the basin acquires a moving white skin, and the towers bend under the weight, and for about nine days the streets are impassable and the eels have nothing to do but open and close.

## The engineers

The ghost lobsters are the only ones who keep the city running, and they do it selfishly.

Each is forty centimetres, chalk-pale, softened, with claws too thin for fighting and antennae longer than the body. Each digs one gallery in the compacted ooze, with two mouths — one flush with the floor, one raised on a turret of packed mud — and the raised mouth pulls the sluggish current across itself and drags water through the tunnel without the animal doing anything at all. Ten thousand of these, across four kilometres, and the entire under-city breathes: the passages in the mussel-rock draw, the deep mud stays oxygenated a hand's depth further down than it has any right to, and warm exhaled plumes rise out of the chimney-mouths carrying the taste of everyone inside.

They do not tolerate each other. A gallery is territory for life, defended by ownership rather than violence, since two lobsters meeting in a tunnel will simply back away from one another for as long as it takes.

And yet they talk. A lobster braces in its gallery and works its abdomen, and the draft changes — a pulse, a slackening, a long slow pull — and three galleries away another lobster feels that change arrive on its antennae as a shift in the water it is sitting in. Ownership. Alarm. Readiness. The state of the brine. It is real language, in the sense that it is arbitrary, learned in the first year, and locally accented. It is also completely indistinguishable, to anything else in the basin, from weather.

## The wall

An eel is a hole with an opinion.

They lie in the mussel-rock, one to a chamber, head out, gill-slit working, evenly spaced along the face of a bed like courses of brick — and they are spaced that way because each has driven off everyone within a body-length, once, years ago, and been left alone ever since. The face of the old bed at the lake's north shore holds two hundred and eleven of them and has held roughly that many for longer than any of them has been alive.

They are grey, loose-skinned, mouth to mid-body. The eyes went first: two beads under the skin, still there, no longer connected to anything worth connecting to. What an eel has instead is a chemical patience — it lies in its hole tasting the water going past and does nothing, for days, for weeks, and then does one thing.

The trouble is that they grow and the rock doesn't. An eel that has held a good chamber for fifteen years has grown to fit it and then grown past it, and the mussels have sealed and thickened the doorway from outside, and at some point the animal can no longer come out the way it went in. Some can reverse — the rock is riddled, there are back ways — and some cannot, and simply stay, and thin, and go on tasting the water until the water stops mattering. Their chambers are the most contested real estate in the basin, and they are contested for about a day, and then they are quiet again for another fifteen years.

At a fall they come out. It is the only time. The wall opens and two hundred grey ribbons pour into the streets at once, and it is the single most frightening thing that happens in this place, and it happens twice a year, and everyone who survives it forgets.

## The corners

The octopuses are the ones who could tell you about it.

They are small — mantle the length of a finger — thin-skinned, and the colour of nothing. The skin still works. It ripples and mottles and throws fine texture across itself constantly, all the gorgeous machinery intact and firing into an absolute dark, at no one, forever. It is not communication and it long ago stopped being camouflage. It is closer to muttering.

They take corners with two exits. They pave the approach with shell and claw and crab-carapace and the small stacked vertebrae of fish, and the pavement is a floor and a doorbell, because nothing crosses broken shell in the dark without saying so. Inside, in the held dark, the octopus keeps the only map in the basin: where the brine line lies this season and where it lay last, which chamber has a lobster and which has a vacancy, which stand of towers the crabs are working, how long it takes to reach the third chimney from here and what it smells like when something is wrong with it. This is the animal the basin's description was talking about when it said *remembering*. It is also why octopuses are short-lived. You cannot afford that much apparatus on this diet for long.

Between falls, when the streets go thin, they eat the neighbours, and they are careful about it, and they remember which neighbours they have eaten and do not go back for a while.

A female's last act is the only intentional construction in the basin: she takes a chamber, walls its mouth with shell and rubble from the inside, hangs her eggs from the ceiling in strings, and stays there fanning them, not eating, for something like a year. By the time they hatch she has come apart. They leave over her. That is the whole architecture.

## The two oddities, and the pickled

The white meadow on the lakeshore is one plant. Eight hundred years of runners under the mud, a single buried clone three hundred metres across, older than the towers, feeding through the bacteria in its roots and holding, in its cells, the entire intact and unusable machinery of photosynthesis — every gene, switched off, copied faithfully into every new blade. It leans all together when the brine breathes.

The dish sponges on the tower crowns look like alms bowls and are not. Their inner surface is hooked. A shrimp settles into one to rest and is held, and over the following week is quietly dismantled where it lies, and the sponge does not move during any part of this.

And in the channels themselves: the pickled. A crab that walked into the brine eleven years ago and is still standing in the attitude of walking. A fish lying on its side in a metre-deep river, unrotted, unvisited, perfect. They are the only figures in the basin that keep their shape, and they keep it indefinitely, and nothing in the city has ever done anything about them except taste them from a body-length away and turn.

## The mouth

Four or five times a night: one dull thud, from nowhere.

It is a fish, pale as a peeled root and nearly three metres, hanging over the towers with its fins doing almost nothing. Nine of them, perhaps, in the whole basin; no one has ever recorded two in the same place. It does not hunt. It stations itself above a chimney-mouth where the lobster galleries exhale, and it reads the plume — the whole under-city's news arriving in one warm column — and when something in that news comes close enough, it opens and the water does the rest. The thud is the closing.

Nothing about it is stealthy. It doesn't need to be. The sound arrives everywhere in the basin at once, carrying no direction, and every animal down there has heard it since larvahood and has arranged its entire behaviour around it without ever locating it.

It is, functionally, the clock.

## The tenants of the tenants

And under all of it, which is to say inside all of it: the actual population.

Cells. Nothing else. In the mats, in the mussels' gills, in the worms' hollow bodies, in the roots of a seagrass that has forgotten what it was for, in the mud, in the pickled dead, in the fine sediment that has passed through a hundred thousand guts and is largely composed of the guts' former passengers. They are what eats the brine. They are the only thing in the basin that has ever made a living directly.

Everything with edges — the masonry, the colonnades, the wall of eels, the ventilated galleries, the shell aprons, the mouth in the dark — is scaffolding the cells built to stay near the seam without falling into it. Six hundred metres of warm water, four kilometres of flat grey plain, a river that kills, a city that never stops, and none of it is for the animals.

They are the housing.
