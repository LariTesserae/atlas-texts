## Who is down there

The honest answer changes depending on how long you can stand to be in the room, and nobody has ever been able to stand to be in it very long. So the census is stratified by endurance. Each layer of it contradicts the one above.

**At three seconds** — the length of a thought before it is wiped — nothing lives in the Ninth Throat. This is not a poetic claim. It is what the room reports about itself, over and over, to everyone who goes down, each time as new information: *there is nothing here.* Brilliant water, white blades, no shadow, no motion, no company. You discover it, lose it, discover it again. Six or seven times a minute the chamber is empty for the first time.

**At three minutes** you see the grazer.

The tenders call it the *lisk*, and they are wrong about it in an instructive way: they think it lives there. It does not. It comes up through the drain.

Eight millimetres, transparent enough that what you actually see is the dark thread of its gut, full of pale scrapings, hanging in the water like a stitch. No eyes. No usable antennae. Nothing anywhere on it that could register a sound — no statocyst, no chordotonal organ, none of the little tuned hairs by which its relatives elsewhere hear the world. It is not deaf in the way the tenders become deaf. It is deaf the way stone is deaf, constitutionally, and this is the whole of its qualification for being here.

But it is not indifferent. Put a lisk in the chamber and watch it, if you can hold a thought long enough to watch anything, and you will see it behave as though the room had walls that are not there. It moves along invisible corridors. It stalls, backs, sidles, finds a lane, and runs it. What it is feeling is the standing wave as a landscape of pressure — not sound but weather, regions where the water squeezes and regions where it does not — and it goes where it is not squeezed. The quiet lines. It crosses to them without knowing they are quiet, the way a beetle finds the underside of a leaf without knowing there is a sun.

It comes up to eat. The softest bloom on a growing rib, the first white fur before the mineral hardens, is the richest food in that whole mountain, and there is nothing else in the mountain competing for it. So the lisk climbs a fist-and-a-half of drain from wherever the water goes — some slower, darker, tolerable place below where they presumably breed in numbers — and grazes the loud edge, which is the only place with food, and retreats to the quiet lane, which is the only place with time, and shuttles between the two until it has eaten, and goes home.

The ones that misjudge are in the walls. Hold a fresh shard up to the light bar and you will see them: small pale commas, dozens in a hand's-breadth, a snowstorm of them, all facing more or less the same way. And their eggs, glued in tidy rows into a node-groove — a bet that the room's tuning will hold for eleven days. The room does not hold for eleven days. The gongspar is very largely made of that bet being lost.

**At three days** you would see the stripes move.

The algae — three species, four in warm years, black-green with the sullen over-pigmented look of anything that lives under too much light — cannot leave, cannot choose, cannot feel the pressure landscape at all. They simply die everywhere except in the silences, and are entombed within a week wherever they are wrong, so what survives is a set of neat dark bands tracing the nodal lines of the chamber's voice.

They are always tracing last week's voice. That is the important thing about them, and the tenders learned it before they learned anything else useful. The stripe is a memory with a lag of a few days: a spore that settled in a quiet place and had time to establish before the quiet place moved. So a tender coming down reads two maps at once — the ribs, which show where the loudness is *now*, and the stripes, which show where the silence *was* — and from the mismatch between them can tell which way the room is travelling, and therefore where the drain will be attacked next, and therefore where to cut before it needs cutting. There is no other instrument for this. Nobody has ever been able to think in that room long enough to invent one.

The algae themselves are a fire rather than a population. No individual persists. The band persists, sliding across the wall a centimetre a season, dying at its trailing edge, seeding forward at its lip, always half-buried in the record of where it has been.

**At three years** you would find the moss, which is the only thing in the Ninth Throat that lives there on purpose.

It is above the water, on the one ledge inside the air bell, in the single worst location in the known world: a hand's breadth from a falling column of meltwater, in air, where the sound has direction and the direction is *all of it, now.* It is drenched permanently. It has never in living memory been dry, or quiet, or in the dark, or alone with itself.

It is a clump the size of two palms and it is entirely well.

What it is standing on is itself. Spray coats it in mineral continuously; it grows out through its own crust; the crust thickens under the new growth and becomes a little white pillar of its own dead, and the moss rides on the top of the column it is making out of its corpses. The tenders call the whole assembly Ede's moss, after a name in the cord that nobody can now attach to a person — the knot for that year is doubled, which meant something once. The column has snapped twice in the record, in 1704 and in the lifetime of the current line's grandmother. Both times the moss went into the water and both times it was back on the ledge within a decade, or something was, green and thriving and shouted at.

It obeys the law of the place exactly. It does not wear out. It grows until it breaks, and then it grows.

**At three hundred years** the room has eleven permanent residents and they are all birds.

They did not come to live there. A snowfinch is taken by the intake, goes down the shaft with the water, drowns in the brilliance, is pressed against a growing rib by the circulation, and is enclosed. Nothing decays here — too cold, too clean, too fast. What is left is not a corpse. It is a bird-shaped part of the wall, at some stage of becoming wall: the young one with a wing still feathered and loose at the wrist, waving very slightly in the current; the one with two centimetres over its head and the beak still visibly dark through the spar like a splinter under skin; the four old ones that are only swellings with a suggestion of a folded neck, and which the tenders count anyway, and which will be gone in a century, not because they will erode — nothing erodes — but because the blade they are in will overgrow its base and snap and fall to the floor and be walked on.

There is a rule about them, and it is the only rule that is not about survival: leave the birds. Not out of reverence. Cutting the wall anywhere except the sill changes the geometry, and the geometry is the note, and the note is what everyone down there is negotiating with.

**And then there are the down-goers**, and whether they count as living there is a question the village itself argues about.

They live in the pastures, four thousand metres below and a day and a half away, in a valley with a lake that would not stay in its basin. They go up twice a year, three times in bad years. Each visit is two hours of work and a lifetime of preparation, and the preparation is the interesting part, because you cannot learn anything in the Ninth Throat. You cannot be taught there. You cannot be corrected there. Nothing said there is heard and nothing thought there survives.

So they built a second Throat in a barn: a dry mock-up of the sill in dressed timber, correct to the centimetre, in a room where a man beats a drum with a felted mallet against the sounding board of the wall while children work the mock-sill blindfolded until the eleven strokes are entirely below thought. What goes down the shaft is not a person who knows how to cut the drain. It is a pair of hands that know, attached to a person who will be absent for the duration. Counting is possible — a number is short enough to survive the wipe — and so the whole of a tender's inner life in that chamber is an integer, rising.

Their bodies keep the room. Every one of them, by forty, has a notch in their hearing exactly where the chamber sings, and a second at its first harmonic: a precise negative cast of the Ninth Throat, worn into the ear, one visit at a time. The oldest of them cannot hear the note at all. They feel it in the forehead and the jaw, and they say — with the mild vanity of the very deaf — that this is the better way.

Which is why the archive is kept by a child. Once a year a child of nine or ten goes down the shaft for no reason except to listen, is held at the foot of the ladder for the count of thirty, is brought up, and hums. An elder who cannot hear the hum tightens a gut string across a bow until the string's shiver matches the child's throat under two fingers, and the length is knotted into the cord. Thirty-one generations of knots. A fall of roughly a minor sixth.

There is a minority view in the village that the mountain has not descended at all — that what the cord records is the slow flattening of a family's memory as it carries a note up forty metres of ladder, generation after generation, each child hearing a truth and delivering an approximation. Nobody can settle it. Both possibilities are the same shape: something enormous quietly getting away from the people who are trying to hold it.

**One of them stayed.**

Eighteen centimetres of gongspar, kneeling, the socket of a chisel handle still open in the region of the right hand like a keyhole. The cord says he was called Ienn and that his mistake was not letting go. He is the only full-time human resident, and he is treated as staff. Of the eleven hand-signs, the one that means *Ienn* is also the one that means *you have stopped moving*, and it is made large and slow, twice, and if it is made at you a third time you are taken up whether you have finished or not.

They touch him going past. Never pausing. A hand on the shoulder at speed, on the way to the sill.

**But none of these is what lives there.**

Every living thing in that chamber is a commuter. The lisk comes up from the drain to eat and goes back down. The algae die and re-seed and are, considered as a band, only moving. The tenders come up from the pastures, spend two hours being nobody, and leave. Even the birds are in transit; they are simply slower than the rest, and travelling in the other direction, from animal into geology, at a rate of about a centimetre a decade. Residency in the Ninth Throat is not a way of life. It is a synonym for having failed to leave.
