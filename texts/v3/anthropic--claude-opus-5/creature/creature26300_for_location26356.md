# The People of the Chamber

Begin with the hands, because everything they are is in the hands, and because the hands are the only part of them that is ever fully still enough to look at.

They are small people. Not dwarfed, not deformed — proportioned normally and simply built at a smaller scale, the way a creature is built that has never in the history of its line had one calorie more than it required. A grown man stands about as high as a surface woman's collarbone. He weighs perhaps as much as a large dog. His resting pulse is slow enough to alarm you. He moves without haste of any kind, not out of ceremony but because haste is expensive and there is nothing here to be early for, and this unhurriedness is so total that a newcomer would read it as sedation, or as grief.

They are pale, but not the flat pale of the unlit. They are the colour of something kept in water: a bluish translucence at the wrists and temples, the veins legible, the fingernails almost clear. They are also greased. Every adult keeps a film of rendered crustacean fat worked into the skin from the ribs down, because to be wet without grease is to rot, and this grease catches the wall-film, which colonises it, which means that a person's back — the one part they cannot reach to scrub — carries a faint growth of the same pale glow that lights the chamber.

So they are lit from behind. In the corridors of noise you see your neighbours going away from you as soft green-white shapes and coming toward you as holes in the dark. Children are scrubbed by their mothers and glow least. The very old, the grieving, the ones who have withdrawn, let it thicken until their shoulders are luminous as a wet stone, and this is understood, and no one is made to wash.

---

**Their ears are notched.**

Every one of them, from about the fourth year on, is deaf in a narrow band — precisely the band the fall occupies. It is a physiological hole cut to the exact shape of the world's one sound. They do not hear the roar. They feel it in the sternum, as the text of their bodies, but the ear has long since folded that frequency out and thrown it away, and what remains is a hearing that is not weak but *strangely shaped*: acute in the low knocking register of the clay bells, acute in the thin ring of stone on stone, acute in the wet slap of a foot placed wrong, and blind in the middle where the water lives.

Infants have not yet made the hole. For the first months a child hears the chamber as it actually is, and screams, and cannot be comforted, and is carried and carried, and then one day stops. This is the first passage of a life and it is unmarked, because to mark it would be to notice that a person has changed.

The old go the other way. Past a certain age the notch widens and takes the rest with it, and they enter a true silence that no one else in the world will ever experience. It is not considered a loss. The deaf-old are the ones sent to work the eastern Screens directly under the Gutter, in the full weight of the noise, because sound-fatigue costs them nothing; they can stand in that hammering for a full shift and come out rested. The strongest young bodies cannot last a third of it. So the frailest do the loudest work, and the sight of some ancient stick of a woman standing calm in the pulverising roar, wrist-deep in the coarse screen, sorting drowned beetles with her thumbs, is the most ordinary sight in the chamber and also its most exact self-portrait.

And every single person here, from the age of six or so, carries a private waterfall inside the head. The ringing. They all have it; it is universal; and having no reason to think of it as damage, they think of it as *theirs*. A child is asked, at some point, what their fall sounds like, and describes it — high and thin, or a wasp, or a wire — and is told the pitch of their mother's, and their grandmother's, and this is the closest thing the chamber has to a soul. It is the one noise in the world that differs from person to person. It begins when you begin. When you die it stops. Nobody has to be taught this; it is simply the obvious meaning of the fact.

---

Their feet are broad, splayed, with toes that spread like fingers, and the soles are permanently white and corrugated, macerated past any recovery, thick as horn at the heel and tender in the arch. Foot rot is the second commonest death.

Because of this, the tending of feet is the central intimacy of their lives. Every sleep, two people sit on the raised threshold of a house with their legs in the water and take each other's feet into their laps, and go over them by touch — between the toes, the arch, the heel-cracks — cleaning, greasing, pressing. It takes a long time. It is done in the dark, silently, in the roar, and it is the thing they mean by love. A pairing is not marked by any word or ceremony; it is simply that from a certain time onward these two tend each other's feet and no one else's. When one dies the survivor's feet begin to go, visibly, and everyone can see it, and the survivor is quietly reassigned to someone — a widow, a cousin, a child of the house — and the arrangement is called by the same sign whether it is passion or charity, because in this chamber those two things have exactly the same shape.

They shuffle. They never lift a foot clear of the water. Partly to feel the floor, which talks; partly because a lifted foot returning makes a slap, and a slap carries; mostly because a lifted foot stirs silt, and stirred silt goes into the Screens, and fouling the Screens is the nearest thing to violence they have. A running person is an emergency, and there is one signal for it, and it is the only signal that everyone in the chamber will obey without asking what happened.

---

They have four languages and one of them is spoken.

The **cords** are the public voice: pull, count, and pattern, ringing a fired pot in a distant room. Everyone can read perhaps forty cord-messages. Water at the third screen. Come. Come now. A birth. A death. Tally short. This is a network of announcements, not of conversation; you cannot argue on a cord.

The **floor** is the near voice: heel-taps on the resonant slabs, felt through the water by anyone within a dozen paces. It carries almost nothing but tone — attention, refusal, assent, the equivalent of a cough or a grunt — and it is what a Screen crew works by, six people in the deafening dark saying *yes*, *stop*, *again*, *mine*, entirely with their heels.

The **hands** are the true language, the one with grammar, and it is asymmetrical, because in this chamber a person almost always has one hand occupied — a lamp, a pot, a rope, a child. The right hand carries the sentence. The left, when it joins, does not add words; it adds *weight*. To sign two-handed is to have set down whatever you were holding, and that means: I have stopped work for this. Oaths, apologies, confessions of debt, declarations of love are all two-handed, and cannot be otherwise, and a two-handed sentence delivered with one hand still full is not merely rude but formally void. There are old people who have signed two-handed a dozen times in their lives.

And because the hands live in the dark, all real conversation requires a lamp — a thumb of crustacean fat, a wick of white fibre, a light barely enough to show a palm at arm's length. To strike a lamp for someone is to ask them to talk. Their word for *listen* and their word for *lamp* are the same hand-shape, held at different heights.

Then there is **the voice**, which almost no one uses. It works only in the acoustic pockets, which are owned, roofed, inherited and few, and so speech is not a public utility here — it is a room. Their word for household and their word for hush are one word. To be born into a family is to be born into an owned quiet. A poor lineage owns half a niche behind a flowstone rib and speaks in it four times a year. The spoken tongue that survives inside these rooms has maybe six hundred words, all of them ceremonial or intimate or legal, and they are pronounced badly and hoarsely by people whose throats get almost no practice, in voices that crack and go thin at the ends of phrases. A man may speak aloud eleven times before he is thirty: at his naming, his adulthood, his pairing, the births, the debts, the deaths. To speak aloud is felt the way we would feel undressing. There are people who cry when they do it, and do not know why, and are not asked.

They have no names that can be said. A name is a knock-rhythm: three to five beats, given at birth, unique among the living, released when you die and reissued perhaps a generation later. You know a hundred and fifty people by the way their pot sounds.

---

The work of the world is the Screens, and beneath the Screens, the tanks, and beside both, the combing.

The comb-women work the amphibole veins in the north wall by feel, teasing out the white silk that becomes cord, cloth, net, wick — the one material the chamber makes and the substance that holds the city's nervous system together. It is a beautiful fibre. It combs out like hair. It is also stone, and stone in the lung does not leave, and so the comb-women die at about forty with a dry cough that has been coming on for fifteen years, and the fine dust of it hangs in the still air of the western quiet where everyone lives, and it thickens everyone's chest a little, and this is what they mean by old age.

They call it *going white in the chest.* They have a sign for it. They know its stages exactly, the way they know everything invariant, and they nurse it with great tenderness and no curiosity, because the cough has always been at this rate and the fibre has always been combed and **nothing in this world is permitted to be a cause of anything.** The chamber does not err. Only hands err. The idea that the great craft is the great killer would require a history in which something began — and there is no such history, there is only the long, and in the long the cough was always there.

---

The tanks are the ledger. Forty or fifty blind white crustaceans to a basin, and the whole herd would fill two pots, and every one of them is counted, and the count is the only number anyone learns to hold in their head past ten. Debts are in crustaceans. Fines are in crustaceans. A marriage transfers three. A serious insult costs one, paid to the injured party's tank, and the entire chamber knows within a shift.

And the dead go into the tanks.

Not ceremonially — or rather, that *is* the ceremony. A body is the largest single delivery of substance the chamber ever receives from any source, larger by far than anything the Gutter has ever dropped except the drowned. To do anything else with it would be an obscenity of the same order as breaking a stalactite. So the body is spoken over in the family's quiet, in the cracked voices, two-handed, and then it is taken to the western basins and given, and over the following weeks the herd fattens, and everyone eats, and no one pretends otherwise, and the grief and the fat are not held apart in the mind. They have a phrase for a person who has died recently and is currently in the tanks. It is affectionate. It is the sort of thing you say about someone who has stepped out and will be back through the door in a moment.

Children are conceived by permission of the tally. The herd sets the ceiling. A woman who is carrying wears a particular cord across the shoulders and is not asked to work the coarse screens; the birth happens in the eastern roar, in a chamber the size of a cupboard, wedged into an acoustic pocket ninety
