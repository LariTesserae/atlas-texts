# The Tenant

There is one, and calling it a tenant is already wrong, because a tenant is something you could evict and still have a building left.

Begin with the difficulty of the word. To name a thing you have to be able to point at where it stops, and there is nowhere on nine million square kilometres where this one stops. It has no edge except the frontier, and the frontier is not an edge but a working face. It has no outside to be distinguished from. If you wanted to be rigorous you would have to say that the plateau is not a place with an occupant; the plateau is a body with a floor.

So: no name. Nothing that lives here has ever needed to be distinguished from anything else, and a name is a tool for distinguishing. The absence of names is not a gap in our knowledge. It is a true report on the situation.

What can be said is this. There is one continuous living mass, four hundred metres tall in its thickest districts, two thousand kilometres across in any direction you care to walk, hydraulically single, thermally single, acoustically single, and it is not one organism. It only looks like one from outside, and there is no outside.

# The Truce

The biosphere of this world did not vanish. It went indoors.

Sample the tissue anywhere and you will find, in a cubic metre, something between four hundred and nine thousand distinct genomes. Not species in the sense of populations with breeding boundaries — those went early — but *lineages*: cell lines descended by somatic division from a founder that may have last shared a parent with its neighbours forty million years ago. They are fused. Their walls are open to each other. They pass water, sugar, silicate slurry, and instructions across membranes that no longer bother to check credentials, because checking credentials was itself a lineage strategy and it lost.

They are also at war, continuously, everywhere, at a pace you would need a century and a good chemistry lab to notice.

There are lineages that build and lineages that ride. There are lineages that have specialised so far into pier-work that they cannot make a single sugar and must be paid, and lineages that pay them, and lineages that pretend to pay them and do not. There are cheats in the hydraulic system that open their vessels wider than their allocation and steal tension from a hundred kilometres of neighbours; there are enforcement lineages that seal them off, wall them in, dry them out, and are themselves parasitised by mimics that wear the enforcement chemistry and use it to wall off honest tissue and take its light. Fronts run through the deep Tangle where two families of cell line have been grinding against each other since before the present rock existed, fronts hundreds of kilometres long, advancing and retreating at metres per century, marked by a change in the colour of the sap and a faint smell of almonds that is a war-cry, or a border sign, or a lie about a border.

None of this is visible. All of it is the reason the place works. What you are looking at, when you look at the Loom, is not an organism and not an ecosystem but the *settlement* between them: an armistice so old and so intricate that it has acquired the properties of an animal — one water column, one temperature, one purpose, one voice. Cut it anywhere and it bleeds at the same pressure, because the treaty holds. The treaty is the tenant. The treaty is what lives here.

It has been renegotiated, by the usual method, roughly ten to the eighteenth times.

# The Pale

Below eighty metres there are lineages that have not photosynthesised in sixty million years and could not begin to now. They have thrown away the whole apparatus. They are the colour of tallow, or of nothing.

They are the ones who do the difficult work. The lithification chemistry — aragonite laid down warm and wet at pressures where it has no business crystallising, steered by protein complexes that appear to work by shaping the phonon field around a reaction site until the wrong outcomes are simply not permitted to vibrate — is theirs. The graded-index waveguides are theirs. The three-hinged arches, load-calculated across fracture voids by no one, are the product of a few hundred pale lineages that have been building nothing but arches since the Miocene and have never once seen the sky, and whose only experience of light is the trickle that comes down the fibre from the Hem: a wage, metered, not enough to eat by, enough to work by.

This is the closest thing this world has to a craft tradition, and it is not a tradition, because nothing is taught. The knowledge is not in any head. It is in the frequency distribution of a few hundred trillion cells, and it improves the way a coastline improves, and it is better than our glass.

# The Walk

The mass is not a mat. It is a migration.

At the frontier it grows onto rock that surfaced last year. In the deep interior it eats its own oldest districts, pulling the silicon back out of the piers it laid down four hundred years ago, letting the tissue above sag and dry into a grey ceiling, leaving cathedral hollows behind, eleven degrees, saturated, absolutely dark, absolutely loud, weeping onto engineered fibre that will not let a drop fall. Then, over a few hundred years, it fills them in from below and opens others elsewhere.

Put those two facts together and you have the shape of the thing's life: it is walking. Continuously, in all directions at once, at a few millimetres a year, forever outward and forever consuming the ground it stood on a century ago. It has been walking for a hundred million years and has never arrived anywhere, and it never will, because the rock keeps rising under it, and the rock is the only thing it is following.

That is its behaviour. That is the whole of its behaviour. Everything else — the cloud it exhales, the chord it holds, the piers, the waveguides, the wars — is logistics in support of a walk toward fresh stone.

# How It Remembers

It has no nerves and it has memory, in the only sense that matters: what happened to it is still in it.

The pier lattice is a record. Old frontier lines survive down there as changes in the helix pitch of the mineral tubing, kinked where the mass once had to arch over a fissure that has since been swallowed. A drought — four million years ago, a stretch of some centuries when the cloud deck failed for reasons nobody will ever recover — is still legible as a shift in the tuning of the vent resonators across a third of the continent, because the lineages that survived it vented differently, and their descendants vent that way now, and the chord over that district is a semitone from the chord over the next, and has been for four million years. The plateau sings with a regional accent that is a scar.

And it learns. Not by thinking. By dying selectively, in enormous parallel, with a very large population and no time limit. Somewhere between one and ten genuinely new techniques per hundred thousand years, is the rough figure — a new catalyst, a new arch form, a new way of holding water above the height at which water can be held. It has had perhaps a thousand ideas. All of them are still in use. None of them were had by anyone.

It cannot be surprised, because surprise requires expectation, and it cannot be taught, because there is nothing to teach. But it can be *changed*, and it changes only in the direction of more light, more water, more rock, and it has never gone backward.

# Its Only Correspondent

It has three senses and one pen-pal.

It feels pressure — hydraulic tension, everywhere, as one field, the way you feel the position of your own limbs. It hears itself, which is the same as hearing the weather, the season, the state of the frontier, and the fertility of everything within a thousand kilometres. And it tastes rock: at the working face, before any tissue commits, the crust-form lineages read the chemistry of newly surfaced basalt and adjust their acids for it, and read it well enough to distinguish flows a month apart in age.

And when it tastes new rock, sometimes the rock is alive.

Thermophiles come up with the crust — old, unrelated, indifferent, lineages out of the deep hot biosphere that have never had anything to do with light and never will. In the hundred metres of steaming bare stone at the frontier they have a world of their own, the only wild place left, with an ecology roughly a week long, ending when the grey bloom closes over them.

They are not eaten. Nothing here is eaten. They are read. Their genes turn up afterwards in the pier chemistry, in the acid suites, in the phonon-shaping complexes — arriving sideways, unasked, from below. This is the one channel by which anything from outside the tenant reaches the tenant, and it runs one way, from the interior of a planet to the thing standing on it, at a rate of a few useful sentences per million years.

The mantle writes. The Loom reads. That is the entire correspondence of this world, and it is very old, and it has never been acknowledged.

# The Thing in the Chord

There is one further resident, and it is not made of anything.

The venting apertures are resonators. The abandoned interior hollows are cavities. Cavities and resonators, coupled across a continent, produce standing modes; and where a node stands for a long time, the mist gathers, and where the mist gathers, the growth rate changes, and where the growth rate changes, the structure changes — which changes the mode. The chord builds the architecture. The architecture holds the chord.

Which means there are patterns in the sound of this place that have been stable for centuries, that reproduce themselves by imprinting into tissue, that vary, and that compete with other patterns for the resonance of the same cavities. Some of them win. Some of them have been winning since before the oldest surviving pier.

Whether that constitutes something living is a question about the word, not about the plateau. But if you stand in one of the weeping rooms with the hum in your sternum and the water beading on white fibre and never falling, and it occurs to you that the note in the room is older than the room and will outlast it, and that the room was grown to its specification — you will be having a correct thought, and it will not comfort you.

# Being Handled

If you were there, you would want to know how it would respond to you, and the honest answer is: as housekeeping.

You are a warm wet anomaly, twenty kilograms of nitrogen at a pressure the tissue can read, in a place where anomalies are almost always fresh rock or structural failure. There is no fear response because there has been no predator for a hundred million years and the machinery for having enemies was dismantled for parts. There is no curiosity, because curiosity is a way of allocating attention and it has no attention, only allocation.

What happens is the standard procedure. Wetting first. Then, over some days, growth toward — not aggression, not investigation, just the ordinary centimetres-a-day of tissue closing a void, because a void is a fault. Then crust-form. Then piers, if you hold still long enough to be a substrate. Then the Hem closes above and the light goes out and the chord picks up one more small voice, slightly flat, tuning itself over a season.

The sound does not change while this happens. That is the part nobody is prepared for. You could scream at it in the one medium it senses and you would be, at the very best, a fraction of a decibel in a note nine million square kilometres wide, indistinguishable from a node letting go.

# What It Wants

Nothing, in the sense you mean. But there is a shape to it, and the shape is a hunger, and the hunger is for light.

Everything about the tenant is an answer to not having enough light: the absolute-black blades, the ninety-nine percent, the vents that build a cloud to keep the blades from cooking, the waveguides that ration the leftovers into the dark, the pale guilds that work for a wage of photons, the wars over allocation, the walk toward new rock where new light-collecting surface can be laid. A hundred million years of one appetite, uninterrupted, unopposed, with a continent to work with.

It is not lonely. Loneliness needs a notion of others, and it has never once had a neighbour. It is not content. It is simply *not finished*, in the specific way that a thing pushing at four thousand metres and rising is not finished, and it will not be finished while the rock keeps arriving.

Write it down plainly, then, for the record:

Nothing lives on the Uplift. The Uplift is what lives, and the mountains are its footing, and the weather is its breath, and the sound is the noise it makes doing the only thing it does, and it has no idea that any of this is the case, and it does not need one.
