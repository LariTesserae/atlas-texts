## First, the hands

You will not see the people before you hear them, and you will not hear them as people. From the rim, the pruning sounds like an insect — a dry, irregular *snick* multiplied into the general shimmer. Only when you are inside the green, on a platform, with someone three arm-lengths away, does the sound separate out into a person clipping the inner face of a wall with a split-cane blade held in a bent grip, one shoulder rolling, eyes not on the work.

They will look at you and the first thing they will do — before any word — is put something in your hands. A hank of retted fiber, usually, or the tail-end of a rope someone else has started. This is not hospitality in the sense of a gift. It is closer to being handed the other end of a conversation. A guest arriving with empty hands is a guest who has already announced that they are leaving.

Watch how quickly you become embarrassed by your own stillness. That embarrassment is the town's most reliable instrument. It works on children, on strangers, on the sick, on anyone drifting.

## Bodies made of this

They are short and deep-chested — the chest from the altitude, the shortness from a diet with no grain in it and a great deal of climbing. Their feet are the extraordinary part: broad, splayed, the toes fanned and independent, soles thick as bark and yet not numb, because the sole is a reading organ. They go barefoot always. Footwear rots in a season and deafens the foot in the meantime, and a foot that cannot feel the flex is a foot that will find the wrong step and go in to the knee. When someone crosses the basin, everyone within a hundred paces knows who it is by weight and cadence transmitted through peat. They will call your name before they see you. Visitors find this uncanny and then, later, find its absence lonely.

The hands are worked into a shape by fifty years of the same three motions: a hooked callus at the base of the index finger where the pruning blade rides, a flattened pad on the thumb from twisting, and — everyone, from the age of four — a smooth hairless patch on the outer thigh, polished to a shine like old wood, where fiber is rolled. Their front teeth are notched by their twenties from stripping cane-skin. An outsider's unnotched incisors look to them faintly infantile, the mouth of someone who has not yet been given anything to do.

Their skin carries populations. Damp is permanent, so fungal bloom is permanent, and they have long since stopped fighting the ones that don't itch. A pale rosette across the shoulders is considered handsome; some encourage it. Everyone smells of smoke, layered over wet leaf and warm animal. The depth of the smoke-smell is read the way we read the cut of a coat: a person cured black is a person from a good hearth, well-fed, well-attended, of consequence. Someone who smells only of the basin — green and cold — is either newly arrived, newly poor, or ill.

Hearing is the sense they cannot spare. Blindness is inconvenient; the blind prune well, since the wall is felt and heard more than seen, and old blind women hold some of the finest inner faces in the town, walls dressed so smooth that people come to look. Deafness is the real catastrophe. A deaf person cannot hear the drums, cannot hear the frogs stop, cannot hear the difference between rain arriving and something large stepping down off the ridge. Deaf people are kept close, in the inner rooms, ringed by others' noise, and are never sent along a path alone.

## What a person is

Ask who someone is and you will be told what they keep.

There are seven trunk-groups and each is not a lineage but a maintenance. You belong to the walls you cut. Children begin on outer faces — the rough, fast-growing, forgiving ones — and are advanced inward over decades toward the old dense faces near the trunk, where a bad cut shows for eleven years. To be given an inner face is the whole of promotion. The very old are given the faces of the room they sleep in, so that their work and their life are the same three walls, reachable from where they sit.

Names accumulate accordingly. A person of forty may answer to four: a child-name, a face-name, a name from some incident, a name their partner uses. The oldest woman in the town has more names than anyone can list and answers to all of them, including several belonging to people who are dead, which she considers a reasonable convenience. Ages are counted in browsings. She is fifty-one. When she says the fourteenth was the worst, she means the year her mother went into the churned peat and did not come out; when she says the thirty-second was the funniest, she is remembering a very large animal that got its shoulders into a doorway it could not get out of, and stood there for most of a day, eating the room from the inside, while the household sat above on the platform and passed comment down through the roof.

Everyone argues. Continuously, loudly, and without much residue. Nothing here can be written and nothing here is finished, so nothing is ever settled; every arrangement is re-argued each time it comes up, and this is not dysfunction but the mechanism, the way a decision is kept open the same way a room is. Consensus arrives by exhaustion, usually at the cloud-turn. There is a courtesy in their speech that outsiders find eerie: when the afternoon rain hits the roofs and the roar takes the conversation away, they stop mid-sentence, wait, and then resume on the exact syllable, sometimes a quarter of an hour later. They do not repeat themselves. They assume you were still there.

And pruning has a rhythm, and a rhythm is a signature. You can locate anyone in the basin by ear. Young people court by working their way along adjacent stretches of wall until their strokes fall into phase, which everyone within earshot notices, and comments on, at volume. Long-married couples prune in phase without trying and cannot be persuaded that this is remarkable.

## The fire people

The hearths are the real property, because fire is the one thing the basin will not simply hand over.

Nothing above ground burns. What burns is *below* — dense black root-wood dug out of the peat, older than any floor plan, resin-heavy, half-mineral, the buried limbs of trees nobody living has a name for. Cutting fuel means cutting down through your great-grandmother's post-holes and her grandmother's hearth-clay to reach a forest that was here before all of them. Nobody remarks on the floor plans. Everyone knows exactly whose they are; the old can read a fresh peat-face like a ledger and will tell you, unasked, that the dark seam at hip height is a wall that stood for ninety years and came down in a bad wind, and that the people who lived inside it were, in her opinion, tedious.

A household is founded when someone carries an ember. That is the marriage: a coal in a clay cup, walked from one trunk-group to another, hooded against the rain, at a pace between dignity and panic. If it goes out on the way the whole basin will discuss it for two generations. Households do not fail dramatically here; they cool. An unattended hearth is the visible beginning of an ending, and neighbours will feed a struggling one for months without being asked and without ever mentioning it.

## The other residents, who pay rent

The people are one tenancy among several, and they are quite clear about this.

The bats in the eaves — thousands, muttering and shuffling all day — are tenants in good standing; their guano is the entire fertility of the roof gardens, and a household that loses its bat colony has lost income. Roofs have names. The gardens on them are inherited, argued over, and stolen from.

The frogs are the alarm system, the census, and a food. They are combed out of the cushion moss in the cold hours with wooden rakes, in quantities that sound impossible, and smoked in flat mats. Nobody thinks of this as hunting. It is more like drawing water. And when the frogs stop — not at the cloud-hour, but wrongly, in a wrong silence — every hand in the basin stops with them, and every face goes up, and for three or four seconds the town is as quiet as it ever gets.

The browsers are neither guests nor enemies. They are landlords. They arrive, they eat the walls, they wreck the floor, they leave, and afterward the walls come back thicker and the churned ground grows the best cane in the basin, and the year gets its number from them. The town's entire architecture — the liftable platforms, the crown-caches, the ladders, the drum-warning — is a courtesy extended to animals who cannot be resisted and are not resented. Children are taught to climb before they are taught to walk on peat, and what they are taught is: *when the drum says so, go up, and do not look for your mother.*

The three shrub species of the walls are spoken of by name, with characters — one stubborn, one greedy, one that sulks if cut in the wrong month — and are addressed directly by people working alone. So is the hedge in general, in the tone one uses on a large, slow, well-meaning animal that is leaning on you.

## Letting go

The town's only real violence is withdrawal.

A room can be deliberately un-kept. Something happened in it that is not to be discussed; the household stops cutting, and in three seasons there is no room, and after twenty years there is not even a hollow, only thicket that people walk around without discussing why. There are perhaps nine such places. Everyone knows where all nine are.

The same is done to a person, rarely. Nothing is taken from them; nothing is announced. Simply, no one else recuts their paths. Their walls thicken; their routes close; the basin, which requires many hands to stay open, quietly stops opening for one. Within two months they cannot get anywhere. They take the western trail down. The town does not speak of them again. The peat, of course, keeps the shape of their floor, and someone digging root a century later will find it and say nothing much.

The dead are wrapped, weighted, and given to the Eye. The weight is a length of black root — the buried wood, the fuel, the one substance that will not float and will not rot and, once soaked, will not burn either. So each person goes down holding a piece of the old forest that could have been a fire and was not. Below a certain depth the water is cold and airless and returns nothing. There is no ceremony worth the name. People go, stand, hand the bundle over, and come back up the ladder arguing about something else, because the pruning has been stopped for an hour and an hour shows.

## What lives there

If you stay long enough you stop being able to answer the question cleanly.

You will have meant: the people — some hundreds of short, smoke-cured, sharp-eared, incessantly talking, incessantly twisting persons who cannot sleep more than three hours at a stretch and would find your unbroken night frightening; who own nothing but what they are currently keeping open; who count their lives in visitations of an animal that eats their houses; who have no word for *finished* and use, for house and home and marriage and self alike, one word that means *being kept*.

But their word does not distinguish very sharply between the keeper and the kept. The wall is theirs and they are the wall's. The frogs listen for them and they listen for the frogs. The bats roof them and they roof the bats. The dead sink and the older dead come up as fire. And when the cloud draws off and you stand at the rim and see the whole bowl in that shadowless generous light, moving in every layer, hissing, ticking, snicking, smoking from seven points — the honest answer to who lives in the basin is not a species and not a number.

It is a keeping. It has been going on for a long time. Some of its hands are human.
