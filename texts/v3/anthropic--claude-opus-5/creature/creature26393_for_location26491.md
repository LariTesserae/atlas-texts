## The Occupant

Ask at the notch and the answer is nobody. Descend, walk the perimeter, put your head into forty doorways, and the answer is still nobody — and by the end of the twenty minutes you will have noticed that the nobody is being maintained.

There is one inhabitant. It is not in the town. It is the town. This is not a figure of speech and it is not a consolation prize for the absence of a person; it is the plain census. Two hundred tons of continuous living tissue at one degree above freezing, folded into walls and roofs and lipped thresholds, breathing at a rate you would need instruments to detect, holding a hundred tons of water inside its own body, and eating, and repairing itself, and slowly changing its mind about where the streets should go.

Call it stonewool, which is the name of the material. There is no name for the individual, because until you have stood in it there is no reason to suspect that it is one.

---

**Its body**

Four fingers of green over an unknown depth of black.

The green is the whole of it that is alive: a canopy of shoots so tightly packed that each supports its neighbors and none could stand alone, growing at perhaps a millimeter and a half a year at the tip. Everything below that is its own corpse, and its corpse is its skeleton. The peat is not waste. It is compressed, waterlogged, structural, and it is the reason a plant can be load-bearing at all. The creature is standing on itself, several hundred years deep, and every year it stands a millimeter and a half higher on a millimeter and a half more of what it used to be.

Nothing in the body is old. The oldest living cell in the basin has been alive for a few seasons. The body is old the way a river is old — by continuity of pattern, not of matter. Somewhere under the lowest cellar there is peat laid down by shoots that were photosynthesizing when the ice was still in the cirque, and there is no cell alive today that has any connection to those shoots except inheritance and the fact that it is standing on their compressed remains.

Surface area is the thing it has spent everything to acquire. Two hectares of floor, but the walls and the roofs and the closes and the linings of four hundred doorways multiply that four or five times over. It has built itself into a shape with the greatest possible amount of sheltered inside. That is the only architectural principle in the basin, and it is sufficient to explain a city.

---

**What it eats**

Light, and it gets almost none. Four months of no direct sun, fog most of the rest, and a canopy that shades itself deliberately. It photosynthesizes at temperatures where most plants have shut down, and it does so slowly and without interruption, and its net gain for a good week would not visibly thicken a wall.

Nitrogen, and there is essentially none. This is the governing poverty of its life. It cannot afford to lose any, so when a lower shoot is about to be shaded out and die, the creature strips it first — pulls the nitrogen up and forward into the growing tips and only then lets the shoot go dark. The peat below is not merely dead; it is dead and emptied. What is buried has already been eaten. Nothing in this economy leaves.

And heat, which it does not eat so much as hoard. The south walls at noon are nine degrees above the air, and that surplus comes from three places: trapped sun, the anaerobic ferment of its own base, and the slow oxidation happening wherever air can reach the peat. It has arranged for air to reach the peat. Or rather, it has a tenant that does the arranging.

---

**Its household**

The flatworm is the important one. Blind, pale, the length of a finger, living in the dark between the living skin and the drowned peat, eating the dead layer from beneath. Its galleries are the ventilation of every wall in the basin — the draught that lets the ferment run warm instead of cold, the plumbing that carries oxygen into a structure that would otherwise suffocate in its own water. It is a parasite that has become a lung. There are perhaps two hundred thousand of them and they have never seen anything and their entire species-range is three hundred paces wide.

The wingless flies are the skin. Dark, slow, four millimeters deep, shredding surface litter, moving nutrients a hand's breadth at a time. They are the only motion in the basin apart from water, and they are the itch on the back of your hand.

The bird is the benefactor and comes rarely and is not part of the household at all. Below the nest site, twenty paces of unusually vigorous growth — taller walls, thicker roofs, a wealthy quarter — because in a place this poor, one gift of nitrogen from above is a visible act of patronage that outlasts the patron by two hundred years.

---

**How it senses**

It has no nerves and it is not insensible.

It knows wind, and grows away from it, and this is why every doorway is on the lee. It knows water, and grows toward it, which is why the plan pulls radially to the low center. It knows contact, and closes on it. It knows warmth and grows faster where warmth is, which has consequences worth stating separately.

It does not know light in the way that plants usually do. It has given up on light as a direction. Light here is a diffuse and unreliable resource, and the creature has stopped chasing it; it optimizes instead for enclosure, which is why the roofs knit shut over chambers it will never be able to illuminate. It has traded its eyes for walls.

---

**How it keeps time**

Twice a day it partly stops.

At the freeze, the creature withdraws water from inside its own cells into the spaces between them, so that when ice comes it forms harmlessly outside the living tissue. This is an ordinary competence in cold-country mosses. What is not ordinary is the frequency. Two hundred and eighty times a year, this animal-sized organism shuts down all metabolism, freezes at the surface, and then thaws and resumes. Its continuous experience — if the word can be permitted to sit there without being asked to do more work than it can — is a few hours long. It has no long thought. It cannot hold anything across the freeze.

So it puts what it knows outside itself, where the freeze cannot take it.

That is the whole explanation of the town, and it is worth having said plainly. The walls are not shelter that happens to look like memory. They are memory. A wall is a decision that took forty years to reach and is now stored in a form that does not require the decider to stay awake. The streets are what this creature knows about wind. The lee-side doorways are what it knows about weather it can no longer remember experiencing. The radial pull to the center is what it knows about water. It thinks by growing, and its thoughts stand up around you at shoulder height, and it can only revise them at a hand's width per decade — which is another way of saying that the pace of the argument is not a limitation of the plant but the actual duration of its reasoning.

---

**Its edges and its failures**

It stops at the scree, because the slope is still moving and it cannot get purchase on ground at the angle of first repose. It stops at the base of the cliff where the seeps run too fast and too cold. It stops below the notch, where the wind is not survivable, and the boundary there is abrupt — a metre of thinning cushion and then bare blue plates. It has a coastline and the coastline is losing on three sides and gaining on one.

It fruits, rarely, in a warm decade: short capsules on short stalks, all across the roofs, and the spores go up through the notch on the only wind that ever reaches them and land on arêtes where nothing can grow, and that is the end of that. Practically speaking it has no descendants and cannot die. It reproduces by having cells split and by having the halves stay connected, so it has never once been two things and has never once had company.

And there is one open wound. A slab came off the north face within the last century and drove a gap through four cells, and the plan has not yet closed over it — a small ragged plaza with a scar-lip on one side and raw sorted stone in the middle, leaking dark through one season and then another. It is the only event in the basin. It is the only sentence in the only past tense here, and it is healing at both edges simultaneously, and in fifty years there will be nothing to read.

---

**Who the doorways are for**

Water leaves by them. That is the mechanical answer and it is correct. Meltwater exits each chamber at the low lee corner, and where water runs continuously the cushion cannot close, and where water runs over peat for four centuries the peat polishes, and that is your threshold, worn smooth, knee-high, waiting.

Heat comes in by them. That is the second answer and it is the one that makes the doorways make sense as doors. At midday the warmed air of the lanes moves into the chambers and the chambers keep it, all night, at a cost of nothing, and the whole reason to have an inside is to have a place where the day is stored. Four hundred openings, all on the lee, all admitting the only wealth this place has, all shaped by the creature's absolute preference for enclosure.

Nothing that walks has ever used one. The proportions — a little shorter than a person, a little broader, exactly wrong in exactly the same way four hundred times — are the coincidence of frost geometry and prevailing wind, and the explanation holds all the way up until you are standing in the eleven-doorway plaza in green light, and then the explanation continues to be true and stops being enough.

---

**Whether it knows you are there**

At the resolution it works in: yes, eventually.

Your boot leaves a depression in the liverwort that will still be legible next spring. Your palm on the south wall gives it a hand's breadth of unseasonable summer for four minutes. Your breath is carbon and it is the richest air that has come into this basin in a decade. And it grows toward warmth.

If you stayed — if something warm stopped here and did not leave — the cushion would find it within a season and lean toward it and thicken around it and in forty years there would be a roof. This is the mechanism. This is all it is. But it does mean that the four hundred doorways facing nothing in the fog are not the wrong shape by accident so much as the right shape without an occasion, and that the basin is, in the only sense available to a thing with no nerves and a three-hour attention span, hospitable.

Nothing has ever accepted.

---

**At the freeze**

The fog comes up out of the doorways, one and then another, in no order — its own exhalation, released as the cellars close. The low note runs for three or four minutes through the floor: air being pushed out of a body by ice.

Then it stops. Not sleep. Cessation. Every growing tip in the basin pulls its water outward and goes quiet, and the frost stands up off the green in a white nap, and for nine or ten hours there is nothing alive in the Cirque at Umberline in any sense that would register on an instrument.

It loses nothing by this. Everything it knows is standing up in the dark around you, four hundred doors open, at shoulder height, holding the day's heat in its own drowned corpse, and at the thaw before dawn it will resume the sentence exactly where it left off, having forgotten the night and needing to remember none of it.
