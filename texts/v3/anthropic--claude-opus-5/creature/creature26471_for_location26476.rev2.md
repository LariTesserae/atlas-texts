# The Tenants

## I. The ones that commute

Start with the bird, because the bird is the easiest to see and the least interesting.

It is a walking bird more than a flying one: heavy-headed, short in the wing, brown in the way that mud is brown, which is to say brown enough to be four or five colours depending on the light. It does not nest in the bowl. Its nest is out on the heath above the rim, a scrape under a dwarf willow, cold and exposed and entirely inadequate, and it endures that because the bowl is a larder it does not have to share. Nothing else up here has thought to come down over the lip.

It arrives in the clear hours, drops onto the gravel apron of one of the nine, and works. It turns small stones with its bill; it wades the shallow sheets on legs that have gone permanently rust-coloured to the hock; it takes the colourless invertebrates one at a time with no apparent enthusiasm, the way a person shells peas. An adult that has been doing this for several seasons is stained up the belly feathers along the shafts, so that when it lifts a wing you see a faint orange combing, as if it had been dusted. The stain does not moult out entirely. Old birds are half the bowl's colour.

The useful thing about the bird is that it leaves between eight and ten minutes before the fissures open. It is reading the pressure, presumably, through the same part of the ear that makes your own head feel packed before an exhale, but it reads it better and earlier. If you are down on the floor and you see the bird go up over the rim in that low laboured climb, you have ten minutes to decide whether you want to be where you are.

The frogs are stranger and sadder. They are small, warm-blooded in effect if not in fact, and completely silent; the throat-flash is all they have, a pale flicker under the jaw visible for about a second in the diffused light, and it is not clear that it means anything anymore. They do not breed successfully. Spawn laid in the sheets takes a mineral film within days and the film does for it. So every frog in the bowl came up from the valley — carried in flood-wrack, in a bird, in the folds of somebody's pack — and every frog in the bowl is old, older than frogs are supposed to get, kept alive by warmth and easy food and the absence of anything that eats frogs. A population of individuals and no generations. During the inhale, when the temperature drops, they crowd the lips of the fissures, ten or fifteen of them along a single crack, shoulder to shoulder in the last of the heat, flashing at each other, and there is no way to know whether that is company or only thermoregulation with an audience.

## II. The spider

She is the first thing in the bowl that can properly be said to live here, in the sense that she could not leave and does not conceive of leaving.

She is squat, pale, long in the leg, and her eyes have gone down to almost nothing; her whole world is the inside of the skirt of the great rosette, a foot and a half of brown folded leaf-leather, wet, hot, and either white-lit or black depending on the hour. She does not build a wheel. She builds horizontal sheets across the pockets between one folded dead leaf and the next, and she builds several, at different heights, and she builds them deliberately weak.

This is the elegant part. During the exhale, the sheets bead. Condensate collects on them, sags them, and at some point the load exceeds the anchors and the whole sheet lets go and drops, dumping its water and everything that has drifted into it down into the pocket below, where she is waiting. She is not repairing damage. She is harvesting a collapse she engineered. Staggering the heights means she gets four or five of these a cycle instead of one. Between times she is doing nothing but spinning the next one, and she has six hours of roar and three of silence and two of clarity to do it in, over and over, and no reason to hurry.

The skirt she lives in is not a home so much as a city, and it is stratified. New dead leaves fold down from above each year, so the skirt grows downward; the deepest layers, where it meets the ground, are the oldest, sixty or eighty years down, and those are no longer bark. They are rind. They have been standing in the seep long enough to have gone to stone, and inside that stone are the spun sheets and shed skins and small hollow bodies of something like a thousand generations of her, held exactly as they were, at the precise angle at which they stopped. The basement of the city is a fossil bed of the city. She walks on her own ancestry, and it is load-bearing.

## III. The film

Now the actual population, which is invisible.

Everything in this bowl that is orange, or waxy, or glazed, or rinded — the flowstone in the fissures, the lip of the rim, the sheath on the bird's bone, the polish-ready surface of the mineral apron by the outflow — is not a chemical precipitate. Or it is, but it is a precipitate that has been organised. Scrape the wax with a thumbnail where a fissure breathes and you will get a slick under the nail that smells of struck matches and is, if you look at it in sunlight on the back of a wet hand, faintly banded: cream, pink, rust, a dark line, cream again. That is the film. It lives on the exhale itself — on the warmth, on the trace of sulphur, on minerals dissolved somewhere a long way down — and it needs neither light nor the plants nor anything else in the bowl, and it was here before all of them, and it is, by weight, more than all of them together.

It is the film that does the fossilising. The bowl's great trick, the thing it does to everything that holds still, is not geology. It is metabolism. The rind grows at a fingernail's thickness in a human lifetime because that is the rate at which an organism, working every hour of every day without pause, can lay itself down in layers and die into rock.

I want this understood plainly, because it makes a difference to Section VI. Whoever comes up here to have something turned to stone is not using a property of the mountain. They are hiring the oldest living thing on it.

The film does not experience the breath as a rhythm. Eleven hours is nothing. For the film the exhale and the inhale are weather, a warm front and a cold one, arriving so regularly that the distinction has no consequence. It has no stake in the interval at all.

## IV. The nine

They are individuals and should be described as individuals, though nothing about them invites it, since they are silent, effectively immortal on the human scale, and mostly indistinguishable in behaviour: fist for three hours, open hand for six.

The great rosette on the fourth bench is the oldest. Twenty feet of trunk, and the trunk makes something like a foot in thirty years, and the arithmetic is unkind to anyone who wants to feel significant standing next to it. It has been drinking steam through the undersides of its leaves since before the green stone axe was quarried. It will flower once. That is the thing about this kind of plant: it holds and holds and then one season it throws a spike out of the crown, twice its own height, sets an enormous quantity of seed that has nowhere useful to go, and dies. Nobody knows when. There is no sign, or none that can be read in less than a lifetime of looking.

The fern on the first bench is younger and behaves like something that knows it. Its croziers uncoil at a finger a week during the warm season, which in this place is scandalous haste.

The paired hollow plant, third bench, two trunks from one root, is the only member of the nine that participates in the noise. The pressure finds the hollows and they moan a fifth apart and the fifth is very slightly flat, and they have presumably been flat for two hundred years, and there is nobody to tune them.

The fifth is dead. It has been dead perhaps fifteen years. It is still standing because the base of it, up to about knee height, has been taken by the film and converted, and a plant with a stone foot does not fall over. It flowered. You can see the stub of the spike in the crown, snapped off at a hand's length and rinded orange at the break. Its leaves are all down and rotted; its skirt is gone; it is a grey pole with a stone boot, and it will still be standing in four hundred years, only shorter and harder.

And on the second bench, in the gravel, twelve or fourteen paces from the dead one, there is a rosette the size of a spread hand. Grey-furred, tight, entirely unremarkable. It is perhaps a dozen years old. It is the only new one in the bowl, and it is the child of the corpse on the bench above it, and whether it lives is not a question anything here can hurry or affect.

That makes nine, counting the dead one, which I do count, because the bowl counts it.

## V. The one who comes

Small. Old, or old-ish; the legs are good and the shoulders are finished — you can tell from the notch-cuts, which are all made at the same height and all made slightly off-square in the same direction, the mark of a hand that no longer rotates well at the joint. Comes up at the end of summer, when the snow tongue in the north crack is at its smallest and the route over the green-stone band is dry. A day and a half from the valley. Sleeps the last night outside, above the rim, in the cold, because nobody sleeps in the roar, and comes over the lip at first light, into the clear hours, into the ten-degree wall of wet heat, with the whole bowl visible and sharp and drawn.

The hands are diagnostic. Anyone who has knelt at those four cup-hollows and worked for a full exhale carries orange in the whorls of the fingertips and under the nails for a month afterwards, and in the cracks of the knuckles longer than that. Down in the valley you could pick them out at a distance of six feet.

The work, as far as the traces allow it to be reconstructed:

They bring something small and soft up with them, wrapped. They put it in the folded-bark vessel wedged in the fork of the paired hollow plant, at head height, and they leave it. The vessel is not a container of water they poured; it is a catcher. During every exhale, condensate runs down the trunks and fills it, and that condensate is the richest mineral water in the bowl, straight off the breath, and whatever is sitting in the vessel is rinded by the bowl itself, a little at each of the two-and-a-bit exhales a day, for a year, or for several.

Then they come back and take out what they left, which is no longer what they left. It is an orange lump with a shape still legible inside it. They carry it to the mineral apron near the outflow notch, kneel — there are two shallow depressions for the knees, of course there are, worn into flowstone that heals at a fingernail per lifetime, which tells you how many years of kneeling that is — and they grind. The four cups are graded. Two are coarse and open, one is tighter, and the fourth is polished nearly to a gloss and is the one that finishes. They take off the excess rind and leave a skin of it, and what comes out is something small, dense, warm to hold, pale orange, the colour of a healed burn, and permanent.

And then they cut a notch. Head height, in the skirt-bark of the great rosette, with a blade that has to be resharpened for the purpose. The skirt grows downward a little each year, so the row of notches marches slowly down the trunk and the old ones sink and blur and are finally swallowed, which whoever does this must know, and must have accepted, because they keep cutting them anyway at the only height a short person can comfortably reach.

Twenty-two are legible. Three of them were cut with a differently-ground edge, in a run, in the middle. One of them, seventeen or eighteen down, has a second short stroke across it. Seventeen or eighteen years is close to the age of the standing dead plant's death, which is close to the age of the seedling on the second bench, and I will not pretend that is proof of anything, but I will say that if you were keeping the only written record in this valley of things that happen once, you would want a mark for the year a plant flowered.

The green-stone axe on the third bench is worked on both faces and barely used, and it is lying flat with its edge away from the path, which is how a thing is set down rather than dropped. It came from the band you cross on the way up. My best guess is that it is not a tool and not an offering but a blank — brought up, left out to acquire its rind, to be collected and ground smooth in some later year by whoever was going to come next.

It has been lying there long enough to have taken the rind. Nobody has come for it.

## VI. A correction

The source of this account guesses that the visitor goes back over the rim before the roar begins, on the grounds that the roar is not something a person sits inside on purpose more than once.

The traces say otherwise.

The four cup-hollows are worn deepest on their far sides, which is the wrong side for a person who can see what they are doing and the right side for a person working by feel with the weight of the shoulder. There are two smoothed patches on the flowstone lip beside them where a hand braced. And on the second bench, out in the empty middle distance between the fern and the seedling, there is a flattening in the moss about the size of a body, facing nothing, which has been re-flattened often enough that the moss has stopped trying.

They stay. They kneel in the white and grind by touch for six hours in a noise that makes thinking impossible, and then they go and sit in the middle of the floor where they cannot see either rim, and then the bowl breathes in, and the mist goes down the fissures, and the air clears, and they are given back to themselves.

I do not know what that is for. It is not endurance; nobody is watching. It is not devotion, quite; the nine are uncut and unmarked except for dead leaf-bark, and there is no shrine, no hearth, no arrangement of stones, nothing that has been made into a symbol of anything. The most I will say is this: the bowl is the only place within four days' walk where a person can be removed from their own sight and their own hearing, entirely and safely, for six hours, on a schedule. Whatever you carried up, you can put it down in there, because in there you cannot hold anything, including yourself.

And then you carry a small warm stone home, and the stone used to be a bone, and the bone used to be somebody.

## VII. Rules

Nothing here is fast enough to be a danger to anything else. The predation that occurs — spider on fly, bird on grub — is conducted at a pace that would bore a spectator, and has been going on unchanged for so long that it functions less like violence than like drainage.

Two things in the bowl keep a record. One is the film, which keeps everything, indiscriminately, at a fingernail per lifetime, without intending to. The other is a person with a bad shoulder, who keeps twenty-two years of something on a surface that will erase them all within a century. They are using the same medium and they are not in competition, and only one of them will still be at it in a thousand years.

The plants want nothing. The bowl wants nothing. The film wants nothing you could recognise as wanting. The bird wants, in ten-minute increments. The spider wants, on a schedule set by condensation. The person wants, once a year, hard enough to walk a day and a half uphill for it, and then goes back over the lip into five steps' worth of ten degrees colder, with a stone in the closed hand, into a mountain that is enormous and cold and indifferent and has not noticed and will not.
