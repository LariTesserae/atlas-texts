## The Bedwright

The first fact of her is the same as the first fact of the room: she has left the heat behind.

Not just this shift's heat. She stopped being a warm-blooded proposition years ago, in the sense that matters here. Above the seal, in the corridors, she is cold to the touch and people notice; they take her hand in greeting and then look at their own hand afterward, puzzled, as though it had been the one that failed. Below, she is exactly the temperature of correct work. Two degrees above freezing at the fingertips, a little more at the wrist, thirty-four and change at the core, which the medical office in the upper tier has flagged four times and declined, four times, to act on. There is a note in her file that reads *acclimatized — do not correct*, in a handwriting not her own, and she has read it, and she keeps it in the same place in her mind where she keeps the stencil on the north wall.

She does not shiver. That went first, and she remembers losing it, the way you remember losing a tooth: a period of instability, then an absence you stop consulting.

She is not tall, which is a qualification for the bay, and she moves along fixed lines — the same lines, shift after shift, the seven paces to bed three and the sidestep past rack two and the half-turn at the bench, so that the dust of the room settles evenly everywhere except in four narrow corridors of floor that are polished to a soft grey shine and are the only autobiography she has left down here. Everything else about her has been either logged or forbidden.

---

She has no name below the seal.

This is not mysticism; it is procedure, and the procedure is sound. Nothing in Bay 14 may be older than its stamp. A name accrues — it gathers dates and relations and prior versions of a person, it *weathers* — and weathering is a different contract, handled elsewhere, by other crews, under a separate authorization. So below the ladder she is her course number, which changes when the bed changes, and she answers to it, and on the tally in chalk she does not sign.

Above the seal she has a name and hardly uses it. It sits in her mouth the way the water in the tin cup sits in the mouth: taking on the taste of the room, faintly mineral, faintly of the cold, no longer quite the thing it was poured as.

---

Her tempo is thirty-eight minutes.

This is the deepest adaptation and the least reversible. Her breathing is not entrained — breath is fast, breath belongs to the body — but everything above breath is: her attention, her hunger, the intervals at which she turns her head. She works eleven minutes with the drip and twenty-seven in the dry, and she has done this for long enough that on her rest days, above, in a bunk with a blanket, she surfaces every thirty-eight minutes to listen for a hum that is four hundred meters away and has nothing to do with her, and finds it absent, and sleeps again badly.

You could ask her to hurry and she would look at you. Not with hostility. With the specific blankness of a person to whom you have proposed an anatomical impossibility, like asking her to be two degrees warmer, like asking her to lay a course coarse and cloudy and call it stone. *You may not hurry the cure.* It is posted on the panel by the ladder. It is also, by now, simply the shape of her hands.

The hands are the part strangers stare at. Nails filed to nothing, because nails harbor. Fingertips gone smooth and papery from the paste, the whorls of them worn to a suggestion, so that the print-plate at the seal has twice failed to recognize her and twice been overridden by a supervisor who then wrote nothing down. Across the back of both hands, a banding: paler bands where the cuff-tape sits, darker where the cold has taken the skin, laid down in sequence, staggered, joints painted out — you could section her wrist and read it, and you would read four hundred shifts, and you would be right.

---

She does not eat below the seal. Animals eat.

That is the whole of the reasoning and it is not negotiable. The trays get their nutrient by pipette and nothing else in this room is permitted a mouth. So she comes down fed and goes up hungry, and in between she drinks the water of the room from the tin cup, because that water is authorized, because that water is her own breath returned to her through the combs — condensed, chilled, released, and by the time it reaches the cup it has been in the bay longer than she has.

That is the other thing about her, and it is the thing she thinks about when the room is nearly quiet.

She is in the rock. Not metaphorically. The combs harvest the room's breath, and the breath of the room is overwhelmingly hers; the mist-heads open, and the film darkens as it drinks, and what it drinks is the water she exhaled sometime in the previous ninety minutes, and that water goes into the pore structure and stays. There is a cliff, unbuilt, above a river in a valley that has not been surveyed, and in the third tier of it there will be eight courses of grey-green siltstone with a ripple-mark on the fourth, and every one of those courses is wet with her.

She has never written this in the log. There is no field for it.

---

There is a further incident, older than her posting, which she knows about because everyone in the understratum knows about it and nobody discusses it on shift.

Forty-odd years ago, in a bay whose number has been retired, a hair went into a bed. Human, dark, eleven centimeters, entombed between two courses in the middle of a formation already stamped and racked and gone. The bed was not pulled. There was an assessment; there is a page about it in a book somewhere with the corners gone soft; the decision was that recovery would require destroying nine hundred square meters of finished stratum and that the probability of the hair ever being found was in the range they call *acceptable*.

So it is out there. In a cliff, or a road-cut, or the floor of a dry wash, with a hundred million years stamped on it, a single human hair lying in strata laid down long before anything with hair could possibly have existed.

She thinks about this more than is useful. She tapes her cuffs. She wears the hood. She has stopped, over the years, wearing the gloves at the bed at all — gloves shed, gloves lint, gloves are for the ladder and only the ladder — and this is why they lie on the bench with the fingers curled, and why the warmth in them is honest: at two degrees, in still air, wool holds a hand for about ninety minutes, and she went up ninety minutes ago, and the room has not yet finished forgetting her.

---

The stamp.

Three-toed, child's-hand-sized, walking gait, slight drag on the left. There is a chart on the wall with stride-to-depth ratios and she consults it and it is correct, and the tracks she presses are within tolerance and will pass any review, and the drag on the left is not on the chart.

Her left hip has been wrong since a fall on a ladder eleven years ago, in a different bay, in a hurry that she was later reprimanded for. The wrongness is minor. It shows in the wear pattern of the stamp's handle, where her thumb takes up the load, and it shows in the last four millimeters of the press, where the tool comes down a fraction shallow and trailing, and it has shown that way in every track she has laid for eleven years. She noticed in year two. She has not corrected it.

So the animal — scheduled for a much later phase, a specification not yet drafted, a body that does not exist and a name in a taxonomy that has not been invented — the animal limps. It limps on the left. It will limp in the wash below the third tier and it will limp in the bedding planes of nine formations across four unsurveyed valleys, consistently, unmistakably, in a way that some patient person will one day notice and write a paper about: *pathological gait in an individual trackway, suggesting injury to the left pelvic girdle, healed*.

Healed, they will say. And they will be right, and it will be true, and the finding will be true, which is not the same as being what happened.

This is the only unauthorized thing she has ever put into the rock on purpose, and she has decided, in the way that one decides in a cold room over years, that it does not constitute contamination.

---

The tally is 216 and counts fives, and it is not shifts, and it is not courses, and she knows what it counts and will not say, and there are three candidate answers among the crews above and none of them has ever been settled, in the same way that the faint granular *shhh* between cycles has never been settled. She has settled it privately. It is the paste. It is also her blood. Cold enough, slow enough, in a room with nothing in it that eats or excretes or wanders or is ever startled, the two sounds are one sound, and she is content to be part of the setting.

---

So: who lives in Bay 14.

The nine trays live, in the narrow sense — the black moss most doggedly, being taught to leave the right traces in the right sequence; the filament-fungus thriving in its covered dish, ignored, the healthiest citizen in the room; the three fronds in the sealed jar, ahead of schedule, unlogged, which is a kind of freedom.

The pocket of water in the corner lives, and is a volunteer, and did not apply. Four centimeters deep, rust-bloomed at the rim, something pink at the base of it doing chemistry that no one authorized. She does not feed it. She does not drain that corner. Every thirty-eight minutes the drains clear the deck and that corner keeps its water, because the panels meet imperfectly, and because she has never filed the maintenance request, and because the question of contamination or progress is, formally, still open, and she intends to keep it open for as long as she is stationed here.

The animal lives, backwards, as a gait.

The one who will put a hand on the fourth course lives here too, in a sense the room is built to serve — inhabiting the future the way the tracks inhabit the past, present as an intention, as an addressee, as the reason the temperature is held two degrees above freezing the way a hand holds a bird.

And the bedwright lives here, in four polished corridors of deck-plate and a chalk tally and a tin cup and eight courses of siltstone gone wet with her breath. She is not here now. She is up in the warm, hungry, sleeping in thirty-eight-minute increments, waiting to be cold again.

Leave the lights as you found them. She will know.
