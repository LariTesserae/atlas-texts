# What Lives There

Begin with who doesn't, because the vacancies are the shape of the tenancy.

Not the spider. The web under the largest block is intact, which is the proof: a web that is being used is a web that is being repaired, and repair leaves history — mended sectors, replaced radials, the slightly different tension of a Tuesday. This one has none. It was spun in a single sitting by something that had arrived by accident, probably as a spiderling on a thread of silk that got sucked down the shaft on one of the chamber's two daily breaths, and it represents a hypothesis: *there will be flying insects here.* The hypothesis was wrong. The web is the only structure an animal has ever built in this room and it is a monument to an error, preserved in perfect condition by a place with no wind and no appetite.

Not the hand that brought the chert. We'll come back to the hand.

Not bats, not crickets, not the pale blind things that a person's imagination furnishes a cave with the moment they hear the word. Those require a delivery of food from outside — a guano economy, a stream, a wide mouth that sweeps insects in. This room has a straight pipe six body-lengths long and the width of a shoulder-span, admitting light, a handful of air, and the occasional confused spiderling. Nothing edible falls down it. A cave ecosystem is a digestive tract for the surface; this is a sealed jar.

---

Two things in the room are alive in the ordinary sense, and neither of them is the resident.

The first is the stripe. In the zone the daily bar of light can reach, there is a hard black-green varnish — lichen, and under it a smear of single-celled algae — living on a diet of a few minutes of sun per day, less on cloudy days, none at all in the weeks when the sun's declination carries the bar off the end of its wall. Consider the budget. A plant in a field is solving the problem of too much light. This organism has arranged its entire chemistry around the assumption that photons arrive in a narrow slot, once, briefly, at a known time, and that everything else must be done in the dark on stored credit. It grows at something like a millimetre a century. Its outer edge is not a random margin: it is the envelope of the sun's annual wander, the figure-eight of the analemma smeared sideways by the year and drawn slowly, over perhaps two thousand years, by a creature that has never been anywhere else. It is a calendar that made itself, and it is also the only inhabitant of the chamber that has any relationship at all with the sky.

The second is the moss in the low alcove, and it is not alive at present. Grey-brown, brittle as ash, indistinguishable from dust held together by habit. It has dried to a few percent of its water and switched off — not slowed, switched off: sugars glassed over inside the cells, membranes held apart by molecules whose only job is to stand in for water and keep the folded things folded. It is not waiting. Waiting is an experience and it is having none. When water comes — a freak year, a runnel down the shaft, a decade of unusual storms — it will resume in about twenty minutes, mid-sentence, with no sense of having been away. Whether that is in four years or four hundred is a matter of complete indifference to it, and this indifference is not fortitude. It is architecture.

Neither of these is the occupant, because neither of them made the room theirs. They are lodgers in a corner, living off a slot of light and a rumour of rain.

---

The occupant is the tree. And the tree is not up there.

What stands on the pan — squat, grey-barked, waist-high to a person, its crown clenched shut through the hot months like a fist — is not the organism. It is an organ, and a seasonal one, and the smallest part of the animal. Something in the region of nine-tenths of this creature is underground, and the great majority of that is in and around this chamber and the rock for thirty paces in every direction from it. The thing on the surface is the part that goes out into the weather, briefly, when the weather can be survived, in order to trade gas. It is held shut most of the year because most of the year the air up there is an enemy: it would take the tree's water and give nothing back. The crown is an excursion. The chamber is the address.

Get the proportions right and the pan above stops being a landscape with one tree on it and becomes what it is: the roof of an occupied space, with a single small vent sticking out of it.

---

Its anatomy, then, because it is not what it appears.

The ropes that come down the shaft and spread along the ceiling and hang in the middle air are not roots in the sense of things that drink. They are pipe. Look at the flattening where they grip the stone, the poured-wax look, the dull ochre skin: that is cork — layer on layer of suberin and wax, laid down as a waterproof jacket. The chamber's air, warm and unmoving and far from saturated, is a desert to this creature. Any naked living tissue exposed to it would lose water outward into the room until it crisped. So every root that crosses the open space crosses it sealed, unbranched, in a single committed run, hurrying in the only sense available to it — a finger's width a decade — and opens into absorbing tissue only at the far end, where it re-enters rock, or goes under the silt and explodes into that fan of white hair.

Which means the striking thing, the beautiful thing, the drapery of pale cords in the dark that would make anyone who got down here catch their breath — is transit infrastructure. It is not the tree living in the room. It is the tree *crossing* the room.

The living, drinking, working part of this organism is in the walls. Tenths of a percent, the description says, and that is right: no free water anywhere, only a film of moisture a few molecules thick lining the pores of the dolomite, held there by nothing but the geometry of small spaces, replenished at some unhurried rate from the enormous cool mass of rock behind. The chamber's real function, from the tree's point of view, is that it *exposes surface*. Eighteen paces by twelve of wall, plus the scallops and flutes and lobes that water left, plus the underside of the silt — a vast damp-ish membrane that the tree would otherwise have had to tunnel for. Water dissolved this room out of the rock a few hundred thousand years ago and then abandoned it, and the room sat empty and finished, and a tree came along and used it as a mine gallery.

---

Its senses, such as they are, are all in the tips, and there are perhaps eighty thousand of them.

Each apex is a small local instrument, running the same handful of tests. Which way is down: starch grains, heavy, settling to the low side of certain cells, and the growth on that side slowing. Is there resistance ahead: a change of pressure on the cap, and a turn. Is it damper to the left: yes, then left, at a fraction of a millimetre a year. Is there light: the room's one bar of sun, and the answer is *away*. This is worth pausing on. The chamber's single daily event, the thing a visitor would describe first — the narrow bar crossing the silt — registers on the occupant only as a place to avoid. The roots have curved away from the base of the shaft over centuries, leaving a faint clear zone around the column of grey nothing-much. The one thing in the room that arrives from outside is the one thing the resident has learned the position of and steers around.

It makes errors, and it keeps them. Those body-length cords hanging free in the middle air are guesses that did not pay. Somewhere up on the ceiling a tip left the stone, at maybe two degrees off the plane, and kept going into open space on the assumption — an entirely reasonable one, in rock — that there would be other side. There was not. They are not dead. They are simply extended into nothing, sealed, and too cheap to retract, and they will hang there, growing a hair a year into vacancy, until the tree dies. A body with no capacity to regret has no mechanism for withdrawal either. What it cannot use, it carries.

And out of eighty thousand such tests, compounded over some centuries, comes the one genuinely astonishing fact about this place: the tree is a copy of the room. Every lobe, every flute, every scallop the water left, every crack that admits a little more moisture than its neighbours — all of it has been touched, and the touching has been recorded in the only medium available, which is the shape of the toucher. There is no map of this chamber anywhere in the world. There is one description of it, in wood and cork and white hair, and it is a cast taken from the inside at a rate of a finger's width a decade, and it is accurate.

---

It does not live alone, exactly. Two other populations share the walls with it.

Threaded through the fine hair under the silt, and sheathing the tips in the pore-network, is a fungus — dark-walled, tough, unspectacular, the sort that turns up in the roots of plants living in places that should not support plants. It is longer and thinner than any root can be; it can get into pores the tree's tissue cannot enter; it hands over water and phosphorus and takes sugar in return, and the arrangement is old enough that neither party could now be described as complete without the other. Call it the tree's tongue: the organ by which the tree tastes stone.

And in the rock itself, in that same molecular film, there are cells. Not many by surface standards — a few million to the gram, which on the pan above would be a sterile reading. They are chemolithotrophs and their kin, making a living off trace iron, off sulphur, off the slow weathering of the dolomite, off gases seeping from far below. They divide perhaps once a century. A single cell down there may be older than the fig, older than the chert flake, older than any tree that has ever stood on the pan; it has spent that entire span not reproducing but merely repairing itself, patching its own proteins, running at femtowatts, declining to stop. They are the true population of this address, by number and by seniority, and the fig is a late-arriving giant that has come down and put its straws into their aquifer, and they have no opinion about it.

---

Its temperament — and I use the word carefully — follows from its rate.

It is an averaging machine. Up on the pan there are ferocious days and two weeks of frost and years with no rain and one year in thirty when the dish fills briefly and shines. None of this reaches the occupant as an event. The rock integrates temperature into a single number and holds it; the pore-film integrates rainfall over decades; the tree, drawing on both, grows at the mean and not the maximum. Good years and bad years arrive as the same increment. It has therefore never in its life experienced adversity, because adversity requires a gradient steep enough to notice, and it has arranged — or rather, has been arranged by a very long selection in exactly this kind of place — to live below the threshold at which anything is steep.

This is not patience. Patience is the endurance of a felt duration. It is something better and stranger: a body built at the timescale where the room's violence has already been smoothed away before it arrives. The chamber holds a thousand years at one temperature; the tree holds a thousand years at one speed; they are the same solution to the same problem, arrived at independently, by a hole and by a plant.

---

It has one memory.

On one of the wrist-thick ropes, a little above head height, a body-length from the base of the shaft, there is a knob — a hard asymmetric swelling, a lump of callus, the cork run thick and confused around a line where the cork once ended. The tree grew around a cut.

Somebody came down out of the heat, for an hour, a long time ago. Somebody who knew this hole was here, which means somebody who had been told, which means at least two people, once. They came down into the blood-warm dark and the room did what it does, which is to present a person's own heartbeat to them at an embarrassing volume. And they found the ropes hanging in the dark, cool, full — because a sealed root crossing a dry space is precisely a pipe with water in it — and they cut one, with a flake of dark chert already worn shiny down the middle of one edge from cutting soft things, and they drank or chewed, and they dropped the flake in the silt, and they went back up, and they never came back, and neither did anyone else.

The tree sealed the wound, which is the only thing it knows how to do about anything, and went on, and the lump is still there because nothing in this room decays and nothing here heals away. The flake in the silt is the receipt. The callus is the scar. They are the same event recorded twice, in two media, at two speeds, and they are the entire history of contact between this chamber and our species.

---

So: what lives there.

A single old plant of the fig's trade — a specialist in discontinuities, a thing that makes its living by finding the gap in the rock and moving in — which has found the largest gap for many miles and spent centuries wrapping itself around the inside of it. Most of it is in the walls, drinking a film. Part of it crosses the open air in sealed cables that do not drink at all. A fraction of it, small and grey and usually clenched shut, stands in the sun on the roof and is mistaken by visitors for the whole organism. It is bound to a fungus it could not live without and shares its water with a few billion cells that were there first and will be there after. It is accompanied, in the corner, by a calendar drawn in lichen that has never seen more than four minutes of sun at a time, and by a cushion of moss that is not currently in existence and is not troubled by this.

None of them is doing anything you could watch. The whole assembly, all of it together, consumes less power than a candle, and the room's sole sources of disturbance remain what they have always been: a few grains of silt shedding off a crack-edge, the pressure sigh in the shaft twice a day, and the bar of light coming and going across the floor with nobody in the chamber capable of seeing it.

Stand in it long enough and the creature will be entirely unaware of you. That is the correct relationship. You register, if at all, as a brief anomaly in the humidity near the shaft — the plume off your shoulders, the water on your breath, the only rain this room has had in years — and eighty thousand tips will not turn toward it, because it will be gone long before the news could travel. You are too fast to be an event here. When you climb out, the dust resettles, the callus stays where it is, the fungus hands over another molecule of phosphate, and the root advances a fraction of nothing, which is the local unit of progress, and is sufficient.
