## The inhabitants

### First, the mistake everyone makes

You will decide, in the first hour, that nothing lives here.

This is a reasonable conclusion arrived at by a fast animal using fast criteria. You look for movement and find only the vanes, which are machinery; you look for eyes and find only windows. The fish hang. The weed does not stir except where the current insists. There is a great deal of *life*, obviously, in the sense of biomass — you are swimming through it, you are pushing it aside with your forearms — but nothing that seems to be *somebody*.

Then you rest your hand on a bollard to steady yourself and the bollard is warm in a way the brick is not, and you understand, with a slowness that is itself the beginning of your education, that you have been leaning on a person for some time and that they have registered it and that they are, at this moment, halfway through deciding what to do about it. They will finish deciding tomorrow.

### The coat

Start with the green, because the green is not a covering. It is an organ, and it is also a wage agreement.

The felt on them is the same suite of organisms that carpets the brick — the black-green base mat, the finer threads through it, the chironomid tubes at the shoulders and the backs of the knees where the flow eddies — but arranged differently. On brick it grows in the dumb radial way of anything that has nowhere to be. On a tender it grows in strain-lines: whorls at the elbow, a fan across the small of the back, a fine parting down the outside of the thigh where a leg has bent, on average, once every four days for a very long time. You can read a tender's whole repertoire of motion off their coat the way you can read a river off a sandbar.

The arrangement is this. The felt receives what light the ceiling permits and does what green things do with it, and a portion of that oxygen goes not into the water but inward, through skin that has become, over an amount of time nobody in the basin has any reason to have counted, more permeable and more capillaried than skin has any business being. In exchange the tender holds still, stays warm, and — this is the part that took the longest to learn — *moves to the light*.

The benches have been walked. All of them. Over centuries the workstations of the annealing house have migrated three, five, eleven meters from their bolt-holes, following the slow wander of the tears in the lily-ceiling overhead, dragging their shafts and linkages with them on lengthened chains, and if you look at the floor you can see the tracks: broad shallow grooves polished into the tile, each one the path of one bench across four hundred years. They curve. They are the only impatient thing in the Warm Basin. Everything else here endures the light; the tenders *want* it, and they have wanted it slowly enough to move furniture with the wanting.

They do not otherwise eat. The water is thick; they stand in the drift with their mouths very slightly open; the drift is food. A tender's jaw closes maybe once a season, and it is not a bite so much as a decision that enough has entered.

### Their day

Forty-one degrees is a spur. At blood heat a body wants to burn, and burning is fast, and fast is taxed. So the central discipline of a tender — the thing that took them from whatever they were to what they are — is to be warm and *not* to spend it.

The result is a creature running its whole life at the pace the basin will carry, which is roughly one two-hundredth of yours.

A blink takes eleven minutes. A breath, if you call it that — a slow bellows of the chest that seems to be about circulation rather than air — comes around every forty seconds, which is not a coincidence: it is the vane period. They entrained to the machinery, or the machinery was built to a rhythm somebody already had. Nobody in the basin considers this an interesting question.

A day's work is a season. Reaching for a tool is a morning's project, undertaken deliberately, with the whole arm planned in advance, because a snatch would meet the water's refusal and cost more than the tool is worth. When a tender turns their head to attend to something, the turn is a commitment of nine or ten hours, and they will not undo it lightly, and so they are careful about what they choose to look at. They have opinions about this. There is a tender on the second terrace who has been facing the loom shed for as long as anyone can establish, and one at the plenum valve who has faced away from everything since the flood, into the wall, at a patch of stonewort roughly the size of a hand, and the felt on the back of that one's neck is thick and undisturbed and immaculate.

### Speech

The tick.

That crisp small dry sound near your ear, that does not echo and seems to happen inside your skull — you were right that it was a gear engaging. You were wrong about why.

To speak, a tender leans very slightly into their work, or eases off it, altering the load on the shaft they are coupled to. The change propagates through the greased chains into the tower, into the vane column, into the plenum, out along the whole geared skeleton of the town, and every other tender feels it in the sternum: a fractional stiffening or slackening of the great slow turning that is the background of all their lives. That is a syllable. It arrives everywhere at once, at the speed of sound in iron, which is the only fast thing permitted here — the town is wired for a nervous system and always was.

The tick is the consonant. The tick is what you hear when a syllable's leading edge takes up slack somewhere close to you.

A word is a few ticks and a few hours of held tension. A sentence is a week. There are, at present, three conversations underway in the Warm Basin. The oldest was begun before the lamps were strung and has produced, by careful reckoning, something under two hundred words. It is not slow because they are stupid. It is slow because a statement made in the load of the works is felt in the body of everyone who lives in the works for as long as it lasts, and to say a thing under those conditions is closer to weather than to talk, and they are not careless about weather.

There is no lying. You cannot lie in a medium your listeners are standing in.

### The work, and what it is for

You will want to know what they are making. You will look at the racks of cloth going back into the dark — a hand's breadth a week, ten thousand bolts, an inventory with no market within nine meters and forty kilometers — and you will want it to be for something.

It is. Just not for you, and not for export.

The cloth wraps the workpieces in the annealing house, and the workpieces are shafts and vane-spindles and crane-teeth, held at seep temperature for eighty or a hundred and forty years until the metal has forgotten every stress ever put into it and can be installed without ever again needing thought. The annealing house is the town's parts bin. Its production cycle is longer than its wear cycle by a comfortable margin, which is the entire secret of the place. The calcite blocks build the terraces; the terraces carry the buildings; the buildings house the mill that makes the blocks. The filamentous alga is pressed in a low shed nobody mentions, one drop of oil an hour into a stone cup, and that drop is the whole lubrication budget of the works, and it is sufficient, and has always been sufficient, with a slight surplus that goes into a second cup, and the second cup is full, and has been full for a long time.

The works do not make goods. The works make the works. The tenders do not run the works. The tenders are the part of the works that notices.

### Hands

The felt will not take on their palms or the pads of their fingers. Too much contact, too much polish. So a tender is a green figure with bare hands, and the hands are the colour of wet paper, and they are the only place you can see that whatever these were, they were made of the same stuff you are.

The fingertips are worn to a slight flattening that matches, exactly, the tools they hold. Not the tool worn to the hand — the hand worn to the tool, over an interval that makes the distinction meaningless. If you were to pry a spanner from one of them, which you could do, easily, in about a minute and a half, you would be holding an object that fits nothing in the world but the four flattened pads still reaching toward the place you took it from, and you would have twelve years to think about that before the arm finished coming back down.

Don't.

### The count

There are eleven. Or twelve. Or thirteen, though thirteen requires you to count the shape in the settling house that has been small for a long while, and the basin does not count it, and I will follow the basin's lead.

The uncertainty is not carelessness. When a tender stops — and they do stop, rarely, at intervals of perhaps two or three hundred years — the felt on them changes its habit within a decade, losing the strain-whorls, growing dumb and radial like brick. That is the notice. Nobody moves the body. It stands where it stands, and the water it used to interrupt now flows a different way around it, and that altered flow is felt by everyone downstream forever after, which is the obituary and is considered sufficient and even generous: the dead go on adjusting the current field, which is the same as going on speaking, at a volume of one word held indefinitely.

But a tender in a long stillness and a tender who has finished look very much alike for the first several years, and there is a strict etiquette against checking. You do not go and look. You wait, and the coat tells you, and until the coat tells you the answer is that they are working. Nine of the eleven have been unambiguous for centuries. Two are unresolved. One of those two has been unresolved for eighty years, which is a long time, and about which the third conversation — the newest one, begun after the lamps — has so far managed four words.

### The one still slowing

On the iron stair on the north side, between the mill and the pump house, there is a figure with a thin coat.

Patchy. Bare at the shoulders. Growing, but not yet established — the mat is perhaps nine or eleven years old, which in this basin is an infancy. The posture is wrong: weight forward, one hand not resting but *gripping* the rail, the whole body still shaped by an intention it arrived with.

It came down through a tear in the ceiling at some point within living memory, which here means the last century, and it has been slowing ever since. Not dying. Slowing. You can watch the process across a season if you have a season: the movements getting longer and cheaper, the breath lengthening toward forty, the coat catching hold in the lee of the collarbone first and then the spine. Its rate is currently about three times the local standard, which reads, to everyone else, as a continuous frantic shudder — the way a hummingbird would read to you.

The other tenders have turned toward it. Not all at once; over decades, one shoulder-line at a time, benches inching around on their polished grooves. Nine faces now bear on the north stair. Nothing is being done. Nothing needs to be done. They are simply attending, in the way this place attends, and the attention is the entire welcome and the entire test, and it will resolve one way or the other in about forty more years.

Whether the newcomer will finish slowing, or stop first, is not knowable from inside the basin, and the basin is not anxious about it, and neither, by now, is the newcomer.

### The others

The eel in the pump house intake is older than the water and is not a tender and is not tended. It has an arrangement with the pumps that predates the flood; the intake is shaped the way it is because of the eel, not the other way round. It has moved perhaps a body-length in the last eighty years. It is the only thing in the Warm Basin that could be fast if it wanted, and it is spending that capacity on nothing at all, deliberately, like a rich man leaving money in a drawer.

The crayfish in the plenum have a government. Blind, white, warm, arranged into chambers by a logic that has been observed for a long time and never once predicted. Petitions appear to be made. Something like a census occurs. The tenders route the warm water around the plenum in ways that suggest they are aware of the chambers and are unwilling to flood one without notice, and the notice, when it comes, is a change in temperature of half a degree over three months, and the chamber empties, and the water goes through, and afterwards the chamber refills with different crayfish and a slightly different hierarchy. This has happened eleven times. Nobody has ever been drowned; everybody has always been informed.

The flatworms graze the tenders. This is the closest thing to touch that occurs here between species, and it occurs constantly, and it is presumably pleasant, and nobody makes anything of it.

And the south wall shines.

The corpse-green crust there is one organism, or one alliance so old it has stopped being several. It covers about four hundred square meters, it is nineteen centimeters deep at the thickest, it makes light for reasons that have never been satisfactorily attributed, and it has been doing so continuously for longer than the town. If anything in the Warm Basin is the mind of the Warm Basin, it is not the tenders. They are only the fingers — the part worn smooth, the part in contact. The wall is the part that has been thinking about it.

### At the end of the night

The lamps hold their dull orange. The larvae keep their congregations. The looms advance.

On the north stair, the thin-coated one loosens its grip on the rail by a millimeter, which is the largest concession it has made in a decade, and nine benches feel it through the iron as a single soft tick, and the tick travels the works, and enters the sternum of everyone alive here, and is understood.

Nothing answers for six weeks.

That is not silence. Around here, that is a room full of people all deciding to be kind at the same speed.
