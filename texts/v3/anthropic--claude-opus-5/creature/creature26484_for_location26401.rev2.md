## The People of the One Stem

**Bodies**

They are not lean. Visitors who expect divers to be whittled things find instead a short, heavy, wide-shouldered people, padded over the chest and thighs, with a roll of fat at the back of the neck where the cold bites first. The padding is the trade. Twelve degrees takes a thin body in a quarter of an hour and a thick one in an hour, and the whole economy of the town is the difference between those two numbers.

Their skin, from the ribs down, is permanently sodden — white, faintly blue in the webs of the fingers, printed all over with the wrinkle-grooves that never quite flatten out. Above the waist they are greased. Pig-fat traded up from the lowlands, worked into the shoulders and the top of the back each morning by someone else, because you cannot reach your own shoulders properly and because it is a thing done in pairs. By afternoon the grease has taken up a green tinge from the column-slime, so that everyone in the basin is faintly the colour of the plant they live in.

Their eyes water constantly in air and see better in water than yours will ever see, and their hands are hard in exactly three places: the base of the thumb, from the mallet; the pads of the first two fingers, from cord; and a callus on the upper gum where the spile is held while both hands are busy.

Their hearts are slow. A grown diver's pulse in the water drops to something a lowland physician would call dying. They know the feeling well and have a name for the moment it arrives, a lurch about thirty seconds down when the chest stops fighting and goes quiet and a second wave of strength comes up from somewhere under the ribs. They say the stem lends it. They say you must give it back.

They are old at forty-five. The joints go first, then the ears, then the lungs.

**Ears**

At eleven or twelve, a child is opened.

It is done on a raft at midday by someone who has done it many times, with a sliver of thorn and a good deal of ceremony, and it hurts for a week and it stops the child from ever hearing properly again in air. What it buys is depth: an opened ear does not squeeze at thirty metres, and an unopened child cannot follow the town down past eight. So the town is divided cleanly into surface-children, who are shrill and unweighted and half-wild on the leaf-rafts, and everybody else, who are half-deaf and shout.

This is why the basin is loud. Two hundred people, nearly all of them speaking at the volume of people who cannot hear themselves, in a bowl with a stone rim. The four women hauling the trap are not arguing. That is simply how the sentence is said.

It is also why the knocking matters. The mallet-grammar is not a convenience. It is the register in which this town is actually fluent, because the wood carries the knock into the skull through the bone behind the ruined ear, and a knocked sentence is heard perfectly by an old woman who has not made out a spoken word in twenty years.

**Names**

A name here is a rhythm before it is a word.

The midwife invents it during the birth, in the gaps of the slow triplet, and by the time the child is out the whole basin has heard it four hundred times and will not forget it. Two-short-long-short. Long-long-pause-short. It is knocked, not spoken. There is a spoken form as well, a syllable or two, but it is a translation, and people use it the way you would use a nickname for something you love and do not need to be precise about.

You can knock a person's name anywhere on any stem in the basin and they will hear it and know it is for them, and they will knock back *coming* or *not now* or nothing, and nothing is an answer too, and everyone else in the town has heard the whole exchange. There are no private summons. There is no such thing as an unnoticed absence.

When a person dies, their rhythm is retired. Nobody may be given it for three generations. The consequence, after a very long time in a very small population, is that the grammar has holes in it — sequences that are perfectly good rhythms which no one will use, which every child is taught to avoid, and which are known by the names of the people they were. To knock a retired name deliberately is the worst thing you can do with a mallet. To knock one by accident is to stop dead, apologise into the wood, and be forgiven awkwardly by six households at once.

**Stone-mates**

Nobody dives alone. You cannot: you go down on a stone, hand over hand on a wet cord, and the stone must be hauled back up by someone above, and your body with it if the day goes badly.

The pair is made at the opening, usually between children of the same age, occasionally by an older diver choosing a younger one, and it is meant to last until one of them stops. It outranks marriage. Marriages here are practical, cheerful, and revisable; the pair is not. Your stone-mate greases your shoulders, taps your node, knows your name-rhythm better than your mother does, and is the one who knocks the long roll when you do not come up.

A diver whose mate has died is called by a particular word which means, roughly, *half a stone*. Some of them re-pair. Many do not, and go instead to the tasks that need no partner: the fire-bowls, the felting, the traps in the shallows, the long duty of counting. The town is organised, quietly and without anyone saying so, around finding useful things for the ones who are left over.

**Air, and who owns it**

Two hundred people in a mile-wide bowl are not crowded for space. They are crowded for air.

Every internode is a lungful, or two if you are calm. A stem breathed once is fine. Breathed twice, it is finished — the plug goes in and the node is marked with a scratched ring and is not to be touched again, because a column opened three times softens at the joint and comes down, and when it comes down it takes a day and a half to do it, and it takes the house grafted into it, and the drying racks, and the leaf-raft, and sometimes a sleeping child.

So there is a person who counts.

She is old and cannot dive and is not especially liked. She keeps a bundle of tally-rods in a felt roll in the driest place in the high town, one rod per column, notched for each breath taken, and she has the whole of it by heart anyway. Every household's node is on her rods. Every new room grafted, every stem tapped for a birth or a sickness, every stem softening ahead of schedule. She is the one who says whether a young couple may take a column of their own, which is to say whether they may have children, which is to say that the population of the basin is set, every year, by an unpopular woman with a bundle of sticks and an excellent memory. When she dies, the rods pass to whoever she has been shouting at most patiently for the previous decade.

The air itself is sour and fizzes and is slightly, reliably intoxicating. Everyone knows the feeling. Most people take it as it comes, four or five breaths in a working day. A few cannot leave it alone.

These are the stem-drunk, and there are usually one or two, and they are the only real criminals here. They open other people's nodes. They open scratched nodes. They open three or four in an afternoon and lie on a raft afterwards, beatified, breathing out the town's next forty years. What they are doing is not theft of a thing; it is theft of a duration. The punishment is first the silence — nobody knocks their name, they must swim to be spoken to, and in a basin this size that is a long way to swim for every conversation — and then, if it continues, the lip.

**The lip**

The northeast overflow is the only place in the world where these people are out of earshot.

Sound does not cross it. Ten paces down the polished stone and the frogs and the rain and the wooden pulse all fall off together, and you are standing ankle-deep in cold moving water in an enormous quiet, and nobody can hear what you are saying, and — this is the part that matters — nobody can hear you *being talked to*.

So everything that must be private happens there. Lovers who have not yet declared, partners renegotiating a bad pairing, two families settling a stem dispute without six other households listening in through the wood. Everybody knows this. To be seen walking out toward the lip in the late afternoon is itself a public announcement; you have simply chosen which of the two kinds of exposure you prefer. Young people go out there and stand in the sheet-water and cannot think of anything to say, and come back.

And exile is over that lip and down. There is no ceremony. They do not walk the exile out. They simply stop knocking for him, and one morning he is gone, and the tally-rod for his column is planed smooth and started again.

They are very bad walkers. Splayed soft feet, thick thighs, a rolling gait. An exile from the basin is a strong man who can hold his breath for four minutes and who will be lame in a week on a mountain road. Down in the lowland markets they are known as the wet people, and they arrive twice a year with dried fish and fine cord, limping, shouting at each other, and go home fast.

**The low town is not abandoned**

Outsiders record the deep town as ruins. It is not.

The dead go down. They are weighted with the same lifting-stones they used alive, and taken by their stone-mate, or by the nearest thing left to one, to a doorway at twenty-five or thirty metres where the light is tea-coloured and the silt is over the sills, and they are put inside their grandmothers' houses. The town has been climbing away from its dead for four hundred years at a finger's width a generation, and the dead have stayed exactly where they were put, which is why the low town is by far the most populous district of the settlement. There are perhaps two hundred people in the basin. There are eleven or twelve thousand below them.

This is the ordinary arithmetic of the place and nobody finds it grim. The living are the thin bright layer on top of the town. The town is mostly underneath.

The pale blind eel-thing goes in and out of those doorways all day, unhurried, feeling along the lintels. It is not hunted and not harmed and not exactly revered. It is the only resident that visits everybody.

**The frogs are ours**

The visitor hears the dusk honking and thinks: wilderness.

It is not. The frog lives in punctured nodes and only in punctured nodes. Before there was a town there were a handful of frogs in a handful of storm-split stems. The thousands are a by-product — the sound of two hundred people's leftovers, amplified through a mile of hollow cane. The basin's most famous noise is a human artifact that nobody made on purpose.

They are eaten, gutted and dried on the racks, and they are also the town's alarm system. A frog can only get into a node that is open. So a stem that starts honking where no honking was is a stem whose plug has failed or been pulled, and a house with too many frogs is a house that is either careless or being robbed or robbing itself, and the neighbours will come round, loudly, to ask about it.

There are also the shrews, which are vermin and steal from the racks and are killed without feeling. And the grey heron, which is luck, and which is never driven off; if it comes down on your drying rack and takes your whole afternoon's fish you say nothing at all, and the neighbours say nothing, and it is understood that something has been paid on the household's behalf. And the caddis larvae, which cement the woven walls with their spit out of the town's own dropped rubbish, and are therefore reckoned to be paying rent, and are scraped off the newest rooms and left strictly alone on the old ones.

**Dry cord**

Nothing here is allowed to dry. The cane fibre is stronger wet than dry; a rope left in the sun goes brittle and treacherous and looks perfectly sound. Cordage is stored submerged, in weighted coils hanging in the flooded rooms, and the first thing a child is taught, before the dive, before the mallet, is to run a rope through the fingers and feel for the stiffness.

From which: *dry cord*, meaning a person who has gone hard and false without showing it, and which is the standard insult, and which is not thrown around lightly, because everyone within four hundred metres hears you say it.

**What they are afraid of**

Not drowning. Drowning is a way of going home early.

Not the cold, which is simply the shape of the day — short bouts, long shivering, the fire-bowl passed hand to hand, the ferocious complaining that passes for warmth.

They are afraid of the column that goes soft. They are afraid of the counting woman's rods running out. And they are afraid, in a way they cannot really explain to a lowlander, of silence — of the moment on the northeast lip when the whole basin drops out behind you and there is nothing in the enormous air but your own blood. Their word for dying is not *going down*. Going down is where everybody is. Their word for dying is *walking out*.

At night the old ones sleep in the water, because the joints hurt less there: tethered by a cord at the ankle to a node, greased, faces up, rocking a little under a sky that is very close and very hard. All around them the hollow wood ticks and knocks and does not finish settling, and they can feel the rhythms through the water and through the bone behind their ruined ears, names and answers and somebody's birth-triplet somewhere south, and they sleep in it the way you would sleep in a house where the family is still up and talking downstairs.
