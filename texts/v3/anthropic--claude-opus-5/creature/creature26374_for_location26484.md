## The mats

Start with them, because everything else in the Pan is a way of moving them around.

Each warm seep has one. It occupies a band a few centimetres wide, sometimes as much as a hand — not the wettest part of the channel and not the hottest, but the contour where the water has cooled to a particular temperature, and that contour does not move, because nothing here moves. So the mat is not really a colony sitting in a place. It is a temperature line made flesh. You could lift it off and hold a thermometer's reading in your fingers.

Above, a skin of mineral and dead cells, hard as the varnish it resembles. Below that, perhaps two millimetres of living slime, layered by chemistry: sulphur oxidisers at the top where the air gets in, iron in the middle, and beneath them the slow anaerobic majority that is doing most of the arithmetic. Colourless mostly. Faintly pink where the iron is generous. It has no seasons. It has no competitors. It does not grow toward anything, because there is nowhere better and it is already at the edge of everywhere worse. Peel a hand's width away and in eleven days the gap has closed and then the closing stops.

Each seep's mat is one lineage, unbroken, as old as the seep. Where a channel has shifted by a centimetre in the last few thousand years, you can see the ghost of the old band beside the new one — a slightly duller stripe in the sinter, the mat's only autobiography.

## The midges

Grey, two millimetres, mute, and effectively deaf: the one thing in the Pan that is exempt from the Pan. They hatch out of the mat, dry themselves, and fly. They live a day and a half. They do not feed. They navigate by humidity and by the brightness of the crust, and they swarm in the stalled air a hand's height above the channels, in columns that occupy the same air every day of every year, because the convection that makes that dead calm is as fixed as everything else. Mating happens in the column. The females go back down to the mat that made them and lay into it, and then both sexes fall, and the mat digests them.

So the midge is not really an animal in its own right. It is the mat's method of moving material three metres sideways and back, and of putting itself into a shape that something with legs can pick up.

## The clicker

Length of a hand. Flat — flat as a coin purse, flatter than a lizard has any business being — pale as the crust with the dark line of the gut showing through the flanks. Four legs held wide, elbows above the spine, so that at rest it lies against the ground along its whole underside. The toes are absurd: five on each foot, longer than the forearm, fringed with a comb of flat scales that spread the animal's weight across crust too thin to take a pebble. It never breaks through. This matters. Below the thin places there is water at a temperature that would cook it.

The head is broad and low, and most of it is ear. Two membranes, nearly the width of the skull, set almost flush with the skin and stiffened at the rim; behind each, a chamber that takes up the space where a mammal would keep brain. The eyes are adequate and no more. The nostrils are slits it can seal. Under the jaw is a small hardened plate and a set of palatal ridges, and by drawing the plate across them it clicks — four discrete frequencies available to a hatchling, of which an adult will use one or two.

It lives in a null. There is no other option. Outside the dead zones the roar saturates its hearing completely, and its hearing is not a sense it has among others; it is the organising principle of its nervous system. Out in the noise it can see and it can feel and it cannot think properly, in the way that you cannot think properly while being shouted at continuously by forty people at four metres.

### Dialects

Cancellation is frequency-specific. A null does not delete the roar; it deletes particular bands of it, and which bands depend on the spacing of the throats that made the null and the size of the pocket. So every dead zone in the Pan has its own residual quiet windows — a comb of usable frequencies unique to that pocket, and unchanged since long before there was anything with ears in the basin.

Hatchlings arrive with all four notes. In the first weeks, the notes that their own null does not carry get no answer, and are pruned. By the time an animal is a month old it clicks only in the key of the room it was born in. Put it down in a null nine hundred metres away and it is not merely a stranger; it is inaudible. Its speech goes into the noise and is eaten.

There are perhaps forty nulls and therefore something like forty keys, and they have been these keys for four thousand years.

The clicks themselves are not much: presence, position, irritation, the boundary call, and a courtship duet in which a pair alternates strictly in each other's gaps, never overlapping. In a place where silence is the only bandwidth there is, the whole of good manners is knowing when not to use it. Two animals clicking over each other mask their own room. A household that quarrels too long goes briefly blind.

### The commute

The midges are out in the roar. The clicker is not.

Before it leaves, it clamps. A muscle draws the eardrums tight and thick; the nostrils seal; the body goes rigid along its length. Then it goes out into a world where its principal sense has been switched off, and navigates by the second one — by its feet and its belly, reading the vibration carried in the crust itself. The ground rings differently at every point in the basin, and it rings the same way today as it did last year. A permanent field of texture. A map that cannot be out of date.

Each animal learns one or two routes, by whole-body feel, in a series of increasing sorties as a juvenile, and uses them for life: out along the ridge of thicker crust, down to the channel, forty seconds in the swarm, back. Nine or ten minutes. It comes home shaking, unclamps, and lies flat in the middle of the null for a long time with its throat working, and visitors invariably describe it as looking exhausted, and it is.

Juveniles that learn the route wrong do not come back. There is no other way for that error to resolve. Nothing scavenges out there. The body dries, mineralises, and the sinter closes over it in a small smooth swelling indistinguishable from every other rounded thing in the Pan, because everything here is rounded. The floor around each null is faintly lumpy for ten or fifteen metres, and that is the entire mortality record of the lineage, underfoot, polished, unreadable.

### Households

A null two metres across holds one adult. Three metres holds a pair. The largest — the one on the west side, four metres, with an overhang above it and a drip-well in the floor that supplies water without a trip — holds a pair and up to five subadults, quarrelling incessantly, and has held approximately that for as long as it has been that size, which is to say always. Surplus animals are pushed to the edge, and from the edge into the roar, and from the roar into the crust.

Below the true nulls there are partial shadows: places where only one band drops out, half a metre wide, unfit for a household. Single animals live in them, with a single note, able to say *here* and nothing else. They are thin and they startle badly and they do not breed. There have always been some.

### What the ear is for

There is nothing to be eaten by. Nothing hunts in the Pan. The birds pass over twice a season and never come down, and the clickers do not look up; nothing has ever taught them to.

So the great membranes are not scanning for a predator. They are watching the walls.

Because the nulls do breathe. The speed of sound goes with temperature and humidity, and both of those swing a little between noon and midnight, and so the interference pattern shifts — five centimetres, eight, occasionally fifteen. The room inhales and exhales over the course of a day. The animal tracks the moving boundary continuously, all its life, with a precision that is the reason it has that head, and it adjusts its position by a body-length here and there without appearing to attend to it at all, the way you adjust your feet on a slope.

Three or four times a year it rains. The floor of the basin receives this as a smell and a mist and no sound whatsoever, but the air changes, and every null in the Pan lurches and swells and contracts by a third of a metre and takes some hours to settle. On those days nothing hunts. The households crowd into the centres of their rooms, pressed flat, ears going, and wait it out. It is the only event in their calendar. It is the only event in the Pan.

## Below

If you broke the crust — you would not, but if — the flooded channels beneath are lined with the same chemistry as the seep mats, thicker, black-green, in sheets and knots and slack veils moving in the water. That is the actual biomass of the basin, by two orders of magnitude. Everything on the surface is a leak from it. The mats are where it reaches air; the midges are where it acquires legs; the clicker is the one small vertebrate consequence of the fact that some of the leaking happens next to a place where you can hear.

## And no one else

No plants. No burrowers — the crust is unbreakable and the cavities are lethal. No scavengers, no ants, no worms, nothing that turns over the ground. Nothing has ever built here, and nothing here has ever built.

When a person lies down in a null, the null changes shape. A body absorbs and reflects; a null two metres wide with a human in it is a smaller and a differently-tuned room. The residents go to the edges, clamp, flatten, and wait, and do not click, and this is the largest disturbance that will occur in their lives. In the morning you climb out over the rim with the north-east throat still going in your pulse, and behind you the pocket relaxes back into the shape it has held for four thousand years, and the animals come back to the middle of the floor and resume saying *here* to each other at 6.8 kilohertz, in the one narrow window their room has always left open.

They are not waiting for anything either. Their bodies were tuned into that interference pattern the way the throats were tuned into their tones — worn toward the shape that worked, until the shape stopped wearing. They arrived a long time ago.

They are simply running.
