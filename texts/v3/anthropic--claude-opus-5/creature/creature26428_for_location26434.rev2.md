# The People of the Sill

## In the general

They are grey, and they are the colour of the thing they cannot sell.

Full sun at three and a half thousand metres, most days of the year, coming off grey wood and grey heath and a white floor that throws it all back up at you — so: dark, weathered, deeply lined about the eyes by thirty. Everyone squints. The squint outlasts the light; it is there in the fog, it is there at night, it is the face of the plateau. Broad chests, because of the air. Slow pulses. Short in the leg, long in the hand.

The hands are the first thing a stranger would notice if the plateau had strangers. Everyone here has climbed a ladder every day of their life. A Sill grip could crush a cup. Handshakes are not exchanged casually and are never a greeting — a greeting is a nod and a count — because the handshake is used for one purpose only, which is the taking of an oath before the Assize, and the strength of it is part of the evidence.

They wear bast cloth from the Draw, woven grey, undyeable. Broad flat hats against the glare, waxed gauze over the eyes on the open heath. The cloth wears about nine years and then goes soft and is put back into the haulm, and there is nothing else. No leather, no wool, no fur, no fire-cured anything. A people dressed entirely in their own landscape, walking on it, living inside it, breathing out water into the fog that will come back over the lip at dusk and be stripped by the combs and run down into the roots underneath them.

The one exception, the one colour, is **varn**. Resin cooked amber at Sparroway, which they crate and send over the edge in sealed crocks and never see again. Foundry apprentices hold the **spill-right**: whatever runs down the outside of the crock is theirs, and they keep it in thimbles, and they brush it on things. A doorframe. A cup rim. The inside of a wedding shoe, where nobody will look. In a stand of eleven thousand people there might be four square metres of colour altogether, and all of it is hoarded drips of the substance the world below is apparently varnishing its ships with, if the world below still has ships.

## What they eat

There is no fire, so there is no cooking. There is pressure.

Every kitchen on the Sill is a bronze pot bolted to a branch line off the main, and everything goes into it at fifteen atmospheres for a quarter-hour and comes out soft. Cane-heart, pith-flour dough, fog-comb shoots in spring when they are still sweet, and the forty fungi. The food of the Sill is boiled, boiled, and boiled, and the entire cuisine turns on texture and on salt, and the salt is scraped off the nodal plates inside the canes where the plant concentrates its minerals — **plate-scrape**, bitter, faintly metallic, sold by the spoon, adulterated constantly, and the adulteration of plate-scrape is one of the three crimes the Assize still hangs for.

Protein comes from underneath. Four metres down the haulm runs at blood heat, and in that layer, in tunnels shored with cane-rib, the **warm trade** grows the beds: a pale slabby fungus called under-bread, fed on the old fibre, cut in blocks, tasting of almost nothing. The tenders work stripped to the waist in the dark, in the only warm place on the plateau and the only quiet one. The haulm down there is dense enough to swallow the chord and the hammers both. You go down a ladder from a world of unrelenting noise and after eleven rungs there is nothing but your own breathing and the tick of moisture in the fibre.

Warm-trade people come up strange. Everyone says so. They talk less than anyone else on the Sill and they are the only people here who do not flinch at silence, and there is a superstition — the Assize calls it that, and does not fund the question — that down in the beds the fossil sound is not older or newer but *closer*, and that the tenders have heard things being laid down that have not yet been said above. They will not discuss it. They cut their blocks and come up at shift-end blinking like something dragged out of the ground.

## The trades

**Valve-men** drive the thief-valves. It is surgery: you go in through bark into living cambium at fifteen to eighteen atmospheres, and if your taper is out by a degree the cane bleeds sap for a month and the district downstream loses its pressure and a valve-man loses his certificate and possibly his arm. They are vain, they are paid outrageously, they wear their resin-stained hands ungloved as a boast, and they die young of nothing in particular.

**Splicers** coax the bridges. A grown bridge — a limb of one cane persuaded across eleven metres of air into the flank of the next, fused, hollowed, floored — takes between forty and seventy years. No splicer has ever completed a bridge she started. You inherit your master's graft with his tools and his notes and his particular way of tying off, and you hand yours on, and the guild's mark is a knot that three generations of hands have each pulled a third of the way tight. Splicers are the only people on the Sill with an easy relationship to time. They are also, as a class, unbearable at dinner.

**Pacers** walk the measured chains, eleven days on rotation, counting aloud, in pairs, with a third at the far end counting back. They have ruined feet and superb voices. A good pacer can count for six hours without slurring a number, and the slur is what they are listening for in each other, because a slurred number is an uncounted step and an uncounted step is how a chain comes back one part in forty short. Pacers get a pension at forty-five and a plot of heath near the stand where they may sit and count nothing at all, and they are the only Sill folk who as a rule go quietly mad, and the madness always takes the same form, which is that they continue.

**Cable-loaders** at Draw End put the plateau's whole product over the edge, a hundred and forty cars on an endless loop, and take back the empties and stack them and send them down again. It is the least respected trade and the best organised. They have a song with no words, only a number sequence, and it is the loudest thing at Draw End except the engine.

And in the empty stands, in the fourteen hollowed and glazed and wired and unoccupied cities, sit the **Hearers**.

There are never more than two hundred of them. They take rooms high in the condemned canes, one to a level, and they sit, and they listen for their own names.

Because occasionally the fossil sound runs the other way, and when it does the interval is hours or minutes, and the thing the plateau plays back has not happened yet. A Hearer who hears her own name called in the voice of someone she knows has between forty minutes and a day, and what she does with it is her business, and the register of what they have done with it is kept by the Assize and is not public. Most of them, by the book, go and sit with somebody. A few settle debts. One, famously, walked out onto the heath and counted a chain nobody had asked her to count, and got it right, and the chain is still good.

They are not respected exactly. They are given the wall side of the corridor. Families do not marry into the Hearers if they can help it, and the Hearers, who know this perfectly well, mostly marry each other, in the empty cities, in rooms nobody has ever slept in, under lights that breathe on the twelve-second cycle.

## How they speak

Everybody counts, so everybody talks, so the Sill is a place of enormous and continuous speech. Carters drone. Gangs sing in numbers. Tram drivers tick the nodes off on a bell and say the number aloud anyway, into the empty car, because the bell is not a voice and the code says *counted aloud*.

Under all of it, the twelve seconds. The lights breathe on the plant's own rhythm and every heart here has learned it, and so has every sentence. Sill speech comes in twelve-second units with a held pause between them, and the pause is not hesitation, it is grammar, and a visitor who fills it is understood to be shouting. Their songs are in twelve. Their hymns — such as they are, being mostly recitations of chain-lengths — are in twelve. A Sill child learning to talk learns to stop.

And because the haulm records, they have an ethic of sound. Out on the heath you are laying down. Most of it is nothing, carters and counting and the shift whistle at 6:40 that will still be blowing in four centuries for a mill with no name. But people go out deliberately. They walk to an agreed spot and say a thing into the fibre — a vow, a confession, a message for a descendant who will never be told where to stand. This is called **sowing**, it is legal, it is common at weddings, and it is entirely and universally understood to be futile, since nobody can choose where a sound comes up or when.

The inverse is also done, and is the second of the three capital crimes. Going out and sowing something vile — a threat, a name repeated in hatred, a child's screaming faithfully reproduced by an adult who can do voices — so that in two hundred years somebody's granddaughter walks through forty paces of it on the Ninestacks road and comes out the other side changed. The Assize calls it **laying**. It has hanged eleven people for it. It cannot undo one second of it, and it knows that, and it hangs them anyway, and afterwards the hanging is a sound in the ground, and there is no end to any of this.

## Children

They are not afraid of height, having never been on the ground. They are afraid of the dark and of standing water and of nothing else. They keep carriers in the blossom weeks, all of them, every generation — the pale six-limbed pollen-runners, warm and waxy and mute, that will not eat because they cannot, and that at the end of eleven weeks lie down wherever they are and go soft. Every Sill child is told not to keep one. Every Sill child keeps one. Every Sill adult remembers exactly where theirs lay down, which room, which corner, and can take you to the spot, and the spot is bare and means nothing, and they take you anyway.

Rain, nine days a year, and they are let out into it on the eleventh-level bridges with their hats off, and they scream, and the screaming is in the ground now and comes up sometimes on the open heath in fine weather and frightens carters half to death.

And once in a generation somebody's child hears the tone — the warning the wood makes ninety seconds before a scale sheds, eleven metres of bark coming down through the bridges edge-on — and does the correct thing, which is to shout the single syllable and run inward, and is afterwards given a brass token by the Assize and treated, for the rest of a long life, as a person who was right about something once.

## Names

The Assize does not reissue a name. A name is entered at birth, carried, retired at death, and struck, and the plateau has been doing this for a very long time with a population that has been falling for most of it. The stock runs down. The old names are one syllable and hard — Cleat, Ounce, Sarl, Foy, Brindle, Vane — and the new ones are compounds of compounds, six and seven syllables, issued by clerks who are visibly running out, and there are families whose children are named with numbers because the clerk had nothing left and would not, on any account, repeat.

They will not repeat. That is the whole of their religion, if they have one. The plant repeats; the plant is *for* repeating; the plant has learned the shape of a room and the shape of a coat and the shape of the way a particular woman held her hands behind her back, and it stands several hundred of her out in the heath at the stand margin where her family come and talk to her, against the rule, forever, unfined. The people are the part of the arrangement that is supposed to go only once.

And there is a Tenth Article which is not published.

Perhaps twice in a decade, somebody is found on the open heath at first light, warm, wet with fog, fully dressed in bast cloth of the correct weave and wear, knowing their trade, knowing their district, knowing everybody's business up to about a week ago — and unable to say, under oath, with that crushing handshake given and the Assize leaning in, by what counted route they arrived. Nobody walks the heath uncounted. Nobody survives walking the heath uncounted. They are brought in, and they are examined, and they are entered in the register under a heading that the clerks pronounce **brought forward**, and they are given a name.

From the struck list. From the retired names. It is the only occasion on which a name is ever used twice on the Sill, and the Assize, which will hang a man for adulterating salt, does it quietly and kindly and immediately, and puts them in work by the end of the week, and the neighbours know, and nothing whatever is said.

That is the people of the Sill, finally. Not that it happens. That nothing whatever is said. A plateau that finished eroding and stopped, and a population that finished asking and stopped, and between them eight thousand years of excellent bridges.

Ask any of them how many are brought forward and they will tell you: very few. Ask how they know they are not one, and you will get the pause — the grammatical one, the twelve-second one — and then they will tell you the number of paces to wherever you said you were going, correct to the pace, and walk you as far as the chain.

## The bucket

There is one bucket of standing water on the Sill and it belongs to Annec Sarl, retired pacer, ninety-one, third node of a cane condemned forty years ago and still perfectly sound at the top where she lives.

She fills it from the catch-gutter each morning and sets it on the sill and sits with it. It is a scandal in a small way. Water is pressure here; water is the entire industry; water at rest is water not working, and neighbours have said so, and she has outlived them.

What she looks at is the reflection. She has explained it exactly twice. Everything on this plateau, she says, repeats — the rooms repeat, the coats repeat, the birds come back ten years later possibly able to fly, the dead come up out of the margin standing the way they stood, the sound comes back and comes back and comes back and does not know that the people it is describing are gone. And none of it will admit what it is. All of it insists.

But a reflection is honest. It is a copy that ends when you stand up.

She sits with it about an hour each day, in the hard clean light, with the chord of ninety canes going under everything and the lights breathing on twelve, and then she stands up, and it stops, and she says — aloud, counting, because she is a pacer and cannot help it — *one*.
