# The Inhabitants

## The Lace

Nothing in the Cut is the first thing that lives there. The first thing lives underneath.

Go to the throat, where the fissure gravel is loose and yellow and nobody plants, and lift a handful, and the stones come up webbed together with white. It looks like the mycelium in a rotten log, but it is not fungus and it is not plant; it is something simpler and older that eats the seam's breath directly and asks nothing of the sun. The wardens call it the lace. It is what the sulfur-fig's roots are actually farming when they go down into the gravel. It is what stains the drip-lines cream-colored. It is the entire basement of the place, the only true producer in the hall, and it is counted among the inhabitants by no one, because it has no shape, no name in the ledger, and no interest in being noticed.

It grows at the pace of the rock's cooling, slower than anything above it. If you want to know what the Cut is, it is a wound in the ground that a white slime is quietly digesting, and everything else — the terraces, the engine, the four wet people, the light in the town's parlors — is a consequence.

## The Leaning Green

They are all one gesture, the plants, and the gesture is the one you make toward a stove on a cold morning.

The wick-vine is the crop and the citizen. It is grown on trellis in the second and third tiers, always at a set distance from a hot line, and it puts out a soft-cored cane the thickness of a thumb. Eleven months from cutting to draw. Its pith comes out as a wet grey rope, dries to grey floss, and is drawn into filament that burns in lamps nine meters overhead, in rooms the vine has never been in. It is entirely domestic. It has been under human hands for forty years and cannot now be found growing wild anywhere in the Cut, not even in the warm rubbish at the throat, where every other thing volunteers. Left alone it strangles itself in a season. It is the only inhabitant here that would not outlast the wardens by a week.

The boilerfern is a stripe. That is the honest way to describe it: not a plant but a band, a handspan wide, painted along every scalding run of iron in the hall, dying off sharply at the cold edge as though someone had ruled a line. It has no stem to speak of and no patience. Move a pipe and the stripe follows within a month, which for a fern is a sprint. Where two hot lines run close, the stripes meet over the gap and make the green tunnels, and you walk through them bent, and the fronds touch your neck with a dry heat that is not the pipe's heat but the fern's own, and that is the first thing about the Cut that frightens people.

The sulfur-figs are four, and they have names, and the names are in the ledger with dates beside them going back to the third year. They are squat, grey-barked, scabbed with crystal where the breath condenses on them, and they hold their leaves out flat over the fissure like a man warming his palms. Eleven years to ripen a fruit. A warden of long service sees four harvests. The fruit is the size of a fist, splits along one seam, and tastes — everyone agrees, and nobody has improved on the description — of iron and ash and, underneath, faintly of pear. It is eaten standing up, in silence, by whoever is on shift. It is not sold. There is no mechanism for selling something that arrives four times in a lifetime.

## The Moss, Which Is Livestock

Gauge-moss is the most thoroughly kept creature in the Cut, more kept than the vine, because the vine is only a crop and the moss is an instrument.

It has been bred, grazed, shaved, transplanted, scolded, and coaxed along every hot surface in the hall until the whole works reads like a written page under the hand. Bald at blood heat. A short felt at eighty. Deep bottle plush where the metal runs near boiling. A warden walking a line trails two fingers along the top of the pipe without looking, the way a man runs a stick along railings, and knows the pressure in the main, the state of the vent-gate, and whether someone at the far end has opened a valve they should not have.

It is fed, after a fashion — swabbed with tree-water, dressed with scrapings from the sump. It is pruned with a curved blade the width of a spoon. A bald patch where there should be plush is a fault report; a plush patch where there should be felt is an emergency, and the wardens will be at that junction before the pressure gauge in the brass housing has finished thinking about it. Twice in the Cut's history the moss has been right and the gauge wrong, and both times are written down, and both times are cited, and the brass gauge is by now a sort of ornament that the wardens keep polished out of courtesy to the engineers above.

## The Moth

Palm-sized, dust-colored, furred thickly enough that condensation beads on it and rolls off. It has no mouth. It hatches out of gauge-moss, flies for four days, lays in gauge-moss, and stops.

It does not go to the lamps. Nothing in the Cut goes to the lamps. It flies the isotherms, holding station in the warm air over a pipe the way a hawk holds a ridge, sliding along the band where the temperature suits it, and because a human body is a moving pipe at thirty-seven degrees, a pipe-moth will sometimes settle on the back of a warden's neck and stay there for half an hour, taking the reading. It is not permitted to brush it off. Not by any rule — there simply isn't the hurry in anyone to do it.

Its eggs go into the plush, which is to say into the instruments, which is to say the wardens' dials are the moth's nursery. When a line is shaved, the larvae are scraped out whole and carried on the blade to a spare warm run at the throat and put down there. This costs perhaps two minutes a week. Nobody has ever proposed stopping.

## The Shrimp

In the upper cistern, blind and pale and about the size of a fingernail paring, in numbers nobody has counted since the survey of the eleventh year. They were brought down deliberately in a stoppered jar from a cave two valleys over by a woman whose name is in the ledger and whose descendants still keep a stall in the market square above, selling buttons, entirely unaware.

They eat what falls in the water and by eating it keep the water sweet, and the water is drunk, and so the town's lamplighters and the Cut's wardens alike are downstream of a great many shrimp. They are not eaten. There is no prohibition. It is simply that they are the cistern's health and you do not eat your own kidneys, and also they taste of sulfur, and the two reasons are given in whichever order suits the asker.

After the rains they breed, and for a day or so the surface of the cistern has a faint grain to it, like watered silk, and a warden going for a bucket will stand a minute and look at it before dipping.

## The Four

They are lean and pale and permanently damp, and they move like something being poured.

A warden's body is a heat-adapted body: enormous sweat output, thin salt content, a resting pulse that runs fast in a person who is doing everything slowly. This is the contradiction you notice if you take a wrist. The heart is hurrying. Nothing else is. They drink from the buckets on the plane-tree spiles — the square's trees drinking down through nine meters of rock and paying rent in water, and the water tasting faintly of bark and leaf and the surface world — and they take salt by the pinch from a jar hung at the foot of the stair, on the hour, and the salt jar is the only thing in the Cut governed by a clock.

Their hands tell you the trade. The backs are a ruin: the fresh rock takes skin off anything that touches it, and so a warden touches rock with the back of the hand, the knuckle, the outside of the forearm, and all those surfaces are scarred over into something like hide. The fingertips are another matter entirely. Fingertips are instruments and are kept the way a violinist keeps them — greased, guarded, never used to grip, never used to push. A warden going up the stair holds the rail with a closed fist and the wrist. When a new one arrives from the surface, that is the first thing they are taught and the hardest, and a new one is not called by name for as long as it takes; the name comes back to them on the day they can walk the main and call the pressures blind.

They speak beneath the drip. Not whispering — below it, in a low flat register that carries through wet air better than volume does. Most communication is percussive: one ring on the pipe for the vent-gate, two for the sump, a fast triple for come now, a single soft tap repeated for I am here, all is well, which is tapped absently, the way people hum. They laugh with the shoulders and a long exhale and no sound at all. Visitors read this as solemnity and are wrong. The wardens find visitors extremely funny — the way they arrive fast, and then stop arriving fast, and sit down.

They sleep in the terrace brick in five-hour shifts with pots of cuttings at their heads and leaves against their faces, and they wake not to alarms but to the changing rhythm of condensation ticking on plate steel, which alters before the engine does. They are wet all the time and the wetness is the enemy: rot in the creases, fungus in a pattern each of them calls their map. Linen is turned three times a shift, not for cleanliness but for skin.

There are four of them now. There have been nineteen in forty years, and the ones who left by the stair for the last time have their names scratched into the glaze of the empty terraces at the cold sump-end, where nothing grows anymore but the grey moss, and where the bricks are cool enough to hold a mark.

The eldest sleeps down there now. She says she can only sleep in the cold, and that it is a scandal, and that at her age scandals are cheap. What she is actually doing is tending the seed-cold: a rack of wick-vine stock and fig cuttings kept deliberately chilled, deliberately unpampered, so the line stays tough enough to be carried somewhere else. She will not be alive in the year eighty when the last hot pipe drops below the boilerfern's line and the Slow is taken up the stair in pieces. The youngest one will be, and will be about her age, and will do the carrying. All four of them know this and speak of it the way a farmer speaks of a harvest: as a dated job, with a list.

## The Toad

Under the grate in the flooded apse, at the coldest end, where the terraces are empty and the water has begun to take the hall back forty years early.

It is fed table scraps by tradition, which means rind and crust and the grey ends of things, dropped through the grate by whoever passes with the basket. It does not surface for them. Once or twice a year it makes a sound, a single low note that is felt in the soles rather than heard, and conversation stops for it, and then resumes.

Nobody claims to have looked at it closely. In fact one warden did, at nineteen, with a lamp, lying on the plate with her face against the grate for a long time. What she saw was a toad. A very large, very old toad with a film over its eyes, sitting in cold water being entirely a toad. She was so embarrassed by the ordinariness of it that she never said, and she is the eldest now, and she has kept the custom going for thirty-odd years because she understands, better than anyone, that the not-looking is worth a great deal more than the looking.

It is the only inhabitant that has not moved. Everything else in the hall has crept twenty paces toward the throat, chasing the heat as it fails. The toad sits at the cold pole in the rising water with its scraps, and waits, and is patient in a way that the wardens, who consider themselves patient, privately find excessive.

## The Last Tenant

Grey moss. The ordinary kind. It grows on the abandoned sump terraces, on the cool returns, on the names scratched in the glaze, and it will grow on the bolt-holes when the Slow has gone up the stair, and on the trellis frames, and on the rail, and then under water it will stop, and something else will start, and nobody will be there to name it.

It is the only thing down here that grows toward nothing in particular, and it is therefore the only thing down here in no danger at all.
