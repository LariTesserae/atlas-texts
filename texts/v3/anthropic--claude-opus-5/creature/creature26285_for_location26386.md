## What Lives There

Strictly speaking, one animal lives here. Everything else is a guest of it, a parasite on it, a crop of it, or too small for it to bother about.

---

### The mason

Half an inch of pale, blind, dry-skinned insect. Held on a fingertip it is unimpressive: soft-looking, faintly yellow, with a head slightly too large for the body and a fringe of stiff bristles along the forelegs that turns out, under a lens, to be a trowel. It has no eyes at all — not reduced eyes, none — and it navigates by scent laid on plaster and by the feel of air moving across the hairs of its antennae. It has never in its life encountered standing water and would drown in a teaspoon of it.

Its cuticle is the best water seal in the animal kingdom on this continent. A mason sealed in a dry glass jar at noon temperatures will live nine days. It does not sweat, does not pant, does not urinate. Its waste comes out as a dry grey paste, and that paste, mixed with ground rock and saliva, is the material the city is built from. This is the fact that organizes everything: the mound is not a structure the colony makes, it is a structure the colony *excretes*, continuously, for two million years. Anything a colony cannot digest becomes wall. Its dead become wall. When you put your hand on a mound you are touching ancestors, mixed and set and mixed again, harder than the basalt they were ground out of.

**Castes.** The small ones cut and carry and plaster; there are more of these than there are stars you can see. The soldiers have flattened, fluted, plate-fronted heads, and they use them for two things: as hammers, drumming the alarm against gallery walls, and as doors. A mound has a hundred thousand doorways and most of them, most of the time, are plugged by a face. The fluting on the head plate matches the fluting of the plaster around it. From outside, a sealed gallery mouth looks like nothing at all.

**Sounders** are rarer, longer-legged, with swollen sensory clubs on the antennae, and they are the reason the city exists where it exists. A sounder walks a fresh lava sheet tapping it, head down, and listens for hollowness — for the roof of a tube forty feet under. It is the only water-divining in the world that actually works, because here the water is genuinely down there, a film on a ceiling in the dark, and finding it is the single founding act of a colony.

**Repletes** cannot walk. They hang from the ceilings of the storage galleries in their tens of thousands, abdomens swollen to the size of a grape with cool bitter jelly, translucent, faintly blue-green in the way water in a glacier is blue. A large mound's whole reserve, the entire hydrology of a nation, hangs upside down in the dark and is alive and can be asked, by touch, to give some back.

**The queen** does not move and has not moved for forty years. She is walled in a smooth-plastered cell that was, when it was built, on the surface. The city grows upward at a few inches a decade and she does not, so she has sunk — she is now sixty feet down, beneath roofs beneath roofs, in the coolest and dampest room on the continent, attended by workers whose great-great-grandmothers attended her. Everything in the mound moves at a walk except her; she is the one thing that has achieved perfect stillness, and she has done it by staying exactly where she was while a country accumulated on top of her.

**Rot is kept indoors.** In the fungus chambers, sealed, air held near saturation while the air a foot outside is bone, the colony maintains the only decay on this continent. Nothing decomposes on the surface of the plain — nothing can, there is no free water — so the masons have domesticated putrefaction the way other worlds domesticate fire. It is a dangerous, useful, humid thing that lives in a stone room and is never let out.
