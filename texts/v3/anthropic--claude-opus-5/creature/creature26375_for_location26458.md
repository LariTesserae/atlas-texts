## The Tenants of the Warm Roof

*A companion inventory. Read it after the ground, not before.*

---

### 1. The problem of identification

There is one animal shape on the plateau and perhaps four hundred species wearing it, and no field guide has ever been written that does not eventually become a guide to *margins*, because the margin is all you get.

You will not identify anything here by its outline, its colour, its posture, its gait, its voice, or its warmth. The outline is a dome. The colour is old bread. The posture is *down*. The gait, where it exists, is one body-length per hour. The voice does not exist. And the warmth is the plateau's, not the animal's — every living thing here has handed its thermostat back to the rock and lies on the floor of the world drawing heat up through its foot, so that a corpse and a sleeper and a stone are all the same temperature, day and night, and a thermal camera on the Warm Roof shows you nothing whatsoever but a flat pale field, faintly grooved.

So you learn margins. You lie down with your cheek on the grit at dawn, when the shadows are longest and the dew has not yet gone, and you look sideways along the ground at the bases of things, and you learn to read:

- **No gap.** Stone. Sit on it.
- **A hairline gap, dry, dust drifted into it.** Stone that was an animal. The shell outlives the tenant by three or four centuries and weathers into indistinguishability, which is why the standard estimate — one third of the domes are rock — is generous to the rock.
- **A hairline gap, wet, with a fringe of pale combed hairs.** A grazer. Do not sit on it.
- **A gap wider on one side than the other, and a smell of nothing at all.** Something has climbed on top of something. Walk around.
- **A gap breathing.** Very rare. Something is dying, and has begun to spend water on air.

That is the entire field-craft. It takes a season to learn and it does not get easier, and every visitor eventually catches themselves speaking to the stones and apologising to them, and this is considered normal and even hygienic.

### 2. The grazers

They are warm-blooded. Under the shell there is a mammal, and under the mammal there is a long argument that it lost.

A great grazer is a haycock of secreted plate, fluted, four hundred kilograms, with a low fringe of hairs around the margin that comb fog and lay it into the grooves. There are no ears. There is no nose to speak of, only a wet plate of chemical sense on the leading edge, and two dark points that resolve light and shadow and nothing else, and a tongue like a rasp, and a gut the length of a corridor doing something patient with almost nothing.
