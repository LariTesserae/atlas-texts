## The Tenants

Take them in the order the water meets them, because that is the only order this place recognises.

---

### The mats

Call them the landlord and not the vegetation, because they are the only thing here with its mouth applied directly to the architecture.

They live on what the water carries up out of the foundations — sulfur, iron — and on what the water takes out of the building on the way: the lime slowly leaching from ten thousand years of clay that turned into something like stone but not quite. Every day the mats eat a little of the mortar. Not the blocks; the joints. They are dismantling the dome from the inside at the rate of a rumour, and they have been at it since before the doorway lost its lintel.

They are also the light, and the light is garbage. The glow is not a signal and not a lure; it is a way of getting rid of something they cannot use, dumped out of the cell as a photon because there is nowhere else to put it. Everyone else in the Sunk Crown sees — insofar as anything here sees — by the mats' waste.

They have colour and the colour has borders. White where the flow is thin. Rust-orange where the iron comes up. Black, and faintly warm to the palm, in the cells nearest the shaft where the water arrives least tired. Each colour is a different consortium of things with no names, and the borders between them move — a hand's width in ten years, in the direction of whatever has recently changed. The pattern on any given wall is weather. It is simply weather at a speed that lets you stand inside a whole front and feel nothing.

Where the builders cut deep, there is more surface, and where there is more surface the mat lies thicker. The mats still hold the carving. They are the last thing here that does.

---

### The shrimp

They are not, properly speaking, animals in this place. They are a thickness of the water.

Translucent, eyeless, each with its gut visible as a dark thread, they hang at every depth in numbers that make the galleries feel grainy against the skin. They graze the mats. They graze each other's shed shells, which drift down and accumulate in the low corners as a pale felt, and they graze the felt, so that the floor of every chamber is ancestors and the ancestors are food. Nothing is lost here. Nothing has ever been lost here. That is less beautiful than it sounds; it means there has never been a shortage, and a population that has never known a shortage never learns anything.

To an eel, the shrimp are not prey. They are the medium. You swim through them with your mouth open.

---

### The nation in the shimmer

The hand's span of shimmer between the cool fresh water and the warm brine is not a boundary. It is inhabited.

Something like a seed-shrimp lives there, each one the size of a pinhead, in a sheet whose thickness is a finger and whose extent is the whole width of the dome. They cannot go up; the water is too thin for them. They cannot go down; the water is too heavy. Their entire world is two-dimensional and always has been, and they have filled it so completely that the shimmer is partly made of them.

They are the only creatures here that keep the sun. When the coin of grey comes down the shaft near midday, the sheet under it sinks a finger's width and rises again over the next hour. Nobody would call that seeing. It is a tide, and it is one of only three clocks in the building.

When you push through the curtain and it closes behind you with that sensation of cloth drawn across the skin — a good part of that sensation is alive, and a good part of it is dead a moment later, and neither the sheet nor you will register the loss.

---

### The snails

The citizens. Everything else here is weather, plumbing, or an event; the snails are the population.

Fingernail-sized, flattened, forty to a hand's breadth of wall, with a shell whose lip is worn flat on the side that rides the stone. They rasp. That is the whole of it: a few microns per animal per day, a tongue like a strip of sandpaper, and a life of eleven years spent inside a range of perhaps two vaults. A snail is born on a wall, grazes that wall, breeds against that wall, dies at the base of it, and is eaten by shrimp who are grazing the same wall. There are lineages in the third gallery that have not left a single niche since before the doorway rounded off. Households. Dynasties in a corbel.

Now: they are also the last copy of the carving, and they are the reason there is no other.

The grooves held more mat; the mat drew more snails; so the snails are densest exactly where the cutting was deepest.
