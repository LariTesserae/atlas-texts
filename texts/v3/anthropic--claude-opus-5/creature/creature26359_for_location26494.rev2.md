# Who Lives at the Culm Works

## The count

Forty-one. It has not been above forty-six or below thirty-four in the hundred and six years the engineer's ledger runs continuously, and the fragments before that suggest the same. Nine of the forty-one are under a first cut — that is, under fourteen — and three are over five cuts. The oldest is in his sixth and has not left the terrace since his second, which was seen at the time as unremarkable and is still seen that way.

A population this size, closed this long, should be in trouble. It is not, quite, because of the barge; more on the barge below.

## What the place has made of them

Short, deep-chested, wide across the foot. The feet are the first thing. Everybody here walks on the outer edges of steps, has done since they could walk, and the arch of the foot has been asked to do something slightly wrong for a lifetime; the result is a rolling, outward-weighted gait that does not look careful but is nothing else. People who have left the works are recognised by it in towns forty kilometres away.

Nobody runs. Running is not forbidden; it is simply what a person does when something is coming down, and so a figure moving fast on the boards will empty a building. Children learn this before they learn to swim, and they learn to swim at three.

The skin tells the same story the brick does. There is a band of trouble from the ankle to just under the knee — tinea, chronic ulcer in the older ones, a permanent grey-purple mottling that visitors take for bruising — and it stops at the knee as cleanly as the spalling stops on the walls, and for the same reason. The works' physics holds for bodies. Nothing rots that stays wet, nothing rots that stays moving, and everything fails at the line where it is neither. Their hands are fine: constantly in water, constantly working, unmarked except that the hacklers have no fingerprints left by a second cut, polished off against combed fibre. Their shoulders are fine. Their shins are a slow catastrophe, and the tar shed keeps a pot of something for it, and the treatment is the same one in the ledger from eighty years ago, and it half works.

Their clothing is bast cloth that starts pale and goes tan within a month, and nothing here ever dries, so a person owns two sets and wears one while the other hangs on the flue rail in the boiler house. Everything they own smells of the retting tanks. They do not know this.

## The ear

The single most important instrument at the works is a child.

Up to about thirteen or fourteen, a person here can hear the jack-wheel's gear whine — a thin component somewhere around nine kilohertz that comes up when the wheel is drying and goes away when it is greased. After that they cannot. The top-cart rail and the belt and the engine have taken a notch out of every adult's hearing at very close to the same frequency, and it never comes back. So the children are sent: *go and listen to the jack-wheel; go and listen to the third hanger; stand under the north clump and tell me if it is knocking or squealing.* They report in a vocabulary of about a dozen terms they are taught at five and cannot really define. An eleven-year-old at this works has authority over a bearing that a forty-year-old does not, and this is not indulged, it is procedure, and the adult greases what the child says to grease.

They lose it at the end of a first cut. That the ear and the rotation come out to the same number is a coincidence that nobody here treats as one. A child's last useful year of listening is the year they are apprenticed to a post, and the phrase for it is *going over* — *she went over at the spring cut* — and it is spoken the way a birthday is spoken elsewhere, and there is a small, uncomfortable fuss.

Adult hearing is otherwise excellent inside a very narrow band. They pitch speech into the gap between the cart's beat and the engine's, which is why their voices sound strangled and overloud everywhere else on earth, and why they can hold a conversation across the Walk that a visitor standing between them cannot hear at all. On the Walk itself, under the cart, they use hands: about forty signs, most of them numbers, directions, and states of material. They use the same signs at home, at table, in bed, where there is no noise to justify it, and this is what makes strangers think them cold. They are not cold. They are simply not in the habit of spending a word.

## The nose

The retting master is always young and always about to be replaced.

The post requires a person who can still smell the tanks, and nobody at the works can smell the tanks after nine or ten years of standing over them, so the office turns over on a schedule the ledger keeps in a column headed *nose-years*. The master is typically seventeen when appointed and twenty-six when finished, and for those nine years holds the only kind of authority here that does not come from age: she walks the six tanks twice a day, leans in, and says a word, and the boiler house raises or drops the exhaust bleed on that word alone. There are eleven recognised smells. Good day-two is *bread*. Good day-five is *bread and iron*. The bad ones are *cucumber*, *hot penny*, and *sweet*, and *sweet* means the tank is lost, and a lost tank is the worst thing that happens here, worse than a death, because a death does not take ten years to make good.

When a master goes over, she trains her successor by walking the tanks with her for a year and arguing. There is no other way to transmit it. The ledger has the eleven words, but the words are only handles; the thing itself passes nose to nose, and it has passed this way maybe fourteen times, and everyone understands that the chain is one bad year from breaking and has understood this the whole time.

## Names

The engine has a name and the people do not, not out loud.

Everyone at the works is addressed by post. *Engineer. Canopy. Nose. Boiler. Tow. Cart.* The children are *first*, *second*, *third* by birth order within a household until they are apprenticed and become their post. Personal names exist, are given, are used by kin indoors and at close range and almost nowhere else, and a stranger who learns one and uses it on the boards causes a silence that takes a while to fill.

The engine is called Hansa. Nobody living knows who Hansa was. The name is in the ledger from before the first rebore, in a hand nobody can attribute, written as though everyone already knew.

## The posts

**Canopy.** Two people, always two, always one old and one young, roped and barefoot, in the crowns for six hours a day every day it is not lightning. They open the light-wells over the drying racks and close them over the tar shed. They cut the fig roots reaching for the flywheel pit and they do not cut the ones that have taken load, and the deciding of which is which is the single hardest judgement made here and takes about twenty years to learn. They keep the second ledger, which is not the engineer's and is not about iron: it is a fig calendar, crop by crop, tree by tree, going back far enough that it predicts.

**Engineer.** Keeps the first ledger, decides what the works will do in the next fourteen years, oils nothing personally. Currently a woman in her fourth cut with a bad left shin. She inherited the ledger at thirty-one from a man who had held it forty years, and she will hold it into her sixth if she lasts, and the handover is done by reading the whole book aloud to the successor over eleven evenings. Everyone can recite the root column. *Bearing, sound. Bearing, sound. Bearing, sound.*

**Oiler.** Hansa's post. Held by an old man now, held by a child before him, and the works swings between these two extremes because the job is either the first thing you are trusted with or the last thing you can still do. Sixty-one oiling points, every one on a schedule, and the engine house floor is the only floor on the terrace that is dry, and the oiler is the only person here whose shins are clean.

**Boiler, Tow, Hackle, Spin, Cart, Sluice.** The rest. Cart is the loud one, walking ninety-two metres behind a machine at the speed of a slow crawl, forty times a shift, and cart is held by the young and strong and is the post most likely to produce a person who leaves.

## Coming and going

Six or seven times a year a barge takes cable downstream and brings back salt, iron stock, oil, carbon filaments, tea, letters, and occasionally a person.

Two marriages a decade arrive by water. They are arranged over years, by letter, at three months a round trip, between the engineer here and people downstream the engineer has never met, and the terms are frank and mostly biological, and everyone involved understands exactly what the arrangement is for and nobody pretends otherwise. The incomer's first year is measured in how long it takes them to stop smelling the tanks. They are not considered to have arrived until they can't.

Roughly one person a cut leaves and does not come back. They go out on the barge with two sets of clothes and a name nobody outside will use, and they are talked about afterward without bitterness and without much else. Two or three in the ledger's span have come back after twenty years. Both of those are recorded, in the margin, in the root column's format.

## Hansa

She runs five and a half days a week and stops twice a year for three days, and those six days are the worst days here.

The works cannot sleep in the silence. They describe it as a pressure behind the eyes, a sense of the terrace lifting slightly. Children cry at nothing. Old people sit up. On the first night of a stop, a certain number of adults simply stay awake in the engine house with a lamp, not working, sitting on the bedplate while it cools, and this is not organised and is not talked about afterward and happens every time. On the third morning the boiler comes up and the flywheel takes its first slow turn and forty-one people feel it through the piles in their boot soles at the same instant, wherever they are standing, and go back to work.

This is the closest thing the Culm Works has to a religion and nobody here would use that word, and if you used it they would think you were talking about the barge people.

## The mother

The oldest living thing on the terrace is not a fig.

Tank three has been at blood heat without interruption for longer than the ledger runs. What lives in it is a consortium — four or five bacteria that anyone here could describe by behaviour and nobody could name, a fungus that makes the day-five *iron*, and something else that is only known by its absence, because when it goes the fibre comes out strong and brittle and useless for cable. The other five tanks are inoculated from three. Three is inoculated from nothing. It has never been emptied. It has been topped, skimmed, bled, and fed, and during the six-day stops a single person stays behind on a hand-fired grate to hold the tanks within two degrees, and that person is chosen by lot and considered lucky, and there is a small superstition about it that the engineer discourages and participates in.

If tank three goes cold for a day and a half, the works makes second-grade cable for ten years while the nose walks it back. This has happened twice. Both times are in the ledger with the date and the hour and no other comment, and both times everyone alive at the works learns the dates before they learn to read.

The culture is not confined to the tanks. It is in the boards of the channel, in the plank lining, in the silt of the tail slough, in the gut of every shrimp in the tail-race. The terrace is, at a certain scale, one warm animal, and the buildings are organs in it, and the forty-one people are a maintenance behaviour it performs.

## The masons and their wasp

Three fig species. Each is pollinated by one wasp species and by nothing else, and the wasp is one and a half millimetres long and lives about two days as an adult and needs a fig crop in receptive phase within flying distance at the moment it emerges or its line ends there.

Two buildings stand entirely on fig. The stack has a fig at the twelve-metre course that is bearing. Therefore: the smallest load-bearing member of the Culm Works is an insect that is eleven days from egg to death, and the canopy pair's fig calendar exists to make certain that there is never a month in which no tree of a given species is in crop. They stagger their cutting to keep the phases apart. They will leave a crop they want to cut, for a year, because the calendar says the wasps have nowhere else to be.

This is in the first ledger too, phrased as a rule, in the same flat way as the other one:

*You may not close the last crop.*

Nobody here would call it care. They would call it structure.

## The animals that are staff

**The shrimp** are an instrument. The tail-race runs clear and thick with them, and when a tank begins to go wrong the shrimp thin out below the sluice two days before the nose can smell it, and the sluice keeper counts them every morning against a scratched plate and reports a number. This is why no poison has ever been laid at the works for anything, and it is why —

**The rats** are still here. One lineage in the tow store, closed for an unknown number of generations, dark, short-tailed, about a third of them with a white throat flash that you will not find in any rat downstream. They are not fed and not hunted and not feared. They are eighty or ninety animals living in the same forty cubic metres of tow their ancestors lived in, and they walk over sleeping people without hurry. They are, in the strictest sense, natives in a way the humans are not: no rat has arrived here or left here in living memory.

**The barker** lives in the engine house and has for years, the same one, they say, though it cannot be. A gecko of that kind lives seven or eight years. The ledger records it as a single animal because the ledger records the post, not the occupant, and no engineer has ever seen a reason to change the convention. There have been, at a guess, eighteen or twenty. It eats the moths that come to the carbon lamps, which is to say it eats a food supply the works manufactures, and it barks three or four times at dusk, and the oiler's last round of the evening begins on the third bark, and has for as long as anyone can reconstruct.

**The ants** on the figs are tended, in the sense that the ones tending scale are left alone because they keep a borer off, and the other species is fought at the drying racks with hot water and has been losing and winning this war on a roughly eleven-year cycle since before the second rebore.

## The heron

It is not a resident. That is the point of it.

Every ninth or tenth day a grey heron comes down onto the sluice cheek, stands in the noise for about forty minutes, takes shrimp, and goes. People stop what they are doing. Not everyone, and not for long, and they do not comment. Somebody a long time ago began entering the visits in the back of the sluice book — date, arrival, departure, weather — and it has been kept up since, four thousand entries and more, and from the record it is perfectly clear that it is not one heron and never was. The record is kept anyway.

It is the only thing at the Culm Works that comes and goes because it chooses to.

## The dead

You cannot dig here. The water table is a hand's breadth under the topsoil and above it in the fifth month.

So they burn, on a rack of spent-fibre brick, three hours, and the ash goes into the head slough — the deeper one, the upstream one, the one that has never taken tank water. Everything the works discards goes downstream. Its people go up.

The canopy pair open a light-well over the pyre, which is the one cut made here for no productive reason, and they do not maintain it. It closes over the following year at whatever rate the crowns decide, and when it has closed, the mourning is understood to be finished, and nobody has to say so.

The name goes in the first ledger, in the margin of the day's canopy entry, in the same hand-size as everything else.

## The disagreement

The fig on the stack is at the twelve-metre course, moving at about eight centimetres a decade, and there is an argument about whether it will one day have to come down.

The argument is older than any living participant. It is conducted in the ledger, in the margins, at intervals of a generation, and each side has been held by four successive people who inherited the position along with the post. The last move was made nineteen years ago by the previous engineer, four lines, courteous. A reply is being composed. It has been being composed for eleven years.

It will not come down. The rule holds and it is bearing, and everyone on both sides knows this, and the argument is maintained the way the culture in tank three is maintained, because a works that stopped arguing about the stack fig would be a works that had stopped thinking about the stack fig, and the one thing nobody here will risk is an unattended load.

---

So: forty-one people, one engine with a woman's name, one culture at blood heat with no name at all, three fig species and three wasp species, a lineage of rats, a succession of geckos recorded as one, an uncountable quantity of shrimp that function as a dial, and a heron that is not one heron.

The count is forty-one, and the count is wrong, and the engineer knows it is wrong, and writes it down as forty-one, because the first ledger has a column for people and the people are the part that has to be fed.
