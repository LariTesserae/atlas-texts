## The People of the Mound

They are small. Not stunted — well made, quick, and strong through the shoulders and forearms — but small, and getting smaller, or rather the ones who stay are the ones who fit. A tall child on the mound is a problem to be solved. By nine or ten they will already be barred from half the rooms they were born knowing, and by fifteen they will be doing outside work: cutting rush in the cold flats, tending traps, walking out with the traders. It is not a punishment and nobody frames it as one. It is simply that the town has an aperture, and some people pass through it and some do not.

Their skin is the colour of the inside of a shell, not by descent but by light. They live in slot-light. Down in the covered lanes the illumination comes in bars and coins from vents three rooms above, filtered through other people's floors, and it moves as the sun moves, and everyone knows the hour by which particular patch of wall is lit. Eyes adapt. They blink slowly, they see very well in near dark, and out on the flats at midday they squint and weep and hate it.

Their feet are horn. The terrace lobes grow sharp in spring and everyone limps a little in the first weeks of the new growth, adults grumbling, children bleeding freely and being told it will pass, which it does, into a sole you could strike a fire on.

And their hands are the most worked part of any of them. The scraping hand has a flat plate of callus at the base of the thumb and a forearm a third thicker than its mate. The stroke is short, damp, hissing, and it is timed to the breath — one push, one huff — so that at dawn and at dusk the entire mound breathes in a loose ragged unison, three hundred people exhaling against stone, and this is the thing that unsettles visitors more than the silence. The silence at least is empty. The breathing is not.

### Hands in the walls

Press a palm to a wall here for a season and the wall takes a mould of it. They discovered this the way you discover anything, by accident and then on purpose, and they have been doing it on purpose for a very long time.

A child is named at its first scraping-right, usually at four or five, and at the naming the small hand is held flat against the door-jamb of the house — held there, by an adult, for as long as the child can bear it, and then again the next day, and the next, for a season. What is left is a shallow negative palm the size of a leaf, which will be there when the child is old, and when the child is a pillar, and when the pillar is holding up somebody else's door.

The jambs are crowded with them. Older prints are shallower, glazed over, softened by a hundred years of film; the deep crisp ones are recent. A man can put his hand into his own hand at four and find it a doll's. Married pairs press side by side to mark the joining of their rights. The last thing done to a body before it is carried to the laying-out terrace is to press its right palm once more, at the height it lived at.

You can read the whole immigration history of the town off the height of the handprints. There is one line — Wet-Foot stock, come in through the traders six generations back — whose palms sit a full head above everyone else's, and whose descendants still duck slightly in doorways they do not need to duck in.

### How they are quiet

They are not quiet out of reverence or discipline. They are quiet because the air will not carry and the stone will.

Their speech has thinned toward what the stone likes. It is a consonant language — clicks, stops, taps of the tongue, hard little edges — with the vowels gone soft and short and almost decorative. When they whisper it at conversational range, twenty paces at most, it sounds like small stones being sorted. When they knock it on a wall with the knuckles, it is the *same language*, minus the breath. There is no separate code. The tapping is not a signalling system laid over speech; it is speech with the air taken out.

This is why a stranger can stand in the middle of the town and feel it is deserted and being watched at once. The floor under him is full of grammar.

At night, in certain seasons, they sing. It is done mouth-closed against the wall, a hum pushed into the stone, and it travels; a house will pick it up, and the house above, and the note spreads through the solid fabric until the mound is very faintly ringing. Lie down in a strange room and you will feel it come up through the bedding and think it is the spring. It is not the spring. The spring has no tune.

### Small-work

The narrowest and most important surfaces on the mound — the vent-throats, the high crawls, the gaps between the shoulders of two houses that grew together three hundred years ago — can only be scraped by someone with a chest the width of a hand.

So children work. From about five to about eleven they are the only ones who can reach a third of the town's growing edges, and they go into those places alone with a paddle and a basket, and the maintenance of the settlement depends on them absolutely. It is the only period of an inhabitant's life when they are irreplaceable, and they know it, and they are unbearable about it.

Then they grow, and the town closes on them.

Every adult here carries a private map of rooms they will never enter again: a bend above the west terrace with a thumbprint in it that they made; a chimney-crawl where the stone is warm all night and you can hear four households eating. They talk about these places the way people elsewhere talk about a country they left as a child. Occasionally an adult will send a son or daughter up into a particular crawl with instructions — *go left where it narrows, feel the ceiling, tell me if the hand is still there* — and wait below, not looking at anybody.

### Rights, names, and the closing of a door

Everything a person owns here is a surface they are obliged to keep open. A household's wealth is measured in doorways, stair-treads, lane-frontage, sleeping-vents. Marriage is the merging of two scraping-schedules, which is a real and difficult negotiation, and which is why the town's genealogies are recorded as maps.

A name is a surface. A person is *the third stair*, *the low lintel*, *the two vents under the snail terrace*. Rights are gained, traded, inherited, lost, and so names move around, and an old woman may have been four different people. When someone dies, their name goes quiet for a season out of tact, and then is given to whoever takes the surface. There is no confusion about this. They know who is in the walls.

The punishment for serious offences is the withdrawal of the right to scrape, and it is measured not in days but in thickness. *Sentenced to a thumb.* The household stands and watches its own doorway narrow, and the offender scrapes nothing, and everyone else scrapes as usual on all sides, and the closing is the whole of the penalty. Two thumbs is grave. Three is almost never given, because after three thumbs a small room may not be recoverable by any labour, and then you have executed a house as well as punished a man.

A person with no rights at all has no name and is said to be *without surface*. They sleep on the west paving where the spread scrapings are drying. They are fed — nobody starves on the mound, it is far too small a place for that — but they are fed silently, and the food is left, not handed, and this is understood by everyone involved to be worse than hunger.

### The cutters at the lip

At the crown, in the blood-warm overflow, a dozen people work the curd.

They stand ankle- and knee-deep in the spill for half the morning with shell spatulas and bone saws and bowls of grit, slicing block after hand-sized block out of stone that is four days old, and passing them hand to hand down the terraces to cool and set. It is the most skilled work in the town and the shortest career. The water is not good to be in. It takes the oil out of the skin, then the skin, then it starts on what is under the skin. A cutter's hands at forty are white, fissured, bent, and half-sheathed in a rind of the town itself; they cannot close, and they do not feel heat.

Retired cutters are the town's authorities. They sit in the warm lanes with their ruined beautiful hands in their laps and are consulted about everything, and everyone touches those hands in passing the way they pat the pillars, and for the same reason. It is understood on the mound that the stone begins taking a person long before they lie down on the laying-out terrace — that it starts at the hands, that it goes on all one's life, and that death is only where the process becomes obvious. The cutters are simply further along.

So the pillars in the doorways are not a transformation of the dead. They are the finished version of the living.

### The listener

In the deepest room of the oldest house, below three floors of drowned centuries, someone lies with an ear to the old stone. It is often a blind person, because they are best at it, but not always; it is a talent, not a compensation.

The listener does not scrape and therefore has no name, and is fed by every household in rotation, and is the only honoured nameless person in the town. What they do is hold the whole of it at once: the tapping in the walls, the rasp of the snails on the north terraces, the shifting of a bad wall four streets away, and under everything the forty-beat pulse in the throat of the spring, which is felt in the bones of the mound before it is seen at the vent.

It is the listener who knocks the five-note figure when the pulse changes. It has been knocked eleven times in living memory. Nine times it meant nothing. Twice it meant a season of scraping the wrong surfaces, and the loss of a quarter of the lower lanes, and a hunger that people still date things from.

### The others who live here

The **snails** on the shaded north terraces graze the algae film with a fine dry rasp that is audible all night in the rooms below, and which is the sound the town hushes its infants with. Nobody has ever said aloud that the snails are doing what the people are doing, keeping a surface clean in the dark, but a woman will nod at them.

The **blind pink fish** come up the warm channels and are taken in wicker traps, and taste of chalk, and are never trapped within a certain distance of the vent, for no reason anyone can now give.

The **pale wader** comes once or twice a day to stalk the shallows, and sometimes stands too long, and finds its legs sheathed and setting. The person who first sees it is the person who must free it, with a wooden mallet, kneeling in cold water, holding a panicking bird the height of a child. It is considered luck. The birds do not learn. *To stand like a bird* is what they say here about anyone who stays too long in a warm place that is quietly closing on them, and they say it about the birds, and they do not say it about themselves.

The **marsh-cat** comes to the edge of the light perhaps eight times a year and sits and does not approach and is gone. They believe it is one cat and always has been. It has a name that means roughly *the one who will not come in*. A fish is left out. The fish is always gone by morning and everyone knows perfectly well what takes it. Children who have seen the cat are permitted to tell about it at length, several times, for weeks, and are listened to.

And the **tree** at the crown — the crooked half-encased willow-thing — is scraped with wooden spoons every eighth day by two households who have held that right for longer than the town can count, who alternate by four days, who check each other's work with the suspicion of centuries, who have intermarried eleven times and are not friends. Their children are the only children on the mound whose whole lives are known in advance. They talk to it while they work. It is the only thing here that is scraped for its own sake and not for the town's, and if you ask them why they keep it, they look at you as though you had asked why they keep breathing, and go back to the bark.

### What they are, finally

Three hundred people inside a stone that is being made out of them, scraping at it twice a day with shell and paddle, breathing in unison at dusk, talking to each other through the floor in a language with the air taken out, their hands pressed into every doorway they ever passed through, their grandparents holding up the arches, their dead patted in passing, digging downward when they need a cellar into rooms where the bowls still stand under the ice of the stone — and a word for that digging that also means remembering.

They know exactly what would happen if they stopped. It would take about ten years. The lanes would close, then the rooms, then the roofs would smooth over into lobes and fans, and the mound would be a clean white swelling in the flats with a column of mist standing over it, and the water would go on swelling and spilling and slumping, forty beats, forty beats, entirely unbothered, laying down its film over everything they ever made and everyone they ever were.

This is not a fear here. It is closer to a fact of weather. It is the reason for the paddles and the rasping and the small cut hands in the dark crawls, and it is why, on a hard dry windy day in late autumn when nothing is growing and nobody has to scrape, they all climb up onto the roofs to be cold and idle and shout at each other across four streets like people in a country with room in it.
