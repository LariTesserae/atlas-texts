## What you will look for, and why you will not find it

You will look up.

That is the mistake, and it is not a foolish one; it is simply the wrong register. You will scan the thirty-metre landmarks for windows. You will go into the slab-roofed halls stooped and look for a doorway sized to something upright. You will decide, after two weeks, that the Level is an inheritance — that its makers are gone, that the swarms are running a bequest, that this is a machine mowing a lawn for a family that moved away.

Then a column you have been leaning against for the length of a Comb will move off upstream.

## The body

Begin, as with the water, with the fact of the depth, because they are the reason for it.

Blood-warm at the surface, a shade cooler at the ankle: that is their skin temperature and their tolerance. Knee-deep in the avenues: that is the draught of a loaded adult. The Level's two great constants are not geography. They are a specification, and the specified object is an animal about four metres long, a metre and a half across the shoulders, and no higher off the ground than a large dog.

They go low and wide, belly riding the surface, so that the water carries most of their weight and their legs are freed to be something better than pillars. The hind pair are short, broad-footed, and push. The fore pair are long, jointed twice, and reach — plant, and haul, and release — so the gait is not a walk but a slow rowing, a hand-over-hand along the floor of the world. They cross a district the way a swimmer crosses a pool: unhurried, inevitable, leaving a wake with structure in it. On stone above the water they are clumsy and unhappy and will not stay long. There is almost nowhere above the water. This is not a coincidence.

At the end of each reaching forelimb is not a hand but a comb: sixty to ninety fine filaments, wet, pink, restless, each one independently muscled. With that comb they feel stone, sweep hobblers, part the mat, test a current, and speak.

Their eyes are set low and very wide, at the waterline, made for glare and for horizontal distance; they read the lifted, bowed, wrongly-ordered horizon as a legible document, and they use the mirage the way you use a map, knowing that a tower standing up out of the haze in the wrong place means a hall forty kilometres off has changed its temperature. There is a second eye, simple, on the dorsal ridge, which does nothing but watch ceilings. It is for the green.

## The stone

Their backs are banded pale, in fine daily laminations, exactly like the walls.

This is not camouflage, or not only. The swarms do not distinguish between a person and a pier. A back left to itself accretes at the same rate as any other wet horizontal surface in the Level, and so every adult carries a growing shell of aragonite that records, in bands, precisely as long as they have gone ungroomed.

So they scrape. Constantly, and on one another, because no comb can reach its own spine. Two adults will lie flank to flank in a current-line for an hour, each working the other's ridge back down to the correct worn radius — and the *correct* is the whole of their culture. There are ways of scraping. There are regions, lineages, schools. A tight scallop with a sharp shoulder is the manner of the eastern sluice districts; a soft continuous dishing, barely a gesture, belongs to the people who work the quarry basins. You can tell where a stranger was raised by the shape of the wear on the body of the friend who last groomed them.

And this is why the city is worn. The swarms do not have an inherited taste for erosion. They learned it. They repair toward the shapes they are shown, and for a very long time the shapes they have been shown are these: the dished threshold that a belly drags across, the scalloped pier base that a shoulder rests against, the four-step stair that is not a stair at all but a haul-out, a place to lift the chest clear of the flood and be visible and be touched. Every soft radius in ten thousand square kilometres is a cast of a body. The continent is a fossil of a gait, refreshed nightly.

## Speech

They do not shout, because shouting is useless, and they do not speak, because speech is a thing you do at a distance.

Language here is conducted comb-to-flank, skin on skin, two bodies pressed side by side and moving together, the filaments working the other's hide in long braided runs — a syntax like weaving, slow, reversible, with the capacity to hold three subjects at once across three separate patches of skin. It is not primitive. It is dense and it is grammatical and it is utterly local. To be out of reach is to be mute. To be mute for a day is a kind of illness. They are never out of reach, because nothing here is ever empty.

At range they have only the green: the same photogenic symbiont that upholsters the roof voids, carried along their flanks in two long strips. It is a poor language, three hundred signs at most, and it is shared — the flyers use it too, and mean roughly the same things by it, and have for so long that nobody can say who taught whom. At dusk a person lying against a pier will flicker, and the ceiling above will answer, and the answer will run off across the district faster than any body could carry it. *Collapse at the third arcade. Mat bloom souring. Bore early.* The city's news travels on the underside of its roofs, sideways to the water, in a language with no word for most things.

And twice a day, the Comb — which is, for them, the parliament.

Eleven seconds of withheld breath, and then the step of water, and every adult in the Level is already braced upstream of stone with its neighbours jammed against it flank to flank, because there is nowhere else to be and nothing else to do. For the quarter of an hour it takes the rollers to pass, the entire population is in contact. That is when the long conversations happen. That is when the teaching happens, and the arguing, and the deciding, and the grieving. Between Combs they scatter and feed in silence. Twice a day the society reassembles by force of physics, talks at full bandwidth in the roar, and disperses. They have organised eleven thousand years of civic life around being unable to get away from each other.

## Hearing

They hear beautifully. They simply never use it for each other.

The roar is not noise to them; it is the architecture, restated. Several billion colonnades dividing a continental sheet of moving water produce a sound with chords in it, and the chords are positional — every hall has a voice, every arcade a formant, every sluice throat a note it makes when open and a different one when shut. A person knows where they are in the Level the way you know which room of your house you are standing in with the lights off, except that the house is ten thousand square kilometres wide and audible in its entirety. When a pier twenty kilometres away reaches the end of its scalloping and goes down, they feel it as you feel a door opening behind you: not as sound, as *information about the room*.

They are, in consequence, unhousetrainable outside the Level. Silence is not restful to them. Silence is the eleven seconds.

## Eating, and the retinue

They comb. A sweep of the forelimb takes four hundred hobblers off a pavement at once, and the comb folds and the mouth takes them, and this is most of the diet, and the diet is therefore governed entirely by the migrations of the mat. When a slick blooms kilometres wide the people go to it; when it crashes they leave, and the leaving is a slow scattered current of bodies moving all night, flickering.

They are followed. Tallwaders learn within days that a moving person stirs the shallows, and a well-known adult will travel with thirty or forty of them stalking alongside, eyeless, absolutely still and then absolutely fast, taking what the wake lifts. Nobody feeds them; nobody drives them off; there is no word for the relationship. It has simply always been the case that a person walking is a person with grey stilted attendants, and a person walking alone is either very young or in disgrace or nearly finished.

## The young

They spawn into the dark — into the pumped lower floors, the lightless levels, where the water is warm and reduced and full of larvae. Nobody attends this. Nobody could; the sub-floors are for blind swimmers and for things with no eyes to spare.

Four years later small crawling versions come up through the sluice throats at night, and are taken in by whoever they touch first. That is the entire adoption ceremony: contact. They are taught by being groomed, and they learn language on their own skin before they can produce it, and their lineage is not blood but *manner* — you belong to the school whose scraping you carry. Two siblings from one spawning may spend their lives four hundred kilometres apart and be, in every sense that matters here, unrelated; two strangers with the same shoulder-scallop will lie down together at a Comb and talk like family.

## Finishing

An old one stops scraping.

That is all it is. There is no other signal and no other threshold. The stone begins to accumulate along the spine, and thickens, and the neighbours will offer once, twice, and then stop offering; and the body slows under the weight it is no longer refusing; and at some point in the last months the finishing one chooses a place and goes to it and braces upstream and stands.

The choice is the only public act of a life, and it is taken seriously, and it is argued over at Combs for years in advance. Because they know what they become. A standing body accretes at the rate of any wet vertical surface; within a season it is a pier; within a decade it is a pier the swarms have begun to repair. So the question is a civic one, and it is asked in exactly these terms: *where is a column needed, and of what size, and facing how?* To complete an arcade. To split a current that has been scouring a filter-fan bank for years. To narrow a throat and raise the chord of a hall half a tone, because the people downstream of it have been complaining about the navigation since before you were spawned.

And afterwards the swarms keep them. Repaired back to worn, forever, at precisely the correct radius, in the manner of their school — because the swarms learned the correct from the bodies, and here is a body, and the loop closes.

Every pier in the Level has bands. Read them from the base upward and for the first stretch they are a life: fast in youth, dense in the good years, interrupted wherever someone was groomed by a friend who scraped too deep. Then there is a horizon in the stone, thin and dark, where the scraping stopped. Then there is everything since.

You have been leaning on them. You have walked, stooped, through halls held up by a congregation. You have felt something sweep your ankles in the dark and thought it was the hobblers, and it was language, and it was a question, and by the time you had decided it was nothing the current had closed behind them.
