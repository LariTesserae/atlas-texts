## What lives here

One thing lives here. I want to say that first, and then spend the rest of this spoiling it, because it is true at the scale of the plateau and false at the scale of a room.

At the scale of the plateau: the Stand is a single organism, or a single lineage so tightly plumbed to itself that the distinction has stopped paying rent. Twelve to fifteen kilometers of it. Eighty to a hundred meters deep. Between four and nine hundred thousand towers depending on the hour, all sharing oil, all sharing pressure, all sharing the one decision.

At the scale of a room, there are half a dozen kinds of resident, and one of them has hands.

## The Stock, as a body

Stop thinking forest. Think animal, if it helps, and then throw that away too, because it eats like neither.

The body is a heat engine with a chemistry hung off it. Its mouth is at the bottom: black rootwork in the hot seam, cables the thickness of a torso, and at their tips a fine grazing fringe that works the rock joints for gases — carbonate, sulfide, hydrogen, whatever the seam is exhaling that week. Its lungs are at the top: the vanes, membrane-thin, the coldest tissue on the plateau, dumping heat into the lid of dust. Its gut is the whole distance in between. Nothing about the oil is digestive. The oil is the working fluid. Thirty to a hundred and forty meters of thermosiphon, straw-colored, terpene-stinking, rising hot in the inner rods and falling cool in the outer, and forced on the way up through capillary beds so narrow that the flow strips charge off the walls. That is where the electricity comes from. Not a nerve. A dynamo. The Stand is a plant that has monetized its own plumbing.

Its waste is the sky. Every polymerization in a growing wall throws off a light lipid vapor — no water anywhere in the reaction, no water anywhere in the world — and the vapor climbs and stays, and has been staying for aeons, and is now the lid.

Which is the tragedy of the tenant, if a tenant this old is allowed one. The lid is a blanket. The blanket warms the vanes. The vanes are the appetite. Every meter the Stock grows, it breathes out a little more ceiling, and the ceiling takes a little more of the cold away, and so it must grow another meter. The Stand is not ambitious. The Stand is running from its own breath, at the speed of a fingernail's thickness of laminate at a time, and losing.

*Height is hunger.* The rule is not a poem. It is a debt schedule.

## How it thinks

It has no nerves and it does not need them, and I would like to be careful here, because I spent a long time deciding it was stupid.

The thinking is done in the manifolds. Every junction in the Combs has a valve-body, and every valve-body is a small dumb comparator: pressure here against pressure there, temperature against a set point held in the stiffness of a resin spring. Thousands of them, in series and parallel, voting. A tower drops behind on its gradient; its return oil comes back a few degrees wrong; the wrongness propagates as a pressure signature; three hundred valves shift by a fraction of a millimeter each; a threshold is crossed somewhere it would take you a week to find; and the tower is cut off and left to cook. From first fault to sentence: about a day. That is not slowness. That is the round-trip time of hydraulic light in a body this size.

Its clock is the reciprocating engines. One stroke every eleven seconds. Everything in the Stand is timed in strokes, and a decision costs eight thousand of them, and there has never been anything worth hurrying for.

And then the gauges. This is the detail that turned the place over for me. The dials were made for eyes — hairline needles, graduated arcs, resin faces polished to a shine that is useless in the dark. Nothing here has eyes. So the Stock has grown, over each dial, a flat pale feeler that lies across the face like a hand across a page, and rides the needle, and reports its position by touch back into the wall. It rebuilt an instrument for a reader that never came, and then grew the reader's fingertip and left the rest out. The gauge is now a sense organ, in the way a scar is a memory.

## The grades

Below the lineage, there are forks. I have used working names; they are mine, and provisional, and I would not defend them.

**Radiator-grade** is the oldest and the least interesting up close and the most impressive from any distance: the rim towers, a hundred meters and up, vanes interlocked into that black ceiling. Pure engine. Almost no manufacture. It exists to be cold, and to hold the ring, and to feed the middle, and its whole life is the ticking. Touch its column at knee height and it is blood-warm. Touch it at shoulder height and it takes heat out of your palm. That is the animal, right there, in twenty centimeters of trunk: the difference is the creature.

**Comb-grade** is the builder and the manufacturer and, in every sense that matters, the town's population. It is shorter, fatter, densely valved, and it spends its living on structure: walls, floors, lintels, ducting, pipe. It is the grade that keeps the plan. Its meristems produce architecture the way another plant produces leaves — a chamber a week, walled and floored and plumbed, with a doorway of a specific height and a stair of a specific rise, and a bench.

**Wire** is barely a body. It is the conductor grade: soft grey pith laced with drawn metal, growing as busbar along the Comb ceilings, splicing itself into every new run within hours. It cannot stand up. It cannot feed. It lives entirely inside the others, and if you cut a meter of it out, the meter it grows back has the same cross-section to within a hair, because the plan specifies bar stock.

**Piper** grows pipe and only pipe, in eleven bores, and dies at both ends into fittings.

**Rotter** is the undertaker: the small hard fruiting bodies out in the Slack, and along the base of every cooked tower, cooking dead structure in their own little kilns and handing the carbon back. Waterless rot. It smells like a hot workshop and a spice you can't name, and it is the only smell in the Stand strong enough to follow.

None of these interbreed directly any more. Their gametes ride the belts in sealed sleeves, and the sorting tables decide who meets whom, and the tables have been running the same crosses for longer than I can put a number on. The factory is the flower — you have that already. What I would add is that the factory is also the chaperone, and the registry, and the fence. Comb-grade has not chosen a mate in an age. It fills out a form.

## The hangers-on

**Bloom** is the white mineral crust on the rootwork, and it may be a symbiont or a secretion or a domesticated something-else; I could not tell, and the Stock treats it as tissue. It insulates. Scrape it and it comes back grey and then white in nine days.

**Wick** is a thief. It is a thin dark vine that grows sideways through a pith wall until it meets an oil line, and taps it, and drinks flow it did not lift. It is the only thing in the Stand that has an easy life, and it lasts about a month, because the valve-bodies notice the pressure drop and the district shuts the line and the district lets the Wick cook. You find them as black scribbles inside broken walls, like veins in marble, dead in the act.

**And the heretics.** Out in the Slack, in the hot voids under the char, where the manifolds have collapsed and no valve has voted in a hundred years, something has dropped the plan.

They are squat. Wide, low domes, vanes spread flat almost at ground level, no towers, no chambers, no ducting, no manufacture whatsoever. They are self-fertile. They go from propagule to spore in a season, which is an obscene tempo here. They do not make fittings. They do not make anything. They eat the digestion of the district that failed, and they crackle faintly as they grow, and they are the only life on the plateau that looks like it is enjoying itself.

They cannot get out. The Combs' meristems overgrow them at the boundary; the Pan is four kilometers of glass; the rim is three hundred meters of the oldest and most orthodox wood in the world. They are a rebellion in a jar. If the plan ever breaks — if the ceiling thickens past what the tallest vanes can beat — I think these are what inherits, and I think what inherits will not build anything, ever again.

## Under everything

At the seam, in the joints of the hot bedrock, there are films. Sheets of hyperthermophile tissue a few cells thick, unstructured, ancient, indifferent, spread through the rock the way damp spreads through plaster in a world that has damp.

They were here first. The Stock's grazing fringe crops them, and also, in a way I can only describe as a very old arrangement, contains them: there are film-derived organelles in every cell of every tower on the plateau. The Stand's whole magnificent apparatus — the ring, the town, the belts, the warehouses — is a device that certain slime in a crack grew in order to stay near the crack while reaching somewhere colder.

Everybody up here is a suburb of that.

## The shift

Now the hands.

I looked for the worker for a long time and did not find it, and then I found it and did not recognize it, because it came out of a wall.

In the working chambers of the Combs, at about chest height, there are sockets — I took them for drainage — and roughly once a week, on a schedule I could not crack, a socket ripens and delivers a body.

Call them Fitters. They are fruit.

Sixty to eighty centimeters of tapered pith trunk, glossy, dark, dry, weighing what a full satchel weighs. Four legs on the common form, short and jointed and bad at stairs. No mouth. No gut. No eyes, though on the upper stub, where the plan clearly implies a head, some of them carry a rosette of shallow thermal pits arranged in a pattern that would be a face if any of the pits worked. They do not. It is trim. It is the ornament on a cabinet.

What they have is hands.

Two of them, on the common form, on long flexible forelimbs, and the hands are *good*. Pads. Opposition. Fine control. Four flexures to the digit where you have three. And every grip on every tool in every rack in the Combs fits them exactly, which is the wrong way round and took me an embarrassingly long time to feel the wrongness of. The tools did not come first and get hands. The grips came first — they are as old as the plan — and the hand is being grown, freshly, weekly, forever, to fit a handle designed for somebody else.

They run on stored pressure. Charged at the socket like a wound spring; no feeding, no metabolism worth the name; blind, deaf in any useful sense, navigating by warmth and lipid trace and the vibration of the belts. They oil pulleys. They clear a jam. They dress a sorting table, square a stack, carry a sealed sleeve from a table to a hopper — which means, if you have been keeping up, that these are the pollinators, and that the plateau's total animal life is nine days long and comes out of the plumbing.

They make a sound. A small dry click as the finger flexures lock and release. In a working chamber with four or five of them and the blue-white node lit above the bench, it is exactly, unbearably, the sound of a room where somebody is doing careful work with their fingernails.

At about day nine the pressure goes. They begin to move toward heat, which is down, and they get to a reclamation floor, or near one, and they stop, and the Rotters find them within the day. The floor around every reclamation vent has a scatter of them, sitting, tipped, hands still closed.

## The one that isn't a tool

Rarely — I saw four in a long stay, and two were dead already — the socket delivers something else.

Two legs. Two arms. A pelvis of pith, a torso with a ribbed sheath, a stub with a full rosette on it, and a standing height of about two meters ten.

Two meters ten is the height of the Comb doorways. The leg length matches the rise and run of the stairs. I measured. I measured several times, in the dark, by hand, because measuring gave me something to do.

They are not good at it. Two legs is hard and nothing here has practiced. They stand up out of the socket and they walk badly, ankles rolling on the laminate, one hand on the wall, and they go — not at random; I would like it to have been at random — through the doorway their height was cut for, and along the street, and into one of the rooms in which nothing has ever happened, and they sit down on the bench
