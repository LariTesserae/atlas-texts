## The Film

Begin with the film, because everything else in the Bowl is an argument about the film.

It is not one organism. It is somewhere between four hundred and four thousand lineages, depending on how you want to count things that trade genes across the gaps between them, and it has been sitting on this stone since before the stone was cut, or since shortly after — the distinction is one of the several that the Bowl declines to preserve. It lives on sulphide and iron and hydrogen coming up warm through the seams of the masonry, and on the masonry itself. The grey-green material is not inert. It carries reduced metals in its matrix, and the mats strip them, molecule by molecule, at a rate that would be undetectable in any reasonable span of observation and has nonetheless taken four metres off the top of the encircling wall.

This is worth sitting with. The film eats the architecture. The film also secretes, as waste, a fine carbonate flour that is the cement in every mound, every tender-packed joint, every original terrace fill anyone has ever broken open. The same organisms are dissolving the Bowl and supplying the material with which it is patched. They are not aware of doing either. They have no strategy for dispersal beyond being torn loose and flung into the dark, which happens to all of them, regularly, and is how they get everywhere.

Everything else here is a way of making a living off that film, or off the animals that do.

## Tenders

Thumbnail-sized, hard, dull as wet gravel. There are more of them than of anything else with legs, and they are the only animals in the Bowl that hold a fixed address.

A tender owns roughly a metre of joint line. It works that metre continuously in the flooded half of the seiche, running its mouthparts along the seam, scraping away the purple mats (which are unpalatable, possibly toxic — no tender has ever been observed to eat one twice) and encouraging the iron-red, which it crops in rotation, never taking a patch to the stone, always leaving enough to regrow. Call this farming if you like. It is a behaviour perhaps nine motions long, executed some forty million times in a life.

They cannot swim. They are, at the scale that matters here, immovable objects on a plain that is periodically swept clean by moving water, and they have exactly one answer to this. A falling cold plume changes the taste of the water before it changes anything else — a thinning, a flatness, oxygen and metal both going wrong at once — and every tender in the Bowl detects it within a few minutes of every other tender, and clamps. The shell locks down into the joint it has spent its life cleaning. The joint fits it. It has made the joint fit it. The silt blizzard passes over a landscape of stone with several million small sealed stones set into its seams, and takes nothing.

Then the water clears and they open, into a world scraped down to bare polish, and starve. It takes six to nine weeks for the mats to come back. In that time a tender resorbs its own gonads, then the soft tissue of its gut lining, then whatever else is not required for the nine motions. Most survive. Those that do will not breed that season. They go on cleaning empty seams — the motions run anyway, on nothing, mouthparts working bare stone — until the film returns, and then they eat, and then they rebuild the parts of themselves they spent.

The walls of the Bowl stand because of this, and no tender has ever been in a position to notice a wall.

## Mound-makers

Palm-wide, plated, low-slung; a suggestion of segmentation under the carapace; eight short working limbs and two long ones for reaching. They take silt into a gizzard-like chamber, mix it with carbonate flour gathered from the mats and a protein of their own, and lay it down in beads, and the beads set hard in about four hours.

What they build is entirely determined by what they have to build with. On a shelf where the seiche drops coarse grains, they make chimneys — narrow, tapering, up to a metre, with the brood chamber at the constriction where flow accelerates and keeps the young oxygenated. Where the silt is fine and slow, the same animals with the same behaviours produce low walls, arcs, the long sinuous berms that a person swimming over would swear were laid out with an eye to drainage. They were laid out by the drainage. The animal has no plan, only a set of responses to grain size, current, and the smell of a seep, and the shapes that fall out of those responses are as various as the shelves are.

They have no memory worth the name. A mound-maker displaced by an overturn — and they are all displaced, every one, several times a lifetime, tumbled anywhere from ten metres to five kilometres — does not look for home. It rights itself, tastes for sulphide, walks up the gradient until the gradient stops improving, and begins. The enormous mounds, the cottage-sized ones with forty generations of re-occupation in them, are not monuments. They are simply places where the smell is good and the silt collects, and so the arriving animals keep arriving and keep building on what they find, which is other building.

Their cement matches the terrace fill exactly. The Bowl offers no opinion on this and neither will I.

## Sift-swimmers

The only animals here that build in something other than stone.

A column is a structure. Thousands of thumb-length bodies, silver-black, holding station in a vertical file with their fans out, each one riding the slight acceleration of water past the one above it — the column steers current through itself, concentrates the thin suspended food into a usable stream, and cannot be disassembled without destroying the thing that makes it work. Take out the top third and the rest starve. It is architecture with no material in it but appetite and position, and it is the most fragile thing in the Bowl.

It gets destroyed every time. Every overturn, every column, torn into dust and scattered across kilometres of dark.

They spawn on the tearing. The same flatness in the water that clamps the tenders triggers release in every sift-swimmer simultaneously, and the collapsing water column — the catastrophe, the blizzard, the thing that strips the mats and erases a season of mounds — is also, for about four hours, the richest broth the Bowl ever produces: mat fragments, silt, and the entire year's spawn, mixed and hurled about and carried everywhere. The survivors reassemble in the clearing water over the following two days, in new places, in new numbers, with whoever they find. Columns do not have identities. There are simply more or fewer of them.

Their eyes cannot resolve the terraces, or the rim, or a hand. They measure exactly one thing, which is how fast the dark is getting darker, and they use it to know when to let go.

## Sluice-eels

Blind, pale, arm-long, unhurried. They live in the corbelled tunnels and the sluice mouths and they are the only animals in the Bowl that travel with intent.

Each tunnel resonates. The seiche drives water through the radiating channels on a forty-minute cycle, and every stretch of slab-roofed passage in the Bowl has its own note, determined by its length and its state of collapse. An eel learns the chord. It knows where it is by pitch — this tunnel, that junction, the low one that runs toward the third seep — and it moves through the Bowl's drainage on a map made of sound, hunting tenders out of joints and young mound-makers off their cones and, when opportunity offers, other eels.

The map is never more than a few weeks old. Slabs shift. A riser gives way and a whole quarter of the Bowl retunes, and the eel, without distress, learns the new chord. It has no capacity to notice that the note it used to steer by is gone forever, because it has no capacity to notice *forever*. It simply lives in a building that is being slowly rearranged around it and updates accordingly, which is more than most residents of ruins manage.

## The slow white

Four feet across at the leg span, jointed, ghost-coloured, a walker on the risers. They eat mats. They eat very little and very slowly and they live, as best anyone can judge from the growth banding in the leg joints, somewhere between two and five centuries.

They do not clamp for an overturn and they do not scatter. They drive their claw-tips into a seam, lower their body mass, and stand — and are buried, and dig out, and resume the same traverse they were making before the water went black. Individual routes have been followed for decades: up the flight, along the fourth shelf, round the collapse, down the ramp to the seep. Whether a route is remembered or merely re-derived every time from the same body walking the same gradients is not a question the animal can be asked. The distinction may not survive contact with the Bowl.

They are, incidentally, the reason anything slow ever crosses open water. A mature slow white is a habitat: mats in the joint of every leg, a dozen tenders riding the carapace and farming it, sometimes a young mound-maker's cone cemented to a femur like a barnacle, and, in the folds of the body, whatever smaller things have found the arrangement congenial. A century of walking moves that cargo nine kilometres. They are the mail. They are also, in the strictest sense available here, the only continuity between the Bowl and itself: the only bodies present now that were also present ten overturnings ago, carrying on their backs a small travelling piece of a shelf that no longer exists.

## Seam-dwellers

Inside the fill.

Break open a failed riser and the packed rubble is honeycombed at fingernail scale — galleries, chambers, blind passages, running metres into the body of the wall. What lives there is soft, colourless, probably three or four unrelated lineages doing the same job, mining the warm seams from within, following the seep chemistry up through the masonry the way roots follow water. They have never been in open water. Generations of them are born, breed and die eight courses deep inside the stone, in the dark, in the interstitial damp.

They dig. What they dig out they pack: spoil cemented into bulkheads that wall off dead galleries and keep the working ones from flooding. There is, inside every wall of the Bowl, a second architecture at one-hundredth the scale, built out of the ruins of the first, maintained by animals that will die if it fails, and collapsing constantly, and being rebuilt.

They are also why the facing goes. A riser with three centuries of galleries in it is not a riser anymore; it is a shell around a void, and one day the seiche leans on it and it lets go, and the sound of it crosses the Bowl as a single dull knock.

## Knock-followers

Which brings in the last of them, and the strangest, because they are the only animals here that move *toward* disaster.

Metre-long, soft, translucent, mostly water, drifting with a slow sculling that gets them perhaps a kilometre an hour. There are not many — a few hundred in the whole Bowl, possibly fewer. They are capable of fasting for a year and routinely do. They have no elaborate senses except one: a pressure organ tuned narrowly, almost absurdly, to the frequency of a hundred tonnes of stone arriving on stone.

When a riser fails, they turn. From across the basin, from the empty water over the polished vacancies, from wherever they have been hanging and doing nothing for eleven months, they turn and begin swimming, and over the following three or four days they converge on the slide — which has crushed a mound colony, pulped a few thousand tenders in their clamped joints, and opened a fresh warm seam to the water. They feed for a week. They spawn there, in the wreckage, which is the only reliable place two of them will ever meet. Then they drift apart into the dark to wait for the next one.

They are patient in a way that looks, to an observer standing in the silt with their sternum still ringing, like devotion. It isn't. It's metabolism. But the Bowl does not offer many animals that arrive somewhere on purpose, and it is hard not to watch them come.

---

Nothing here signals. No colour means anything, since there is no light to mean it in; no animal in the Bowl makes a sound on purpose. Everything that passes between one body and another passes as chemistry in water, or as the shifting of stone, and both are indifferent messages sent by nobody.

What lives here, then: a film that dissolves the walls and supplies the mortar; farmers who seal themselves into the seams they tend and eat their own reproductive organs to outlast the dark; masons with no memory, building whatever the local silt dictates, on the sites of the buildings of strangers; a structure made of hungry bodies that is destroyed annually and reconvened out of survivors; navigators with a map of sound that is retuned around them and relearned without complaint; century-long walkers carrying small portable countrysides on their backs; miners four courses deep in the ruin, walling off their own collapses; and a few hundred slow pale things hanging in the emptiness, fasting, listening for the next knock.

After an overturn the water clears from the top down and the shelves come back into the dim, scoured, mounded with nothing. And somewhere on a polished terrace a mound-maker rights itself, and turns until the sulphide is stronger on one side than the other, and walks up the gradient, and stops where the smell stops improving, and takes in silt.

It has no idea where it is. It never did. It begins.
