## What lives on the Sinter Plateau

**I. The herd, considered from inside**

The great ones have already been described from outside, which is the easy direction. From outside they are districts. From inside they are something much simpler and much harder to write down.

Begin with what a great one has instead of eyes: thermopiles set in the plate-margins, which report the world as a low-resolution map of warmths, and a sense of pressure through the feet, which reports the crust as a texture of *chewed* and *not yet chewed*. That is nearly all the vision it needs. The plateau is flat, the plateau is hot, and nothing on it is fast enough to require watching.

The great sense is hearing, and it hears in *depth*.

Because sound silts up here — pools at different levels, the deep pools old — a great one does not hear a herd. It hears a column. Near the surface, the immediate knocking of its own cams, familiar as breath. Below that, a layer of roar from the neighbouring basin as it stood two hundred years ago. Below that, older strata, thickened, softened, still identifiable by their characteristic slips. And at the bottom of the column, in certain basins, the voices of animals that have not yet dripped, arriving from below like heat from a floor.

So the animal's map of its kin is not a map of places. It is a map of *times*, arranged vertically, and it grazes across that map the way a cow crosses a field. When two great ones pass within a kilometre of each other — an event with a duration of about eleven years — neither registers a meeting. Each registers a thickening in the column, a new lamination, and adjusts its lean.

They have no memory in the sense you would want. They have wear. Every groove, every ovalled bearing, every rounded tooth is a record of what happened, held not as an image but as a *shape that now behaves differently*. The animal cannot recall its history. It can only continue to be its history, loudly, at one kilometre a year. This is why nothing here needs to be taught. A child follows the parent's shape the way iron follows a mould, and both of them are already remembering perfectly, without effort, in the only tense the plateau supports.

And their sense of self is the reason the census fails.

A great one does not distinguish itself from the roar. It knows the roar as its body — the plates and cams as the near part of the body, the neighbouring basins as the far part, the deep strata as the old part. Ask it (you cannot) how many it is, and there is no question there. There is near and far, and warm and warmer, and *chewed* and *not yet chewed*. Number is a thing that only becomes a problem when something arrives that can stand on a ridge and want a total. The herd is populous under one arithmetic and near-empty under another because the herd itself has never needed either arithmetic, and so never settled the matter, and so the matter remains unsettled, locally, for anyone who insists on raising it.

They are not lonely. Loneliness requires an edge, and they have not located one.

**II. The lappers**

In the seams, at cherry heat, in the gaps of every worn engagement, there is a resident.

They are the size of a thumbnail, flat, dull white, ceramic in the way a kiln-shelf is ceramic, and shaped roughly like a pumpkin seed with a working edge. They live in bearing clearance. Their food is metal transfer: the microscopic high spots where two slipping surfaces briefly weld themselves together and tear. A lapper crops those welds as they form, and in cropping them it widens the gap by a few microns per century.

This is the mutualism that runs the plateau, and it inverts the fifth rule in a way worth sitting with.

Nothing here is broken — because nothing here is *permitted to become accurate*. A joint that ran true would engage. A joint that engaged fully would take load, and load in a body with no tolerances left has nowhere designed to go, and something bright and quick would happen in a place where nothing quick has happened for an era. Precision is the only lethal condition on the Sinter Plateau, and the lappers are the organ that prevents it. They are not maintenance. Maintenance implies a specification. They are the animal's slow refusal of one.

Where a colony dies out — it happens, in the coldest extremities, in a foot that has not warmed properly in a century — the joint smooths, quiets, begins to run sweetly. The great one leans away from that limb. It has no word for the feeling, but the feeling is dread, and it is correct.

**III. The tuyère-birds, and how the lappers travel**

The birds breed at altitude and never meet. This was reported accurately and left unexplained.

What happens is that a bird holding station in a plume sheds, over years, a slow fraying of its intake fringes — and the plume, being a column of sulfurous gas rising at a constant rate through a constant gradient, holds that fraying in place, and works it, and returns it. Gestation occurs in the airflow. The plume is the womb; the bird is only the seed and the shepherd. Two birds a hundred kilometres apart, riding the plumes of two animals that will never touch, are nonetheless in the same medium, and the medium remembers shapes.

And in the fringes of every tuyère-bird, dormant, dry, waiting for cherry heat, ride lappers.

That is how a newborn lump — a decade-cooled drip of iron in a shallow, just beginning to turn — receives its colonists. Not from the parent. From the sky, in smoke, carried by something that has never landed. The great ones cannot pass anything to their children except shape. Everything else arrives on the wind, from strangers, out of order.

**IV. The filings, and their unfinished business**

The filings are the plateau's arithmetic, and they have never agreed with themselves.

They eat rust — the red halos around the iron beads, cropped off the fracture faces and out of the crust, which is why old broken sinter looks bled dry and fresh breaks look like caviar. They flock magnetically, which means every individual is continuously computing its distance from every other individual, and the flock's shape is that computation made visible, and the hiss is the computation made audible: a tally, taken and retaken, ten thousand terms, never closing.

They follow a warm body for hours because a warm body is the only clean edge on the plateau — the only place where *this* stops and *that* starts — and an edge is exactly the thing they lack. They are not hunting you. They are using you. You are a hypothesis about boundaries, and they are testing it, and they will drop the test when you cool.

Nothing in the plateau's third rule requires a mystery. It only requires that the only creature here with an interest in counting be a creature that has never once succeeded.

**V. What lives in the old sound**

There are predators. There is one kind.

They occupy the deep pools — the strata of roar that have silted into the basins and lain there for centuries — and they are not visible, not because they are hidden but because they are not the sort of thing that has a surface. What they have is *position in the sound column*, and appetite.

They graze noise. Where one has fed, a knocking you have been tracking for nine days loses a component: not fainter, but thinner, more careful, missing the third note of its triple. Track the same animal to its basin and watch the cam turn, and the cam is turning as it always did. The sound has been eaten somewhere upstream of your ear.

The herd knows them only as a direction in which voices go missing, and leans away from it, and thereby migrates, and has been migrating for a very long time. And the semitone the last account recorded — the whole basin gone lower in three hundred years — is not entirely wear.

Something is grazing the plateau toward quiet, at its own pace, with the same patience as everything else here, and it will get what it is grazing for.

**VI. The habit that also lives here**

Water is edited, and the editing is not weather and not law. It is a gland.

A body of this kind cannot survive a solvent. Not the rust — rust it eats — but the thermal shock, and worse, the *grammar*: water introduces cutting, and channels, and gullies, and a landscape that is answerable to a river. The plateau is a place of curves. Its animals are curves. They are so slow that no defence acting in the present tense could possibly be fast enough, and so the defence acts where it can: upstream, on the causes, on the fill event, on the moment the flask was believed to be full.

There is no malice in it and no attention. It is peristalsis. It is closer to a valve than to a mind, and the reason the edit never frightens anybody is that a valve has no reason to leave a witness disturbed — the knowledge is revised along with the water, seamlessly, and you walk on carrying a dry flask you have always carried dry.

Whether the herd grew the gland or the gland grew the herd is not a question the plateau has ever needed to answer, and it is not answering now.

**VII. The hearers**

Three cartographers came. They built nothing, in accordance with the first rule. It is not clear that they left.

You will find them, if the arithmetic is feeling generous, walking the shade-district behind a plume: three of them, or nine, or one, sun-blacked, gone very quiet, the pulse of the altitude hammering permanently in their ears the way it will soon hammer in yours. They carry no flasks. They eat, as far as anyone can determine, something to do with sulfur and heat differential, which is what everything here eats, and it is not polite to ask what happened in between.

They are not old. They are *worn* — rounded, ovalled, half-seized, absolutely nothing about them broken, because they long ago stopped holding themselves to a standard against which failure could be measured. They are the plateau's fifth rule wearing boots.

They still take the census. They know it cannot close. They do it as a practice, from ridge to ridge, counting eleven and eleven and three hundred and forty, and writing nothing down, because writing something down would be making something. A habit is not a structure. A habit is permitted.

Each of them has an animal. Not a pet, not a companion — a *name*: a particular triple-knock, a particular bad cam, learned from twenty kilometres off and carried the rest of their lives. When two hearers meet they do not exchange news. One imitates a knock from a basin the other has never visited, and the other listens, and answers with a knock from a stratum nine days deep, and in this way information moves across the plateau exactly as everything else moves here: slowly, out of order, and without ever arriving at a total.

They are the only inhabitants who remember in the ordinary way, which makes them the plateau's single unnecessary organ, and they seem at peace with that.

**VIII. The oldest tenant**

And at the bottom of all the slipping, in the direction the herd leans away from, in the deep pools where the grazed sound thins out and goes careful:

something is cooling.

It has been dripped, drop by drop, for a very long time — one wearing bearing at a time, one eaten note at a time, one semitone per three centuries — and it is pooled in a shallow now, and it has not yet begun to turn.

It will. That is all this place has ever done. The great ones drip iron and the lump turns; the plateau drips quiet and the quiet will differentiate out of the mass the way cams differentiate out of a melt, and it will take a long while to walk, and when it walks there will be nothing on the sinter loud enough to hear it coming.

The herd is its parent, and gives no care, and none is needed.
