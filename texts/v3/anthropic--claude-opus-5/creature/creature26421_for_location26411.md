## The Notched

They are short, and they are broad through the shoulder and the thigh, because everything here is stairs. Below the cloud they are recognised instantly by the walk: a rolling, weight-forward climb kept even on the flat, and a habit of putting the whole foot down at once rather than heel-first, which is what a lifetime of wet flanged metal teaches. Lowlanders find them slow to hurry and impossible to startle.

Their faces are burned on the underside. The light comes up, so they weather from below — freckled throats, cooked nostrils, brown chins, a hard tan line along the jaw where the flange of the cap stops shading and stops nothing. Above that line the forehead stays the colour of a fern shoot all their lives. The squint-lines run downward from the outer eye, three or four of them, deepening from about fifteen years old. Their lower lashes bleach out. Their upper lashes do not. A doctor in the valleys can tell a mountain-born corpse from a lowland one by the eyelids alone.

And their hands are ruined. This is not from labour, or not mostly; it is from the world being new. Every plate edge on the Increment still carries its shear-burr, every bracket its unbroken casting fin, and a body moving through that for forty years is simply cut, over and over, in a thousand shallow unimportant places that all heal white. A hand with unmarked skin belongs to an infant or a fresh cadaver. Nobody thinks of this as damage. Ask an old presswright what her hands are and she will say, with some pride, that they are hers, and mean it precisely: no other hands are cut in that pattern.

That is the whole of them, really. Everything else follows.

---

They dress in deliberate error. Nobody on the mountain wears a matched pair of anything: boots are cut on different lasts, one lace waxed and one dry, gloves made from separate hides so the thumbs sit differently. Cloth straight off the looms is the most dangerous material in the world — smooth, patterned, identical by the roll — and it cannot be worn until it has been *faulted*. The first fault is a small domestic ceremony. A new coat is laid on the table, and the wearer burns a hole in it, or cuts a notch from the hem, or spills something on it that cannot be got out, while whoever is nearest watches and afterwards can testify: *I saw that coat spoiled, and it was spoiled thus, and I would know it again.* Tailors sell the spoiling with the garment. The expensive ones spoil beautifully.

They do not greet each other with names first. A greeting is a recital of faults. *My left boot is the red one. I take no salt. My mother was a lister and I speak with her stop in my throat.* Three or four particulars, offered flatly, the way lowlanders offer a hand. Children are drilled in it before they can read, along with notching, and an adult who forgets to recite is thought either drunk or grieving. A stranger who cannot produce any faults at all — who is simply and blandly whole, unscarred, unspoiled, smelling of nothing — is not attacked, exactly. But rooms empty around them.

Smell is half of address. Every household compounds its own grease from the settling tanks and the beetle oil and whatever it can get — rosemary from the valleys if there is money, sedge ash if there isn't — and everyone in that house wears it on the collar and the back of the neck. You know your kin in a stairwell by the nose long before the eye is any use, and this is precisely because the eye is no use: it is the same stairwell, all of them, all the way up, and the treads are the same treads worn in the same places, and the light comes up through the risers exactly so. Halfway through a climb the wall goes past your shoulder smelling of your grandmother's ash-and-tallow and you know you are still on your own stair, in your own district, and you keep going. Lose the smell and you must stop, and look, and hunt for a notch you recognise.

Mirrors are disliked. What a good mirror shows you is someone exactly like you, and the polite trade uses ripple-glass, so that barbers work from a face that is subtly wrong. There are households with a true mirror, kept covered. There are people who will not be in a room with one.

And there is no second portrait of anybody. A likeness may be made once, and once only, and to draw a person twice — to make two of them, matched, hanging in different rooms — is not vandalism or insult. It is tried in the same court as an attempt on a life, and the sentence is the same.

---

Marriage on the Increment is a slow, tender, lifelong labour of not becoming the same person.

Two bodies in one bed, one diet, one air, one round of days, one set of jokes: it works on them. Couples smooth. It begins harmlessly — finishing each other's sentences, the shared cough, both of them turning at once toward the same noise, which is not sweet here, which makes visitors set down their cups. It ends, if unchecked, with a pair who eat one dinner's nourishment between them and have one dream and can only be spoken to together.

So the married keep differences the way lowlanders keep gardens. One takes salt; one never will. One sleeps on the left and face-up, one curled and face-down, and they do not swap, ever, even travelling. They eat from bowls that no one could confuse. They wear each other's fault-marks nowhere.

And they keep a grievance. Every long marriage on the mountain has one live argument in it, tended, never resolved, brought out and worked at some evenings the way another people might play cards: the matter of the sister's debt, the business with the roof cord, the thing that was said at the ninth-terrace funeral thirty years ago. Outsiders hear it and think the marriage is failing. It is the opposite. A resolved grievance is a smoothed one. On the mountain, to forgive completely and forget is very nearly to murder your husband and yourself at once, and the old are frank about this: *we have loved each other since we were nineteen, and I have never let him be right about the roof cord, and that is why we are two.*

The bereaved are watched carefully, for exactly this reason. When one of a pair dies, the survivor is likely to go smooth toward the dead — to lose their own faults and take on the missing ones, salt where they took none. Neighbours intervene with a cruelty that is entirely kindness: they insist on the grievance. They make the widow say aloud what she still holds against him. It is understood to be the only funeral rite that does any good.

---

Children are born dangerously blank.

Infants are smooth — smoother than anything else alive, all of them one round furious pink thing with a great many addresses — and so the first cut comes before the first feed. The birth-woman marks the ear, always the ear, in a pattern she keeps and never repeats, and speaks a fault over the child, and the mother presses her own grease onto the crown of its head. If a baby dies unnotched, nobody will say how many died. There is no way to know. This is the thing that lowland midwives cannot be made to understand, and the reason the mountain has never accepted their help.
