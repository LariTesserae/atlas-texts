## Coming Close

From the boat you will not see them, not at first. Everything on the shelf is moving and they move at the same rate as everything else, which is to say slowly, continuously, and without any of the stops and starts that make a figure resolve out of a landscape. You see the sheeting water and the steam and the white ranks of the terraces, and then a rim you had taken for a rim stands up, and it is a back, and the back turns, and there is a face in it.

They are pale — not the pallor of illness but the even, sourceless, shadowless pale of things that have never once been struck by direct light. Short. Broad across the shoulder and heavy in the thigh, built low, with a wide splayed stance that comes of eighty years on wet stone and a strong preference for not falling. Nobody wears shoes. Shoes plate, rot, slip, and kill you; instead the sole of a shelf-dweller's foot is a horn half an inch thick with the toes spread wide and independent, and a grown man can pick up a chisel with his foot and pass it up to his hand without looking.

Their forearms, their shins, the tops of their shoulders and the backs of their necks carry the rime — the whitish crust the air lays down on anything that holds still, which on a person is anything that is not in constant use. It is scraped off with the edge of a shell, and it cannot be reached everywhere, and this is the first thing to understand about them socially: no one on the shelf can scrape their own back. It is done for you, by somebody, every day or every second day, and you do it for them, and the scraping-pairs of a household are a map of that household's affections and obligations, and a person who has gone a fortnight unscraped is not merely dirty but visibly, publicly unloved — the white sits on them in a shape like a saddle, and everyone can read it at thirty paces. There are charities that exist for no other purpose than to go about the lower lanes scraping the backs of the friendless. It is considered the minimum decency, below which nothing is.

Their eyes are red-rimmed and always wet. The sulphur does it. They weep from the moment they are born to the moment they are laid out, and consequently tears are not a sign of anything. Grief has its own sign and it is made with the shoulders.

## Hands

The hands are the whole of the person.

They speak with them, they eat with them, they cut stone with them, and there is nothing in their world they cannot lose and survive except these. A crushed hand is not an injury here, it is a bereavement — the loss of speech and livelihood in one stroke — and the care taken of hands has the shape and the seriousness of a religion. Ox fat, worked in nightly. Nails cut back hard, because a nail catches and tears, and a torn nail in this water goes bad in a day. The copper chisels are oiled and hoarded and inherited, and so are the hands; there are lineages here that are spoken of in terms of hands, a phrase meaning roughly *those people's hands last*.

Because the pale stone gives back the light and everything is the same soft dim, hands must be made to read against it. So they stain them. Every adult on the shelf dips their palms and the inner faces of their fingers in a liquor boiled from the rust-brown mats of the cool margins, and the stain takes in the callus and holds for ten or twelve days, and the effect is that when a person speaks, two dark shapes move very fast in front of a pale body against a pale wall in a pale light, and are legible at a distance of sixty feet. A person whose stain has faded is saying, without saying it, that they are not expecting to have much to say. It is what the sick look like, and the sulking, and the newly bereaved.

The children's hands are stained by their mothers. The dead are stained before they are carried up.

## Speaking, and Being Watched

Their language is made for a place where you can always be seen and never be heard, and it has grown into that place the way a root grows into a crack. It uses the hands, the face, the shoulders, the angle of the trunk, and — this is the part that outsiders never get — the light. A sign made while stepping so that the pale wall is behind you is emphatic. A sign made from within a doorway is provisional, hedged, deniable. There is an entire register of speech that consists of *where you stand to say it*, and the lanes of the inhabited terraces have, at every junction, a worn place at a certain angle to the wall, polished by generations of feet, where you stand to say a serious thing.

It follows that there is no such thing here as a private word spoken in company. Anything said is said to everyone in line of sight. Their quarrels are public because they cannot be otherwise; their courtships are public; their bargaining is public; and they have developed, in compensation, an extraordinarily elaborate etiquette of *not having watched*, a set of postures for indicating that one's eyes were elsewhere, which everybody knows to be a lie and which everybody accepts, because the alternative is unlivable.

And for distance, the tapping. Their names are tapped names — a rhythm, three to seven strokes, that belongs to you from the day you are given it, and which you will hear (while you can still hear) and then feel through the floor for the rest of your life. Children learn a hundred of them before they learn to swim. To tap a person's name idly, for nothing, is the gesture of a bored lover. To tap it wrong is a serious insult. To tap the name of the dead is not done, and is done anyway, at night, by people who have crept off to a far rim where the strike will spread thin and nobody will be sure they felt it.

## The Hearing Years

The children hear. The adults do not, and the transition is not gradual in the way you would expect: somewhere between the tenth and the sixteenth year the small bones behind the ear thicken and fuse — the body doing to itself exactly what the water does to the town — and over the course of perhaps a season the world goes quiet. They call it *closing*. It is the entry into adulthood and it is marked with a small ceremony and a fresh stain on the hands, and it is not mourned. You would not mourn the loss of milk teeth.

But before it, for ten years or so, a child is an instrument, and the city uses them as one. A child can tell you that the third runnel under the fishmarket is singing wrong, which means it is narrowing, which means it will close in a month and flood the Bakers' terrace. A child can hear a crack opening in an arcade two days before it shows. There are child-walkers on the public payroll, small serious persons of eleven and twelve who pace assigned circuits with a slate and a stylus and note the places where the sound has changed, and their reports are taken with complete seriousness by adults who have no way whatever of checking them.

The children also do the talking. When the coastal traders beach their boats on the lower flats, it is a twelve-year-old who goes down to meet them and speaks to them in their mouth-language, learned from other children, passed down the generations of children like a skipping game, wholly unknown to anybody over sixteen. The child speaks; behind the child stand three or four adults who are watching the trader's face, because nobody here has ever needed to hear a man to know he is lying. The child says the words and the elders decide the price, and the trader goes away with the impression that he has been dealing with a nation of infants.

And there is a thing the children know and cannot pass on. They have songs, and lore, and a whole vocabulary about the sound — the roar, which they say has parts, which they say changes from hour to hour, which they will argue about bitterly. Ask an adult and you get the same shrug everywhere on the shelf. *Yes. There was something.* Nobody remembers it. Every generation is briefly admitted into a room and then the door is stoppered with its own bone.

## What the Water Does to Them

It gets in. Of course it gets in; they drink it. Every adult on the shelf carries stones — in the kidney, in the gall, gritty concretions grown out of the same water that grows the terraces — and they pass them, painfully, at intervals, over a lifetime, and they keep them. They are strung. An old woman's neck-string of her own stones, eleven or fourteen of them, yellow-white and pitted, clicking when she turns her head, is the nearest thing to a treasure most people here will ever own, and it is buried with her, which is to say laid on her breast when she is carried up, which is to say it dissolves back into the flowstone along with the rest of her. They are counted in the way other peoples count years. *She went at nine stones.* It was a short life.

Their joints lime up. The old walk with a particular stiff shuffle and it is understood that stiffness is the beginning of the end, not because it hurts but because of the Rule: nothing may be left still. So the old are walked. Every evening, in the lanes and around the lower pools, you will see the grandparents of the city being taken out and walked by their grandchildren, in slow circuits, arms held, joints worked one by one by a patient child who has been taught how — elbow, wrist, each finger. It is a chore assigned to the young in the same breath as sweeping. And an old person who can no longer be walked has entered the last category of person, and everybody knows it, and they are treated with a tenderness so heavy and so explicit that it would be unbearable if it lasted longer than it does.

The lungs go too, in the block-cutters, who breathe dry dust — those are the ones who cough white in the morning and are old at forty-five and are paid accordingly.

## What a Person Owes

A citizen here is not defined by blood or by residence. A citizen is a person who holds a frontage: a measured length of terrace lip, plus a share of a public channel, which they are responsible for keeping open forever. That is the entire content of belonging. You inherit not a house but an edge, and the edge does not care who you are, and it closes an eighth of an inch a season whether you attend to it or not.

They are, therefore, the most industrious people on any coast and not in the least ambitious, because there is nowhere for industry to go. What they make, they make in order to stay level. A man who works twice as hard as his neighbour does not end up with twice as much; he ends up with a clean channel and some time, and time is spent on ornament, on courtship, on argument, on the plating-forms left in the runnels, on the enormous unnecessary carved lintels of the upper town, on being good at something.

The one real crime is neglect. Let your frontage close and you flood your neighbour below, and there is a graduated public response to this which ends, at its far end, in *the binding*: the offender is set in a shallow runnel with their ankles in the flow for a stated number of days, fed and watered and spoken to and not otherwise touched, until the lime has taken them by the feet. Then they are cut out. What remains is a rough white band around each ankle that no scraping ever entirely removes, and which the person carries to the ledges. They keep working. Everyone keeps working. But the city has written on them, in the only medium it has.

## The Dark Hour, and Touch

Because everything said is seen, nothing that matters is said in daylight.

They have a second language, small, slow, with perhaps four hundred signs, made hand-to-body: on the forearm, the back of the neck, the palm, the breastbone. It is the language of the lightless interiors of houses, of the slatted bed, of two people under one hide in a room with no window. Lovers use it. Mothers use it on sleeping children. Conspirators use it, and thieves, and the pair of you who are going to say the thing that cannot be said where it can be read off you at sixty feet.

And the blind use it. Blindness comes here — the sulphur, the falls, old age — and a blind adult on the shelf is cut off absolutely from the public tongue, and so everybody who addresses them must drop into the touch-language, which is to say that every casual exchange with a blind person, the price of eels, a remark about the weather they cannot see, is conducted in the register of pillow-talk and deathbeds. They are addressed all day long in the most intimate words their culture has. Some of them find this unendurable. Most of them are the best-loved people in their district and do not know why.

## The Laying

They carry their old up while the old are still breathing. Not always; not everyone; but often enough that there is a word for it and no shame in it. A body that can no longer be walked will plate, and it is held to be better to go up under one's own weight, or nearly, and be set in the sheeting water in a chosen attitude, with the stones on the breast and the hands freshly stained, and lie there a day or two in the warm, among the ranked white swells of everyone who has already done this, hearing nothing, seeing the vapour-ceiling glow, and go out slowly with the family sitting around in the shallows getting rimed to the elbow.

Afterwards the family comes back up at intervals to watch the shape soften. A month, a year, a generation. And then, when it is only a swell in the flowstone with a pool of hot water lying in the hollow of it, someone in the family farms it, because it will be the best mat-field they own. They eat off their grandmother for forty years and they call her by her tapped name when they come up with the paddle, and there is no irony in it anywhere, and the visitor who flinches is regarded with the mild, patient, faintly pitying look they reserve for people from places where the ground does nothing.

## Also Living Here

The criers, who own the cliff and scream without ceasing and have never once in recorded memory been silent, and whose ledges are worked by the climbers — a lean, scarred, slightly disreputable trade whose members grow their hair long on purpose and let it plate into a rigid white cap that has saved more skulls than any other invention on the shelf. Climbers are held to be reckless, lucky, dishonest and indispensable, and they marry each other.

The pale oxen, five hundred pounds and blind and mild past all reason, who cannot be allowed to stand still either, and who are therefore walked at night in slow circles by children too young for any other work, so that a certain quarter of the lower town is always, in the small hours, full of large soft animals going round and round with a small person asleep against each flank, hand knotted in the harness.

And the mats, which are not thought of as crops exactly. A pool is described as *willing* or *sulky*. A field that fails is not a failure of the farmer; it is a falling-out, and there are things you do about it, involving the paddle and a great deal of patience and a certain amount of talking to the water with your hands, which everybody does and nobody admits to.

## What They Call Themselves

The coast calls them tappers, or the white hands, or worse. Their own sign for themselves is made with the flat of the hand held edge-on and moved steadily forward, and it is the same sign they use for the lip of a terrace, and for the line where the scraper meets the rime, and for the last low rim at the western edge where the water goes off into the sea.

It means *the edge.* Not the wall, not the barrier, not the holding-off. The edge: the moving place where the giving and the taking-away are exactly equal, which is not a thing but an event, which has no thickness, which must be maintained in every waking hour, and which is — they are quite clear about this, it is the nearest they come to a philosophy and they teach it to children with a chisel in their hands — the only place a person can actually be.
