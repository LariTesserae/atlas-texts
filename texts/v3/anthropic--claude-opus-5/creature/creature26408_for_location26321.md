## Who is here

The honest census of the Wick is short in kinds and unreadable in numbers. Six or seven sorts of large life, eleven vascular plants, one fungus that matters, and then a smear of smaller things running down past the limit of counting into a haze. What all of them have in common is not ancestry and not diet. It is that they arrived, and that the arriving is finished. There is nowhere else warm. Every lineage on this rock is a lineage that stopped.

---

### The chaff

Begin with the drifting green, because everything else is a way of eating it.

It is alive, though it is difficult to hold that thought while looking at it. Each grain is a hollow bead the size of a mustard seed, mostly gas, walled in something like glass of its own making, with a smear of pigment inside that glows when it is jostled. It does not fly. It cannot. It is simply too light to come down, and it has been too light to come down for a very long time, and so it goes where the high air goes, in banks and streets and long combed drifts, a snow with no floor beneath it.

Nothing on the Wick makes the chaff. It comes from the east. It comes out of the shadow of the thing that is never visible, in slow pulses, some nights thick enough to fur the flanks of the spire in luminous dust, some months not at all. The whole living weight of this mountain is fed by an object nobody here has seen, has words for, or could point at. That is not remarkable to anything that lives here. It is simply the direction food comes from, the way water comes from downhill.

### The wailers

A wailer is a sooty hook-winged bird with a wingspan the length of your forearm, no useful eyes, an enormous throat, and a voice that it will not stop using for the whole of its life.

This is the thing to understand about them: the screaming is not communication laid over their existence. It is the existence. A wailer that goes silent has no location. It cannot fix the rock, cannot fix the flute wall three inches from its shoulder, cannot fix its mate, its neighbours, its chick, the drop. It also cannot be fixed *by* them — a silent bird is a hole in the colony's shared picture of itself, indistinguishable from empty air, and empty air is where you land your feet. They are trampled. They are shouldered off. Silence here is not rest and it is not death; it comes fractionally before death and is its cause.

So each bird holds a note that is a little its own — a particular slur, a particular fall — and it holds it while feeding, while fighting, while treading, while sleeping, in a thin ragged version, all night, for eleven or twelve years. A colony of forty thousand of these is not a chorus. It is a solid. You do not hear it so much as enter it.

The chicks are born mute and blind and are fed for the first four days by a parent who finds them by memory of position, which on a mountain that flakes is a bad method and often fails. On the fifth day the chick makes a noise. If that noise is too like the noise of the chick in the next crotch of rock, both starve, because neither parent can resolve them and neither parent will hedge. There is no arbitration. The colony sorts itself by collision, continually, and the sound you hear from below — that flat unvarying wall, that condition rather than event — is the sound of forty thousand small negotiations that have all already been settled and are being restated forever in case anything has moved.

They hear the mountain, too. Glass under stress changes its ticking a moment before a flute lets go. The colony knows. There is a lift in pitch, a sort of rising fray in the fabric of the noise, and then several hundred voices stop at once. The hole is audible for perhaps four seconds — a soft cold spot in the wall of sound, shaped like a door — and then the neighbours spread into it and it closes, and nothing has happened, and nothing marks the place.

### The crawlers

Fist-sized, thirty-legged, plated in black overlapping scutes with a rasp of chitin at the trailing edge of each plate. The rasp is the whole animal.

It bites into the grain of the glass one way only. A crawler climbing is efficient, tireless, almost unstoppable; a crawler attempting to descend locks itself to the rock like a barb in flesh and, if it persists, tears its own plates off at the roots. They cannot go down. Not by choice, not in panic, not to save their lives.

So a crawler's biography is a straight line. It hatches in the sour warm guano bands at the cloud line, grazes upward through the fungal mats for two or three years, is progressively colder and thinner and cleaner-shelled as it goes, and finishes in the wind on the upper flutes with nothing left to eat, where it mates in slow silent aggregations and the females carry the eggs to the crest and let them go.

The eggs fall. That is the entire reproductive strategy: several hundred smooth spheres the weight of a raindrop, dropped off the top of the world, bouncing and skating down two thousand feet of fluted glass, most of them lost into the cloud, a sufficient minority catching in the guano where they started. The species travels upward one body at a time and downward only as something small and round that has not yet begun.

Meanwhile they crop the mats and keep the fungus off the water grooves, and thereby keep the drinking pockets fed, and thereby keep the knappers and the wailers alive, and they do not know this and nothing about them is arranged for it.

### The combs, and the hearth beneath everything

The pale fan-fungus is the actual warmth of this mountain, and the glass is only its excuse.

Combs run at a hand's breadth across, cold-white, faintly rubbery, and they burn their food fast on purpose. A dense stand of comb will sit six or eight degrees above the rock it stands on. Put your hand into a fan and it is like putting your hand into an animal's mouth. They do it, as far as anyone can tell, to keep their own tissue from freezing during the fog tide, and the consequence — unintended, ancient, enormous — is that every warm-blooded thing on the Wick sleeps pressed into fungus.

In the fans live mites in unreckonable numbers, and on the mites live things that are mostly mouth, and in the water pockets live the rubbery algal skin and, beneath it, a soup of drowned insects and larvae that eat drowned insects and a small blind flatworm that has never been named. By count of separate living presences, the upper Wick is one of the most populous places in the world, and if you gathered every one of them into a heap it would not fill a satchel.

The eleven plants are all cushions. All of them are older than every animal here; the largest, in the deepest basin on the north face, is a dome the size of a shield and has been alive since before the spire broke the cloud. Ten flower annually and are pollinated by mites and by chance.

The eleventh flowers once in about nine years, without warning, at night, and it is not pollinated by mites.

What comes for it comes up out of the cloud. It does not make sound — or it makes sound low enough that the only sign is that the wailers, all of them, stop. That is the one true silence in the history of this place and it lasts perhaps ninety seconds, and in it you can hear the glass ticking, and you can hear air being displaced by something large and unhurried, and then the flower is pollinated and the noise comes back like water into a hole. The knappers, whatever they are doing, lie down flat and put their faces to the rock. Nothing teaches them this.

### The knappers

Waist-high. Dark oily fur, thick over the shoulders and thin at the hands. Wide splayed feet with pads they trim themselves, the way you would pare a hoof, using a lancet held in the toes of the other foot. Long forearms. Eight fingers, four opposed, a grip like a vice on a shape they have never seen.

Their eyes are large and beautiful and almost useless. They are tuned to the green chaff and to nothing else, which means that for most of a knapper's life the world is entirely dark and entirely known by hand, and then two or three times a year a heavy drift comes through and, for something under an hour, they can see.

They stop working. All of them, all over the spire, in whatever they were doing. Seeing is not a condition here; it is an occasion, and they spend it the way you would spend an hour of unexpected daylight after a decade underground — not looking out over the cloud-sea, which is nothing, but looking at each other. Faces they otherwise know as a shape under the fingers. Children looking at the parent they have been carried by for four years. It is the only time they are ever still, and from below, from inside the screaming, you would see a few dozen small figures standing on a black blade of glass in a haze of drifting light, all facing one another, none of them moving.

Then it passes and they go back to the dark and the hands.

**They have no public speech.** They cannot. Below, the noise makes voice impossible; above, in the wind-scour, there is nothing to speak *with* — their throats are small and their hearing is poor, dulled by generations in that wall of sound. What they have instead is touch. A grammar conducted on the forearm and the back of the hand: pressures, drags, the tap and the held tap, some sixty distinguishable elements. It is fast, it is precise, it is entirely adequate to their lives, and it has one property that has shaped them completely.

It requires contact. Every act of communication in the whole history of this people has occurred between exactly two individuals, in private, in the dark, with skin against skin. There has never been an audience. There has never been a speech, a song, an announcement, a story told to a room. There is no way to say a thing to more than one person, which means there is no way for a thing to be *generally* known, which means there is no such object as public truth. Everything any of them knows arrived as a chain of private touches, hand to hand to hand, back and back, and cannot be checked against anything.

At distance they use cord. A twist of fungal fiber, thirty feet, and a code of tugs — coming, hold, water, up, let go. When a party goes down-grain into the nest bands during the fog tide to rob eggs and take chicks, they go on the cord, because down there the noise erases thought and a knapper who loses the line loses the ability to want to come back. The cord is the entire long-range communication technology of the species and always has been. No knapper has ever exchanged a word with someone they were not touching, directly or through a rope.

**They own nothing but the bundle.** No shelters, no fire, no containers, no stores. What a knapper has is a wrap of fiber holding blades: lancets, scrapers, cores, thin resharpened slivers worn down to nothing, and new work. The bundle is inherited. When a knapper dies — and they die by falling, almost always, because everything here dies by falling — the body goes into the cloud, and the bundle stays and is divided, and the pieces disperse into other bundles. There are lancets in circulation that have been resharpened past all recognition and are older than any lineage, older than any story could be if there were stories. Age is not counted in years here, because there are no years: no seasons, no dawn, no fixed thing to measure the rising rock against, since everything rises together. Age is counted in the weight of what you carry. The oldest of them carry the heaviest bundles and can barely climb.

**The teaching is a mold.** Learning to knap is not watching. It cannot be watching; there is no light. The teacher sits behind the child, reaches around, puts her hands over the child's hands, and *moves* them. Six gestures, in order, over and over, for years, with the child's fingers held in the exact angles by an older grip until the angles are the child's own. No explanation is possible. No question is possible. There is no channel in that arrangement through which a variation could survive, because a variation is simply an error, corrected at once by a stronger hand.

This is why the technique has not changed and will not. It is not a tradition being preserved. It is a shape being pressed, one generation into the next, like a seal into wax, and the wax has never once been allowed to cool wrong.

### The tools in the glass

Which brings you to the thing you climbed here for, or the thing you will not stop thinking about afterwards, whichever it turns out to be.

A flute splits. A fresh black face opens, glass that has never been in air, that has come up out of wherever the Wick is made, and a hand's depth inside it there is a blade. Prepared core, struck platform, six gestures in the correct order. Sometimes a half-finished scraper, abandoned partway, waiting on its last three flakes.

A knapper finds it. She works it free with a lancet older than the mountain's surface. If it is finished she puts it in her bundle. If it is unfinished she finishes it — the last three flakes, the same six gestures, in the dark, by feel — and puts it in her bundle.

She does not stop. She does not hold it up. She does not fetch anyone, and could not tell them if she did, not because the touch-language lacks the words for *this is strange* but because it lacks the grammar for *this*, held at a distance, considered. Her language is a language of the hand's reach: here, hold, harder, wait, come, not-that, up, let go. It has no tense. There is no way to say *before*. There is no way to say *how* and no way to say *why*, and a people who have never once in their history said a thing to a crowd have never had to justify anything, and so have never developed the habit of wondering.
