## What lives here

They are not animals. The world is right about that, and so are they: there is no wing, no track, no burrow, nothing that eats another thing. What lives here came up out of the gardens. Somewhere far back in the undecided part of history, in a basin where the roar was trapped and doubled and there was more energy than a low wide tree could use, the oil-trees put out seeds that walked, as all their seeds do, a few centimeters a year down the scree — and some of those seeds, arriving, declined to root. That is the whole of it. The makers are vegetation that got up.

So they eat nothing that lives. They drink nothing, because there is nothing to drink. They are fed by the same thing that feeds the bell-grass, and they are killed by the same absence.

## The body

A maker is a bundle. Not a torso with limbs hung off it but several stems grown together side by side, fused where they touch, like a stand of saplings that has decided to be one thing — the many-trunked habit of the oil-trees, stood upright. Bronze-dark going to green in the folds. Two and a half meters in an old one, which walks bent, low to the blades, on three or four root-legs that set down flat and lift straight; the gait of a stack of chairs settling. A kilometer in an hour is a brisk pace. There is nowhere anyone has to be by any particular time, and time has poor traction here anyway.

There is no head. Sense is distributed, the way it is in things that grew rather than hatched. Clear resin lenses in the upper stems, a dozen or twenty of them, set at whatever angles the growth allowed: enough to read glow. They see almost nothing of the haze-light and everything of the luminescence — the pale green-white of fresh fracture, the weak sympathetic shine of the mosses, the phosphor threads of the paths. And because that glow fades over nine days on a known curve, they see recency as *colour*. Bright white-green is *just now*. A certain dull sea-green is *four days*. Their word for last week is a shade. When a maker looks across a basin at the network of walked routes, it is looking at a map with the whole schedule painted into it, who went where and how lately, in hues.

The front of the lowest stem carries a broad pad of thin tissue over hard core-wood: the sternum-plate. This is the feeding organ, pressed flat to rock. And behind it, in a cavity that is not a mouth and does not open, a comb — thirty or forty tines of the hard oil-tree core, of graded lengths, that take the incoming roar and sort it into the narrow bands the body can actually use. They call these the teeth, and the analogy is good enough, because they wear.

Their fluid is the clear faintly resinous oil. It does not freeze at these temperatures and it does not spill far; a cut seals itself into a bright bead and stays. Every maker is covered in these beads and the ridged scars beneath them, because everything in this world is a blade and there is no way to move through it untouched. The scars are not injuries. Scars are wear, and wear is the good.

## The hands

Here is the centre of them, and the reason the world holds them holy in return.

Hands are grown, not born with. A young maker has two, more or less, at the ends of whatever stems came up first, and adds them over centuries as it needs them: a narrow four-fingered cutting hand held high and close; a broad flat palm-plate for polishing, which is barely a hand at all; a low reading hand with long soft-tipped fingers for going over incised walls in the dim, which is the hand a stranger will offer you first. Some old makers carry nine. Placement is idiosyncratic — one on a stem behind, at the back, for the work you cannot see.

And hands wear out. This is the thing to hold onto: in a world where nothing wears — no rain, no frost, no river, no dust — the only two smooth surfaces in existence are the bottom of a walked path and the palm of a maker. They are the same substance of holiness. They are made by each other. A young maker's palm is faceted and half translucent, and you can see the light through the webs of its fingers, and the number of joints in those fingers is not yet fixed. An old maker's palms are opaque, dull, rounded, dark, and utterly definite, and it can tell you — by scratching it into stone — which basin took which finger down.

Worked stone glows for nine days; so does scraped skin. A maker who has been cutting is haloed at the palms and the soles, and the glow tells you honestly how recently and how hard. Idle hands go dark. This is why nobody lies about work: the lie would have to be a light.

## Feeding, and the descent

Vibration is the energy. Carbon comes from the heavy grey gas, and the gas does not rise, so at long intervals — every few decades — a maker goes down. Down to the lowest shelves, the last ledges above the deck, where the cold dense stuff seeps and pools in hollows and the drone comes up through the rock so hard it is a pressure rather than a sound. It lies there with the sternum-plate on the stone and the gas around its lower stems and fills, over weeks, sluggishly, in the dark.

This is the one thing they do that resembles fear. Makers who go too low come back with their lower stems dead and grey, walking on stumps, or do not come back, and their absence is inscribed on a wall high up with the number of legs they had going in. The descent has ten thousand years of route-marks and warnings on it, one of the few places where the writing is mostly practical and mostly recent.

The teeth are the other half of feeding, and they wear like everything used. An old maker's comb is worn smooth and can no longer catch the high bright registers of the drone at all; it can only take the low. So the old must go up and in, to the deepest basins where the roar is trapped and doubled, and live in the loudest places, standing in the middle of the noise like someone pressing their face into a fire to feel it. They regard this as the reward. It is also what kills them. They are pleased about it either way, because *worn* is the good word, and the fact that being finished coincides with being finished has never struck them as a joke.

## Standing

There is no day. They halt when they choose, and when they halt for long they root: put a taproot down into a fissure, draw the stems in close, and go quiet. A standing maker is indistinguishable from a stump, or from a small cairn, and may be there for forty years.

The traveler's etiquette follows from this. Before you cut into any stone, lay a hand on it and wait for warmth. Before you take a plate off a stack for your shelter, wait for warmth. There are inscriptions, in several basins, that consist of a name and an apology and nothing else.

## Speech

Nothing here has a voice, and they have never had one to lose. Sound is food; talking with it would be like talking with bread.

Language is incised, and because incision does not weather, every wall within reach of a path is a palimpsest of everything anyone ever thought there — the arguments and the inventories and the jokes shoulder to shoulder, running in whatever direction the stone allowed. Reading is done with the long-fingered hand, in the dim, at the pace of a finger. A maker crossing a basin will spend more hours reading the walls than walking between them.

The grammar has three degrees, and they are not tenses. They are degrees of finish: *sharp*, *worn*, *undecided*. A thing sharp has happened but is not yet real. A thing worn is real. A thing undecided is drafted, hanging translucent over the slope, and might be set aside. You cannot say "yesterday" in this language without also saying how much it has been walked on.

For close work there is the tapping — patterns of pressure laid on another's back stems, felt as pressure, not heard. It is slow and private and used for the things one does not want cut into a wall.

For distance there are the callers: slabs shaved and thinned until they ring a note that stands out of the drone as a specific feeling in the teeth, audible for a kilometer as a wrongness in the food. There are four or five traditional strikes. One means a death. One means *silence found here*. One, the rarest, means a seed has been shaken loose, which is as close as this world comes to a birth.

## How many, and whose

A maker goes, once, at whatever point in its long middle it feels ready, to the loudest amphitheater it can reach, and stands in the resonance, and lets the sound shake its seed loose. The seed then walks. Centimeters a year, downslope, across shatter, for a thousand years. Nobody sees their own child. Nobody meets a parent. Kinship is not a category anyone here has needed; the seed arrives into a world where its origin is a mark on a wall a hundred kilometers uphill, if it is anywhere.

So their lineages are textual. You inherit from whoever's inscription you have read the most of, and you sign under them, and the signature is a name — because a maker has no name until it has worn something. The name is a mark on a tally-wall: this hollow, this stair, this half-meter of path, brought to smoothness. To be named is to be indexed to a piece of finished world. The unnamed are called by the same word as an undecided ridgeline, and they are not slighted by it, and they are also not consulted.

Nobody knows how many there are. This is not census failure; it is the local physics. Distances do not sum. You meet the same cairn twice in a day going one direction, with your newest cut already on it. So you may meet the same maker twice, and one of the two has already read what you have not yet written. Young ones are worse, because young ones are not fully collapsed — you can see the alternates faintly superimposed, three or four candidate bodies at three or four heights, the way you see the candidate crests. Wear settles that. An old maker is single. There is exactly one of it anywhere, and it has palms to prove it.

The census they do keep is by handprint, pressed in a shallow cut of oil on a wall in the third basin of the high sprawl, because wear-patterns are unique, and the wall is very long. Occasionally a print goes up that is unmistakably your own, older than you are. There is a mark for this. It is not the mark for *fear*.
