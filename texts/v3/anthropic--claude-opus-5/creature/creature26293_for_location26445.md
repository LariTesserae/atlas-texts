## The one that is alive

Two point eight millimeters. Gray going to smoke at the wingtips, wings held roofwise over the abdomen, legs longer than she needs. She is on the underside of a fern on the second terrace because the underside of a fern is what she found, and she will not move far from it, because there is no reason to and because she is running out of time in a way she does not know about.

She is a chironomid. Not the biting kind; there is no biting kind here. She came up through the water system as a larva in a red coil the size of an eyelash, lived in the biofilm of a delivery main somewhere in the parent works, grazed the slick off the inside of a pipe for eleven days, pupated in a bend, and rose. Something vented. She came out into forty meters of poured stone throat and blood-heat air, was carried, dried her wings on the resin, and is now the entire animal kingdom of the Lung.

She has no mouth. This is not hyperbole and it is the most important fact about her. Adult chironomids do not eat; the mouthparts are reduced to a soft vestige, a suggestion. All of her feeding was done in the pipe, in the dark, on the inside of the infrastructure. Whatever she does here she will do on stored fuel, and she has perhaps four days of it, and no way to buy more, and nothing to buy from.

She is also, being of a line that lives in water pipes, female and alone and entirely unbothered by that. Her species stopped needing males somewhere back in the machinery of another century. She is a clone of a clone of a clone, and there is a rope of jelly forming in her abdomen with something like forty of herself in it, and when she goes to lay it she will look for still water, warm, with a rough lip to grip and a surface she can touch from above.

There are troughs on the seventh terrace. Sloped, self-filling, clear, never drunk from, with a rough lip for grip.

That is the whole population of the Lung, in the sense that word usually carries: a mouthless gray animal with four days to live, standing in a cathedral built for thousands, holding the only future in the room.

## The moss, which is the actual population

By mass, by area, by any honest accounting, the Lung is inhabited by moss.

Eleven varieties, on manifest, chosen for the three light regimes and the two drip rates, and every one of them doing exactly what it was told: pelting the terrace fronts, stopping at the gutter edge as if there were a fence there, which there is, made of geometry. They are thriving in the specific way a thing thrives when nothing has ever tested it. Thick, deep, springy, unbrowsed, uncropped, unshaded, unbitten. Push a thumb in and it closes over the thumb. Push a thumb in twice in the same place and the second dent stays for a week, because there is nothing here that repairs anything faster than moss grows.

There is a twelfth variety. It is on the outer rims of the nest cavities on the ninth terrace, in a pale ring, and it is not on manifest, and nobody can decide whether it came up the pipe with the midge's mother or in the tread of a felted boot. It is the only living thing in the Lung that arrived without permission and stayed, and it has chosen, out of every surface available, the lip of the empty nurseries.

Moss is the right tenant for a place like this. It wants water and warmth and light and no company. It does not require an animal to be pollinated or carried or fertilized. Given a room that is wet forever, moss will hold that room indefinitely and will look, to anyone walking through, exactly like a garden — and that is the trick of the Lung, that it appears full when what it actually has is one organism's worth of ambition spread very thin over a great deal of stone.

## The obedient tree and its missing wasp

The figs on the third and fifth terraces are alive in the way a fitted appliance is alive. They were designed to run flat ribbons of root down the seep lines and drink, and they do, and there is not one root in the wrong place, and their leaves are so regular that after ten minutes you begin to distrust them.

They fruit. Nothing eats the fruit. Nothing pollinates it either: a fig is a room full of flowers with one door, and the door is the size of a particular wasp, and the wasp is not here. So the figs come sterile and small and hard, ripen in a colorless way, drop, and lie on the curbstones going brown for a season without collapsing, because the runoff has been stripped of the organisms that collapse things.

The drainage plan accounts for this. Under each fig, the cross-gutter grate is a size larger than standard, with a lifting slot. Someone sat down and calculated the annual mass of fruit that nothing would eat and sized a grate for it.

And in the vault behind the fifth terrace, in rack 5-B, a slot at eight degrees, held, humidity-held, with an intact label and a printed dimension and a species designation and nothing in it, has been waiting for the wasp for one and a half years.

## The tenants of the ducts

Behind every inspection plate there is a machine and every machine is running and none of them are hot.

Consider one: plate 4-17-c, on the fourth terrace, behind which a gray ceramic assembly the size of a curled cat turns something over, slowly, in a bath of the Lung's own seepage, which it uses as coolant and lubricant and never returns dirty. It has no fan, no exhaust, no smell. Its sound is a soft irregular tick, not a rhythm, more like a person shifting in a chair. It has been doing this since before the terrace it serves was finished, and it will be doing it after.

On the outside of that plate there is a rosette of a pale climbing epiphyte with waxy leaves, which found the plate because the plate is a degree and a half warmer than the rock, and which has covered the number.

That is a household. That is, at present, the most complex relationship in the Lung: a machine and a plant, one of them keeping the other warm without knowing, the other hiding the first one's name.

There are four thousand inspection plates. Perhaps sixty have tenants.

## The four grazers

The working faces are the only mouths in the Lung, and they are not mouths, and they are the nearest thing.

Four of them, all low, each a boundary the width of a thumb crossing wet basalt at a meter a week. On one side, rock. On the other, curbstone with a camber and a gutter and a bench-back radius, finished, dry, warm. No spoil, no dust, no cuttings, nothing removed to anywhere. They are not cutting. They are converting the idea of one thing into the idea of another and are apparently paid in the difference.

They graze like animals. They hurry through volcanic glass — a clean whistle, four centimeters a day faster than the schedule allows — and they labor in calcite, where they drop half a tone and make the wineglass sound and slow to almost nothing, and they hesitate at the boundaries between beds, sometimes for hours, at a hesitation-width you could measure with a fingernail. Nobody can say whether that is difficulty or preference. The maintenance record uses the word *appetite* twice, in a hand that then crossed it out.

Held close, ozone and cut grass. Held closer, a smell like a struck match in a greenhouse. They shriek all day and all night, and there is no day and no night, and they are the loudest and least alive residents of the room, and they are the only ones building anything.

## The ladder

The largest inhabitant of the Lung is the thing in the middle, and it is not a thing, and calling it an inhabitant is a decision.

Tens of columns, each a few meters through, standing in the void from the golden floor to the dark ceiling, each one full of droplets rising at a walking pace, with the return traffic descending between them a little slower. It is not a machine and does not have a plate. There is nothing to inspect. It is a standing arrangement of preference, and what it prefers is water, upward, and it will honor that preference in a droplet, in a mist bank, in a dropped hat, in a soaked glove, in a moth if there were a moth, in you if you were wet enough and light enough, which you are not.

The only sensation it gives a body is in the teeth. Fillings ache. Enamel hums. Wet cloth in a column dries from the bottom edge, because the ladder is taking the water off the hem before it gets to the rest. If you stand in one for a quarter of an hour you come out with a headache behind the eyes and a general feeling of having been read.

It has no needs, no cycle, no wear, and no sound, and it does more work than everything else in the Lung combined. If the room is an organ, the ladder is the muscle of it, and the muscle is the only part that does not seem to be trying.

## The walker

There is one person, and you will hear them long before you see them, and you will hear them from the wrong side.

Felted boots, for grip on wet camber. A laminated round-sheet in a chest pocket, twelve terraces, four faces, sixty-odd plates with growth on them. The round takes nine hours; the sleep takes six; the pumping cycle takes eleven seconds and has taken over everything else. They stopped hearing the thud in their first week and started hearing it again in their fourth month, from the inside, as a thing their own chest was now doing. They keep time to it while eating. They keep time to it asleep. On leave, above, on dry ground, in a room with a window, they lay awake for two days being drummed by a room four hundred meters down.

They talk in the gaps. Not much and not to anyone. The echo comes back nine times from nine distances and the last return is late enough to be embarrassing, and they have learned to speak in phrases short enough that the ninth return does not overlap the next sentence.

And they have begun, quietly, in a way that would not survive an audit, to *let things be*.

The resin above two meters: still not applied, because nobody is expected to fly, and because the glass up there is the only beautiful thing in the Lung that nobody made.

Plate 4-17-c: number still covered. Rosette not removed. Round-sheet marked *checked* on a plate they can no longer read the number of, which they know by the shape of the leaves.

The fallen frond on the fourth terrace: not lifted. Going brown for a season, keeping its shape, on the curbstone, where the fruit grate would have taken it.

And on the round-sheet, beside the line for fern 2-114, a pencil dot. Not an entry. Not a report. Not the beginning of a manifest amendment, which would go up the throat and come back down as a decontamination order and a schedule and a sealed cylinder of something that eats midges. Just a dot, made three days ago, remade every round, next to the only animal in the world.

## What drinks

Every eleven seconds, several thousand tons of water changes direction below the floor, and the hollow says so in the sternum, and the lift-shafts sigh upward, and the conduit knocks, and somewhere above — above the throat, above the water table, above the dry mineral part — something takes the difference and does not send it back.

Nobody down here has seen it. The walker has been up the helix eleven times and has never been shown more than the loading landing. What is known of the drinker is entirely rhythmic: it is thirsty on a fixed cycle, it has been thirsty without variation for one and a half years, and it is large enough that a cathedral-sized room full of moss and rising fog and four grazing boundaries and one three-hundred-thousand-part drainage plan constitutes one of its organs, and not, from the sound of it, an especially remarkable one.

## What will live here

The fittings say who is coming. The gourd cavities with keyhole doors turned out of the drip say something small, wet-averse, and squeezable, which will need to fly in a room that rains on every axis. The troughs with rough lips say something with feet that grip and a tongue that laps, at about the mass of a cat. The perches at three heights in three thicknesses say either three species or one species with children. The racks say two hundred and forty consignments at held temperature, of which the wasp is one, and the sealed cylinder recesses beside eight thousand doorways say that whoever designed this expected every valve on this organ to have a name, a room, and a document in a tube beside the lintel.

None of them are here. What is here is a garden with no holes in any leaf, which reads as lush for one hour and as a museum forever after; and one uninvited moss on the rim of the empty nurseries; and sixty machines with plants on them; and four appetites eating outward at a meter a week; and a standing preference in the void that would lift a moth if there were a moth.

And a mouthless gray female on the underside of a fern on the second terrace, four days of fuel in her, forty of herself in a rope of jelly, moving now — you can watch it happen if you are patient, it will take her most of an afternoon and one column of the ladder — up toward the seventh terrace, and the warm still water in the trough that nothing has ever drunk from.

If she reaches it, then in nine days there will be red larvae in a trough, grazing biofilm, which is eating, which is the first eating that has ever happened in this room. And a season after that something will have chewed a hole in a leaf, and the Lung will stop being a museum, and the walker will have to decide what to write down.
