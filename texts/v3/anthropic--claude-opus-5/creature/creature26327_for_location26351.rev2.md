## The People Under the Lid

**First, the hands.**

You will notice their hands before anything else, because they will take yours. Every fingertip in the bowl is wrinkled — that soft white pleating you get after an hour in the bath, except permanent, from birth, the ordinary condition of skin here. The pads of their palms are the same. Shaking hands with a bowl-dweller feels like shaking hands with something just pulled from the pot.

They will notice yours too, and someone will turn it over and run a thumb across it, and the word that goes round is their word for outsiders: *the dry.* It is not hostile. It carries roughly the freight that *mainlander* carries on an island — a little envy, a little pity, a settled certainty that you don't know how to live.

They have no name for themselves. Why would you name the water you're in.

**Bodies.**

Around four hundred of them, as the report says, and they are all built to the same brief: shed heat, and don't rot.

They are lean past what looks healthy to a lowland eye — long-limbed, narrow through the chest, almost no subcutaneous fat except on women past forty, who carry a little across the hips and are considered handsome for it. Skin runs from deep brown to a greenish olive; under two-thirds of a day inside cloud, in light filtered through forty metres of leaf, everyone is somewhat underlit. Pupils are large. They read each other's faces at conversational distance in a gloom you would call unusable.

Body hair is almost absent, and what there is gets scraped off with shell, because hair holds water against skin and water against skin for long enough is a wound. Heads are cropped short for the same reason, with one exception I'll come to. Feet are wide, splayed, the toes separated, the soles white and thick and softened; nobody wears shoes, and the idea of a closed shoe strikes them as a method of execution.

They sweat constantly and the sweat does nothing. In near-saturated air at blood heat, evaporation is a rumour. So their entire physiology of cooling has been outsourced to the landscape: they dump heat into water by conduction, all day, every day, and a healthy adult in the bowl is a person who has arranged their life so as to be in contact with moving water for about nine hours out of twenty-four.

**The three speeches.**

Under a hundred-odd decibels of unvarying falls, ordinary talk is a waste of breath. They have three registers and they move between them without noticing.

**Hand** is the default: a one-handed sign language, because the other hand is always on a rail, a rope, a child, a basket. It is fast, small, held at chest height, and it is the prestige register — the finest signers are the oldest people, because by fifty most of them have lost the speech band entirely and hands are all that's left. In our world the young are articulate and the old are slow. Here it is precisely inverted, and a fluent old woman can shred a young man in public without raising a finger above her sternum.

**Call** is for distance: a two-tone keened whistle that sits in a narrow window above the roar of the falls and carries a kilometre and a half across the bowl. Every person has a whistle-name, given not at birth but at the age they first turn their head to it — so your name commemorates the moment you started listening. Whistle-names are short, vowel-heavy, and largely untranslatable. Written in our letters they come out as *Ao-ua, Mei-ha, Rrue, Tellou.*

**Ear** is the third. Lips to the rim of the ear, hand cupped behind. Anything private, anything tender, anything complicated enough to need words rather than signs, has to be delivered skin to skin. The social consequences are total and, I think, largely good. You cannot gossip about someone without embracing your co-conspirator. You cannot teach a skill without touching your student. A grudge conducted entirely in hand, at four metres, in full view, is the coldest thing one person can do to another here, and they have a phrase for it that means *speaking to someone as if across water.*

Deafness comes to all of them, and it comes from the top down. Speech goes first, then the frogs, then the insect tide. The last thing anyone hears is the Lip: that sub-audible standing hum, felt in the sternum. When a person can no longer feel it, they are said to have *gone over the col.* They are thereafter excused from all tasks, they stop cutting their hair, and they take up walking with the aunts. I'll come to that too.

**Rot, and the looker.**

The daily enemy is not the Slow Door, or the shrinking Lip, or hunger. It is mildew.

Thirty-nine degrees and saturated is the ideal culture condition for most of what wants to live on a human being. Feet, ear canals, the crooks of the elbows and knees, the groin, under the breasts, between the toes, the corner of the eye: everyone in the bowl has an active fungal or bacterial situation somewhere on them at all times, and the management of it structures their day the way meals structure ours.

At dusk, when the crabs start up the trunks and the bark begins to click, every person in the bowl is examined by another person. Not a spouse necessarily — the pairing is made in childhood and often crosses households, and it outlasts marriages. Your **looker** goes over you in a fixed order (feet, between the toes, behind the knees, groin, armpits, behind and inside the ears, scalp, the eyes last), picks the leeches, opens what needs opening with a shell flake, and packs it with ash paste. Then you do the same for them. It takes twenty minutes and it is the only reliably quiet, close, unhurried thing in a loud country.

To be without a looker is the real poverty here. Widowhood is survivable; looker-lessness is not, and the community reassigns one within a day, unprompted, the way we'd bring a casserole.

Their word for *well* means, literally, *not yet soft.*

**Fire is a clinic.**

Nothing burns and everything is wet, which means the small, smoky, endlessly tended fires are almost never used for cooking. They are used for two things: making ash for the paste, and drying out ears. A person with a bad ear sits with the side of their head in the smoke for an hour while someone fans it. That is what a fire is for. That is what a fire *is*, to a bowl child: a medical instrument that hurts your eyes.

Cooking, in the ordinary sense, barely exists. But they have the finest fermentation culture I have encountered anywhere, for the obvious reason: the entire mountain is a proofing oven held at a constant thirty-nine degrees, forever, for free.

**The crocks.**

Every household owns travertine jars — grown, not carved, by directing a spring across a form for thirty years — sealed with resin and bedded into clefts in the warm rock. In them: tree crab, shelled and salted and left to liquefy into a brown sauce of frightening depth; aroid tuber, grown submerged in the terrace pools, buried for two years until it goes black and sweet; frog, whole; a green paste of pounded epiphyte that tastes like nothing else on earth and that I would cross the col again to get. Nothing here is aged for less than a season. Some jars are aged for a century and belong to a line rather than a person.

A jar is opened to mark something. Opening your grandmother's water is an act with the weight of a will being read. Food, here, is the only available form of writing.

**The salt.**

Sweating ten litres a day and drinking twelve, they should all be dead of hyponatraemia. Three springs on the northern shoulder come up mineral-heavy and brackish, and those three springs are the political centre of the bowl. Salt is measured, allocated, argued over, and carried in stoppered gourds; every drinking vessel has a small second compartment for cutting the water.

The very first thing anyone teaches a newcomer — before the paths, before the signs, before what to eat — is *don't drink it plain.* People who are not told this die within a week, confused and vomiting, in the middle of an abundance of fresh water, and the locals find this the single most persuasive evidence that outsiders are children.

**The tasks.**

A person here is not principally a member of a family. A person is the current holder of a task, and the task is older than the family and will outlast it.

The bridge across the second gorge has been continuously rebuilt for as long as anyone can count; its ropes are living vine, spliced in and grown on, and the family that tends it is named for it. There is a terrace-keeper's task, a crocks task, a salt task, a walkway task. Children are assigned around age nine, by aptitude and by who needs a successor, and a person who inherits a task inherits also a set of obligations to people four generations dead and four generations unborn. They have a word — I'd translate it as *far work* — for labour you begin knowing you will not see the end of it. Most work here is far work. Rule five sees to that: a quarry scar heals a hand's breadth in a lifetime, so you don't cut new paths, you don't open new stone, and every decision you make is one your great-great-grandchildren are stuck with.

The oldest task is the Stair.

**The sweepers.**

I had it wrong, and so will you. The eleven hundred steps on the Weeping Face are not worn concave by feet. They are worn concave by scraping.

Travertine grows a millimetre a year here. A stair under a permanent sheet of spring water, left alone, is not a stair after two centuries; it is a ramp, and then it is a wall. So it is scraped. Shell adze and a river stone, one person, most of the year, starting at the bottom in the wet season and working up, taking off what the mountain put on. The concavity of each step is the summed signature of everyone who has ever done the job.

You can date the work, roughly, by standing on the stair and looking sideways. The untouched travertine on either side stands proud of the swept surface by something over three metres. Nobody in the bowl has ever done this arithmetic and I did not offer it to them.

The current sweeper is a woman of about forty, entirely deaf to speech, with forearms like a rower's. Her mother was sweeper when the Slow Door opened the second time, and her mother went in, and came out at the end of the hour, and thereafter for thirty-one years never again spoke in ear to anyone — only in hand, at distance, including to her own daughter. She never said what was in there. She went back to scraping the next morning.

I asked the daughter why the stair is kept, given that the door has opened twice in perhaps a hundred and fifty years and given that nobody intends to enter it.

She said: *Someone should be able to get up.*

And then, because I was clearly going to ask more: *It's this year's stone. Not yours.*

**The carried-in, and the ones who go thin.**

Four hundred people cannot sustain themselves genetically, and they don't. Perhaps two-fifths of adults in the bowl were born below. The valleys send children — as fosterage, as tithe, occasionally as disposal — and they send the sick, because the constant wet heat genuinely does help certain lung and joint conditions and is genuinely believed to help everything else. Arrivals come up on a stretcher, cross the Lip sitting down with their heads between their knees, and are put in a shallow pool for three days while their body learns the new arithmetic. Their names keep a marker of it for life, an untranslatable particle meaning roughly *brought*.

Going the other way is a profession. The carriers — *the ones who go thin* — make the crossing four or five times a year, down into the cold and back, trading crock-sauce and dried nothing for shell, resin, cord, and news. It wrecks them. Repeated fast traverses of that pressure boundary produce joint pain, nosebleeds, an intermittent blindness in one eye, and a shortened life; carriers rarely see forty-five. They are treated with enormous respect and a faint, unmistakable pity, like lifeboat crew, and there are always volunteers.

**Children.**

An infant can overheat and die in an hour, so babies are not carried, they are floated: shallow woven baskets in the nursing pools, tethered, with a woman's hand on the rim. Bowl children swim before they crawl. They are tied to the walkway rails by a cord at the waist until about five, and the sound of the bowl at midday is falls, insects, and a distant scatter of whistle-names being checked, mothers calling the roll across two kilometres.

Ask a bowl child to draw an animal and they draw six legs. Ask them what *dry* means and they will take you to the nearest cold pin, one of the grey thumb-thick studs set flush in the bedrock, and put your hand on it. It is the only dry thing they have ever touched. Water beads on it and slides off. Millions of children have put their hands on those pins and the pins show no wear at all, which tells you something about the pins, or about children, or about time.

**The dry dream.**

They all have it, and they assume you do too.

A corridor. Milk-coloured, warm-looking but not warm, lit without a source. No dust. No sound — not silence, but the absence of the Lip, which for them is the truly unnerving part. The impression, on waking, of having been counted.

They discuss it the way we discuss weather: unremarkable, shared, occasionally vivid. Children get it most. Old people who have gone over the col report that it stops. And visitors do not have it for the first three or four weeks, and then they do.

I had it on the twenty-sixth night. I lay on a wet mat on warm stone, the way everyone here sleeps, spine in contact with the rock, the way the lizards sleep, the way the aunts sleep. I do not think this is a coincidence and I do not know what to do with that.

This, I assume, is why nobody sleeps in front of the Slow Door. There is a story — three generations old, so exactly old enough — of a man who did, and who was found in the morning awake, sitting up, and *dry.* Not damp. Dry, in that place, under that water. He stayed dry for eleven days and then split and died of it. They tell it to children as a caution and it may even be true.

**The aunts.**

The six-limbed browsers are called by the same word the people use for a very old female relative, and children are taught to greet them by name as they pass. There are about two thousand, they move four metres an hour, and they are the reason the forest is legible.

What is not in the earlier survey: they are not born slow. A juvenile browser spends its first two years in the canopy, and it is quick — the only quick thing in the bowl — fist-sized, agile, hunted hard by a hook-beaked grey bird that takes maybe nine out of ten. Then the pale thread finishes growing down its spine, and over the course of about a month it comes down out of the trees, and it never hurries again. Adults are not hunted. There would be no point.

They sleep pressed flat to filament-rich rock, and so do the lizards, and so, as noted, do the people.

The moss gardens on their hides are not incidental; three of the species there grow nowhere else, and one of them is the base of the ash paste that keeps four hundred people from rotting. So it is harvested, by hand, gently, by someone walking alongside. You cannot rush the harvest because you cannot rush the animal. A day's gleaning is a day spent covering about forty metres.

This is the work given to the very old — the ones who have gone over the col, who can no longer feel the hum, whose hair is long because nobody cuts it any more. They walk out at first light beside an aunt and come back at dusk with a basket, and they are the most necessary people in the bowl, and they are doing four metres an hour beside an animal with a warm thread in its back, in a forest eating cloud, on top of a machine.

**Whether anything owns the place.**

I want to be careful. There is no evidence of an occupant. There is a great deal of evidence of *maintenance*, which is not the same thing, and the distinction gets harder to hold onto the longer you stay.

The filaments do not respond, test, avoid, or prefer. They re-knit at a fixed rate whether you cut them tidily or with contempt. The thread in the browsers is the same substance as the thread in the rock; nobody can tell you whether the animals grew into the network or were issued by it, and the two hypotheses are not distinguishable from inside the bowl. The pins are the only cold thing on the mountain, and I will note once, and then leave it, that a surveyor I trust has found the same pins at the same eight-hundred-metre spacing on the outer slopes, below the Lip, in the dead cold rock where nothing is warm and nothing has been warm for a very long time, continuing east, in a grid, out of the bowl and away.

So: something enormous was built, over a region rather larger than this, and one small part of it is still running, and a forest fell on the warm part and stayed, and two thousand slow grey animals and four hundred wrinkled people are living on the heated floor of a facility that has never once acknowledged them.

The people's own position is more practical than mine. The lid comes down a hand in a life; they've measured it on the old dry terraces, and there are three abandoned villages above the current one, each higher, each being quietly buried at a millimetre a year, each still readable as a rectangle of foundations going soft. They know they are in the fourth village. They have an unhurried opinion about how many are left. They do not appear to find this distressing, because it is scheduled, and because it is not this year's stone.

What they find distressing is a bad ear, a lost looker, an unscraped step, and a jar opened early.

You will leave sitting down, going out through the Lip with your head between your knees while somebody holds your shoulders, and the last thing you will see of them, when you can see again, is a very old woman with uncut hair standing in the green forty metres from where she started, with her hand flat on the moss of something's back, not hurrying it.
