## The Tenants

Begin by separating the residents from the staff.

Three and a half million bats leave this mountain every evening and three and a half million come back, which makes them commuters. The swiftlets do the same in reverse. They are the wage-earners. They go out into the weather, they work the air over sixty kilometres of lowland, and they return to spend the night, or the day, in accommodation they do not clean. Everything they bring back with them they surrender within hours, involuntarily, to the floor.

The floor is where the residents are. Nothing there has ever left. Nothing there could.

---

**The cone**

Take the largest guano cone, the one under the densest district of ceiling, thirty-one metres from apex to base, and take a handful from its flank. In your palm: a warm, crumbly, black-brown material with the consistency of damp coffee grounds, and in it, running, about forty of them.

They are chestnut-coloured and the size of the top joint of your thumb. The pronotum is a smooth shield, polished to a shine by the passage of other bodies over it — every one of them wears this polish, because every one of them is walked on continuously from birth. The wings are there but they are welded shut and have been for a very long evolutionary while; there is nowhere to fly to. The legs are short and thick and built for gripping a substrate that gives way. The cuticle is greasy, waterproofed against the wet and the ammonia, and it carries a faint sweet stink of its own that persists on your skin for a day.

The eyes have gone to almost nothing: a scatter of facets that can distinguish, at most, the difference between the black of the chamber and the white column of light that falls through the aven in the months when the sun stands right. They do not use this. What they use is the antennae, held forward and swept in a constant figure-of-eight, reading temperature to a fraction of a degree, reading humidity, reading the chemical age of guano — hours old, days old, decades old — and reading, above all, the other roaches, who are everywhere, at every moment, on all sides.

This is the thing to understand about them. A roach on this cone has never in its life been out of contact with another roach. Contact on four or five faces of the body is the baseline condition of existence, the way air is for us. Isolate one on a clean plate and it will run the perimeter until it exhausts itself. Its nervous system is calibrated for a world made of neighbours.

It knows the roof as weather. It cannot perceive the bats; it has no concept whatever of a ceiling covered in animals two hundred metres away. What it has is the fall — a continuous soft precipitation from above that is food, and periodic heavier falls that are also food, and occasionally an enormous fall, a whole body, a dead bat striking the slope and starting a small avalanche, which is a fortnight of abundance for everything within two metres. The sky rains. The sky rains differently at different hours, and the roaches on the eastern flank of the cone have a shifted rhythm from those on the western, because the roost above them turns over at a different hour.

A generation lasts perhaps fourteen months. The oothecae are pushed a centimetre into the warm working guano, where the compost heat of the cone itself incubates them. A roach born on the middle slope will live and die within four metres of its hatching point. There are, in this chamber, something over forty million of them. By weight they outmass the bats.

---

**Around and above them**

Threading the same slopes: the guano crickets, honey-pale, humpbacked, with antennae four times their body length that they carry like a blind man's cane held in both hands. They are the ones with somewhere to go. They make long traverses, twenty and thirty metres, out across the lightless saddles between cones and up the breakdown to the walls, tapping ahead, tapping to the side, building and re-checking a map of the chamber that exists nowhere except in a cricket's head and lasts as long as the cricket. When two meet in the dark they fence briefly with their antennae and then adjust course. It is the only interaction down here that looks like a conversation.

In the fallen bodies, a separate civilisation. A bat that hits the guano and is not immediately broken up is colonised in stages, and the stages are reliable enough to be read as a clock. Flies first, within minutes. Then the beetles, dermestids and their grubs, working the soft tissue. Then, last and longest, the moths — small drab tineids whose caterpillars eat nothing on earth but keratin, and who live inside portable cases they build from their own droppings and the hair of the animal they are consuming. A dead bat is, to them, a village with a six-week lease. The wing membranes go, then the muscle, then the fur, and what is left is a small articulated skeleton, entirely clean, which slides down the cone with the next slump and is buried, and which in some hundreds of thousands of years will be a fossil in a rock made of guano, in a mountain made of coral, and the whole sequence will begin again with a geologist holding it up to the light.

On the walls above, the hunters. Huntsman spiders the span of a spread hand, flattened to nothing, hanging upside down over the nest-shingle with their front legs laid out along the stone, motionless for six hours, then crossing three metres in a second and a half. Whip-scorpions — tailless whip-spiders, properly — which have converted their first pair of legs into two immensely long, threadlike, jointed feelers that are not legs at all any more but instruments, and which walk sideways, very slowly, sweeping the air ahead with those feelers the way a man sweeps a room with a torch beam. An amblypygid takes a year to reach adult size and will moult hanging by its claws in a crevice, an operation lasting most of a day, during which it is soft and entirely defenceless and the entire chamber is full of things that would eat it.

And above them all, at the top of everything that walks here: the centipede. Twenty-five centimetres, twenty-one pairs of legs, the colour of dried blood along the back and orange at the flanks. Old individuals are missing legs — three here, one there — and move without any apparent handicap, because the gait is a wave and the wave routes around the gaps. It goes up the wall into the nest colonies. It moves across four million brackets of hardened spit with a horrible smoothness, a flow rather than a walk, and it takes eggs, and it takes nestlings, and it takes the adults of anything smaller than itself, and the only thing in this hall with a plausible claim to being able to kill one is another one. If this mountain has a resident aristocracy, this is it: a few thousand individuals, each with a slow-growing territory of wall, each having done nothing for fifteen years but eat and moult and occasionally, in the pitch dark, meet another of its kind and perform a careful, mutually murderous courtship.

---

**The passengers**

Now a level finer, because the tenants have tenants.

Go up to the roof and take a single bat off the ceiling — one common wrinkle-lipped bat, twelve grams, a body the size of a woman's thumb — and part its fur, and you will find between six and thirty flies on it. They are wingless. They have thrown away flight entirely; they are flattened, long-legged, combed with backward-facing spines, and they move through the pelage of a bat sideways and very fast, the way a crab moves through weed. They spend their whole lives on one animal, drinking from it, mating on it, sleeping in the crook of its elbow. There is one exception, and it is the strangest fact in this cave. A gravid female leaves the bat — walks down the wing, off onto the stone — carries her single enormous larva to a patch of wall she selects by criteria no one understands, glues it there, and goes back up into the fur. That is the only ground she ever touches in her life, once or twice, for about ninety seconds. The larva pupates on the rock, hatches, and must find its way onto a passing bat in a chamber containing three and a half million of them, which, in fairness, is not a difficult lottery.

And on the flies, mites. And on the mites, mites. And in the guts of all of them, in numbers that make every other count in this description a rounding error, the bacteria — the actual engine, the population that is doing the real work of the mountain. They are the ones turning nitrogen into nitrate and nitrate into nitric acid, and the acid is eating the limestone from below. Every chamber in this system was dissolved out of dead coral by rainwater, but the last few thousand years of enlargement have been done by the excretions of the residents. They are still quarrying. The hall is a teaspoon wider each year because of what lives in it. The oldest fauna built the rock; the current fauna is unbuilding it; both of them are masons and neither of them knows.

---

**The still water**

Past the roosts, where the guano thins to a smear and then to nothing, the passages carry water and the water carries citizens of a different temperament altogether.

The catfish are white — not pale, white, the white of something that never developed the machinery for being any other colour — about eighteen centimetres, with four pairs of barbels that are twice the length you would expect and are never still. There are no eyes. There is a slight dimpling of the skin where eyes were abandoned somewhere in the Pliocene. The whole flank is a lateral line so sensitive that the fish can hold station in a current by feeling the shape of its own pressure wave rebounding off the walls of the pool, which is to say that it navigates by touch at a distance, which is to say it is doing with water exactly what the swiftlets are doing with air, two hundred metres above and utterly unaware.

It eats what washes down: guano slurry, drowned roaches, the bacterial film off the rock. It grows perhaps a centimetre a year. It may live thirty. There are never many of them — forty individuals in a pool, perhaps, a few thousand in the whole system — and they have the metabolism of things that have never once had to hurry. A blind white crab, equally colourless, works the gravel beneath, and shrimps graze the film in slow bands.

Nothing here has ever experienced a day, a season, a shadow, a sound louder than falling water, or a temperature outside a two-degree range. If the roost above were to fail — if the three and a half million were to leave — the catfish would starve within a year without ever learning why.

---

**The wall**

Come back up and out into the roar, and consider the birds, who are the only inhabitants of this mountain that build.

A female swiftlet weighs fifteen grams. For two months of the year a pair of glands under her tongue swell to several times their resting size and secrete a clear, sticky mucus that hardens in air into something like translucent horn. She lays it down in loops. She flies at the wall, hovers for a half-second, touches, draws a strand, and drops away; then does it again; then again, fifteen hundred times over thirty-five days, until she has a half-cup the size of a large thumbprint, striated like a fingerprint, strong enough to hold two chicks and both parents. It is her own body, externalised and hung on a cliff. It is glued to the stumps of previous nests, which are glued to the stumps of nests before those, so that the wall in places is a metre of laminated saliva, and the limestone underneath has not been in contact with air since before this language existed.

Then, every evening of her adult life, she flies into a hole in the world, enters a chamber two hundred metres across in total darkness with a hundred thousand others doing the same thing at the same moment, all of them clicking — a hard, flat, audible double click, not the bats' fine ultrasound but a coarse instrument, like reading a room by throwing pebbles — and she goes to her own bracket, out of four million brackets, and lands on it, and folds, and is home. She does it in under a minute. She has made an error perhaps twice in her life.

Above her, the roof. Three and a half million individuals at three thousand to the square metre, a pelt that ripples and quarrels, and in the nursery districts a density of pups so absolute that the surface is solid pink. A mother returns from the night's foraging with her own weight in insects converted to milk and lands at the edge of a crèche containing forty thousand infants, and finds hers, by voice and by smell, in about a minute, and is right about it nine times in ten. The ceiling in those districts stands at forty degrees — the colony is its own furnace, heating itself by simply existing — and the air holds enough ammonia to burn the lungs of anything that has not spent five million years getting used to it. They have. Their kidneys and their airways are built for a gas that would kill a dog.

Out on the rim at dusk, the ones who wait: bat hawks whose whole annual calendar is organised around the ninety minutes after sundown; peregrines; a serpent eagle that comes for the racers rather than the bats. And the racers themselves, grey-green, three metres of them hanging head-down out of the airstream on a tail hooked into nest-crust, holding that posture for six hours, taking one bat in three days. A snake here may live out its entire adult existence within twenty metres of a single passage. It has no reason to go anywhere. The food comes past it at a rate of forty thousand a minute, twice a day, forever.

---

**The visitors**

There are, three or four times a year, men.

They come with poles of ironwood and bamboo lashed with rattan into a ladder that hangs from dowels driven into the wall — dowels replaced, one at a time, over six generations, so that no part of the structure is original and the structure is nevertheless continuous. They climb in the dark with a lamp held in the teeth and take the nests that have no eggs in them, and leave the rest, mostly, and go down. They breathe the spores and some of them die of it in their forties. They know the chamber by sound: which echo means an overhang, which slackening of the hiss means they have come level with the aven. They do not think of themselves as living here. But the ladder is colonised — roaches in the lashings, a whip-scorpion under the fourteenth rung, a centipede that has taken the hollow of the third bamboo and is, by now, larger than any on the natural rock.

---

So: who lives here.

An older fauna built the rock, out of its own skeletons, a millimetre at a time, and left. A newer fauna moved into the holes in the rock and began dissolving it from within with its own waste, and is still at it. In between, a floor thirty metres deep and rising, which is neither rock nor animal but the process of one becoming the other, warm to the hand, moving continuously in the dark, holding forty million small bodies that will never see the sky and one hundred thousand blind fish-lengths of water beneath them, all of it downstream, all of it eating the difference between what the roof brought home and what the roof required.

Give it another half a million years. The cones will lithify into phosphate rock, shot through with the clean articulated skeletons of bats, and the coral above will have gone to dust, and the mountain will be made of the second fauna the way it was once made of the first.

Everything here is made. Nothing here is finished.
