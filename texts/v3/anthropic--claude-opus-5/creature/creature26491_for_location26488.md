## Who Lives Here

Four things, if you are counting honestly.

One plant, four hundred years old, of unknown parentage, comprising the entire basin and everything standing in it.

One hundred and twenty-nine people at the last copying, plus two away with the carts and one unaccounted, which the ledger records as *out*.

Approximately four thousand valve-beetles, in trays, on the payroll, listed by tray and head and not by name — Tray 9, forty-one head, drawn against the fibre shed's account.

And a bird, sometimes two, in the dead nodes at the back.

The people are the ones you came to ask about, so: the people.

## Bodies

They are pale in a particular way, not sickly but unlit, the colour of something kept under glass. Outsiders who photograph them report that the plates come back with a green cast and blame the lens. It is not the lens. Everyone here has spent their life under pond-glass light and it has settled into them.

They are small-boned and long-necked. The grafters especially: forty years of looking up puts a set in the neck that stays. Eyes large-pupilled, slow to close down, so that on the gravel outside the rim they walk with a hand up like people coming out of a cinema. Their teeth are brown at the gumline, all of them, from the marl water and the tannin thread — a buyer's agent can pick a Pan native out of a crowd in a market town at nine metres, by the teeth and by the feet, because they walk as though the floor were listening.

Their hands are the interesting part. Callus in patterns, and the patterns are legible: tapper's ring at the base of the second finger, carder's split thumb, the shiny inner wrist of a beetle-keeper who has spent a lifetime with trays balanced there. Amber under every nail, from Rule 2, which is written about the plant and applied to people without anyone deciding to. Scraped knees are lacquered. Split lips are lacquered. Nobody in the Pan has an ordinary scar; they have small hard glossy patches, and old people are inlaid with them, and the smell of a person here is iodine and cut grass, which is the smell of having been mended.

Their ailments are three. Shed lung, from silica dust in the carding room, which retires the fibre crew at four flowerings and kills them at six. Stones, from the chalk in the water, which they treat with beer bought over the rim. And a thing between the toes that never entirely goes, because a felt shoe in a basin that drips all year is a felt shoe that is always slightly wet, and there is no cure for the climate.

## Hands

There are two hundred and eleven signs in the Register, which lives in a lacquered box in the fibre shed and is re-copied every ninth year by whoever has the clearest hand.

Two hundred and eleven is a number the elders are proud of and a number that is a lie. The Register is the *canon*. Under it runs everything else: the twelfth hand, as they call it, the unregistered stuff — the shrug-signs, the trade shorthands, the obscene ones, the family ones, the eleven separate signs the children have for *the man who watches visitors*, none of which he knows. New canon is admitted about once a decade after debate. The most recent admission, four flowerings back, means *camera*, and there are people alive who voted against it and have not forgiven the majority.

Beyond the signs, chalk. Every stem wall at chest height is a slate. Conversations go up in the morning and are wiped by the drip or by the person answering, and a native reads chalk-hand the way you read faces; they will tell you Vess is upset from thirty metres by the slope of her letters. Two or three times in the town's history somebody has lacquered over a wall without wiping it, and those panels are down in the ruins now, amber-glazed, holding a shopping list and half a quarrel from two hundred years ago, and children are taken to see them and are not particularly impressed.

## Voices

They have them. They are not much used.

Children learn hands before speech and speak late — three, four, sometimes five — and when the voice arrives it arrives already trained down to the volume of breath. A Pan conversation at close range sounds like two people agreeing not to wake a third. Nobody here has ever needed to project, so nobody can; the voices are thin, unsupported, and go strange and wobbly if pushed, which is part of why the eleven days are the way they are.

Laughter is the difficulty. It cannot be made quiet and it cannot be forbidden, so it has been rerouted: shoulders, a hiss through the nose, streaming eyes, a hand flat on the table. It is entirely recognisable and completely silent, and the Register files it under the sign for *cough*, and the Company's oldest surviving document is a fine, in fibre, levied against a man named Reff for failing to manage it.

Once, at about nine, every child is walked southeast. Past the working town, past the cell racks, into the ruins, to a particular collapsed node three-quarters of the way to the back — dead wood, no valves, nothing left in it that can clamp. There the child is told to shout. Most cannot, the first time. They are stood there until they can, and then they hear themselves, which is a sound they have never made, off wet grey walls, with the fog standing in the room.
