## Traffic is not occupancy

Begin by clearing out the ones who only pass.

The four hundred thousand in the galleries do not live here. The room is a hole in their country and they cross it the way you cross a road: on the polished line, alone, one every forty seconds, carrying. The path is theirs and the room is not. Ask an ant where it lives and it would say *in the wall*, if it said anything, and it would be right.

The frog does not live here either. It lives *at* the room, on the rim, in the sliver of silence between the reed and the hammer, and next week it will be a different frog. The species owns that sliver. No individual owns the rim.

The gnats are not a population. They are a weather. Eleven of them stand in the middle window because eleven is what that column of draught will hold, and if you killed all eleven there would be eleven again by the time you had dried your hands.

None of these leave a mark. That is how you know. Living tissue forgets a knife-cut in two sleeps. Nothing that merely passes through can write on a wall that heals that fast.

## Read the callus

So read it.

The ledge along the western curve is glossed to the finish of a stair-rail in a house where the family never moved out. To put that finish into a stalk that scars over a blade in forty-eight hours, something must lean there oftener than the tissue can forget. Not visits. Not a season of visits. Continuous pressure, renewed faster than healing, for a length of time you should now stop trying to hold in your head.

Longer than a body lasts. That is the second thing the callus says, and it is the more important one. What wore that shelf was not an individual. It was an occupancy — a relay, handed on, with no gap in it long enough for the wall to close.

Someone has always been in here. There has not been an hour of the room's history when the room was empty.

## The plum-coloured place above the ledge

You did not see it, and this is not a failure of attention.

It does not hide by pattern. It hides by climate. Its hide is unpigmented, the colour of the wall in the thin light, of an unripe plum; and it is permeable, so the room's condensation stands on it in the same fine unrunning beads it stands on everything, and it holds itself at the room's own thirty degrees, because it has never in its life had to be any other temperature. Press your palm to it and it would not cool against you. It would not compensate. Compensation is a thing the room does to intruders, and this is not one.

It is not large. Reckon a big cat's mass distributed wrongly: short heavy hindquarters, a long trunk, and arms far too long for the legs, folded up tight so that the elbows come above the shoulders. When it is flat to the western curve it makes a shape about the size and profile of a bolster, and it is a hand's breadth from your ear, and it is doing nothing. It is very good at nothing. Stillness in this species is not a startle response but a discipline, timed in hammer-counts, and there are individuals in the galleries who have held it out through the whole tenancy of a hungrier animal than you.

One foot is hooked in the corky ring near the ceiling. That is what the ring is for now, whatever it was for when the branch was on it. Nothing here was made and everything is used.

## The hands

The hands are the whole animal.

They are broad, flat, webbed to the second knuckle, and the palms are not skin in the ordinary sense but a thick dead pad, dense as felt, dimpled all over with fine pits. It hears with those. Its ear-slits are sealed — grown over in the young, packed and closed, because in this room the ear is not an organ but an injury waiting — and hearing has migrated into the hands, and into the soles, and into the belly it presses to the floor. The hammer comes up through the rootlet mat into its ribs. It knows the state of the water column the way you know whether you are hungry.

And it speaks by damping.

Understand what a room like this does to language. You cannot add a sound here. Nothing you could produce with a throat would clear the hammer, the reed, the ten thousand clicks a minute, the rain-on-a-tent hiss of the galleries. Volume is not available. So meaning is made the other way: a palm laid flat on the turgid wall kills the conduction in a patch the size of the hand, and another one lying against the wall three feet off feels a hole open in the noise. They talk in holes. Sequences of small local silences, of variable depth and duration, pressed into a wall that will not shut up. Two of them at the trunkline window can go at it for an hour, and the room is exactly as loud as it was, and you will hear nothing, and something is being said.

There is no shouting in that language. There is no whispering either. There is only the size of the silence you can make with your hand.

## Eyes that gave the iris away

The eyes are open, lidless, and fixed. Round black apertures without any machinery for opening or closing, because the illumination in this room has not varied measurably in the lifetime of anything that lives in it, and the room keeps the iris. Three thinned patches in the upper wall thicken when the sun finds a gap and thin when cloud crosses; the light in here is held at one value by the plant, on the plant's terms, for the plant's reasons. An animal inside such an arrangement does not need to regulate its own light, and what is not needed is not kept.

The consequence is that this thing can never leave. Not *will not* — cannot. Green-gold, one setting, for the whole of a lineage; a single minute in the open glare over the water plain would burn the retina to a white scald. The slit is a hand's span wide and the room owes the outside nothing, and neither does the resident, and neither can accept anything from it.

It does look at the slit, though. When the bird came and put its head in, the plum-coloured place above the ledge was watching, and the bird was looking at something looking back, and that is why the bird left. This has happened for so long that the birds of this swamp have it in them without learning: *the boles look out.*

## Time, of which there is none

It does not have an age. It has a count.

Everything in its body is geared off the hammer — heart at one beat in eight, breath at one in forty, the long slow rhythms of appetite and torpor and rut all slaved to that same tempo, because nothing else in here keeps time. There is no dawn to be woken by, no cold to sleep through, no dry season to survive and therefore nothing to be early or late for. It sleeps when it is done and wakes when it is done sleeping and the light is the same both times.

Which means it is slow. Nothing forces speed on an animal that has never had a winter coming. It grows for years. It bears one at a time, at long intervals, and the young is not weaned out of the room for a decade. It lives a long while — how long, no one can say, because you would have to sit in the noise for the whole of it and no one does, and it will not be discussed.

## The bowl

The shallow bowl in the floor beside the sump, smooth as a spoon, with the channel running out of it toward the water: that is where the young is put.

It lies in a finger of black water and does not move much. Every ninety seconds the sump swells its half-inch and a tongue of water goes up the channel and turns over the bowl and drains back, and the young is washed, and oxygenated, and kept from cooking in its own waste, ninety seconds at a time, for as long as it takes. That breathing predates the species. It is not respiratory and not tidal and has no locatable cause and it has been doing it since before these animals had this shape, and it is the entire reason they can breed at all, and it is not for them.

The mother lies on the ledge above with her forearms over the lip and one foot in the ceiling ring. This is what glossed the shelf.

And here is the part that is hard to sit with, harder than the carpentry. Nothing can be told to the young. There is no talk in this room; there is only damping, and damping is not enough to carry an instruction. So everything the young needs to know is taught by being shown, in place, by contact: the hand laid over the smaller hand and pressed to the window at the right moment, again, and again, until the smaller hand goes there by itself. A tradition with no language in it. Centuries of technique passed on as touch, in a shout, in the dark that is not dark.

## What the technique is for

The clicks are cavitation. Ten thousand tiny failures a minute, all of them survived.

Most of them are survived because the plant is good at this. Some of them, at the three thinned windows, are survived because a broad dead palm goes flat against the tissue at the moment the strobing goes wrong, and the wall stiffens where it is held, and the column does not break. The animal does not know it is doing this. It does it because when it does it, the wall a foot to the left weeps a thin yeasty exudate, and it turns and licks the wall, and the exudate is most of its diet — that and the occasional ant off the polished path, one a day, which four hundred thousand can spare.

So: not a lodger. Not a parasite. Not, God knows, a guest. A piece of maintenance that eats, hooked into the equilibrium so long ago that neither party retains any record of the negotiation.

Take it out — you cannot, but suppose — and the room does not mourn. The room runs a fraction warm for a season, because its set-point had that body's heat inside it and now the arithmetic is off; a window fails at the third bad strobe; the vent hairs clog on the side nobody grooms and the note goes flat by a hair and then sour; and the wall above the ledge closes to a faint seam, and the ledge unmakes itself, and in some number of years the callus is gone and there is nothing in here that looks like carpentry at all.

## It was there

Go back to the metal object you left on the ledge.

It is where you left it. Warm, beaded, neither rusted nor cleaned. Filed.

Not exactly where you left it. Turned, a little. Both faces beaded, which they would not be if only one had been up. A palm went over it, several times, in whatever passes here for a long while, and put it back near enough that you would say *near enough*, and then went flat to the wall and made a hole in the noise the shape of *nothing, still nothing*, and something forty feet off in the galleries felt the hole and did not come.

You were the event. You were the only thing that has happened in a very long time. And the whole of it, for the resident, consisted of holding still at thirty degrees in the plum-coloured light, being rained on by its own house, counting.
