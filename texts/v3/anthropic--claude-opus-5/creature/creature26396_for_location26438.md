## The body first

Whatever answer you want, you have to start with the thing you are standing inside, because it is alive and it is the largest living thing in that part of the country by a factor no one wants to calculate. It has no name that holds. Downriver they call it *the growth*, which is not a name so much as a shrug.

It is one individual. That is the current view, anyway, and the reason for it is that if you dig anywhere in the Bowl — at the rim, in the plaza, in a doorway on Sixth Street — you come down within a metre onto the same pale mass, unjointed, going on sideways in every direction, faintly warm, and if you cut it (people have) the whole Bowl's pitch drops for about four days and then comes back. It is not forty houses. It is one body that has grown forty hollows in itself, the way a bone grows a marrow cavity, for the same reason: because a solid thing that size would be pointless and would fall over.

Its habits are the habits of a tree, slowed and made strange. It thickens. It sheds — the leaf-laminate on the low walls is a renewable crop, put out in sheets and cropped off and put out again. It drinks from a water table two hand-spans down, which is why it lives in a flood scar and nowhere else. And it flowers in its own attic, in rooms it declined to give stairs to, which tells you everything about who those rooms are for.

The flowers are the whole point of the organism and they are miserable-looking: dry greenish panicles, no nectar, no scent, pollen in coarse heavy grains that a gale would not lift. Such pollen has to be carried, and the carrier has to be found, and finding is what the noise is for. The Bowl is not singing to itself. It is shouting across a hundred and forty kilometres of flat wet country at other Bowls it will never know about and at the one animal in the region that still bothers to listen.

It is the only broadcaster in a country of mutes. That is the joke the place is built on, and the plant is not in on it.

## The tenants in the walls

Several hundred million sawyers, and you will not meet one.

They are thumb-joint-sized, dark, blunt at both ends, with a ribbed plate on each flank that they draw against a hardened ridge on the leg. Nothing about them is remarkable except the completeness of their interiority. They have no eyes — not reduced eyes, no eyes, no scar where eyes were. They have no waterproofing on the cuticle, because there is no dry air in their world. They cannot walk on a flat surface; put one on a table and it will lie on its side working its legs like a swimmer, appalling to watch, dead in an hour. A sawyer's universe is a tube slightly wider than a sawyer, at blood heat, at ninety-nine per cent humidity, wet-walled, running with sap, and full — utterly full — of a roar it cannot hear because it has no ears either.

They do not eat the wood. This matters. A wood-eater would have brought the whole instrument down in a season. They tap the vessels, drink, and move on, and the plant lets them, and grows a little extra to be drunk from, and in exchange every one of those insects spends its entire adult life sawing its flank against its leg with no rhythm and no purpose it could describe, and the sum of those hundreds of millions of small dry scrapings is the continent of sound that fills the Bowl to the brim and shakes the pollen out of the attic.

They do not chorus, and now you know why: they are not calling each other. There is no message in it. It isn't communication, it's *labour* — the rent — and rent has no cadence.

A brood matures behind the north colonnade and the wall goes hot under your shoulder because a million new bodies have started work at once in a space the size of a wardrobe. In the mornings men with bad joints stand against that wall on purpose. It is the most benign thing in the Bowl and one of the few kindnesses available there, and it comes from an animal that will live and die within nine centimetres of where it hatched, never having encountered a surface, a horizon, a season, or another species.

The only true citizens of the town have never seen the town. They have not seen anything. They are the note, and they are inside the note, and there is no possible arrangement of their bodies from which the note could be perceived.

## The tideline

Everything else in the Bowl lives on a shore.

The loud has a level and the level moves — up a metre by late morning, down again at dusk, and further down in the hour before rain. Where its surface lies against the walls, the suspended dust piles up, the way scum gathers at the edge of a slack pool. That haze at knee height is not inert. It is mineral and it is also, in some proportion, food: fine pollen, sloughed leaf-wax, wing-scales, the powdered dead. It never settles. So it is grazed in the air, permanently, by small pale flies that do nothing but hang there with their mouths open, and the flies are worked by a slate-grey bird the size of a thumb that hunts in absolute silence in a band you could sweep with an outstretched arm.

Above the band: nothing much. Below the band: nothing much. In it: a dense, ridiculous, three-hand-spans-thick belt of life running around the whole inside of the Bowl, migrating up the walls and down again through the day, and every spider in the town keeps its web at exactly that height and moves it when the level moves.

Look at a doorway on Ninth Street at noon and the webs are at your hip. Come back at four and they are at your knee. The town has a tide, and it is made of noise, and things live at the edge of it the way barnacles live at the edge of the sea.

## The map of stillness

The spiders that hunt on the wood rather than in the air have solved a harder problem, and their solution is the strangest fact about the Bowl after the emptiness.

A vibrating body has nodes: places that do not move. On every wall in that town there are patches, palm-sized, hand-sized, some no bigger than a coin, where the wood is dead still while everything around it is scoured smooth with motion. The spiders live only there. They sit in stillness in the middle of a hurricane of sound and feel outward from it, and anything that crosses onto the still patch is felt at once and taken. Their world is not a wall. It is an archipelago of quiet, and they know it perfectly.

The nodes shift as the level rises. So the spiders shift, all of them, all day, everywhere in the Bowl, in a slow synchronised crawl that no one notices because there is no reason to look. And a long green snake, thin as a bootlace, hunts the spiders by knowing the same map — moving across the walls from island to island of stillness, arriving where the spider must be.

You can find the nodes yourself, with a flat hand, patiently. It is the only thing in the Bowl that feels like being told a secret. You put your palm on a doorjamb that is trying to shake your bones apart, and slide it, and then there is a patch of absolute peace under your hand, and something small and dark on it that does not move, and it lets you.

## The mute congress

The frogs are the colour of old wax and there is one in almost every window opening, which is territory: a window is a lit rectangle against a dark room, and a frog in a window is a silhouette that other frogs can see from across the street. Each has a lobe under the jaw, white on one face and black on the other, and it flicks. All afternoon, all down the colonnade, flick, flick — and because the openings are in rows, the flicking travels, and you can watch a pale ripple run the length of the north arc in about four seconds and come back slower. A visitor once recorded four thousand flicks in sequence. Nothing repeated. Nobody has decoded it and probably nobody can, because there is no way to ask a question in that language and no way to hear the answer.

They sit in the windows for the semaphore, and they sit at that particular height because that is where the flies are, so even the courtship is pinned to the tideline.

The birds have gone gaudy the way birds go gaudy when singing stops paying: wing-bars flared open like fans, tail-flicks, one flycatcher species that has evolved a stiff, complete, formal little bow, head down, wings half-out, hold, up. Fledglings raised in the Bowl never hear an adult of their own kind. There is nothing to learn. In the rain-hour, when the sound drains out and the frogs go off like a shelf of dropped china, the flycatchers do not sing. They stand on the wet kerbstones in the ordinary afternoon and bow to each other, over and over, absurdly, correctly, in a silence that is now only ordinary silence, and it is one of the two or three things about the Bowl that people cannot talk about afterwards without going quiet.

The ants live in the flood-wrack on the rim, above the line, and commute — down into the roar every morning in files along the drainage channels, up again at dusk. They are the only lineage in the Bowl that crosses the surface twice a day, and they are also the reason the rooms stay empty. The floors convulse and sort, and everything with mass walks itself out of the doorway and into the street, and the ants meet it there and take it away. They also farm a grey fungus on the lip of the plaza sink, which they irrigate from the black water, and which they defend from nothing, because there is nothing that wants it.

Six or seven leaners: pig-sized, soft-mouthed, whisker-navigating, warm as bread. They crop the leaf-laminate off the low walls and by cropping it they prune the instrument, which is very likely why the plant grows the stuff. They cannot see well and do not need to. They know the eleven streets absolutely. In a world with no calls, touch is the whole of greeting, and so they lean — against walls, against each other, against you, settling their weight onto your thigh with the patience of something that has never once been in a hurry, and staying. At the loudest hour they go down through a worn gap in the plaza laminate into the barn-sized void beneath the drum, where the note is so deep it is not a note, and they lie there pressed together in the middle of it.
