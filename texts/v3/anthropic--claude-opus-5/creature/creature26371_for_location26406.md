# The People of the Table

## Bodies

Start with the hands, because the hands are the part that has changed most.

They are long-fingered out of proportion to the palm, and the palms themselves are narrow, and the skin of them has gone to horn in four places — the pads of the first and second fingers, the heel of the hand, and a bar across the inside of the knuckles where a lifetime of notched poles has left its groove. A child of six already has the beginnings of it. An old woman's hands look less like hands than like tools that have been repaired many times with the same material. They climb constantly. Everything here is above or below something else.

The rest follows from thin air and no water. They are short — a tall man comes to the chin of a lowlander, if the lowlanders were ever to come up, which they do not — and they are deep through the chest, so that a person seen from the front seems ordinary and from the side seems to have been built for a different climate, which is true. Their blood is thick and dark and slow to stop when it is spilled, which is one of the reasons blades are not used on each other. Their resting heartbeat is unhurried. They do not run. Running is not forbidden; it is simply understood to mean that somebody has died or is about to, and a running figure in a street makes every head turn and every conversation stop, even a hundred paces off, even under the drone.

The skin is a deep grey-brown, and over it, always, two layers that are not skin: black flour worked into every crease until it is indistinguishable from complexion, and above that a dressing of column wax, scraped, warmed in the hand, and rubbed in. They wax themselves for exactly the reason the columns do. A person who has let their wax go dull is a person in trouble — grieving, ill, ashamed — and this is legible across a street at a glance, and someone will come.

They sweat very little. They cool by holding still, in shade, which they are extraordinarily good at; a keeper can sit against a trunk so long that the ground-birds walk over his feet. And they do not go into the beams. Where a shaft of that hard narrow sunlight comes down through a gap in the canopy it lands on the floor as a white coin with an edge like cut paper, and the whole traffic of the town flows around those coins the way water goes round a stone. Children are taught it before they are taught their own names. To be caught out in the open, on the bare plateau beyond the grove, with the sun standing on you and nothing between — this is the death they use in curses.

Their eyes are set deep under heavy brows, permanently half-lidded, and they read the bronze dimness with an ease that would be uncanny to anyone else. They look at each other sideways. Straight staring is for enemies and for the gulf.

## The Shout and the Hand

Everyone shouts. Not in anger — the drone simply eats anything less. Their speech is built out of clicks, stops, and hard barking consonants that carry through the sawing noise, and to an outsider a mother comforting an infant sounds like an argument between stonemasons. Under it, and doing at least half the work, runs the hand-language: a fluent grammar of the fingers held at chest height, which does not translate the speech so much as accompany it, carrying tone, doubt, irony, the identity of the person being discussed. Two people can hold an entire private conversation across a crowded platform while shouting about something else entirely. They do this constantly. It is considered good manners rather than deceit.

Because of the noise, silence is a formal event. To lower the hands and stop speaking in company is to make an announcement. It is how a person asks forgiveness, and how a council indicates that it has, at last, noticed a decision.

Their taboos are all one taboo, expressed several ways. Spitting is monstrous. So is letting a nose run without catching it. So is any careless leaking. Urine is not waste but stock, collected in waxed gourds, used for curing fibre and softening sap and a dozen crafts, and a child learns the household's gourd before it learns the household's tools. Nothing here is thrown away in the state of being wet.

And tears, therefore, are the only extravagance in the world. To weep in front of someone is to hand them a piece of your body's small hoard, and it is caught — genuinely caught, on a palm-sized square of paddle-fibre held to the cheek by whoever the weeping is for. The squares are kept. Everyone has a bundle of them, dark stiff little rags with a faint salt bloom, and a person's bundle is the honest account of their life; it is the first thing anyone would save from a fire, if fire were possible here. At a reconciliation the two parties exchange bundles for a season. At a wedding they begin one together.

A child's first cloth is usually cut at the edge. They are taken out at four or five years old to see a storm working the country two thousand feet below — the grey braid, the lightning going sideways under their feet, the thunder arriving thin and late like an apology. Most children cry. Nobody has ever satisfactorily explained why, and the adults do not press them about it; they simply have the cloth ready.

## Which Flowering

Nobody counts years. Years here are all the same year.

They count flowerings, and because the flowering produces a great pulse of conceptions, most people belong to a cohort — a crowd of two or three hundred born within a year of each other, who grow up as a single mob, are initiated together, marry mostly out of each other, and go grey and stiff and die in a slow wave that leaves visible gaps in the town for a decade. Ask a person's age and you ask which flowering; they answer with a name, and everyone within earshot immediately knows their politics, their probable rivals, and roughly which level of the town they sleep on.

But a fifth of the population is born in the long dry middle of a cycle, in the thin years, and these are the *between*. They have no cohort. They grow up among adults, among the last cohort's children and the next one's, belonging properly to neither, and the town has made a use of this: the between are its messengers, its arbiters, its clerks of memory. A between child is trained early to carry an accurate account of a dispute from one platform to another without editing it. A between adult sits at the council not to speak but to say what was said last time, and the time before, and what the grandmother of the man now shouting had agreed to in the year of the last flowering but one. They are trusted precisely because they have nobody. It is not always a kind life. There is an old, half-joking, entirely sincere blessing given to a woman who conceives in a thin year: *may your child be dull enough to be happy.*

## Tappers

The tapping families are the aristocracy, if a place with four hundred people can have one, and they do not look it. A tapper is a small waxed person with a knife of black glass in a resin haft, a coil of spine-lattice ladder over one shoulder, and a way of standing at the foot of a column with her head back and her mouth slightly open, doing nothing at all, for as long as an hour.

That hour is the job. The cutting takes four minutes. The hour is spent reading the trunk — the colour under the wax, the way the fluting has gone at the shoulder of the last segment, the fatness of the paddles forty times her height above, the pale ladder of ovals climbing the north face where her mother and her mother's mother and six more before that took theirs. She is deciding whether the plant can spare a finger's width of wound this decade, and where, and at what angle it will scar clean.

The answer is very often no. A tapper's chief professional act is refusal, and the households that hold rights on a slow column go thirsty on schedule and do not complain, because complaint would be an accusation of theft against the future.

Their crime, then, is the deep cut. It is easy, it is invisible for eighty years, and it kills. A column that has been overtapped browns at the arm-tips a lifetime later and goes hollow at the base a lifetime after that, and by then the guilty hand has been sitting dry in the black flour at its own column's roots for four generations, entirely beyond reach.

So they punish the dead. The name is struck from the column's history — not erased, there is no writing, but formally *unsaid*, which is worse, since the histories are recited and a gap in a recitation is audible. And in the extreme case, where the deep cut is proved and the body can still be identified among the seated ancestors, the body is taken up, carried across the bare plateau in the killing light, and dropped over the edge.

They call this being given to the weather. It is the only thing on the Table that anyone is genuinely afraid of.

## Keepers, and What They Keep

The shearer-keepers are a different sort of person, and everyone says so. They walk two hundred paces a day, which is to say they walk nowhere; they spend their lives inside the pace of an animal that will outlive their grandchildren's grandchildren.

A keeper family does not own a shearer. It attends one.
