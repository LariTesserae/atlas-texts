**The Attendants of the Sixth Draught**

Start with the hands, because the hands are what the Flat has actually made. The rest of the body is a mounting for them.

They are large hands on small bodies, long in the palm, the fingers not tapered but even-thick to the tip, and they are maintained the way the gauges are maintained. Callus is permitted on the heel of the palm and the outer edge, where a person braces against a housing; it is forbidden, by nothing more than good sense and everybody's opinion, anywhere on the pads. Attendants keep a scrap of fine slag in a pocket and work their fingertips smooth against it while they walk, absently, the way other peoples whistle. An attendant who has let her reading surfaces go horny is spoken to. Twice, and she is moved off the pressure circuits and onto the chutes.

The body they are mounted on is short — an adult stands about a meter and a half — and dense, and almost hairless on the scalp and thickly haired on the forearms, which is the reverse of what an outsider expects and is not vanity. There is no wind on the Long Flat, but there are drafts inside the works, and a failing seam announces itself first as a cool thread against the hair of the wrist. They cultivate it. They comb it. An old attendant can hold a forearm along a joint and tell you which of four bolts has gone slack.

They do not sweat. Water is too dear for evaporative cooling and has been for longer than the ledger runs, and so the skin is dry, close-grained, faintly waxy to the touch — the same phrase the surveys use for the rock, and not by accident; they have been made out of the same economy. What this means practically is that they cannot hurry. A body that cannot shed heat by wetting itself overheats in about four hundred meters of running. Their famous slowness is not ceremony. It is a boundary condition. When an attendant does run — and it happens, a gauge falling, a lagging blown — she runs, and then she lies face-down on the cooling terrace afterward with her arms spread, and somebody comes and reads her every quarter hour until she is ambient again, and this is a serious event and is written down.

Eyes: still there, still used, and enormous in the socket. The iris is thin and pale and the pupil almost always wide. They see, in the sense that they can find a doorway by the blue ghost-glow of old slag and can read the colour at a furnace throat to within about fifteen degrees. But sight is the sense they use last, because sight is the sense that costs someone else something. Every photon they see has left the system. An attendant asked to describe a person will describe the person's mass, gait, hand-warmth, breath-rate, and the sound their boots make on polished rock, and will not mention the face, and would be puzzled to be asked.

---

Their language has three bodies: the spoken, the tapped, and the written, and the tapped is the oldest and the one they think in.

Speech is low and slow and used at short range, because speech masks the note, and the note is how everyone knows the district is well. To raise the voice is a thing done perhaps twice in a life. But the conduits run everywhere, and they carry a knock beautifully, and so the Draught is threaded with a second and continuous conversation of stems tapped against housings: position reports, requests, complaints, jokes of a very dry sort, and names.

A name is a rhythm before it is a word. Four clicks half a kilometer off, arriving thin and clean — that is a person announcing herself at a junction: three-and-one, going north. She has a spoken name too, which is the rhythm said aloud, and a written one, which is the rhythm cut into her handrail tokens. When she dies the rhythm does not die. Within the ninth-day weighing it is reissued, and a child somewhere grows up knowing that she is the forty-second three-and-one, and knowing the circuit, and the notch-count, and the small peculiarities of the seventeen valves that have been three-and-one's business since before anyone can say.

Their colour words are furnace words, and they use them for everything. A child is *cherry*: bright, careless, throwing off heat that has to be budgeted for. The long middle of a life is *plum*. An elder is *at ash*. To say of someone that she went dark in the ninth month is to say she died, and the phrase carries exactly the weight of a casting stopping its glow in the sand at the end of an hour: expected, orderly, the completion of a cooling.

---

Now the thing that governs them, which is not a belief but an arithmetic.

A body is a heater. Two hundred bodies are twenty kilowatts, give or take, poured continuously into a plant tuned to the fourth decimal. The roster is therefore not a population. It is a coefficient. It appears in the ledgers among the dampers and the flue diameters, and it is not adjustable by desire.

So: when someone goes dark, the note shifts by an amount no instrument but a listener can detect, and the Assay House issues a warrant, and a conception is permitted. Death precedes birth here in a strict and administered sequence. Every child in the Sixth Draught is, precisely and by the numbers, somebody's replacement, and knows it from the beginning, and does not find it grim. What they find grim is the opposite condition — being unwarranted, uncounted, outside the balance — and they have a word for that too.

Childhood is the one permitted inefficiency, and it is confined. The young are kept together in the low houses near the south knuckle, where they are allowed to be loud and to waste, and they run, which they will never do again, and they cry, which they will mostly never do again, and the district hears them faintly across five kilometers of bare rock and takes a complicated pleasure in the sound. At about eleven a child is fitted for felt soles and stops being exempt. The thing they are taught first is not a skill. It is the note. They are sat at a knuckle with a palm on the stone until they can hold the pitch in their heads without reference, and they are tested on it for years, and an adult who has lost it is not trusted with anything.

The dying are attended by a tallower. It is set on the back of the hand and it does what it always does, which is crawl toward the warmer side; and it is moved, and crawls again; and there comes a point at which it stops choosing, and settles, and does not care which way it is turned. That is the reading. No one has ever improved on it.

Afterward the body goes out to the north terraces with the castings and is laid flat, and the family and the circuit stand around it in the dark and put a palm on it every hour, taking turns, until it reads the same as the stone. This takes most of a night. It is the only occasion on which they will deliberately spend that much time doing nothing measurable, and they consider it not a waste but the last legitimate entry in a person's column: *returned, in full, at the proper rate.* Then the body is calcined and the ash is let up, and joins the ceiling, which is what the ceiling is. The lid that keeps the warmth in is made of everyone who has ever kept it.

Of the eleven occasions on which a column of that ash came down and stood on the plain and walked a straight line for a day and a half, the ledgers say only the dates. What the attendants say to each other about it, privately, in the dead space between double doors, is not written anywhere, and if you ask they will tell you the dates.

---

They eat when something breaks.

There is no plant life and there never was. The mineral cakes give them bound water and a thin sufficiency of the rest, and they chew them for hours at a stretch, which is also why their jaws are heavy and their speech is slow and flat. Meat is tallower, and tallowers are harvested only from a failed lagging, when the failure is repaired and the colony that gathered on it has to come off anyway. So hunger is the signal that the plant is running perfectly, and a good meal is the taste of somebody's error. There have been fat generations. They are named in the ledgers and they are named with shame.

Their hours are their own breathing. A trained attendant breathes at a fixed ratio to the note and can tell you the hour to within four minutes with her eyes shut, standing in the middle of sixty kilometers of nothing. The count drifts at the end of a life, and an elder who has begun to run fast against the note is understood to be going, and is offered the last posting, which is the one they all want: a stone cell at a knuckle, a cake ration, and twenty years of sitting still and hearing the whole district at once. Listeners report by tap, twice a day, four clicks. They are instruments, and they know it, and they are the only people here who are ever described as fortunate.

And they sing. It is the only extravagance, and it is done in the dead spaces between the double doors, where nothing carries: a low massed humming, tuned deliberately to intervals that lie underneath the note and do not mask it, felt in the sternum more than heard. Six or eight of them will stand shoulder to shoulder in a stone box the size of a closet, in absolute darkness, at exactly the same temperature as everything else, and do this for an hour. Outsiders have been let in to listen. They report that nothing happened.

---

Once, at about sixteen, every attendant walks out to the Fifth.

It lies forty kilometers west: the same rails, the same rounded low works, the same sealed windows, all of it cold. Not cold as the Flat's rim is cold. Cold as nothing here is ever cold — perhaps fifteen degrees down, enough that a hand laid on a housing comes away wrong, enough that breath in the throat has an edge. The young ones go in pairs and are required to stay one full night, and what they are being shown is not the ruin. It is the silence. The Fifth has no note. You stand in the middle of a foundry hall the size of a village, and there is nothing in your feet, and nothing in the air, and nothing at all to hold the pitch against.

They come back different, and they hold the pitch for the rest of their lives.

---

So if you come up over the rim with a lamp, they will take the lamp, and they will be polite about it, and it will be entered as theft, and you will be given a figure in joules and a schedule. And then, before anything else, they will read you: a hand flat on your chest and one on your back, a temple laid against the hand, a long pause, a thumb on the wrist. They will tell you how far you have walked and roughly what you ate and that you are carrying more water in your body than exists in their Assay House, which they will mention without envy, the way you might mention the weather. They will compute what you are going to cost them, and it will be a real number, and it will matter.

And then they will take you in and seat you and put a cake in your hand, because you are warm, and warmth belongs in the system, and the sooner you are inside the sooner you are part of the balance instead of a leak in it.

That is the whole hospitality of the Sixth Draught, and it is meant sincerely, and it is the same hospitality they show the rock.
