## The People of Sough

Six hundred and eleven, on the board in the guild hall. The number is chalked, and the chalk is renewed on the first of the month, and in the last forty years it has not gone below five hundred and ninety or above six hundred and forty. The board has a second column, ruled in the same hand, headed *settled*, which has not been added to since 1889 and which nobody rubs out.

Start with what you can see of them.

They are not pale so much as unlit — the colour of people who get their light sideways. Under the arc-lamps everyone in Sough looks faintly green about the jaw, and at the reading hour, in that milk-coin noon, you see that this is only partly the lamps. The comb crews really are green: verdigris takes in the cuticle and the nail-bed and the crease of the knuckle and does not come out, and an old scraper's hands look like something dug up. It is thought unhealthy and it is not thought unhealthy enough to stop.

Everybody's skin is soft. Not delicate — soft, the specific waterlogged softness of a hand that has been in the bowl too long, but permanent, and not just on the hands. A Sough face at fifty has a smoothness that reads at first as youth and then, when you get closer, does not read as anything you have a word for. The lines are there. They are simply not creased in. Fingerprints go by about thirty; the ridges remain but the skin above them has been pliant so long that it has stopped taking a clean impression, which caused a great deal of official trouble in the nineties and was resolved by the terraces simply agreeing not to require it of them.

They are broad in the chest and quiet-moving. They do not startle. You can drop a spanner behind a Sough man on Tin Walk and he will not turn round, partly because he did not hear it and partly because he has already worked out from the ring where it landed and whether it went through.

---

Their hearing is the interesting thing, and it is not what you would guess. They are not deaf. They are deaf in a band — a notch, roughly two octaves wide, sitting exactly over the engines. Below and above it they hear better than you do, appreciably better; a fourteen-year-old on the pump gang can tell you which of the seventeen vents is passing wet steam from inside a shut room. But shout the word *stop* at a Sough adult in the crest of a stroke and it will not arrive. It is the wrong shape. It goes into the hole.

Consequences, all practical.

They talk in the trough. A Sough sentence has a natural length of about nine words, because that is what fits, and their speech everywhere else in the world comes out in these nine-word units with a hanging pause between, which is the rhythm outsiders find hypnotic and which is nothing but arithmetic. Their letters do it too. Old Sough correspondence is full of a mark like a short vertical rule dropped into the middle of a sentence — the crest-bar — and the parish register keeps it as late as 1902, so that entries read *born of Attie Cattril and of Ruel Cattril her husband | a daughter, Susan, at the fourth hour, wet week*. The pause is written down. The engine is in the handwriting.

And they sign. Not a whole language — a working one, maybe four hundred terms, all of them one-handed because the other hand is holding something. Numbers, tools, wetness grades, the names of the four engines, *go up*, *go down*, *don't*, *later*, *how long*, *I heard it too*. Courting couples use it across the sheds and think themselves unobserved, and are not.

The drip-listeners are real and there is nothing to them. Any child of nine can place themselves in the town blind; the old ones can tell you the water table to a finger and whether the low pump ran last night. There is one woman on Slate Walk, Merrell, eighty-one, who is said to be able to hear the difference between rain on the vapour ceiling and rain on the berm, which are separated by four hundred feet of air and about ninety seconds. She says she cannot. She then tells you which it is.

---

They grade the world by wetness and they organise their lives around one commodity, which is not water. It is dry.

Every household has a dry: a cupboard, a shelf, a boxed alcove backed onto a reheater flue, and the size of it is the most exact statement of a family's standing that the town produces. In it go the papers, the flour, the one good coat, the fiddle if there is one, the photographs. Everything else is wet and is expected to be wet and is fine. They sleep slung — box-cots on chains off the ceiling joists, a foot and a half of air under the mattress, and a Sough person cannot sleep on a floor and will tell you the floor feels like standing up.

They wear waxed reed-cloth, which is stiff and pale and goes the colour of strong tea in about a year, and under it a knitted stuff spun from the same coarse allotment reed, which is scratchy and warm and holds no smell. Gutta-percha soles, cut and re-cut, four or five to a pair of uppers. No leather, no wool, no horn, no bone. This is not doctrine. There is simply nothing here that any of those come out of, and what comes down the berm in a crate is regarded as slightly unlucky and rather grand, like a foreign coin. There are eleven leather objects in private hands in Sough and most people can name eight of them.

At the reading, if it is a good one, they come out. Steps, the low roofs, the berm terraces. Six hundred people sitting in the pale for an hour with mail and ledgers and the terrace papers three days old, and this is when marriages are announced and disputes are put, because it is the only hour of the day the whole town can see one another's faces. A stranger up on the rim at noon looks down and thinks: a congregation. It is a reading. It is exactly and only a reading.

---

What they do, in order of how wet it makes you:

The **drowners** keep the piles under. Twenty-two feet of larch, a century and a half of it, and their whole trade is making sure no timber in Sough ever gets the chance to be half-wet. They run the sumps, they set the flood-boards, they know the raft the way a man knows a boat. They are underground more than not. They are the largest guild and the shortest-lived and they know it and are impossible in company.

The **scrapers and brine-men** work the four sheds — forty ranks of comb apiece, fins a knuckle apart, and every fin gets gone over on a rota you could set a calendar by. It is close, hot, streaming work in a green library, and they come out with that stain in their hands and a cough they call the catarrh and treat as weather.

The **timber crews** replace. They do not repair. Nothing in Sough is repaired; it is swapped out before the bracket fungus gets a purchase, and the crews carry their own gantry sections and think of the town as a thing perpetually being handed a new bone.

The **pumpmen** go down to the underdays on the slow cycle, in pairs, always in pairs, with a whistle each.

And the **featherers**, who do no wet work at all.
