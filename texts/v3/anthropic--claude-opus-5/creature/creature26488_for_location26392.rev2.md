# The People of the Inhale

They have no name for themselves. The word they use means, approximately, *the ones in here*, and the word for the Well itself is the word for the held breath — the pause at the top of an inhalation, before you decide what to do with it. Everything else is *out*, or *up*, or *the Finished*.

## Bodies

They are short and very deep. An adult man stands about a meter and a half and measures more around the chest than around the hips; the ribcage is a barrel of the same design as everything else here, built to squeeze thin air for what it has. Resting heartbeat is slow. Blood is thick and dark and slow to clot, and a cut that would be nothing at sea level is taken seriously, because blood is water, and water that leaves the body has left the town.

Under the fig raft, in green half-light, five thousand years have taken most of the color out of them. Skin is the pale grey-olive of the underside of a rosette leaf, and it goes blotchy and warm-looking by lamplight. The exceptions are the ones who work at the rim or cross the plateau: guano-men who come up through the flute-mouths into the noon glare, cairn-walkers, the few who trade. They tan in a hard line at the collar and the wrists and they squint permanently, and by forty they have thick white wings of tissue creeping across the eyes from the corners. Everyone can tell at a glance who has been up. It is a caste mark and it cuts both ways: outside work pays, outside work is admired, outside people go blind and die coughing.

The feet are the most specialized thing about them. Wide, high-arched, with a callus ridge along the inside of the big toe from a lifetime of dished stairs and corbel edges. Nobody wears anything on the foot. The children climb before they walk level, because there is almost nothing level to walk on, and a child of four can go from the ninth terrace to the floor faster than an adult by a route no adult can use anymore.

They do not sit on furniture. Furniture is volume, and volume is the only true wealth. They sit in niches — personal ones, cut to the shape of a particular back over a particular lifetime, inherited and then re-cut, so that a grandson's shoulders slowly erase his grandmother's. A stranger placed in the wrong niche is visibly uncomfortable and cannot say why.

Everyone has mites. Everyone smells of resin, formic acid, warm carton board, and the roach bins. They do not notice this. Traders notice it from thirty meters and never stop noticing it.

There is a mottled fungus that maps itself across the shoulders and flanks of nearly everyone past adolescence, pale on pale, in shapes like coastlines. It is harmless. It is read. An old woman's shoulder-map is a subject of mild, appreciative comment, the way elsewhere one might admire a scar or a ring; the maps are sanded off before a marriage and allowed to grow back afterward, which is understood to mean that the two of them will now be making a new coastline together.

## Speech, and the Seams

The cicada roar sits in the frequency band where consonants live. So their language, over two hundred generations, has moved out of that neighborhood. It is spoken from the chest, low and long, all open vowels and voiced stops — *m*, *n*, *w*, *l*, *r*, *g*, *b* — and there is not one sibilant in it. A trader's language, full of hissing, sounds to them like a leak: a thin, wasteful, wet noise, faintly obscene.

But mostly they do not speak. They sign. The hands came first and are still first; infants sign before they vocalize, and the full manual language carries across the pit, terrace to terrace, level to level, through the whole roaring afternoon. A woman on the seventh level can conduct an entire negotiation with a man on the third while both of them continue working. At night it is done with lamp-shutters, a flicker-code of covering and uncovering, and from the rim at midnight, if you knew how to read it, half those six hundred orange points would be talking.

Voice is reserved for the near and the serious. And for the seams.

The three cicada species change shift at intervals, and for anywhere from four seconds to two minutes the Well drops into something that isn't silence but is a great deal less. The seams are unpredictable and the whole town is tuned to them; a conversation pauses mid-gesture, twelve hundred people draw breath at once, and the important sentence — the proposal, the confession, the price — is spoken into the gap. To say a thing outside a seam is to say it lightly. There is a legal distinction here. An agreement made in a seam is binding; the same words in the roar are *weather*, and everyone is entitled to have meant nothing by them.

## Water Manners

No one in the Well has ever seen still water. Not a puddle, not a dish, not a reflection. When they drink from a tapped stem they hold a cupped leaf under the chin, and every child learns on a dry stone floor with a clean slab beneath, and the slab is inspected afterward. The verb for *spill* is the same verb they use for *betray*.

Spitting is close to assault. Fines are paid in sap, by volume, measured in a carved bone tube, and the sap goes to whoever was spat upon, who is expected to drink it in front of witnesses and look bored.

The hardest problem is grief. Tears are the only water a person makes without being able to help it, and within a minute of a child crying on an open terrace the ants have found the salt and are climbing the child. So weeping is a managed act. Each household keeps a grief-board: a soft sheet of unfinished termite carton, absorbent, about the size of two hands. You press your face into it. When it is done being used it is planted — pushed down into the root-mat under the floor, where the fungus takes it, and in this way a person's grief goes back through the mycelium into the leaves and eventually into the sap that somebody's grandchild will tap. This is not metaphor to them. It is the actual route. They can draw it.

Birth happens over the mat, for the same reason. So does the end of any wound that will not stop.

And then there are the swiftlets, who build their nests out of their own saliva. The town finds this appalling — an entire bird species that pours water onto a wall to make architecture — and harvests the nests anyway, in enormous quantity, and sells every gram of it away down the plateau. Nobody here will eat one. They are called *the spenders' houses*, and small boys dare each other to touch one and then wipe the hand for a long time afterward.

Twice a year the traders come across the Finished: people who carry water in bags, who *slosh*, whose loads audibly move when they set them down. Parents bring children to the rim to watch them arrive, the way children elsewhere are brought to see a freak or a famous beast. The traders bring obsidian, salt, copper wire, and news of a world with rivers in it, and take away carton board, resin, swiftlet nests, and a powdered alkaloid from a wall succulent that is worth more than all the rest together and that nobody in the Well has any use for at all.

## Rooms

There are thirteen hundred people and a fixed volume, and the whole social order is downstream of that.

You cannot marry until there is a room. There are no unoccupied rooms. Therefore the room must be cut, by hand, with a chert adze, out of the calcrete wall, by the two people who intend to live in it — and so a courtship in the Well is measured in depth. *He is three arms in.* *They are five arms in and have started the ceiling.* A courtship that ends is visible forever afterward as an abandoned socket that someone else will eventually extend, and there are families living in the joined ruins of four separate failed engagements.

Where you may cut is decided by the Cut-Warden, who reads the wall by tapping it and listening — one of the few jobs that requires a seam, and the reason Cut-Wardens are famously patient. Cut in the wrong place and you break into a flute, and two hundred thousand bats come through your bedroom wall at dusk. This has happened eleven times in living memory. Four of those families were ruined. Seven became rich, because a new flute-mouth is a new guano seam, and the family that opened it holds it.

Density has produced an elaborate etiquette of not-seeing. Privacy cannot be built, so it is performed. There is a facial expression — flat, half-lidded, chin slightly lowered — which means *I am not here*, and it is absolutely binding. A man wearing it can be weeping into his grief-board on a terrace where nine people are eating and not one of them has seen him. Children are taught the expression at about the same age they are taught not to spill.

## The Archive

The wall rosettes put out one leaf a year. This is the only reliable annual tick in a place with no seasons worth the name, and the town noticed it a very long time ago.

Each household keeps a rosette. On the third day after the new leaf has hardened, the household's eldest inscribes it with a bone stylus: the births, the deaths, the exact boundaries of the room, the debts outstanding, the day the sun-coin first touched a particular marked stone that year. A leaf holds perhaps forty marks. A three-hundred-leaf rosette is therefore three centuries of deed registry, genealogy, and climate record, growing out of the wall in a spiral, oldest at the bottom, brown and hard and still perfectly legible.

The stylus, in most houses, is a relative. Bones are lifted clean from the planting-mat a year after death, and a straight ulna makes a good stylus, and a good stylus lasts four or five generations before it wears too blunt and is returned to the mat. So the year-leaf is carved by a grandmother, held in the hand of her grandson. Nobody finds this remarkable. Outsiders find it hard to hear about calmly.

The catastrophe is flowering. Once a rosette throws its four-meter spike it is spending everything and will be dead in a season, and three hundred years of a family's record must be got off it. The leaves can be cut and dried and stacked into a sheaf and stored — there is a library of these behind the north wall, a dry hot room smelling of resin and old wax, sheaves bound in caterpillar cable, tended by leaf-readers who go half-blind by fifty. But a sheaf is not a rosette. A sheaf is a dead thing, and the law has always held that a living leaf on a living rosette outranks a cut one. So the year a household's rosette flowers, every boundary it recorded is reopened, every debt is re-argued, and the neighbors come out of the woodwork with claims they have been keeping warm for two generations.

These are the flowering suits. They take months. The whole town attends. They are, without question, the most enjoyable thing that happens here.

The oldest legible leaf in the Well is three hundred and forty-one years old. It records a quarrel about a doorway. The quarrel is technically still unresolved.

## Work

The guano-men go into the flutes in leather hoods and resin lamps and come out grey to the eyebrows, and are paid more than anyone, and cough for thirty years, and are buried — planted — with their hoods. Their children mostly do something else, and they mostly encourage it.

The carton-wrights keep the termite logs in racked galleries, feeding them fig trimmings, harvesting the board in sheets, and they smell so strongly of chewed resin that a carton-wright is recognizable in a dark room.

The roach-keepers tend the bins, and the bins make a sound like rain on a roof, which nobody here has ever heard and so nobody compares it to anything.

The coin-gardeners move plants along the sun-track through the year, hour by hour and week by week, and the sun-track is common land — the only common land — and they are the most complained-about people in the Well and the most necessary, and the office rotates every four years for exactly this reason.

The ant-watchers claim to be able to predict a raid. They are right about a third of the time, which is better than nothing, and they are consulted very seriously, and their failures are held against them for decades.

The cairn-walkers go out onto the Finished. They leave at dusk, walk all night at minus fifteen under stars that do not twinkle, sleep under stone in the day, and come back with obsidian or do not come back. They are the only people here who have seen a horizon. They are slightly feared. When one of them dies out there, the town does not plant anything, and the absence of a grief-board in the mat is considered the worst part.

## The Agreement

Nobody hunts the small grey monkeys in the fig raft. Nobody takes their figs. Twice a year a girl chosen by lot climbs the west trunks and leaves a bundle of dried roach paste and a carved bung in a particular fork, and comes down without looking back.

The orthodox account is that the monkeys hold the lid of the sky, and if they leave, it closes.

The heterodox account, which a stubborn minority has been advancing for at least four generations and which is written on at least two surviving leaves, is that the monkeys are incorrigible breakers of growing tips: they snap the fig's new shoots out of pure idleness, and that is the only reason the raft has never closed over the sun-coin, and if the monkeys go, the fig grows across the hole in about ninety years, and the coin goes out, and everything green in the Well dies standing up.

The two accounts are, in every practical respect, identical, and the town has never felt much urgency about choosing between them.

One man killed a monkey, eleven generations ago. Everyone knows his name and no one uses it. He was walked out onto the Finished, and that year — the leaf says so — the coin's track was two paces shorter at its western end and never recovered.

## Children

They have never seen rain, a river, a horizon, a reflection, or a flat open space. They have never been cold. They have never been more than a hundred and ten paces from another person.

They know, to within a few minutes, where the sun-coin will be at any hour of any day of the year, and they use it as the rest of us use clocks and calendars and also as a map: *meet me where the coin is at the seventh*. They sleep in boat-shaped carton cradles hung on silk cable, because of the ants. On ant-days they are lifted, laughing, and held while the floor goes black and bright again beneath them, and the oldest children play the standing game — feet flat, absolutely still, while the raid front parts around the ankles — and the record is held by a girl who stood for nine minutes and was bitten forty times and is now, sixty years later, a leaf-reader with excellent handwriting.

Every evening they go out onto the terraces with everyone else and watch the bat column climb out of the mouth for forty minutes while the swiftlets come home the other way through it, and not one of the two hundred thousand touches one of the thousands, and the whole town watches this, every night, forever, and has not yet got tired of it.

## What They Expect

They know. There is nothing hidden from them about it.

They know the walls shed a block a century. They know the fig will fail or the breath will stop. They know that whatever ends it will arrive in a season and finish in a season, and that the plateau above is patient and already finished and simply waiting for the lid to come off.

Their word for *the future* is also their word for *the last leaf* — the one a rosette makes just before it spends everything and throws its spike.

There is a rosette on the east wall, above the fourth level, with three hundred and forty-one leaves. Its crown has been tightening for two years now, and the grey has gone out of it toward a hot dull gold, and the leaf-readers have been up to look at it four times this season and have come down each time without saying anything in a seam.

The household that owns it is old and small: two brothers and a granddaughter. She is nineteen. She has been sitting with the sheaf-readers every night since the equinox, learning the whole thing by voice, three centuries of doorways and debts and the dates the coin touched the marked stone, in a low chest-register with no sibilants in it, so that when the flower comes and the suits begin, there will be something left standing that remembers.

She will have it all by the time it opens. Everyone assumes so. It is what people here do.
