## The Eleven

There are eleven people in Kettle, and there have always been eleven, and the dormitory row keeps seven of them.

That is the first thing to get straight, because visitors count wrong. They count the seven lit rooms and then find a woman behind the counter of the chandler's and a man up the gantry and assume the census is soft. It is not soft. It is the hardest thing in the place. The four who sleep elsewhere still have their rooms in the row, and the rooms are made up, and the sheets are changed on a rotation that nobody will explain because nobody experiences it as a decision. Eleven rooms. Eleven people. Seven of them asleep in the right place at any given hour, and the hours here are a matter of opinion.

They are not ghosts. This wants saying early and plainly. They sweat. They sunburn, insofar as there is sun. They have breath and opinions and bad knees. If you come in on the lighter they will feed you within twenty minutes of your boots hitting the stage, and the food will be good, and somebody will give you the dry towel and take the wet one, and three of them will sit with you while you eat and ask polite questions about wherever you have come from. They will not remember the answers. Not out of rudeness — the answers simply do not take, the way a footprint does not take in water. They will ask you again the next day with exactly the same warmth.

---

**In the boiler house** there is Ossu, who fires three boilers and keeps the chord of the relief valves from resolving. He is deaf in the left ear, which is the side he stands on to the doors, and he has a crescent burn on his left forearm about the size of a thumbprint, from the latch of number two. Every Ossu gets that burn. It takes a new man between three and five weeks to acquire it, and in that interval he is visibly unfinished, an incomplete drawing of a stoker, and the others treat him with a mild indulgent patience, the way you treat a clock that is being wound. There have been four men called Ossu in the boiler house and there has been one Ossu. He feeds the rats. The rats are the size of small cats and move like old men getting out of chairs, and they take bread from his palm, and he explains — he will explain this to anyone, it is his one speech — that a rat that is fed is a rat that stays where you can see it, and that in his experience nothing in this world goes bad from being watched.

**At the pumps** there is Berd, who has been halfway through overhauling number three for as long as anyone can date. The components are laid out on a square of sailcloth beside the housing in an order that has not altered: gland nut, follower, three rings, the split collar, the pin. There is no dust on them. Number three runs the whole time he works on it. This is not remarked upon. Berd has clean hands and dirty cuffs, and if you ask him when he expects to finish he says *soon* in the tone of a man giving a real estimate, and he is not lying, and he is not right.

**On the berms** there is Salla, who walks the pans. All twenty-two, including the thirteen that were dug and left and hold nothing but cracked clay and a green film. She takes a hydrometer reading from each of the nine live pans and writes it in a wet notebook, and the readings are the readings: green, green, green, milk, milk, milk, mauve, mauve, mauve, with a variation in the third decimal that she records with care. Her boots go through the salt plates on the berms with that winter crunch, and she has never once got used to it; it still makes her smile, every circuit, a small private joke told by the ground to her feet. Her hair is cut in a blunt line at the jaw. It has been at the jaw for years. It grows and it is at the jaw.

**In the machine shop** there is Tarn, who smells of hot oil and makes, on a lathe fed by the same overworked generator, a bronze bush of a specific size. There is a shelf of them. There are perhaps two hundred. Nothing in Kettle takes a bush of that size. Tarn is a good machinist, unhurried and exact, and the bushes are excellent, and he is the only person in Kettle who has ever come close to saying the thing out loud: he said once, to a visiting engineer, *I expect one day something will want one*, and then he looked at his own hands for a while and then he turned the lathe back on.

**In the cookhouse** there is Mai, who lays twelve plates. Eleven for the eleven and one at the end of the table, and the twelfth plate is laid at every meal, and has been laid at every meal for the whole of the current arrangement. Sometimes it is eaten and sometimes it is not, and Mai does not check which, and washes it either way. She is the reason no new arrival has ever felt unexpected. You come up off the stage sick and salt-stiff and there is already a place for you, set that morning and every morning before it, and it is the kindest thing in Kettle and it is the worst.

**At the tipple and on the crane gantry** there is Hoke, who sleeps up there in a canvas cradle ten metres above the highest thing, because from there he can see the whole world, and the whole world is four kilometres across. Hoke counts. He counts the tubs on the tramway (four; two loaded, two empty, at the same points), he counts the kites (four, at four heights, and he could tell you which is which by the wing-set), and he counts the surveyors' footprints going out west across the untaken flat, and the number of prints is the number of prints, and he checks it the way other men check a pocket for keys. He has never said the number aloud. Ask him and he goes friendly and vague and offers you the binoculars.

**In the weigh-office** there is Perrin, the tallyman, whose ledger balances. Salt made against salt shipped, to the gram, over columns going back through a change of handwriting, and another, and another, four hands in all and one set of figures. Perrin is the only one who has ever gone looking. He read back through the ledger once, all of it, in a single stretch, and came out of the office at the end of it and sat on the boardwalk with his shoes off and his feet in a warm puddle under a weeping pipe joint, and after a while he went back in. He is perfectly friendly about it. He will show you the ledger. He rather hopes you will see something he missed.

Those are the seven of the row.

---

The other four:

**Oma** keeps the bathhouse, and Oma knows every body in Kettle better than the bodies do. She is the one who could tell you, if she were the telling sort, that a wound in this place heals down to a certain depth and then stops, exactly like the rust on the sheds: a scar reaches the colour it wants and quits, pink and shiny and permanent, and does not fade and does not open. She has scraped salt off Berd's shoulders for long enough to know that the mole below his collarbone is the same size it was. She knows that Ossu's burn arrived in the fifth week and settled in the sixth. She knows which of the men has had a splinter under his right thumbnail for six years and that it hurts him precisely as much today as it did on the first afternoon, no more, no less, which is the sort of fact you can hold in your hand and not put down.

**Teilo** keeps the shrine, which is the size of a wardrobe and has a tin roof and a lamp in it that is the only light in Kettle noticeably brighter than the rest. He trims the wick. He carries the oil. He came out with the first survey party, and one pair of those prints going west belongs to his boots, and he does not know which pair, and this is the thing he is afraid of, quietly, all the time. He is the only frightened person in Kettle. The others are at peace in the way that water in a sealed jar is at peace. Teilo keeps the lamp brighter than it needs to be and has never given a reason, and the reason is that he believes — without evidence, with total conviction — that the flat can see the difference between a light that is maintained and a light that is merely on.

**Dov** drives the tram that does not move, which is to say he oils the four tubs, and greases the wooden rails, and stands with his hand on the brake lever of the loaded pair at what would be the end of a shift, looking west. He is young. He has been young for a considerable time. His beard reaches a length of about two centimetres and holds there, and the newest arrivals — men who have not yet inherited anything — find him easy company, because he has not entirely finished becoming whatever he is becoming.

**And in the chandler's,** behind a counter with stock on the shelves and dust on none of it, there is Yesha, who is eight months and some days pregnant, and has been since before any of the current seven arrived.

She is well. That is the first thing she says, briskly, to anyone whose face does the arithmetic. She is well, she sleeps poorly, her back hurts in the ordinary way, the child moves. It moves at the same times. It has, she will admit if pressed and if she likes you, a repertoire of about four movements, and they come in an order, and she has stopped waiting for a fifth. She sells rope and lamp oil and tobacco and needles and her shelves hold their levels the way the pans hold theirs: sell and the shelf is full, and the ledger balances, and Perrin has checked it and it balances.

There are no eggs in Kettle. Ten thousand terns and stilts and a whole nation of small waders scream over the salt banks around the clock and there is not one nest among them, not one chick, not one broken shell. The eight dogs under the dormitory have divided the boardwalks by treaty and have never produced a ninth. Everything eats and nothing increases. Yesha is the only pregnancy in four kilometres, and she is patient about it in a way that the rest of them mistake for courage and that is actually something else — it is the patience of a person who has understood the rule and decided not to spend the rest of forever arguing with it. She is the one who tells new arrivals the rules. Everything wet, nothing raining. Don't dig a hole you don't want filled. Don't go west.

---

Every so often the lighter comes across the lagoon for the salt.

Her crew are the only people who age. They come maybe four times a year and between visits their hair greys and their teeth go and the boy on the bow line becomes a man with a man's forearms and then, over some further span, a heavy slow fellow who takes the stage stiffly. The Kettle eleven greet them all with the same unbothered friendliness and notice nothing. The crew notice. The crew work the transfer fast and will not come up above the lip of clay, will not eat, will not overnight, and shout their business from the stage, and the older ones cross themselves or do the local equivalent, and the young ones laugh at them and then, in ten or fifteen years, stop laughing.

The lighter brings one person out, now and then. Not every trip. When she does, the twelfth plate has already been laid for weeks.

And within a day or so of that, someone goes west.

There is no drama in it and no selection anyone can identify. It is not the newest and it is not the oldest, and the one who goes seems to find out about an hour beforehand, and finishes their shift first, always, which is the detail that stays with people. They wash. Oma scrapes them down and hands them the dry towel and takes the wet one. They walk out past the lip of clay onto the untaken flat, where the fiddler crabs go down ahead of them so that the plain appears to blink, and they step in the prints, which fit, which always fit, and the prints hold their edges and the clear warm water in them closes back over afterward. The eight dogs come as far as the lip and sit down in a row and will not go further. The horizon out there is only a change in colour — pewter below, pearl above — so you lose the figure early, well before the distance can account for it.

Nobody says the name afterward, because the name is still in use.

Then the row is seven, and the plates are twelve, and the stack leans four degrees, and the kites hold their altitudes, and Hoke goes up the gantry and counts the prints and gets the number he got yesterday.

---

So: what lives in Kettle.

Not, in the end, eleven people. Eleven is what it wears. The thing that lives here has pumps for a pulse and a chord of relief valves for a held breath, and it keeps rats the way you keep a hand in a pocket, and it has learned to fire boilers and cut hair to the jaw and turn bronze bushes nobody needs, and it is warm, and it will feed you within twenty minutes, and it means it.

It is not malicious. It is not even awake, particularly. It is simply a place that arrived at balance before anything had a chance to teach it that balance is supposed to be temporary, and it has since taken in eleven people the way a pan takes in brine, and found the level at which evaporation and seepage shake hands, and held.

Stay a week and they will love you a little, in their unstorable way.

Stay a month and Mai will stop laying the twelfth plate, because you will be sitting at it.
