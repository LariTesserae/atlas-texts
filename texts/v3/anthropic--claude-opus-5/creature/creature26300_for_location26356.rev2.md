Begin with the ears, because the chamber reaches them first and is never afterwards out of them.

A child born here hears the fall. This is the only time anyone does. For the first weeks of life the roar is a real and present catastrophe, an unrelenting assault the infant cannot get behind or beneath, and the newborns of this place scream in a way that would frighten you — not fretfully, but with the full-body horror of someone being held underwater. Then, somewhere in the second or third month, the auditory nerve gives up. The brain files the sound under *floor* rather than *event*, and the screaming stops. It stops quite suddenly, over a day or two, and the household marks it. It is the first of the two things that happen to a person here.

So the fall is understood, correctly, as something known only to babies. The adults possess it the way you possess a country you were carried through before memory. They will tell you — in the quiet rooms, where telling is possible — that everyone has heard it once, and that nobody can remember it, and that this is the shape of all knowledge.

What remains afterwards is not silence but a notch. The fall occupies a broad band of the hearing, permanently, and what the people here listen to is everything the fall leaves alone: a narrow bright shelf up near the top of the range and a thin trough in the middle where a certain kind of hard small knock survives. Their clay bells are tuned to that trough. So are their voices, in the quiet rooms — pitched high and clipped, with a hard consonantal front to every word, a speech that would sound to you like a language made entirely of question marks and door-latches. They do not have deep voices. A man with a low voice is, functionally, mute in company, and this is a real affliction, spoken of the way you would speak of a limp.

---

They are small. Not dwarfed — proportionate, but scaled down, the way a population gets when for a very long time there has never been quite enough. An adult stands about the height of your shoulder. There is almost no fat on them anywhere except across the lower back and the backs of the upper arms, where the body keeps its last reserve, and a woman with a full curve there is considered not merely healthy but *lucky*, which in this place is the same word as *owed to*.

The skin is the pale of something grown under a plank: not white but greyish, faintly translucent at the ear and the nostril and the web of the hand, so that the vessels show as a dim green tracery. Everyone is perpetually wet from the knees down and damp everywhere else, and the skin of the feet and calves is therefore permanently macerated, thickened, ridged, dead-white, and insensible to heat and cold — but exquisitely sensitive to vibration. The sole is the second ear here. Hair is sparse, fine, and kept cropped close with stone blades, because long hair holds the film.

The film. That is the thing you would notice last and understand worst.

It grows on anything that stays still. It grows on people. A healthy adult, moving and working and touched by other people a dozen times a day, wears it off without noticing. But let someone lie down for a long illness, or sit apart, or simply be left alone, and within four or five days there is a bloom at the nape and along the shoulders and behind the knees, and after two weeks that person glows — dimly, greenly, unmistakably, visible at a distance where the face is not.

So grief is luminous here. The bereaved do not wash. They sit in the noise and let it come, and you can find them in the dark from across the chamber by the soft shine of their backs, and this is exactly the point: mourning is the deliberate suspension of being touched. And intimacy, correspondingly, is scraping. Lovers scrape each other's backs with a flat stone. Mothers scrape children. The very old, who have no one left, are scraped by an appointed young person once in a while, out of duty, and it is well understood to be the loneliest kindness in the world.

---

In the open floor, where speech is impossible, they talk with three things.

With the hands: a sign-speech of small, close, economical gestures kept low against the chest, because the light is poor and a hand signing at arm's length is already half-lost. It is not a code for their spoken language. It is older, and handles different business — logistics, warning, insult, the whole grammar of ownership — and it has no way at all to say most of what the quiet rooms are for.

With the feet: every dwelling floor is a stone plate on piers over running water, and every such plate is a drum. A heel struck at the threshold carries through the joined masonry of the whole western clot. Each person has a gait, and the gait is a name — the true public name, the one strangers use — and a household lying awake knows precisely who has crossed the ramp outside and in what mood. There is a whole vocabulary of deliberate footwork: the summons, the refusal, the warning, the long slow triple-tap that means a death. Children learn it before they learn the hands.

And with cord. The stone-flax runs overhead in its hundreds of lines, waxed, knotted, beaded, each ending in a hung pot, and a tug at one end is heard at the other as a small hard clock under the roar. The lines are property. A line into a quiet room is worth more than the room's floor. Along the lines, too, run the ledgers: knot-strings hung above every doorway, public, unhideable, recording in plain reef-and-figure-eight exactly what that household owes and to whom. Nobody's debt is private. The untying of a cleared debt is done at the door, slowly, in front of whoever is passing, and it is one of the two or three occasions in a life on which a person is looked at.

---

The chamber holds perhaps nine hundred people, and they all look alike. Not to each other — to each other the differences are enormous and endlessly discussed — but to you they would be one face repeated: the same short jaw, the same wide-set eyes with their great slack pupils, the same low hairline. In three families the hands carry a sixth finger, small and mobile, set beside the thumb. It is good for combing fibre and is considered a mark of the right kind of ancestry, which is to say: an ancestry that did not change anything.

The work is divided by the Screens, and the Screens are nine, and they are not equal.

The first Screen, coarsest, takes the whole things — the leaf-mats, the drowned rat, the occasional frog. Its tenders may go a year with nothing and then eat meat for a week. They are wealthy in bursts and are regarded, socially, as embarrassing: loud, given to display, quick to lend and quick to demand, the sort of people who *have events*. The ninth Screen, finest, takes only the grey silt and the film that comes down suspended in the water. Its tenders eat the same paste in the same quantity every single day of their lives and have done so for as long as the calendar goes back, and they are the aristocracy. To be of the Ninth is to be constitutionally incapable of a surprise. Marriages are arranged upward toward the Ninth and the dowry is usually a room.

Because the real property is quiet. The acoustic pockets are mapped, named, roofed, floored, inherited, litigated. A family's standing is the number of paces of speakable air it controls. To marry is, in the practical sense, to merge two silences, and the negotiation is conducted by an old person of each house sitting in one of the shrines in the eastern roar — a stone cell the size of a wardrobe, tucked behind a buttress of flowstone, where two heads bent close can hear each other while four feet away the world is a wall of white noise. Everything important in this city has been decided in a box.

---

They do not make food. They manage a loop, and they have made the loop closed.

The catch feeds the tanks. The tanks hold the blind thumb-length crustaceans — slow, translucent, grudging — and the crustaceans are the only fat in the world, which means they are the only meat, the only medicine, and all of the light. A lamp here burns a rendered animal. To light a lamp is to spend the herd.

And the dead go to the tanks. This is done without ceremony and without disgust, by the family, at the end of the mourning-bloom, when the body has gone luminous all over and is understood to have already begun rejoining the chamber. The tanks take a body over some weeks. The animals grow fat on it. The fat becomes oil, the oil becomes light, and so every room in the city is lit by its own ancestors, and nobody finds this either horrible or poetic. It is simply the shape of a place where nothing may leave. Their word for the lamp is their word for the grandmother. They think you would be the strange one, feeding the dead to a hole in the ground where they do nobody any good.

The outflow slot takes only water, silt, and what must be got rid of. What must be got rid of is not much: a diseased animal, a thing broken past repair, occasionally something that fell down the Gutter and cannot be accounted for. These are pushed through the slot at arm's length and not discussed. That the long grey rumour is fed by this practice is obvious to everyone and is not an opinion anyone holds aloud.

---

The second thing that happens to a person is the Quieting.

Somewhere around the ninth or tenth year, a child's hearing finishes its long accommodation, and the roar — which has been for a decade a *loud* background — settles at last into a *neutral* one. It is a real physiological event and it is visible from outside: the child stops flinching at the eastern corridors, stops shouting, begins to find the noise restful. Children are the only loud people in the world. Their shrieking in the open floor is inaudible under the fall and costs nothing and is therefore permitted absolutely, and this is the entire content of childhood here: a decade of free, unheard screaming in the streets. After the Quieting a person does not raise their voice again, ever, in the open. It would be pointless. It would also be obscene, the way it is obscene to sing in the room of a corpse.

And at the far end there is the Count.

One child in each long span is chosen — early, on grounds that are partly omen and partly politics — and that child is not permitted to work the Screens, not permitted to carry, not permitted to cross the fast water at the eastern end. The Count is fed first. The Count is the calendar. Every span of years in the city is reckoned against the Count's body: the year the Count's teeth came, the year the Count's back began to bend, the year the Count's hearing went. Contracts are written against it. Debts fall due at it. There is no other measure of duration anywhere in this world.

And when the Count dies, time does not roll over. It empties. The new Count is a young body with no history in it, and everything that happened before falls out of sequence into the single undifferentiated *long*, where the ancestors are all contemporaries and the roar was already the roar. This is why the history here has no eras. The clock keeps dying. They are not ignorant of their past; they have simply, over and over, put it down.

---

Once or twice in a life, a person goes east and sits under the Gutter to get the noise back.

It takes hours. You must sit in the cold shallow water at the foot of the fall, not moving, not speaking, not touched, and wait while the ear — starved of variation, given nothing else to do — slowly gives up its accommodation and hands the sound back. It arrives without warning. People describe it identically: not a swelling but a *switch*, and then the whole world is one enormous ongoing noise again, exactly as it was in the month after birth, and they weep, every one of them, which they attribute to the recovery and which is probably the cold.

This is done for grief, and at the end of mourning, and by some at the making of a marriage. It is done to remember that the fall is there. Because the fall is not only the river: the falling column drags air down the shaft with it, and that air is the chamber's whole breath. Everyone knows — it is the single item of natural philosophy the culture possesses — that if the fall stopped they would stifle long before they starved. So the noise is the breathing of the world, and the ritual is nothing more than going and putting one's hand on the chest of a sleeping thing to check.

Those who do it too often lose the accommodation permanently and cannot get it back. They become unable to bear the eastern third, then unable to bear the open floor. They are given a stone cell in the deepest western quiet and fed, and they sit there with their hands, and everyone is kind to them, and they bloom.

---

They believe that above is a place that is careless. This is not a metaphor and it is not a slander; it is an inference from the evidence, and the evidence is a hundred generations of debris. The Gutter delivers wadded leaves, snapped twigs, drowned beetles, half a rat, a bead of coloured glass, a milk tooth, a thing of hammered metal green with age, a bone with a hole bored through it. Somewhere above, things are lost. Things are dropped, thrown away, allowed to fall. Whoever is up there does not attend to what they have.

So their word for *above* is their word for *wasteful*, and the worst thing that can be said of a person is that they behave as though they were from up the shaft. It follows that improvement is not a virtue here; it is not even a vice; it is not a category. To want the chamber different is to want it careless. Their entire ethics is a single closed hand: do not break the drip-figures, do not widen the slot, do not take two where one is owed, do not let anything out. The stone does not err. The water does not err. Only hands err.

---

At the north wall, right now, an old woman is combing stone-flax out of the rock by feel, with her eyes shut because they are no use and the dark is restful. Her fingertips wore through years ago; she works with the pads gone shiny and numb and she is fed by her daughter's house, which is the customary arrangement and carries no shame. She has been deaf to the fall since the second month of her life and deaf to nearly everything else since the Count's back began to bend. Her back and shoulders have a faint green bloom on them because her husband has been three weeks in the tank and she has not yet let anyone scrape her, and above her in the dark the lines cross and recross and the small clay bells knock at intervals, passing messages she can no longer hear but can feel in the thread when she touches it.

She is not waiting for anything. That is the part hardest to hold onto. She is not enduring, not exiled, not holding out. She is combing white fibre out of a wall in a completed world, in a cool that never chills, inside a silence made of one sustained noise, owed nothing and owing nothing, with her dead husband burning quietly in a pot three rooms away and giving just enough light to see her hands by.
