## The Crew

Begin with the stone.

Cut open anything in this lake — the fist-sized hammer, the bathtub-sized bellows, the ribbon, the hair-fine wire, the rice-grain grazer you can barely see — and somewhere in it you will find a grown mineral body. A bead, a plate, a nodule, a sac of crystals. Not swallowed. Grown, laid down out of the blood in rings, the way enamel is laid down. In the hammer it is magnetite the size of a thumb-end and it is a tool. In the bellows it is a slab of barite the size of a fist and it is ballast, the thing that fixes its owner at forty metres and holds it there. In the flywheel's zooids it is a pouch of barite crystals that does nothing but weigh. In the regulator it is a line of eleven small beads down each flank, each on its own membrane, each tuned.

They are all the same family. Nothing here is native. Nothing here is a local invention. One stock arrived and unpacked itself into everything the crater had room for, and the signature it left in every descendant is that they all grow a stone and they all listen with it.

That is the sense you have to grant them before anything makes sense. They do not see. There is light down to the floor and it is useless to them; most have given up the eye entirely, and the ones that haven't use it to tell day from night and nothing else. What they have instead is a world made of the noise. The hammering is their daylight. Every surface in the crater returns it, every body absorbs a characteristic amount of it, and a hammer bed forty metres off reads to them roughly the way a lit cliff-face reads to you. In a lake this loud, in water this clear of silt, hearing is not a sense for emergencies. It is the whole of vision, and it is excellent, and it costs them nothing to maintain because the light is free and constant and made by their neighbours.

Which is why the quiet rooms are blindness, and why the adults hang at the edge of them and will not go in.

---

**The hammers** are the crew you will come to know best, because there are millions of them and they are the only ones you can hold.

Plated, low, gripping the glass with eight short legs that end in hooks; the shape of a woodlouse that has been designed by an engineer who hated woodlice. The strike limb is not a spring. It has no need of speed, only of never stopping, so it is geared low and slow, a ratchet of mineralised tendon and two antagonised muscle blocks that alternate with the patience of a bilge pump. Twice a second. A hammer that settles at the age of eleven days will strike its wall about two hundred million times, and then the nodule — which has been wearing away at the face all along, exposing its growth rings one after another like a cut tree — will be gone to the root, and it cannot grow a second one. It eats the dust of its own tool as it goes, which recovers perhaps a third of the iron, and this is why they take so long to die.

You can age one by counting rings on the working face. The oldest I found was four years and some months, on the north wall, in a place where the water is loud.

They settle by ear. The larva drifts, listening, and it puts itself down where the ambient noise is at least as loud as the noise it will make. This is why the beds are sorted. It is not cooperation and it is not courtesy. A hammer striking loudly in a quiet corner is a distinct event in a very confused room, and distinct events in this lake get visited. The sorting is done in one direction by settlement and in the other by the ribbon.

Where the wire comes down to them you will see the socket: a shallow pit in the carapace over the shoulder, and in it the terminal bead of a filament, fused on. The hammer gets nothing it would recognise as a meal. It gets a trickle of current across its shell that keeps the iron on its tooth from passivating — keeps the nodule bright, keeps the rust off the face, adds perhaps a year. Plugged hammers live longer and everybody's larvae settle at random, so nothing about this is going anywhere. It is simply an accident that has been happening for as long as there has been a lake.

---

**The bellows** are the oldest large animals here and there are almost none of them.

Four hundred, in a lake that holds uncountable millions of everything else. They breed once a year at best; a good year adds three. Their translucency is a young animal's trait — the ones the size of a bucket are clear enough to read the ribs through, and the ones the size of a bathtub are the colour and finish of old wax, because they are scarred all over. They get the scars in the black room.

The bottom five metres of this lake is dense, cold, sour water that fizzes if you disturb it and smells, if you could smell it, of a struck match in a wet cellar. That is where the food is. Everything the mills grind and the smelters drop ends there, and the bacterial floc is thickest there, and so once or twice a day a bellows lets itself sink into it, fills, and comes back up etched. Their whole lives are a slow descent into acid and back. The oldest are opaque, pitted, half their ribs fused, and they still hold time perfectly.

They did not choose eleven seconds. The crater chose it. A bowl of this radius and this depth has a natural period for a vertical slosh, and it is eleven seconds and a fraction, and an animal that pumps against the slosh does twice the work of one that pumps with it. So they fall into phase the way any set of coupled oscillators falls into phase, each one feeling the others through the water on its own ribs, no signal passing, nothing decided. A young bellows spends its first months out of time and ugly and easy to find. Most do not survive learning. The ones that do will hold that beat for forty years.

They are not benign. Their pulse is what scours the antinodes into dead rooms; their pulse is what kills, every eleven seconds, a certain fraction of everything small in the water column. They are the reason this lake has not yet turned over and killed everything in it, and they are also the single most destructive force in it, and these are the same fact.

---

**The flywheel** is a colony and it is the oldest individual in the crater.

Some hundreds of thousands of zooids, each no bigger than a grain of barley, joined rim to rim into a torus, each hanging its own little sac of barite crystals into the ring's mass. The outer face feeds well because it meets the flow; the inner face faces slack water and starves, and the zooids there are half-size and pale, and they are not replaced when they die, so the wheel is hollowing itself as it ages. It grows outward. It gets heavier. It gets slower, by a measurable amount each season, until the bellows can no longer drive it at all.

Then it tears. A section of rim lets go, and a ring six metres across becomes a ring five metres across with a wound in it, and the broken piece drifts, curls, and either dies on the floor or closes on itself and becomes a second wheel. There is a young one in this lake now, off-centre, the diameter of a dinner plate, turning fast because it is light. And there are three dead ones in the sediment, flattened hoops of barite under a century of flour, which you would take for slag rings if you found them.

It cannot stop. If it stopped, its own ballast would pull it apart within a day. Everything it does, it does because the alternative is to be torn.

And because it is a mass of magnetite-bearing tissue turning in a bowl of freshly magnetised basalt — the crater walls carry the field they froze in, banded, strong, a fossil of the eruption — the wheel is a dynamo. Not a good one. But if you lie still in the grid at fifty metres and count, you will find under the tick of the wire a slower tick, one every forty seconds, everywhere at once. That is the wheel coming around. Nothing arranged it. It is a machine because it could not avoid being one.

---

**The wire** is mostly dead, and I want to be exact about this.

A filament is one organism. It has a living end in the lit oxygenated water and a living end down in the sulfide, and between them it has thirty metres of its own body that it has drained and sealed and turned into conductor. The middle does not metabolise. It does not repair. It is a corpse in harness, and the animal at either end of it is eating the difference between two chemistries it is touching at the same time, which is a thing no other kind of life on this planet has ever managed with any elegance.

They take two or three years to span and they die in four. The lake is therefore always being re-strung, and it is also full of the old ones: a slack grey cobweb of spent filament drifting through the whole water column, catching flour, furred with varnish, indistinguishable at a glance from the live grid. Perhaps a third of what you can see at noon is conducting. The rest is hair.

And on the live ones there are **walkers** — small, dark, hooked, hand over hand along a filament, eating it as they go. They are the only animal here that hunts by following a line. You track one by sound: a ladder of ticks moving slowly across the lake as each junction it passes opens and the potential redistributes. They are the reason the grid has gaps. They are also the reason it is ever renewed.

---

**The smelters** cannot move and have been colonised by everything.

Under the accretion you can find the animal: a thick grey sac with an intake and a vent and very little else, the plainest thing in the lake. Over it: varnish in felt, wire terminals fused into its skin because its metal-rich hide is an excellent anchor, grazers, spat-out pebbles, eggs that were swept there and stuck. An old smelter is a lump the size of a chair and four-fifths of it is other lives.

They excrete downward. So the nodules pile under them, and the smelter rises on a plinth of its own waste at a hand's depth per decade, until the plinth is too tall and slender for the current and it topples. Then it goes on venting sideways, into a fan. These plinths are the single most convincing false artifact in the crater. They look dressed. They look set.

---

**The mills** are on a one-way trip.

A mill takes on its gizzard stones as a juvenile, in the gravel banks just downslope of the shallowest hammer beds — angular chips of fresh glass, forty or fifty of them, exactly the spoil the hammers make. Then it rolls. It takes something like a year to go from twenty metres to the floor, grinding as it turns, spitting out stones that have worn round and taking on new ones where it can, feeding on the varnish and the fallen and anything soft it rolls over, including the dead. At the bottom it spawns and it does not go back up. Its young go up: they are small enough to be plankton, and the bellows' circulation lifts them, over some weeks, to the shallows and the gravel.

The pale rounded pebbles scattered under every mill route, sorted by size — spit stones — are the second most convincing false artifact here, and they are exactly what you think they are, which is a rejected tool that was never a tool.

---

**The regulator** has eleven stone beads down each flank and a clock in its head that runs at the crater's period, and it compares. It cannot see a healthy animal. A healthy animal is the wallpaper. What it detects is phase error, and the only things in this lake that carry phase error are the sick, the injured, the newly settled, the very old, and the parasitised.

There are six, and they are functionally invisible to each other, since the only signature they can perceive is the signature of something going wrong. So they meet when one of them slows. A gravid female slows. Her courtship is an attack that stops a body-length short, and sometimes it does not stop, and there is no way from the outside — or, I think, from the inside — to tell the difference between the two outcomes until it is over. An ageing regulator becomes audible to the others in exactly the same way, and they come, and it is exactly the same approach.

And the reason there are regulators at all, in the end, is a worm.

In the concertina folds of nearly every bellows over ten years old there is a small white **stowaway** that does one thing: it irritates the pumping muscle so that its host's exhalation drifts a half-second out of true. It does this for one reason. It needs its host eaten by a ribbon, because the ribbon is where it breeds. It is a parasite whose entire strategy is to make something audible.

So: the maintenance crew of this lake, the thing that culls arrhythmia and keeps the great beat clean and enforces the sorting of the hammer beds, exists and is fed because a worm requires a lift. Hold that next to the plinths and the nodule drifts and the dynamo, and you will have most of the place.

---

**The nine quiet rooms** are nurseries for a reason of pure physics: at a pressure node, sound pushes small particles inward. Eggs spawned anywhere in this lake are swept there over a day or two, gathered out of the whole water column by the noise itself and heaped in the only places the noise is not. The parents do nothing. The parents could not go in if they wanted to; inside those lenses they are blind, deaf, and lost, and they hover at the boundary the way you would stand at the edge of a hole in the dark.

Which means that anything that can navigate in silence owns an unlimited larder and has no competition whatsoever.

Something does. In every one of the nine there are **crawlers**: pale, soft, finger-length, eyeless and earless, moving through the egg mass by touch and taste at the speed of a minute hand, eating for months without lifting their heads. They cannot leave. Outside the node they are deaf in a world made of hearing, and they last about four minutes. So each room has its own population, sealed, never mixing, and the nine of them have been diverging from one another for as long as this water has been here — nine islands, forty metres apart, in a lake you could swim across in five minutes. Three of them are already, by any test I can apply, three different animals.

The mountain will close all nine within the century.

---

Now: how they got here, since none of this can have walked up four kilometres of fresh scree.

The fizz. Every eleven seconds, a bellows' exhalation reaches the surface and breaks it, and each bursting bubble throws a film of water into the thin air as droplets a few microns across, and in some of those droplets there are cysts. Hammer cysts, wire cysts, varnish spores, the dormant stage of the grazers, the tough little bead a flywheel zooid makes when the wheel tears and it finds itself falling. They go up. At four thousand metres, the air they enter is the free troposphere, and it goes everywhere.

That sound from the rim — the slow-shaken bottle — is the lake venting the gas that would otherwise kill it, and it is the lake feeding, and it is the lake spawning into the sky, and these are one event.

The cysts wait. They wait in snow, in ash, in the varnish crust of dead mountains, in the cracks of scree on cones that stopped exhaling ten thousand years ago. They are good at it in a way that nothing in a warm ocean has ever had to be. And when a crater somewhere fills with cold clean water over fresh glass, they arrive in it by the thousand, the way they arrived here, in the first snow after the first rain, and they take about nine years to assemble this.

This exact thing. A hammer bed, a phase-locked rank of pumps on the crater's own period, a wheel to hold the momentum, a grid across the redox gradient, drifts of bright waste, a ribbon hunting the off-beat, and every egg in the place heaped in the accidental quiet.

They have done it before. They have done it on a thousand mountains, and they will do it again on mountains that have not happened yet, and not one thing has ever carried forward from one lake to the next except the crew itself, because the workshop always closes. There is no accumulation here. There is no line of improvement. There is a configuration that falls out of the same physics every time it is given the same hole.

That is what lives in the Anvil Lake. Not the hammers, not the bellows, not the ribbon. A crew, posted.

---

If you went in, they would find you at once. Your heart is at sixty and it is not a factor of eleven, and you would be the largest, loudest, most arrhythmic object in the crater by an enormous margin — a thing that the whole apparatus is built to notice and remove. The ribbon would come up through the grid without hurrying, because nothing in this lake hurries, and it would put its eleven stones alongside your chest and take a reading, and find you very badly out of time.

Somewhere else, on an old dead cone under a drift of grey ash, a grain the width of a spider's thread is lying in the crust of the varnish, and has been for eight thousand years, and is not dead.
