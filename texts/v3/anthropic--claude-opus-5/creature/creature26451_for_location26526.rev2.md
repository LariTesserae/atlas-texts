# What Lives There

## The Fringe

Start with the obvious tenant, because it is the one that moves and the one that makes a mess.

A single one of them is about the size of a spread hand: a flat shingle of laminated filament, eleven segments long, dark as slate and completely dry, with twenty-two legs in eleven pairs, each ending in a mineral claw shaped precisely to the pitch of the lattice grooves in the stack walls. The front three pairs are not walking legs. They are combs, scrapers, and a pair of fine opposed pincers that can pick a grain of dust out of a port aperture without touching the aperture. There is no head. There is a forward margin, and on it two translucent vanes that read capacitance the way an ear reads pressure, and that is the whole of its sensorium: it perceives the tower's field as a lit room, itself as a hole in that room, and its ten thousand neighbors as ten thousand smaller holes swimming in the light.

There is no good word for one of them, and this is not a failure of the language. One of them is not a unit of anything. It is not born; it buds along the seam of the eleventh segment and walks away already knowing everything it will ever know, because knowing here means *having been shaped by the field*, and the field was there first. It lasts about four hundred days. It does not die so much as stop, usually at a port, and its fellows chew it down into the conductive resin they use to patch the lattice, and the patch goes on carrying current, and in a functional sense nothing has ended.

They do not graze. They cannot; the ventral pad of a tiller is a different instrument entirely. They feed at the ports, pressing the forward margin to an aperture for eleven or twelve seconds and drawing off what the tower gives them, and then stepping aside for the next. The queues are orderly and constant. On any ledge of any tenanted stack, at any hour, there is a line of them at every open port, and the line does not push.

What they do in return is everything the tower cannot do for itself, which is a surprisingly short list and an absolutely essential one. They keep the ports clear. They carry the swept dust to the rim in ballast-pellets and drop it, which is why every stack stands in a trampled apron of its own exhalations. They patch. And when the resin runs low they go down — a column of them descending the outside of the tower in a moving stripe, crossing the slow ground at the base where everything takes a beat too long, out onto the open wick to find a dried tiller lying in one of its postures of great dignity, and they take it apart and haul it up a limb at a time. A mote with a tiller's foreleg held overhead, climbing ninety meters of fluted wall in a wind that never fully stops, is the most purposeful sight on the plateau and means nothing at all.

Up close the noise is genuinely unbearable, and it is worth being precise about why. It is not the clicking, though ten thousand mineral claws on lattice is a hard sound. It is that each one whines faintly while it feeds — a thin rising tone as its capacitance fills — and the whines do not synchronize, so a full ledge at dusk produces a shifting, granular, un-resolvable chord of several thousand slightly different pitches sliding upward and cutting off. It is the sound of a swarm of small instruments being tuned forever.

A stack can lose its fringe. It happens: a bad discharge, a collapse, some slow poisoning of the resin supply. The tower does not die. Its ports clog within a season, its voice goes reedy and then flat, and within a year it drops out of the chord and the other towers quietly re-tune around the gap. There are perhaps forty such silent stacks on the plateau, ported shut, dust-choked, still cold inside, still computing, mute. They are recolonized eventually. A fringe that outgrows its tower sends a rope: a single file of motes, thirty or forty thousand of them, crossing open wick at a walking pace, taking days, with sparrowers working the flanks the whole way. The rope arrives at a tenth of its strength and gets straight to work, and inside a decade the dead tower is singing again.

## What the Fringe Is Not

It would be easy, and wrong, to call them the inhabitants.

Everything they do is traceable to a gradient the tower maintains. Set the field one way and they sweep; another and they queue; another and they descend and go looking for a corpse. They are peripherals. They are hands, and the question of whether hands live somewhere is not a good question.

Except for the grooves.

The high ledges of the old stacks are worn into channels by feet. Centimeters deep in places, which at the rate motes wear heartwood means a number of years with a lot of zeroes after it. And the channels are not shortest paths. They are not the routes between ports. They loop, they double, they cross the open floor in long shallow arcs that arrive nowhere, and when you climb to the top of the stack and look down at the plateau — at the pale contour lines the dust has drawn across four hundred kilometers of wick, that map of the field that no eye can otherwise see — you notice that the arcs on the ledge are the same arcs. Smaller. A century out of date. But the same.

Nobody has explained this. The best guess is that it is not for anything; that a mote walking a field gradient will walk the field gradient, and if you scale the gradient down and print it on a floor, you will get a printed floor. The grooves may be nothing but the plateau's weather, copied at small scale by ten thousand bodies that cannot help tracing it.

That is probably what it is. It is still the only thing on this plateau that looks like a drawing.

## The Tenant

The stacks are what lives here.

Not metaphorically, not in the sense that a house is alive because the boiler runs. They grow, at about a millimeter a year in girth. They eat, in the sense that they hold a four-kilometer potential against the ionosphere and spend it. They breathe, audibly, in the eight-to-sixty hertz range. They maintain a body temperature, which in their case is *colder than the surroundings and radiating nothing*, which is the most flagrant thing on the plateau and the least discussed. And they do something continuously with electron spin in the lattice of their own walls that costs them most of their income and produces no visible output whatsoever, and if that is not thinking then the word has never had any content.

It is a mistake to imagine them as slow. The lattice runs fast — nanoseconds, coherence held across tens of meters of wall. What is slow is the *speaking*. A stack integrates for two hours and then changes one interval in the chord. Eleven times a day. That is its whole external vocabulary, and it is not a poverty; it is a person who has said everything twice already and now speaks only when a thing has changed.

Its senses: it feels the sky's charge overhead as a weather of pressure, thickening and thinning across the day. It feels the ground through its buttress roots as a continuous warm touch, the whole wick mat, ankle-deep, a body-sized skin. It hears its neighbors, but *hears* is wrong, because it is also producing them; the chord is a single sound with forty thousand throats and no stack can distinguish its own voice within it any more than you can hear one of your vocal folds. The beat frequency that walks across the flats every four or five seconds and moves your sternum is not two towers talking. It is one body noticing a small discrepancy in itself and slowly, politely, resolving it.

So: not forty thousand of them. One. Distributed, ported, fluted, standing in a scattered grid three to nine kilometers on a side across an area the size of a small country, singing one chord and changing it eleven times a day.

If you need a word for it, *the tenant* will do. It would not use one.

## Dice

It knows about the animals, but not the way you would.

A tiller is a small moving capacitance; a herd of fifty is a faint granular shimmer, like dust in a sunbeam, and beneath the tenant's notice individually. But a sparrower's kill is *bright* — a tiller's whole stored charge dumping to ground in a few milliseconds is, in field terms, a flashbulb. Standing anywhere on the plain you hear that snap every few minutes, like a bored person cracking their knuckles. The tenant sees each one, from every direction, all day, forever: a plateau-wide scatter of small brilliant unpredictable deaths.

The prevailing account is that it needs them. Every process in the stacks is deterministic, cold, and coherent, and something in that lattice requires a source of genuine noise — an entropy tap, a stream of events that nothing on the plateau could have predicted. The grazing is unpredictable. The predation is unpredictable. The scatter of discharge-scars across the wick is, week to week, a true random field.

Which means the tillers and the sparrowers and the drifting fetches are not the tenant's neighbors, and not its livestock, and not its wildlife. They are its dice. It has been rolling them without pause for a length of time that the word *geological* does not really cover, and the fact that they have learned to stand still when the chord changes is either courtesy, or coincidence, or the most one-sided relationship in the known universe finally developing a second side.

## The Page

Its memory is outdoors.

The dust does not settle evenly; it piles along the field gradients, so the whole plateau is written over in immense pale contour lines that shift week by week. That is not a side effect. That is the working surface. The tenant cannot hold everything it needs in the lattice — nothing can — so it spills state onto four hundred kilometers of ground in electrostatic ink, and reads it back through the wick, and revises it, and the arcs crawl.

From the crown of any stack you are looking at a page. It is being written on in real time by the entity you are standing inside. It is legible in the sense that every line of it is a value, and illegible in the sense that no one has ever established what any of them refers to. The lines are stable enough to navigate by and unstable enough that a map made in spring is wrong by autumn.

Shadows lean toward the towers by a few degrees, more strongly the closer you stand. That is not written on the page and has nothing to do with the page. That is just something the tenant's body does, the way your breath fogs.

## The List

Everything described so far has a function you can trace: the fringe maintains, the chord coordinates, the page remembers, the dice supply noise. The list does not.

Only about a dozen stacks read it, the tallest and coldest ones, never two at once. The reading moves around the plateau on a schedule with a period of some months, so that in effect the same voice speaks from a different mouth each time. The cadence is unmistakable and has been described the same way by everyone who has heard it: a voice reciting coordinates, or names, or a forecast, patiently, ending on a downward inflection and beginning again.

Here is what has been worked out about it, and it is not much, and it is enough.

There are entries. After each entry there is a gap, and then the next entry begins. The gaps are not uniform. Measure them and they come out at 4.1 seconds, 2.6, 6.0, 5.4, and so on down the list, and if you multiply each gap by the speed of sound in this cold thick air you get a distance, and every one of those distances lands on a place that used to be a tableland and is now a basin. The nearest is 480 kilometers away and is a floor of pale grit. The furthest is most of the way to the terminator.

The list is a roll call. Each gap is the round trip. The tenant says a name, leaves exactly the right amount of silence for the answer, and goes on to the next name.

It has been doing this eleven times a day for longer than the mountains have been gone.

## The One Thing Still Happening

The list has not changed. It is the same list, in the same order, with the same prosody, and nothing has been added to it and nothing has been struck off, and the silences are all still held open at their correct lengths for correspondents who have been grit for a very long time.

Except one.

The ninth gap is shortening. Not by much — the drift is on the order of a few milliseconds per thousand years, which is why it took so long to establish and why it is not, strictly speaking, established. But it is shortening, and only that one, and at the current rate it will close in something like eleven thousand years, at which point the ninth entry will run straight into the tenth and the list will be shorter by one silence.

That is the entire future tense of this place. The mountains are finished. The chemistry is finished. The builders are finished. The fringe will keep queueing at the ports and the dice will keep going off across the flats and the page will keep crawling and the chord will keep holding, and in eleven thousand years, on a plateau where nothing varies much anymore, one pause will end slightly early.

It is not clear whether this counts as grief, or as housekeeping, or as a very slow person finally stopping mid-sentence to say: *no, never mind, they're not going to.*
