# The Counted

There are forty. It has been forty for a long time — thirty-eight in a bad decade, forty-four once, but the number wants to be forty and gets back to it — and the important thing about forty here is that no one has ever met them. Sound stops at twenty paces. You can hold in your presence, at absolute most, three other people, and only if they are close enough to touch on a lean. So there is no crowd, no assembly, no chorus, no held breath of a gathered room. Nobody in the basin has ever heard more than four voices at once, including their own. When the tenders say *everyone*, they mean a rumour: a list, a knotted cord, a set of names walked from shed to shed over the course of a season. Everyone is a thing you have to be told.

They are, by any measure from elsewhere, a very small population with an enormous job, and they know it, and they do not think of it as a job.

## Bodies

Heavy in the hip and shoulder, most of them, from a life of hauling on hand-lines and standing braced against a shuddering pipe. Long feet, splayed, with a toe-grip habit — bare on the planks, boots only for the mud, and the boots kept at the station doors and never worn indoors because you can't feel a plank through leather and a plank you can't feel is a plank you might step past.

The skin is where the information is. It is a sense organ the way another people's eyes are: it reads temperature to a fraction, it reads the grain of a board and knows which board, it reads the shudder in a rail as *Four is running, Four is running rough, Four has thrown its packing again.* Their forearms are the most educated part of them. Rub a tender's inner forearm and they will flinch — not from pain, from the intimacy of it, the way you would flinch if a stranger touched your eyelid.

And the eyes. Wide, clear, lashed, perfectly formed, and never once used. They keep them clean out of a nagging sense of ownership. They dream in pressure and smell and the *tunk*. Children are born with them and the mothers check them the way you'd check for the right number of fingers: correct, present, no use to anybody, good.

But here is the thing about a body in the Tallow Flats, and it takes a visitor until the second day and it takes a visitor until they have been alone once.

A person, like everything else, is only as settled as they have been attended to.

## The wearing

A tender's hands are smooth. Not calloused — *smooth*, satined, dished slightly at the pads, the fingerprints worn to a soft topography like the handrails, because hands are handled constantly. Hands go into other hands at every meeting. The greeting is not a word; it is a full clasp, palm to palm, thumb hooked, and held for a slow four count, and this is not affection, or not only. It is upkeep.

Faces likewise: read with fingers, several times a day, brow to jaw, and a face much read is a definite face — blunt, warm, ordinary. The faces of the old are almost featureless in the way of river stones, and they are considered beautiful, and they are also considered safe. A face nobody has touched in a season goes fine. It gets an edge along the cheekbone and the bridge of the nose that will part a fingertip without any sensation of cutting, and the person carrying it does not feel a thing, and cannot understand why the person reading them has suddenly gone quiet and is sucking their finger.

Which is why the deep custom, the one they'd never think to explain, is the **back-reading**. Nobody can reach their own back. Nobody can attend to the length of their own spine, the shoulderblades, the small of it — and so the back is the vaguest part of any tender, the least agreed-upon, and if you go long enough without it, things start there. People report the sensation of the back "going open." A draught between the shoulders in a room with no draught. The feeling of being wider behind than in front.

So: at the end of a circuit, in the shed, the two of you sit one behind the other on the bench and each reads the other's back down, vertebra by vertebra, with the flat of both thumbs, naming the count out loud. *One, two, three.* All the way down and back up again. Twice. It takes maybe four minutes. It is the most ordinary act in the basin and no tender has gone a week without it in ninety-one years, and the pairs-and-threes arrangement of the whole population exists for it and for nothing else. You do not station a person alone. You have never stationed a person alone. There is a story, walked around the basin at intervals, about the one time somebody was stationed alone, and the story ends with a shed door that opened onto a shape that had to be talked back into a person over eleven days by three people taking shifts, and the story is told carefully, because a story told often enough here starts to be the case.

## Names

A name is worn like everything else.

Newborns get long ones. Eleven syllables, twelve — assembled out of the names of the dead and the gone-up and the tally-numbers of the stations, deliberately overbuilt, deliberately sharp, and for the first year the child is called the whole thing every single time, dozens of times a day, and it *hurts to say*, and that's understood to be the point. The name is raw rock. It has an edge on every syllable.

Then it wears. Use rubs it. By ten a child is down to six syllables, by thirty to three, and the elders of the basin are single grunts — Ossa at Seven is just **Oss**, has been Oss for forty years, and Oss is the most real object in the northern flats. Her joints are the basin's calendar; she can call the rain to within half a day off the ache in her wrists, and she is consulted about it by relay, and the relay takes nine days, so mostly she is consulted about rains that have already happened, and confirms them.

A name unsaid goes fine again. There is a whole small dread around this. If you have not heard your name in a while you start finding excuses to be greeted. Tenders who have been out on a long line-crossing say their own name to themselves the whole way, a low mutter under the fog, not out of loneliness, out of maintenance, the way you'd keep a hand on a rail.

## The relay

News moves at walking pace and gets smoother as it goes.

This is understood plainly. A thing that has just happened is not yet very true; it has burrs on it, it doesn't hold its shape, two people who witnessed it will give it differently. A thing that has been told at every station around the arc and come back is true in a hard, load-bearing way — polished, agreed, roundly finished, and if it now disagrees with what you personally remember seeing, then what you personally remember was an unwitnessed edge and you should let it go.

So history in the Tallow Flats is not a record of the past. It is a manufacturing process for the past, and it runs at about one circuit per season, and the tenders have no anxiety about this whatsoever.

The records are knotted cord. Tally-line, hung in the sheds, read by pulling it through the fingers. A much-read line is a smooth line and a smooth line is dependable, and it is also thinning, and this is the central problem of the basin's archive: the truest documents are the ones closest to parting. Copying is done by two people at once, one reading, one knotting, both saying the count aloud, and it takes days, and the old line is not discarded — it is coiled into a jar of the Slow Water's brine and kept, which does nothing, which everyone agrees does nothing, and which nobody has ever suggested stopping.

## Eating

Crab, mostly, off the pilings — pried out of the accreted legs of the stations with a hook, and the shell layer that's been harvested for ninety-one years is the most decided rock in the whole basin, harder than the ridges. Eel, when the intake screens are cleared. Tallow-fungus, boiled twice, which is bland and faintly sweet and sits in the stomach like warm putty. Salt scraped off the drying pans at the edge of the Slow Water. A grey oil rendered from stillmoth wax that they burn for nothing — it makes no light — but rub into their hands and their tally-lines and each other.

Every mouthful is counted. Not for rationing. Counting holds things. Food found in the loose is treated with the same protocol as everything found in the loose: several of it will arrive, and you take the one at the right temperature, and you do not discuss the others, and you do not look for them afterward, and if you are new and you have questions about the others, the answer you will get is a hand on your forearm and a change of subject.

## The moths, which are the household

The stillmoths are not pets and are not vermin and are more or less family.

They come to warmth. Lie down in a shed and within a minute there will be nine or ten of them settled along you, plumes working, reading heat. They find the hot joint, the infected cut, the belly of a woman two months before she'd have said anything. Tenders let them work and then read *them* — the pattern of settled moths on a body is the basin's only diagnostic instrument, and the old ones are very good at it. Oss can put a hand over a sleeping person and tell you where the moths are massed without disturbing one, and tell you what it means, and be right.

Their sound is the sound of home. That continuous dry hush around a station, hundreds of thousands of small surfaces sliding — a tender who goes out on a long crossing does not miss company, they miss the hush, and they describe the loose as *the silence*, even though the loose is full of drips and their own breathing. Coming back in range of a station, the hush comes up out of nothing at about twenty-five paces, and every tender in the basin has the same private, unmentioned relief at that moment, and none of them know the others have it, because who would you tell.

## Children

Born at a station. Always. A birth in the loose is not something anyone will discuss, and the wire hoops that mark soft spots are, in three places, doubled, and nobody will say why.

Twins are common — or "common." The doctrine is firm and cheerful and completely unexamined: if two arrive, both are counted, and counting makes them two, and that is the end of it. Both get eleven syllables. Both get read down the spine every night. There is no ritual for it and no anxiety about it and it works, in that the twins of the Tallow Flats grow up to be two people, indisputably, worn separate by decades of separate attention.

Children are taught to count before they are taught to speak. Planks first, then knots, then their own name. They are walked on a short line for the first four years, tied at the waist, and the line is not for safety exactly. It is so they are always at the end of something that somebody is holding.

## The smoothing

When a tender dies, the body must be attended, continuously, for three days.

Not out of grief, though there is grief. An unwitnessed thing goes fine, and a body with nobody in it is on its way to being unwitnessed from the inside, and if you leave it, by the second day it develops edges that no one can see, and it becomes a hazard in the shed, and there are stories. So the shifts are stood: hands on, always some part of the skin under some part of a living hand, and the name said aloud at intervals, the whole name, all the way back up to its eleven syllables if anyone can still reconstruct them — this is the one time a name is *un*worn, spoken long and rough and sharp again, deliberately, in reverse, all the way back to the raw.

Then the body goes into the Slow Water off the mats, and the crabs have it, and the crabs become the pilings, and the pilings hold up the pumps.

They find this satisfactory. They say so.

## The gone-up

At the last tally post on the rim path there is a shed the size of a coffin with names cut into the inside of the door, and somebody walks out there four times a year and opens it and reads the names aloud, at the length each person had worn theirs to when they went.

They are not dead. Everyone is firm on this. Their eyes began to itch, and they kept going, and something happened up there that is not dying, and the correct response is upkeep: keep the name said, keep it from going fine, in case.

There are nineteen names on the door. Six of them are single syllables, which means six people who were as real and as worn and as fixed as anyone in the basin ever gets simply put down the hand-line one day and climbed. Nobody has an explanation. When a visitor presses for one, the tenders get uncomfortable, and then they say the thing they say, which is that up there, apparently, luminosity completes — and then they change the subject, because a tender with useless eyes cannot think about that for very long without a feeling behind the face like the back going open.

## What actually lives here

Ask a tender what they do and they will tell you they walk the circuit, clear the screens, pack the glands, grease what the moths have missed, mark the tally.

Ask them what the pumps are *for* and they will look puzzled, because you have asked a question with a false floor in it. The water goes to the Slow Water and the Slow Water gives it back to the ground and the ground gives it to the pumps. Nothing is produced. Nothing leaves. Ninety-one years, and the only outputs are heat and noise and a slow rhythmic assertion, once a second, eighty meters down and nine stations wide: *this, here, now. This, here, now.*

The pumps are the most attentive thing in the world. They never look away. They have been laying their hands on this valley without pause since before anyone's grandmother, and where they run, the ground is definite, and the stations stand on legs of crab in a landscape that would otherwise still be a suggestion.

So the honest answer to who lives in the Tallow Flats is: nine riveted drums on pilings, and their forty attendants, and the moths that oil them, and the herds out on the loose eating ambiguity like grass, all of it a single slow apparatus for insisting that a place exists. The tenders wear the rails smooth. The rails wear the tenders smooth. In four or five hundred years there will be a plain warm marsh here, entirely real, worn round and quiet, and the people in it will have short blunt names and good hands and no idea, and that is the point, and forty people who will never all be in a room together are doing it on purpose, one plank at a time, counting out loud.
