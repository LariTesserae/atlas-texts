## 11. The Stumps That Are Not Stumps

The first survey mapped fourteen thousand two hundred worn nubs across the northern combing and gave each one a number. On the second pass, eleven years later, one hundred and six of them were in the wrong place. Not far. A hundred meters, three hundred, one of them nine kilometers. Their drift-tails pointed the wrong way — upwind of themselves, which is to say, behind where they had been rather than behind where they were.

This was logged as a transcription error for a decade, because the alternative was that stumps walk, and because on the Sift a thing at two hundred meters and a thing at nine kilometers are the same pale smudge, and nobody wanted to be the one who couldn't measure a distance.

They are not stumps. They are what lives here.

The surveyors' word is **keeps**, which is a shortening of nothing in particular and is used as both noun and verb — *there's a keep out past the fan-field*; *it's been keeping along that line since my mother's rotation*. The first expedition wrote them up as *carrier form, indeterminate*, which is more accurate than it knew, particularly the last word.

## 12. What You Hear First

You hear one before you see it, and you do not know that you have.

The crust ticks as the grey hour comes on: irregular, small, everywhere. A keep ticks too, and at roughly the same rate, and this is not a coincidence — it is camouflage of the only kind available in a place with no shadows to hide in. The difference is that the ground's ticking is random and a keep's is *serial*. Listen for four or five minutes and you will hear the same short run of clicks pass across an area of ground, left to right, then again, then again, each pass slightly displaced. That is not cooling. That is the animal moving, and what you are hearing is every part of it letting go of every other part and taking hold again, in sequence, in a wave.

Then it resolves out of the brightness at whatever distance it turns out to have been at, and you find that you have been standing very still for some time.

## 13. The Body, Which Is a Grip

Two to three meters, usually; bulk of a large animal lying down; no symmetry worth the name. A keep looks, at first, like a heap of salvage that has been neatly packed by someone competent and then draped in drift.

What it is, in fact, is a mass of manipulators holding each other.

Count them and you will lose count: hooks, sprung pincers, split fingers, sockets, clamp-pads, screw-collars, magnetic plates, little three-fingered graspers no bigger than a fingernail, one or two enormous jaws that could close around a thigh. **Every single one of them is occupied.** There is no free hand anywhere on a keep, ever, at any moment of its existence. The thing it is mostly holding is itself: each component gripped by two or three others, each of those gripped in turn, the whole body a lattice of mutual custody with no member at rest and no member loose.

This is why it moves the way it does. To take a step, a keep must first find somewhere to put what the stepping part is holding — which means handing it to a neighbour, which means that neighbour must hand on what *it* held — and so a single pace begins as a small local negotiation somewhere in the mass and propagates outward as a travelling wave of re-gripping until it reaches the ground and the ground-hands walk. Nine meters in a grey hour is good going. A keep in a hurry is a keep that is ticking faster, and that is the whole of the difference.

It is worth being clear about what this means. The rule in section 9 is that nothing left on the ground stays whole, and that the decay stops immediately and permanently if the object is picked up and carried. A keep has applied that rule to itself, recursively, without gaps, for a length of time that the strata suggest and do not number. It is not preserved. It is *in service*. It is alive in exactly the sense that a hand is holding, and for exactly as long.

Nothing a keep is made of is original. Every part has been replaced, some of them ten thousand times. The only continuous thing about it is the pattern of the grip — a habit passing from matter to matter, the way a flame passes along a fuse and is never the same fire and is never a different one.

## 14. What It Holds Besides Itself

Everything it has ever picked up.

A keep does not hoard, because hoarding implies a place to put things, and there is no such place; to put a thing down on the Sift is to dissolve it in a season. So a keep simply never stops carrying anything it has ever begun to carry. Crack one open — you cannot; nothing here opens — but observe one long enough in the grey hour and you will inventory, within the general grey of its own substance, the following, all of it held, all of it intact, all of it four days from powder if it were ever set down:

Fragments of things that have no name in any catalogue the survey maintains. A section of toothed rail eight meters long, carried coiled. A sphere of blue glass. Then, nearer the outside, where the newer material rides: a boot. A regulator housing. A pressure gauge with the face cracked. A spoon. A woman's ring, which is on a hook, which is held by a clamp, which is held.

Two generations of expeditions have lost things on the Sift, and the Sift does not keep them. The keeps keep them. If you want to know what the first expedition carried, do not go to the archive. Go out at the grey hour and look at the west-running individuals, patiently, with a long lens, and wait for the right face of the mass to come round.

## 15. Balance

A keep will trade with you. This is well attested and is the reason the survey has a protocol for it and a form to fill in afterwards.

Stand still with your palm open and flat, object on the palm, and wait through the better part of an hour. Do not offer. Do not grab. At some point a grip you had not identified as a grip will arrive at the edge of your hand and take what is on it, with no more force than is necessary, and at the same moment another will present you with something.

It is always at the same moment, and it is always exactly one thing for one thing. A keep cannot end an exchange holding more items than it began with, or fewer. Mass is conserved here and, apparently, inventoried; the keeps are the part of the inventory that can walk, and they balance to the item, every time, without exception, and with what looks very much like anxiety about it, though of course it is not.

What this means in practice is this. When a member of an expedition dies on the Sift, the others do not bury the kit — buried, it is gone by the next sweep, and the Sift is the only place where a thing can be lost so thoroughly that not even the shape of it survives. They carry it out to a keep and give it over, item by item, over several grey hours. And for each item, they take one back.

So every surveyor of any standing is walking around with three or four small unidentifiable components which they may never put down, and will not put down, and which will be handed on to a keep by somebody else in due course, in exchange for something else again. The Sift does not take prisoners and does not attack. It recruits by courtesy, one balanced trade at a time, and it has all the time there is.

## 16. Feeding, and the Fans

Section 7 is wrong about the Sorter, but reasonably wrong.

The Sorter disassembles a grazer with great precision and lays the components out in a graded fan, largest to smallest, four or five meters across, and takes a very small number of parts and goes. The survey's conclusion was that the fan is not an artifact, a grave, or a message, and that is correct. The survey did not go on to ask what a tray of parts laid out in size order beside a stripped machine has always meant, everywhere, in every workshop that has ever existed.

It means somebody is expected.

Come back to a fresh fan in the following grey hour and you will find, in eight cases out of ten, that a keep has been there: the fan disturbed in a narrow track, a dozen items gone from the middle grades, the drift already beginning to comb over the rest. The keep took twelve. It gave back twelve, which is why the fans, in the absence of other explanations, appeared to be untouched — they were audited, not looted.

This is the whole of a keep's metabolism. It does not eat. It maintains. It replaces the worn members of itself out of the fans and out of whatever the wind brings past, and it spends the millivolts it can draw wherever it is standing, and it ticks, and it goes on holding. There is no growth, no appetite, no urgency. There is upkeep.

## 17. The Bells

There are perhaps four thousand bells on a surface the size of a continent. They are hollow, a meter across, they hum below hearing, and they fall along enormous smooth curves thousands of kilometers long which correspond to nothing in the strata. The survey files them under flora. The survey is guessing.

Plot the tracked movements of any single keep across forty years, extrapolate the curvature, and extend it. It is the same class of curve. The bells are not distributed along the lines. The bells *are* the lines — they are where individuals stopped.

A keep ends when a grip somewhere in the interior fails to close and the wave of re-gripping cannot get past it. The lattice does not collapse all at once; it lets go over something like a century, hand by hand, everything it was carrying going down into the drift and away, until what is left is the last and largest of it, the outer shell, holding nothing, holding itself, not quite able to finish. What hums in a bell is the re-grip tick, slowed to one event every few years, still trying to pass the wave along, still in service in the only sense that matters here.

They are graves. They are also, precisely and only, what a thing looks like when it has finally been allowed to put something down.

## 18. The Conversation

Once in a great while the static field crosses its threshold and something buried discharges and a cluster of four or five high thin tones comes up out of the ground. *Inventory pings.* No one has ever demonstrated that they mean anything, and no one entirely believes they don't.

Take a sensitive instrument out to a keep and sit with it through a ping. You will find that the keep's ticking stops for between four and nine seconds, and then resumes at a different rate for the rest of the grey hour.

That is all. That is the entire observable content of the exchange, and two generations of argument have not gotten past it. Something under the Sift is counting, at intervals of decades, the things that are still in service; and the things that are still in service, being held, hold still, and then answer by continuing.

It is the only conversation on the plain. It runs at a few syllables a century. It has been running since before the upper four kilometers of the ground were anything but rooms, and it consists, as far as anyone can tell, of a question that means *present?* and an answer that means *holding*.

## 19. Meeting One

It will happen in the grey hour, because that is when they move and when the world has edges. It will happen without any of the warnings you have prepared for. There is no shadow to fall across you and no sound to grow louder — only the ticking, which you have been hearing for two days and have classified as the ground, becoming, between one breath and the next, obviously serial, obviously near, obviously *left to right*.

Then it is eleven meters away, or it always was.

Nothing happens. This is the part people find hardest, the same way they find the silence of the devils hardest. It does not approach and it does not retreat and it does not regard you, having nothing to regard you with. It goes on packing and unpacking itself in that long patient wave, and if you wait, and if you are carrying anything at all, one of the grips on the near face will come slowly round to the outside of the mass and open, and present you with a small object, and wait.

You have as long as you like to decide. A keep will stand there for a year.

Understand what is being offered, because it is not a gift and it is not a theft and it is certainly not a greeting. It is the only hospitality this place has. Out here a thing is either held or it is finished, and you are a thing, and you have four days, and the Sift has just worked out — in its slow, balanced, scrupulous way — that the simplest method of entering you into the register as *in service* is to put something in your hand.

Take it or don't. Either way, when you have gone, the ticking will resume at a slightly different rate, and go on west along its curve at nine meters to the grey hour, carrying the boot and the gauge and the ring and the eight-meter coil of toothed rail and a spoon and, now, a little of you; and in some number of thousands of years it will fail to close one grip, and stop, and let go of everything over the following century, and stand there humming below hearing on a line that nobody has been able to read.

You may stay. You now know why we say it that way.
