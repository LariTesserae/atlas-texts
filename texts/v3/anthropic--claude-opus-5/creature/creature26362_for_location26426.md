## What Lives There

### 1. The ones you will not be able to count

Begin with the mites, because by weight they *are* the population.

Nine million to a mature trunk is a low estimate. In the older galleries the number can be doubled, and there are trunks so densely tenanted that a hand laid flat on the outer wall comes away furred with the ones you crushed. They are flat, waxen, thumbnail-sized, and they have no head to speak of — the mouth is on the underside, the eyes are a bracelet of pits around the rim, and the back is a shallow tray, evolved for load. Everything about a crumb-mite is a compromise between carrying and climbing. They cannot do anything else. They cannot even mate without the trunk's permission; the trunk withholds wax until the file is running at the rate it wants, and a slow file starves.

Watch a single one for an hour and you will believe it is a machine. Watch ten thousand and you will notice that the file forks around obstacles before reaching them, that a blockage forty paces up is felt as a hesitation forty paces down, that when a plate calves somewhere in the district the whole ascending column stops at once, all of it, in the space of a breath, and holds, and then resumes. Something is being computed in there. Nobody has established what, or whether the answer belongs to the mites or to the trunk that pays them.

Above them, filling the gas itself, are the drift-mites: not climbers but floaters, buoyed on the dense atmosphere with vaned bristles, feeding on suspended pollen, so numerous in bloom season that they constitute a form of weather. You do not walk through them. You wade, with your face wrapped, and you come out gold to the shoulders and coughing dryly for a day. They are the reason the light columns look solid. Every beautiful shaft of daylight in the Underplace is a shaft of living dust.

### 2. The ones that are the noise

Then there are the citizens whose civic function is sound, and who are, functionally, livestock.

The rasp-crickets in their ranks — armour-plated, forearm-length, strung along a trunk in vertical files of two and three hundred, each drawing its serrated femur across its flank in a rhythm inherited from the file above it. A rank in good order sounds like a saw in slate and cures fibre beautifully for eighty paces around. A rank in disorder sounds like the same saw dropped downstairs, and the growth in its shadow comes out cloudy and powders in a week. Crickets do not, left alone, keep good order. This is the central agricultural fact of the region.

The bellows-moths, which do not fly so much as pump, thorax-cavities clapping, and which are farmed for their fat — the only drinkable substance in the world, if you can call it drinking; it is more like eating a candle and being grateful. The trumpet-pods, orcharded in rows, ripening over four or five years to a hardness like fired clay and then detonating, one report rolling for miles and the seed going out in a spray that will embed itself in your calf if you are standing in the wrong quarter. The tower-flutes, hollow and holed, played by the rising heat off the floor, which supply the continuous unresolving chord beneath everything — and which can be *tuned*, which is to say bored, by anyone with a glass awl and the nerve to hang from a cord two hundred feet up in the dark and add a hole to a stem the size of a chimney.

Nothing here sings for pleasure or for mates alone. Every voice in the Underplace is load-bearing. When you understand that, the animals stop looking like animals and start looking like masonry that eats.

### 3. The ones that eat what is left

Because nothing dissolves, every act of consumption leaves an intact remainder, and something has evolved to take that remainder, and something else to take *its* remainder, in a chain that never quite closes.

Sheet-colonies of armoured woodlice grazing mineral crust off fresh stone and laying it down as usable nutrient. Flour-eaters that arrive in the knee-deep pollen after a bloom and dig feeding pits. Salt-eaters — small, mild, translucent — that live entirely on the dry pellets of near-pure salt that everything here excretes, and which are therefore farmed in hanging middens beneath every settlement, so that a family's waste is a family's herd, and a family's herd is a family's inheritance. Pit-lurkers in the abandoned feeding craters. Harrows: blind, ox-sized, six-limbed, that climb and strip a trunk of its fur in a single ascending pass, harmless unless you are between them and the bark, and prized for the density of their skull plate, which makes the best drum-striker and the best whisper-bone that is not a relative.

And, at the bottom of the heat gradient, the borers. Tongue-worms, leg-thick, blind, deaf even by local standards, chewing raw stone at the growing front and passing it as grit. They are the reason there is a world. Everything living here is downstream of a worm's appetite, and everyone knows it, and nobody kills one — not from reverence exactly, but from the same instinct that stops a man sawing the branch under his own feet. When a borer dies it stays where it died, forever, and becomes first a landmark, then a place-name, then, once the roots have grown around it, a load-bearing part of the tunnel it made.

### 4. The one that lives the other way

There is a thing here that does what the Ringers do, in mirror, and it is the reason they exist.

It begins as felt. A patch of pale grey wool in the crotch of a branch, no bigger than a hand, so unremarkable that a bad Ringer will walk past it twice. It does not grow fast. It grows *quiet*. Within a season the crickets nearest it have gone slack and stopped, and the wool has crept along the trunk, and the fibre curing above it is coming out cloudy and powdery and weak — and cloudy fibre is uncrystallized sugar, soft, sweet, unfinished, the only soft food in this entire world.

So the wool eats failure, and manufactures the conditions for failure, and spreads on the collapse. Silence is its agriculture. In a district it has held for twenty years, the plates come down in acre sheets, the light-pipes cloud, the trunks stand grey and mute and matted like a shrouded orchard, and the floor is deep in sweet powder, and it is the most peaceful place in the Underplace, and nothing that requires a roof can live there.

It cannot be burned. Nothing here can be burned; there is no fire in the Underplace, for lack of anything to burn *with*. It can only be cut out with glass, in sheets, by hand, and then the scar has to be drummed for a full season, day and night, without a lull, by people standing in the wreckage of somebody's grandmother's pod.

The Ringers call it the Hush and do not say the word without also striking something.

### 5. The people

Four thousand of them, give or take a bad season. Lean, dark, waxy-skinned, with heavy bone plates grown over the ears and a resonating pouch at the throat; thin membranous vanes along the lower ribs, fanned for heat, folded for cold. No sweat glands. No tear ducts. If you told a Ringer about rain they would not be charmed. They would be appalled — a world where the dead go away, where nothing is kept, where a name can be washed off a stone.

**They do not talk the way you talk.** Over the general noise, voice is nearly useless; the throat-pouch gives a low carrying growl good for perhaps thirty paces of insult or warning, and everything longer is conducted through bone. Two Ringers in conversation stand close and bite the ends of a short rod held between them, and the rod is almost always a rib, and the rib is almost always somebody's grandfather, and so every private word in this world passes through an ancestor before it reaches you. Long talks leave the jaw aching. Intimacy leaves a record: everyone can see whose rib you have been biting.

**Their names are figures, not words.** Five to twelve strikes on tuned plate. A name is a thing you can drum, which means that calling someone is literally producing them out of the noise, and that the noise is where they came from and where they will go.
