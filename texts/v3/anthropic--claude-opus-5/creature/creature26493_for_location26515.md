## Who is there

Three things live in the basin, and it is possible there are only two, and one persistent minority view holds that there is only one.

The leaf lives there. The climbers live there. And something with hands has been building, and does not appear to have stopped.

## The body of a climber

Take the animal first, because you can hold one in your hands, and because it is the only thing in the basin that will let you.

The length of your forearm, tail included, though the tail is not much: a tapered counterweight, no grip in it. Four limbs of nearly equal length, so that it does not so much climb as *reach*, distributing itself across a lattice the way water distributes across cloth. Each limb ends in a hand — a real one, four fingers and a thumb set well back and truly opposed, with flat nails and pads that are dry and faintly tacky, the exact tackiness of everything else in that atmosphere. The fingers are the length of matchsticks and they are absurdly strong; a dead one still gripping its cane can be lifted, cane and all, and will not let go.

The fur is short and dense and the colour of the light, which is to say the colour of everything, which is to say it is invisible. Paler on the belly. There is a bloom on it, a dull mineral dust from the travertine and from the drops, that comes off on your palm as chalk.

The eyes face forward and are set close. Nothing has ever hunted anything here, so the eyes are not for watching the world; they are for watching work at arm's length, and for reading another one's hands, and they are not good for much else. The pupils are pinpricks and stay pinpricks. There has never been a night, so there is no dark adaptation to speak of; a lamp brought in and shone on a climber produces no reaction whatsoever, not fear, not interest — the animal simply has no category for a small bright thing, and the light is treated as a feature of the air. Against the ceiling glare a third lid draws across, milky as skim, and a climber crossing an open slot in the decks goes briefly and completely blind and stops for it. That pause is the nearest thing to a habit anyone has recorded of them.

The teeth are ground flat, all of them, front to back, with one notched incisor kept sharp for splitting fibre.

## The only rot in the world

They eat the dead.

Not carrion — there is none, or rather there is nothing but, everywhere, holding. They eat the lattice: the dry cane that the plant put out a century or a millennium ago and never took back, the brittle pale leaves lodged in the crossings, the whole immortal skeleton of the basin's own history. They rasp it, and wet it in the mouth for a long time, an hour at a mouthful, and swallow it into a warm chamber of a gut that occupies most of them.

And in that chamber, and nowhere else under the roof-leaf, **things rot.**

This is the fact to hold on to about them. The basin has no fungi, no decomposers, no bacterial slick on any stone or pool or wound. The entire process of decay in that eleven-kilometre world is sealed inside a few hundred bodies the length of your forearm, carried about at walking pace, at four hundred metres an hour, in the dark, at blood heat. They are not animals living in a place without rot. They are where the rot is kept.

Which is why a dead one smells. For four or five days a dead climber produces the only smell of its kind in the basin — sweetish, appalling, unmistakable in an atmosphere that otherwise offers hot sap, wet limestone and faint paper — as the chamber consumes its host from inside, and then it goes quiet, having cooked and dried itself out along with everything else, and the smell stops, and the body dries, and the body holds.

Pick one up afterwards. It weighs nothing at all. It is a hollow of fur with hands on it, and the hands still grip, and it will be there in a thousand years.

They also drink, and drinking is the only thing that ever makes one travel vertically. A climber will spend two days going up — through the decks, out along a secondary, up a stem to the underside of the world — and hang there upside down among the beaded veins with its mouth around a drop the size of a plum, sucking it away before surface tension can let it fall. Seen from the floor, from eighty metres below, silhouetted on a glowing membrane, this is the one sight in the basin that looks like a picture of something.

## The speed

You know the speed already: as though the air were syrup. But the syrup is not weakness and it is not disease. It is the correct rate.

Nothing here is competing. Nothing is hunting them; nothing is racing them to the cane; there is no season closing, no rain coming, no night falling, no rival at the water.
