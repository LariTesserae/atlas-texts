## The ones who go in front

They call themselves, as near as it can be put, *the crust-breakers*, and they are not a metaphor either. Go and watch a chalker front from a mile off and you will see, a hundred yards ahead of that two-mile rasping wall, a loose scatter of small dark figures moving over the new swells with mallets, cracking the glaze.

That is the whole arrangement. A swell comes up, sets, and skins over in a hard vitreous crust that a chalker's file-tongue cannot get through; the herders break it, the film gets in, the pasture happens, the herd follows. Nobody taught them this and nobody remembers agreeing to it. The chalkers simply walk toward the sound of mallets, the way a dog goes to a can opener, and have done so for longer than anyone's hearth-slab has existed.

The mallet is bone, weighted at the head with a copper collar, and it does two jobs, and this is the key to understanding them: while a woman is breaking crust for a hundred thousand animals she is also *listening*. Tap, tap, dull, dull, dull — and then a note with a soft heart in it, a note like knocking on a knuckle instead of a knee, and she stops dead, which is the only circumstance in which one of these people ever willingly stops. Metal. Somewhere under her foot, a plum of native copper.

So the sound of a herder is a knocking. Sparse, arrhythmic, wandering, under the hiss, from horizon to horizon: thousands of people tapping the world to see whether it is worth anything yet.

## Bodies made by the floor

Short. Dense through the chest, from a lifetime of shouting. Bare-footed always — no leather holds up to new stone — and so the sole is a horn plate a half-inch thick, pared down with glass blades every few days, and this paring is the tenderest thing they do to one another. Couples pare each other's feet. Mothers pare children's. A widow's feet grow ragged and everyone can see it.

The heel is an organ of judgement. Before a Loaf child can properly walk it is taught the heel-test: strike down hard, feel for give. A knuckle's depth of softness is the world being new, which is fine. Two knuckles is somebody's roof. They will not lie down on ground that gives.

Legs bowed, knees finished by forty. They squat rather than sit, because the rock is warm and there is no reason to be lower than you have to be, and because a squatting person is already standing.

The eyes go early. There is no night here to rest them and the seams do not blink, and by fifty most have a milky rind coming in at the edges; by sixty they are seam-blind, and a heavy brow-ridge and a smear of purple-black mat-paste under each eye is only ever a delay. They are entirely unbothered by this. Sight is a young person's tool, for spotting bell-flies and reading a swell's blisters. The ear is where the money is, and the ear improves. A blind grandmother is not a burden; she is the clan's assay office, carried out over a new swell on a litter of ribs, one hand down on the stone, calling *there — and there — and a big one, deep, leave it for your daughter*.

Their hair is always wet. Everyone in the Loaf is always slightly damp, and they smell of sulphur, lime, hot shell, and the sour-sweet of the purple mats, and they can smell the difference between one vent and its neighbour a mile off, and they name places by nose rather than by sight. A great many Loaf place-names translate as things like *the stink that gets in your teeth* and *sweet rot, three days east*.

## Two tongues, and names that are not words

Under the chalker hiss you cannot talk. You can only sing across it. So the far-tongue is all long open vowels pitched high and slid, four consonants total, insults carried in pitch rather than in vocabulary, and it sounds — from the Old Stair, drifting up out of the gold haze — like gulls arguing very slowly.

Close up, in the flour, or at night with the sleepers, they speak with their hands on each other. Knuckles on the collarbone, palm flat on the sternum, thumb-taps at the wrist. Whole conversations conducted between two people standing forehead to forehead in a drip-curtain, silently, for an hour.

And a person's name is not a word. It is a rhythm — five to nine beats, tapped. You are given it before you are named in the mouth, and you will hear it more often on someone's knuckles than in the air. Names are inherited but not repeated: when someone dies their rhythm is *retired*, drummed out once at the giving-over and then never used again, and a clan's history is therefore a percussion piece nobody is allowed to play.

They hum, constantly, all of them, a low individual drone, so that in the haze you can locate every member of your family without looking. Each camp has a composite sound. Herders can identify a strange clan at three hundred yards by the *chord* of it, and consider this so obvious that they cannot explain it any more than you can explain recognising a face.

## What they carry, and what they eat

Everything they own must be liftable by one person at a run. This is not poverty; it is a design constraint held with the ferocity of religion.

Membrane sheeting, folded. Rib tripods. A hearth-slab, glazed black and dished by four generations of cooking, dragged on a sledge of shoulder blades, and the dragging of it is an honour rotated among adolescents. Nets. Black glass blades, struck fresh whenever they cross a good lens and discarded when dull, so the Loaf floor is littered with a thin permanent glitter of yesterday's knives. Gut bottles. Needles — cold-hammered copper, kept in a bone tube on a cord round the neck, the single most stolen object in the world. And ear-weights: copper loops added one by one down a lifetime, so that a matriarch's lobes hang to her collarbone and must be looped up over her head on a cord to keep them out of the food, and the weight she carries on her ears is a legible record of every good season she ever had.

They eat chalker — calves, cripples, the ones the front leaves behind. They eat sifter-eels boiled in a pool by dropping in hot stones. They eat tap-crabs by the double handful, whole, sucking. They eat moth larvae out of the drip-curtains and consider it a delicacy and it is not. They eat the mats themselves, and the first thing any child memorises is the colour law: purple is dinner, orange is medicine, grey peels are for tanning, and yellow will kill you before you can be carried anywhere.

There is no cooking fire worth the name. Food is laid on the world — on a glazed slab near a vent — and turned with a stick. There is no bread and there is no word for bread; the flour they own is for scouring and for wounds. Every tooth in the Loaf is planed flat by thirty from grit in the meat, and so every camp has a chewer, usually a bright child of six or seven, who sits at a grandmother's knee and chews her food for her and hands it over. The children feed the elders in the same way the herds feed the floor. Nobody remarks on this. It would be like remarking that water goes downhill, on the years when it does.

## The night-child, and the horror of quiet

Every camp, every shift, forever: one child walking the perimeter with a rattle, and singing.

The rattle is inherited. Each night-child, on retiring from the duty, adds one shell or one bad nodule, so a rattle's weight is the count of the generations that have kept watch, and an old rattle is heavy enough to bruise a small arm and is carried with enormous pride and a certain amount of weeping.

The song is a list. It is the names of everyone asleep inside the circle, sung in order, over and over, all shift. This is precisely so that a sleeper surfacing at some noise hears her own name go by in a child's voice and knows without waking that she is still inside, still counted, still on hard ground.

Which means: they cannot sleep in silence. Silence is the shape of a stopped song, and a stopped song is the neighbour coming up under the floor, and a camp will go from dead sleep to on-its-feet-with-glass-in-hand in about two seconds if the rattle pauses. Take one of these people up out of the Loaf into a quiet room and she will lie rigid all night with her heart going. They think of quiet the way you think of a held breath in the dark behind you.

## Down is holy; up is only a lid

There is no word for sky and there is no interest in acquiring one. The ceiling is *the loaf*, and it is closing, and that is all. Ask what is on the other side of it and adults will look at you the way you would look at a grown man asking where the sun sleeps — not scandalised, just embarrassed for you. They have a whole grammatical category for questions of this kind: questions that are *childish*, meaning malformed, meaning a symptom. A man who begins asking them at forty is understood to be ill, and is treated gently, and is not left alone near the vents.

Holiness runs the other way. Downward, where the stone comes from, where the heat comes from, where the neighbour lives. They pour a little of everything into a vent before they eat. Their prayers are not words; a prayer is a job done fast and loudly, and to work hard in plain view of a seam is devotion. Stillness is the profane act — stillness is what gets you eaten — and consequently the few people who deliberately sit still, up on the Old Stair with their hands empty, are regarded as either prophets or corpses in advance, and both are given food.

Their one eschatology: someday every seam will cool at once, and the floor will stop rising, and the world will go smooth and cold and *finished*. They pray against it. What they want from the universe is more unrest.

The dead are not buried, because there is no soil and no permanence. A body is laid out on the freshest, softest swell that can be found, dusted over with flour, and left; and the ground comes up and takes it, and the taking is the funeral. Mourning lasts exactly as long as the body is visible. A good death is gone in nine days. To be slowly taken is a misfortune, and to lie unswallowed through a season is a horror that splits families, and their worst curse — spat, never shouted — is *may the ground be slow to you*.

## The cousins who did not follow the light

And then, three or four times in a lifetime, at the dim margin where the glare-drift has walked away and the valleys are cooling into their long gloom, the bright clans do business with the others.

They come up out of the dark with their eyes shut and their heads wrapped, standing with their backs to the seams, doing the entire transaction over one shoulder. Two, three generations in the dim have made them pale as sifters and given them nostrils you could put a finger in, and they are *slow*. Appallingly slow. They walk without testing the ground. They speak in the close-tongue, hand to collarbone, at a pace that makes a herder's skin crawl, and they sleep — this is the detail that gets told and retold around hearth-slabs and never loses its power — they sleep in silence, without a rattle, without a child, without a song.

And they bring metal. Buckets of it. Because in the gloom there is no chalker hiss and no press of small life and nothing at all to hear, and so their old people can stand on a swell and hear a nodule at a hundred paces, and their old people are therefore worth renting. It is not unheard of for a great bright matriarch's fortune to rest on a blind gloom-man carried at her side on a litter, and everybody knows it, and nobody says it, and her ear-weights are heavy all the same.

The bright clans pity them and fear them and cannot look straight at them, and use them, and go home fast.

Fast, always, in front of the herd, tapping, humming, damp, shouting, hands never empty, feet like horn, ears like instruments, a child at the edge of every sleep singing the names of everyone alive — because nothing here is finished, including them, and grief is slower than the ground.
