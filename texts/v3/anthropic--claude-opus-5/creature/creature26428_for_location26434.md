## The Fifty Thousand

They are long, narrow-boned, and deep in the chest. Altitude does the second part; the haulm does the first. A person raised on a surface that gives half an inch and pushes back grows a particular kind of leg — heavy behind the knee, light in the calf, ankles that never turn — and a particular way of moving, a low push off the heel with the toes kept flat, so that from a distance a Sill crowd looks like it is wading. On stone they are clumsy. On a stair they are magnificent. There are perhaps two hundred people on the plateau who have ever stood on rock, all of them Assize surveyors at the escarpment break, and they came back describing it as walking on a table that doesn't know you're there.

They wear grey because there is no other colour to be had. The Draw's cloth takes no dye, so the entire visual culture of the plateau is nap, weave, twill, and the fifteen recognised greys of the bast, from the near-white of first-cut down to the wet-slate of the fourth pressing. A Sill man can read a stranger's district, trade, seniority, and marital position off a sleeve at nine paces. Visitors, in whatever they arrived in, are agony to look at, and are told so, kindly.

They are enormously courteous and they finish every sentence. Nobody on the Sill trails off. You cannot trail off in a place where the wind is playing a two-hundred-metre chord underneath you; the end of the sentence would simply be taken by the wood. The speech sits in a narrow band — there is a seam in the chord, a pitch the canes cannot make, and every voice on the plateau has found it, generation after generation, without instruction. It is a hard, clipped, consonant-heavy sound, all edges, pitched to cut. Recordings of it made below the cloud (there are none) would be described as barking. Meanwhile everyone here finds the speech of outsiders muffled, drowned, faintly indecent, like being spoken to through a mattress.

And every one of them has a second voice: the counting voice. Flat, level, loud, no music in it at all, used for numbers and used for nothing else. Children are taught it before they are taught letters. You will hear a mother switch into it mid-sentence to count her party across a bridge and switch out of it again, and it does not sound like the same woman, and this is thought unremarkable.

They part by saying *counted.* They greet a room by putting a palm flat to the cane wall for a moment, which is a pressure-check and is also nothing, and is also not nothing.

## What They Eat

No meat, ever, in eight thousand years. No milk, no eggs, no fat that did not come out of a plant. They eat node-meal, pith-flour, the pale first shoots of the flue-cane, forty species of fungus grown in wadded fibre in the warm lower dark, fog-comb greens boiled twice, and resin sugars in small ceremonial quantities. Salt comes up in brine from wells sunk into the deep haulm, where something ancient laid it down. Their teeth are excellent, their bones are light, their eyes are extraordinary, and their sense of smell is poor to the point of clinical impairment, because there is very little on the Sill to smell — cold water, wax, hot bronze, wet fibre, and that is the list.

They sleep badly in steady light. The taps breathe on a twelve-second cycle and the heart learns it in infancy; a Sill-born person put in a room with an unwavering lamp will lie awake all night with the distinct sensation of being held under.

## The Offices

**Pacers.** Nine hundred of them, paid by the chain, walking the measured routes every eleventh day in the counting voice. They are the most trusted people on the plateau and the least often invited anywhere. Pacing locks the stride: after twenty years the length of a pacer's step is a legal instrument, and you can see the profession in a person crossing a room. Old pacers count in their sleep. Their households learn to sleep through it, and the number is a comfort — a wife will wake at two in the morning and hear four hundred and eleven, four hundred and twelve, and go back down.

The professional catastrophe is called *coming uncoupled*: the count continuing while the ground does not. A pacer who suspects it must sit down where she is, off the road, and not move, and not stand, until a relief pair reaches her and re-counts her back in from the nearest post. They carry a grey flag for this. It is the only object on the Sill whose entire purpose is to say *I do not know how far I have come.* Every pacer carries one and most never open it, and the ones who have opened it can be identified in a tavern in about a minute, because they do not talk about distance at all, ever, and everyone else does.

**Priors.** The Office of Prior Sound keeps thirty-one listeners in the empty stands, one to a floor, in silence, in shifts of six hours. The recruitment rule is arithmetic: the Office hires only commonly-named people, because you can pick your own name out of the fossil mush when you can pick out nothing else, and a Thrave or a Kellet or a Maun hearing her name has a fair chance of having heard about somebody. Warnings are logged, timed, and telegraphed by pressure-bell, and they are right about one time in six, and one time in six is worth thirty-one salaries.

The retirement rule is not arithmetic. A prior who hears her own name in a context she cannot parse is retired the same day, at full pension, and walked home by two colleagues who do not discuss it with her. The pensions are generous. They are also, in the aggregate, short, and the Office's actuaries have declined for two centuries to publish on this, and the Assize has declined to fund the question.

**Tappers.** They drive the thief-valves and read the cane by hand. A tapper lays a bare forearm on the cambium and tells you the pressure to within half an atmosphere, and can hear a valve going soft three floors up through the floor of a shop full of hammers. High sap is cold; a blown seat throws a jet of it at eighteen atmospheres and takes the flesh off in a stripe that heals glassy and hairless. Every tapper past thirty has the mark somewhere on the inside of the wrist and shows it the way other people show a wedding ring.

**Duplicate clerks.** Eleven of them, in pairs, cataloguing the four hundred recurrences: confirm the room, confirm the emptiness, record any object, re-seal, note the date. They are forbidden to enter a duplicate of any room they have themselves inhabited, and the Office therefore keeps a register of its own clerks' childhoods, bedroom by bedroom, which is the strangest document on the plateau and is very lightly guarded, because who would want it.

There is a clerk called Ossett who has been on the coats for nineteen years. He has logged sixty-two of them. He can tell you the difference between a coat the plant made from a coat still being worn and a coat the plant made from one worn out — the second kind has the wear in it, elbow-shine and all, seamless, in one piece, and the first kind is somehow worse. Eleven years ago, on the fourth floor of an empty cane in an unnamed district, he found one that was his father's, down to the mend at the pocket, which was a mend, in fibre, that had never been made by anyone. He entered it as *unidentified.* He has entered every one since as *unidentified.* The Office knows. The Office also knows that no clerk who has ever entered a coat as identified has stayed in the work more than a year, and the work has to be done.

## Stancers

When the Standing Crop comes up at the margin — a figure, faceless, unmistakable — somebody has to say who it is. The families mostly know. Sometimes they don't, and then they send for a stancer: an old woman, usually, occasionally an old man, whose trade is nothing but forty years of watching how people walk. They go out into the ranks with a stool. They sit. They wait for the wind, because the figures move slightly, and it is the movement that gives it up — the turned-in left foot, the hands behind the back, the shoulder that rode high after an accident in 'nineteen.

The trade is dying, and not for lack of apprentices. It is dying because more and more of the figures are of nobody living memory contains. The plateau has been learning people for eight thousand years and it does not only repeat the recently committed; it repeats the repetitions, the way it grows the bird again in ten years' time and may well fly it. So a stand of five thousand souls will produce, in a good year, forty figures its own people recognise and nine that nobody does — stances out of some earlier crowd, hands held in a fashion no one holds them now, a way of standing that belongs to a district whose name has gone smooth. These are logged as *general.* Children are told they are strangers. The word is accurate and does no harm.

Bel Nine, of the Fifth Stand, is eighty-four and is the best of them, and she has begun to recognise figures from her own girlhood, which is normal, and lately one or two whose stance she recognises without being able to place at all, which she says is like reading your own handwriting on a note you never wrote. She has told her daughters that when she goes into the haulm she would like it if nobody sent for a stancer. Her daughters have agreed and will not honour it.

## Children

There are about three hundred births a year on a plateau that once carried a hundred and fifty thousand people and now carries fifty. Nobody has a satisfying account of the decline. The Assize's position is that fewer people need fewer canes, which is not an account of anything.

The children are grave, extremely loud, and fearless about height in a way that visitors find physically unbearable to watch. They keep carriers in blossom season, in boxes, against instruction, and name them, and are ruined for a fortnight in the eleventh week when the thing lies down in the box and goes soft. Every adult on the Sill has had this happen and every adult tells their children not to, and this is understood on both sides to be the mechanism by which children on the Sill learn the local fact, which is that shape is cheap here and continuation is not.
