## Who You Will Not See First

They are the last thing you notice. You come out of the throat blind and weeping, and when your eyes come back you see the ground, and the ruins, and the pale flow of a herd two kilometres off, and only after some minutes do you understand that four people have been standing near you the whole time, watching to see whether you are going to fall down.

It is the shadowlessness. A human body in this light has no relief. The dust has powdered them the colour of the floor, the floor is the colour of the walls, and without a sun there is no modelling on a face, no dark under the brow, no crease beside the mouth — just a flat pale oval with two slits of blackened bone laid across it. A person standing still against a wall is very nearly not there. A person walking is a disturbance in the texture of the middle distance.

So they draw themselves. The soot is not ornament; it is the shadow the world refuses to provide. Two bars beneath the eyes, one down the length of the nose, a smudge under the lower lip, sometimes a line along the jaw — placed exactly where the light would have put them if there were light from anywhere. It is applied every morning with care and a little water, and the skill of it is a real skill; a badly sooted face reads as a smear, and a well-sooted one can be recognised across a hundred metres of flats. Children are taught it at about the age they are taught to walk on a bridge. An adult who goes out unsooted is either mourning, or ashamed, or has decided, for a while, to be nobody. Nobody is a recognised condition. You do not address a blank face, and it does not address you, and this is respected the way other peoples respect a closed door.

There is a small vanity industry in false shadow. A woman may deepen the line under her cheekbone to sharpen a face she thinks too soft. A man may paint himself a heavier brow. It is considered funny rather than dishonest, and there is an old joke, very old, worn as smooth as the friezes, about the man who sooted a shadow of a hat onto the ground beside him.

## Speech

Whispering is the ordinary register. Everything said is said at breath-distance, into the side of the head, with a hand cupped, and so the whole social world is conducted at the range of an embrace. There is no oratory here. There is no such thing as addressing a room. A decision that concerns forty people is made by forty consecutive whispers, which takes most of a day, and the shape of that process has become the shape of their politics: slow, lateral, unwitnessed, impossible to dominate.

For anything further than a whisper, they use the hands. The forearm language is the real one — older, richer, and the only language in which anybody says anything difficult. It runs from the inside of the wrist to the inner elbow, and it is read by the person being touched, not seen, so that two people can hold a full and furious argument while facing in opposite directions with their faces perfectly still. Bad news is always given this way. So are proposals of marriage. There is no equivalent at all for shouting, and consequently no equivalent for being overheard: a thing said on the arm has one recipient forever. Half their law is about what may be repeated.

They laugh with the glottis shut, which produces nothing but a rapid shaking of the chest and shoulders. A room of amused people is a room of people trembling. It looks, to a stranger, like a room of people in the early stages of freezing to death.

The walls are covered in writing. This is what happens when a population cannot talk at volume and has a floor it can score with a thumbnail: every flat surface at hand-height in the inhabited quarters is scratched over with notices, prices, directions, accusations, obscenities, and the names of people who were here. All of it is dissolving. A good insult lasts eight or nine years. There are walls where you can see three generations of graffiti at three depths of legibility, the oldest reduced to a faint rhythm of nothing, exactly like the friezes above them, and nobody finds the comparison instructive because it is simply what writing is: a thing that fades at a known rate.

Only mica is permanent. The archive of the city is a stack of scratched mica leaves in a dry room, and it is not large, and it contains no history to speak of. What it contains is measurements.

## The Listeners

The room is white, empty, and very bright, three metres by three, plastered smooth and repainted with lime slurry every few years so that nothing on the walls can catch the attention. There is a stool. There is a bowl under a drip, and when the bowl is full the shift is over; a shift is about four hours and no two shifts are the same length, which everyone accepts as the nature of time. There is a niche in the far wall, and hanging in the niche on a single human hair is a needle of clear selenite the length of a finger, and beneath the needle is a slate dusted with talc.

The Listener sits with eyes closed. When they open them, it is to look at the talc.

This is the whole of the office, and it is the most serious office they have, and it is given almost entirely to the old, because the old are the only ones who will sit still for it and because the old are no longer needed to carry anything. The difficulty is not boredom. The difficulty is that an old ear rings on its own. Every Listener, by the time they are appointed, has spent decades acquiring an exact and private knowledge of the sound inside their own head — its pitch, its variation, the way it rises when they turn too fast, the way it thickens before a bad night. They are being asked to distinguish that from a note at the very edge of hearing produced fifty metres above them by a stressed crystal the size of a district. Hence the needle, and the talc. The needle is cut to the pane's voice and nothing else. If the note is real, the talc walks.

A Listener who calls and is confirmed is fed for the rest of their life and spoken to with great gentleness and is, in a quiet way, finished; there is nothing further to do with such a person. A Listener who calls and is not confirmed — who empties a quarter in the dark hour, sends four hundred people across bone bridges at speed, and stands there afterwards with the talc unmoved — is retired the same day, and also fed for life, and is not spoken to at all. Both outcomes are pensions. The difference between them is a layer of dust on a piece of slate, and every Listener knows it, and that knowledge is the actual substance of the job.

There are perhaps twenty of them in the city at any time. They know each other. They do not compare what they hear, because comparing is contamination; it is the one professional rule.

The city's single unforgivable act is to imitate the note. Adolescents do it — a thin whistle between the teeth, in a deep avenue, for the pleasure of watching adults go rigid. It is the only thing they punish with expulsion. You are walked to a throat, or you are walked out to the dark, and which of the two you choose is regarded as saying everything about you.

## The Dark, and the First Years

An infant cannot be silenced.

This is the hinge of the whole society and they do not make anything of it; they simply do it. Women go out to bear. The birthing quarters are in the fallen districts, the dead ones, where a panel came down long ago and the shock crazed the crystal for a kilometre around and the light went out of the ceiling forever. Nothing more can fall there. It is the only place in the world where noise costs nothing.

So every one of them spends their first three or four years in the dark, being shouted at.

They are carried, mostly, by the ones who stayed — the herd people, who followed the walkers into the abandoned quarters generations ago and never came back in, and who are loud. They click their tongues to navigate. They call to each other across two hundred metres of ruined street in voices the city cannot imagine producing. They laugh out loud, a thing a citizen may witness two or three times in a life and will describe afterwards as obscene and as the most enviable thing they ever saw. They burn walker fat in little bone lamps and their eyes are useless in the pane-light, so that when they come in to trade they arrive squinting like the blind, wearing the visors backwards out of habit, with a pebble of selenite held in the mouth for the duration of the visit, which is the customary and sufficient guarantee.

And every citizen of the bright city carries, underneath everything, a set of memories from before memory: dark, warmth, a lamp, a voice at full volume, and the feeling of being held by someone very large who was not afraid of making a sound. They are brought in at three or four, sooted for the first time, and taught to laugh with their mouths shut. Some cry for a year. Some never entirely arrive, and go back out at twelve or fifteen, and their mothers are not surprised.

It is why the city is sad in the particular way it is sad. Not for the surface — none of them want the surface, the surface is a rumour with an unbearable sun in it. They are homesick for the dark.

## The Bridges

There is no soil and very little fuel, so the dead are given to the acid pools under the veils, which take a body down to bone in nine days and to nothing in a season. But the long bones are taken out first, at four days, by the bridge-wrights.

Every span between two dwellings in the living city is made of human femur, tibia, and fibula, lashed and pegged and continually replaced, and every span is named. You know whose bones you cross. Children learn the crossings of their quarter as a recitation — not of streets but of people, in order, out from their own door. A good bone lasts eleven or twelve years in the damp before it goes chalky and is retired, and there is a mild, universally understood competition about this which nobody will admit to: the family of a long-lasting bone is quietly pleased. Walker bone does everything else — ladders, tools, visors, needles, roof-beams, the frames of the mica books — but the crossings between houses are human, and always have been, and when you ask why, they look at you as if you have asked why a road should be on the ground.

## The Light-Cutters

With sound gone, everything that would have been music went into light.

A light-cutter takes clear selenite and shapes it into prisms, rods, lenses and blocks, and sets them through the stone so that brightness caught at the surface of a table-top is carried down eighty metres and delivered into a room as a soft warm lozenge on a wall. That is the trade. The art is what a master does with it: pieces cut so that the lozenge moves across the room through the day, slowly, at the pace of the surface sun; pieces that split into three and rejoin at the dusk hour; pieces that hold a faint pearl of light all night when everything else has gone grey, and go out exactly at the coldest hour, and are commissioned by the old.

A house's worth is measured in its light-work, inherited and fought over and carried away when the quarter dims. When the city moves — and it moves, every century or two, drifting across the chamber after the pane like something following a receding tide — what people carry is their prisms and their bones. Everything else is stone, and stone is free, and stone is already dissolving.

There are bad light-cutters, too, who sell flawed blocks that green the light or scatter it to nothing in a decade, and the dispute is the commonest dispute in the courts, and is conducted entirely in whispers and on the forearm, for days.

## The Readers, and the Number

Against the Listeners, who wait, stand the ceiling-readers, who predict.

They work the flats with polished mica mirrors and long sight and a great deal of memory, mapping the underside of the pane — the pendants, the pits, the blisters. A blister is thin stone with a void behind it, and a blister is where the soft-bodied hangers live, the pale things that drop on what passes beneath. So the animal is the omen. When hangers move into a district's ceiling, the readers mark it, and the mark means: a decade, perhaps two. Nobody kills a hanger. Parents tell children that it comes down first so that the sky will not come down on you by surprise, which is close enough to true.

And the readers keep the Number. It is in the mica archive, and it is a count of generations remaining to the pane, arrived at by measurement and re-measured every fifty years, and it has always been revised downward by more than the fifty years that passed. It is a large number. It is in the thousands. It is not an eschatology, and there is no cult of it, and nobody alters their life by so much as an hour on account of it — but every adult knows it, the way you know your own age, and knows that the people who carved the illegible friezes knew a bigger one.

## Taking the Shadow

Where a panel has come down, there is the hole, and through the hole comes the one bar of true sun in the world, leaning across the chamber, moving a white unbearable lozenge across the rubble through the day. It is the only shadow-caster. The rubble field around it is left unbuilt, always, everywhere it happens, and has been for ten thousand years.

People go there to die. Not all, not even most, but enough that there is a word for it and a customary way of asking. You are carried out across the flats — the flats are ruled all over with the drag-lines of walking sticks, months of them, everybody's paths crossing and slowly dissolving, which is how they find their way — and you are set down at the edge of the burning lozenge, and your visor is taken off.

You look at your own dark shape on the ground.

Nobody who has done it has been able to explain it afterwards on the arm, because the ones who do it are past explaining, and because the language has no vocabulary for the thing and has never needed one. They come back blistered and sun-struck and half-blind, and are fed, and are very quiet, and die within the season, and the family says the person went and took their shadow, and everyone nods, and no more is said about it in a world where nothing is said about anything above a breath.

The rest of them get up in the grey-pearl hour before the pane brightens, and soot their faces, and go out under a sky that weeps one drop at a time, forever, and listen.
