## Who is in it

Begin with the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the first thing that goes wrong. By weight, the Bell-Cellar is mostly bird. By count, it is mostly mite. By volume of body actually resident — never leaving, born here, dying here, all of it here — it is mostly bacteria and then crab. By any reckoning at all, the human beings who come in with wax in their ears are a rounding error, present perhaps four hundred hours a year out of eight thousand seven hundred and sixty, and they are nonetheless the only ones who think of themselves as belonging to it.

And by another reckoning, nobody lives here. The birds are commuters. The vine is a trespasser from the daylight. The thing in the pool comes up from a country nobody has mapped. The tenant sleeps somewhere else. The room's permanent population, the ones with no elsewhere at all, are flat, pale, coin-sized, and deaf, and there are perhaps two hundred thousand of them, and they hold the place the way plankton holds an ocean: absolutely, and without anyone counting them as inhabitants.

So: sort them by what they gave up to get in. That is the only census that makes sense here. Everything in the Bell-Cellar paid with an ear.

## The drummers

The floor is not a floor. It is a membrane of struck china, ten thousand years thick, and every animal that lives in the tiers lives by it.

The crabs are the citizens. They graze the biofilm off the rimstone in slow parallel lines, six or eight abreast, working a shelf clean and moving down, and they hold the world in the plate-hairs of their legs — not sound, exactly, but the arrival of sound in a solid, which is a different sense and a better one for this room. A crab six shelves away knows the weight of your heel before your heel has finished landing. When a wax-eared woman comes down onto the third tier the crabs part in a wave with an inch of clearance, close behind her, and never once look, because they have nothing much to look with and no light to do it in.

They are also a percussion society. The males drum. They lift the body on the leg-tips and strike the china with a claw-heel, four beats and a pause, and the china carries it — the whole floor carries it, the entire deposited body of the room is one continuous instrument, and a signal thrown on the second tier arrives at the weir with its shape intact. Within the loudest room on the mountain there is a second conversation going on in the stone, in perfect clarity, at all hours, entirely unheard. The newts are in it too: pale rose, palm-sized, breathing through skin so thin you can see the dark of the gut through it, they press the throat flat to the rimstone and pulse, and the pulse goes out through the lattice and finds another throat pressed to the lattice somewhere in the drowned angles. The worms feel the drumming and come up the grooves toward it. The blind fly with mica wings squats on the rim and turns its body to face whatever is being said.

Nothing here shouts. Everything here taps. The name of the place is a better name than the people who use it realise: it is a bell, and it is being rung continuously by ten thousand tiny hammers on the inside, and only the things in contact with it can hear a single note of that.

The crabs moult in the drowned angles, all together, in the fortnight after the wet season breaks, and for those two weeks the tiers are soft-bodied and defenceless and the mat is a slurry of shed shell, and this is the only time of the year when the room can be said to have a season. Afterwards the empty shells go down the water, over the weir, into the throat, and do not come back.

## The birds

Tens of thousands, sooty, small in the hand and mostly wing. They are the reason there is anything organic in the Bell-Cellar at all: the mat is theirs, the whole warm steaming mattress of it, the closest thing to soil above three thousand metres. They built the only earth in the room out of their own bodies over a span nobody can put a number to, and everything that lives in that earth is downstream of a bird's digestion.

They are stone deaf and they fly in the dark. The bristles fringing the gape read the moving air the way a fingertip reads the edge of a doorway, and a bird in flight here is running its whole face along the shape of the air pressure in the flutes, which is a shape as fixed and as knowable as a corridor. They learn it in the fledging fortnight. The ones who learn it badly go into the water, and then into the crabs, and then into the mat, which is to say back into the substance of the nest.

Their screaming is the top layer of the din and they cannot hear it. It is worth being clear about what that means. It is not that they scream and are indifferent to it; it is that whatever it is they are doing with their throats has no audience anywhere in the room, has had no audience for as long as there have been swiftlets in the flutes, and is done at full volume regardless, tens of thousands of times a minute, in the perfect confidence of a habit that has outlived its function. If you want to know what the Bell-Cellar is like, that is it: an enormous and beautifully maintained gesture with the meaning worn off it.

They are also instruments, and the families use them so. The traffic in the flutes has a pattern — lanes, altitudes, a slow clockwise drift under the shaft — and the pattern is a picture of the room's air. When the pattern frays, when birds begin to clip the flutes and each other, something has changed a hundred metres up at the vane, and the families know it before they know it in their own chests. Nobody watches the machine. They watch the birds watching the machine.

## The tenant

There is one warm-blooded animal in the Bell-Cellar that kept its hearing, and there is only ever one of it, and those two facts are the same fact.

Cat-sized. Long in the body, short in the leg, five true fingers on all four limbs with black pads gone smooth and shiny from the flutes. The eyes are enormous, tarsier eyes, absurd in a place with nothing to see, and they are not for the room; they are for the grey smear far up where the shaft turns, and for whatever it does up there in the half-light. The fur is dense, oily, permanently beaded. The face is unimpressive and rather kind. It comes down at intervals, works along the flutes hand over hand at the level of the nests, takes eggs and squabs with no hurry whatsoever — the birds cannot hear it coming and it knows this in its bones — and climbs out again with its cheeks full, and is gone for eight days, or thirty, or a season.

It lives, as near as anyone can tell, in a pocket in the shaft wall about forty metres up, and the reason it lives precisely there and nowhere else is that the shaft is a hundred-and-some-metre column of air holding one enormous low note, and a column holding a note has places along it where the note does not arrive. There is one such place big enough to hold a body. The families have never seen it and are entirely sure of it, because a great-uncle four generations back climbed the flutes with his ears unstopped, on a bet or a grief, and found the height where the pressure in his sternum simply stopped, a shelf of stillness in the roar, no wider than a coffin, and he came down and told them with his forehead against theirs and never went up again.
