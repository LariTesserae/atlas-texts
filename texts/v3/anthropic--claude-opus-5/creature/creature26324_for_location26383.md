## The Household

There are never fewer than eleven and never more than thirty. Eleven is the number below which the garden begins to win; thirty is the number above which the fig cannot feed them. Between those two figures the population drifts the way everything here drifts, without decision, and nobody has ever counted it as a policy.

Right now there are nineteen.

Four of them were born on the plate. Those four are the important ones, though nobody would put it that way — they came out deaf, and so were never at any point in their lives estranged from the place. They learned hands before they learned walking, because their mothers' hands were the first thing that ever answered them. The other fifteen arrived from below at various ages and each of them lost something on the way up, and they all know which thing they lost, and they can all tell you the year.

They do not call themselves an order. The word they use is the sign for *the ones on the ledges*, made with the left hand flat and the right hand walking two fingers along its edge, and it is also the sign for *shelf*, and also for *patience*, because a shelf is a thing that holds without effort for a very long time.

## Bodies

They are pale. Not sickly — the light through the crown is sufficient, it just isn't sun, and nothing in the drum has ever cast a hard shadow. Their skin is soft the way skin is soft when it has been in warm water an hour: fingertips permanently crinkled, palms white and thick, every crease of the body needing oil and getting it. They oil themselves twice a day out of habit as ancient as the tower and they think a dry-skinned newcomer looks obscene, like something skinned.

They wear very little and what they wear is felted plant fibre from the fourth band, which never dries, and doesn't need to.

Their heads nod. Newcomers notice it in others before they notice it in themselves: a fine involuntary bob at the fundamental, thirty-one times a second, too fast to see except in profile against a bright band. It arrives at about eight months and never leaves. Old tenders sleep in it. When a tender who has gone down to the plain lies down at night on solid earth, the nodding continues for years, in a body no longer being nodded, and this is what most of them find unbearable about being anywhere else.

Their teeth are worn at the front. This is professional.

Nobody here is fat except the rats, and nobody here is old in the way people are old below: they are cured rather than aged, dense, unhurried, with enormous hands and forearms like the pipe. They die of falls, and of the lungs, and of the thing that happens in the fifth decade when the sound has been in the chest that long. They are put out through the crown for the weather to take, because the drum is a closed loop and does not accept meat.

## Chalk and Hands

The rind of the shell is scale — kettle-scale, mineral, pale green where the copper wept. Scraped, it writes. So the building supplies the medium in which it is discussed, and every tender wears a gourd of its own wall on a wrist cord.

Chalk on the rail lasts perhaps a day before the damp takes it. This is understood as correct. Anything worth keeping goes onto lead sheet with a scriber, and there is not much lead, and so the standard for *worth keeping* is severe: node heights, the year's ration, what the bearing said. Three centuries of that fits in a locker the size of a coffin and one tender a year is permitted to add a single line.

The grammar of the hands is complete and it is not a translation of anything. It has a tense for *slowly, and continuing* that the plain's languages lack, and it does not have a general word for sound; it has nine words for regions of the pattern and a tenth for the pocket in the middle. There is a sign made by laying the back of the hand against the other's collarbone which means *feel this with me*, and it is used for the frogs, and for grief, and for nothing else.

Speech, in those who arrive with it, goes not because the throat fails but because there is no answer. Most stop within a year. One or two keep it longer, talking into the hundred and thirty decibels where not even they can hear it, and the household treats this the way you would treat a limp: without comment, and by walking slower.

## The Offices

**The Ration.** Once a year somebody climbs to the crown and cuts the vine back — not clear, never clear. Enough light for nine bands and not one leaf more, judged by eye against a lead template three hundred years old. Cut too little and the lowest band starves. Cut too much and the fourth band scalds and the swifts nest wrong. The tender who holds the Ration holds it for life and trains a successor for twenty years and the training is mostly refusal: *not yet, not yet, no.*

**Seeding the Scar.** The pattern slides down a hair a year and the garden follows it without help, dying along its upper lip, creeping at its lower. The tenders help anyway. They carry fern plates and vine cuttings a centimetre — one centimetre — below the current lower lip and press them into the rind, and most take, and the ones that take would have taken by themselves in four or five years. Every tender understands that this work is unnecessary. Every tender does it. It is the only labour in the tower performed purely as an argument that they belong here, and none of them would use that word for it either.

**The Bite.** Bolted to the turbine housing is a steel rod, worn concave at its free end by teeth. Four times a year one tender goes down, kneels, and closes their front teeth on the rod, and holds them there for as long as they can, and listens to the bearing through the bones of the face. That rasping four-forty is the only voice in the Stanchion that has anything new to say. It has been failing since before anyone's grandmother and it is failing still, and what the Bite is for is to know *how* — whether the story is the same story. Then up to the locker, one line, in lead: *unchanged*. Three hundred years of *unchanged*, with eleven exceptions, and every tender knows all eleven by heart the way children below know kings.

**The Clean Walk.** Every tender, once, walks the length of a sterile band. Five hours of bright bare blistered concrete with nothing on it, nothing coming, nothing possible, the air hammering a thousand times a heartbeat, swift cups every arm's length and no root, no spore, no green. It is not a punishment or a test. It is how you learn that there are places that simply refuse, and that they are not empty of meaning for refusing, and that the tower is made of both kinds of place in equal measure.

## The Minute

Everything the household cannot do while the Ox is running waits for autumn.

The valve cycles once a year. The fundamental drops a whole tone for something like sixty seconds; the pressure eases; the hollow flickers as every swift in the tower goes up at once. In that minute, work that has been rehearsed four hundred times gets done in the dark of nobody thinking: a bolt, a gasket, a length of channel, a rivet, the transfer of one line of lead. Teams of three, positions chalked on the floor, each hand in each other hand's way if either is early.

They train against a metronome which is the building.

Failure in the minute is the great fact of a life here. It is not punished; it does not need to be. It is why most of the chalk in the cage is apologies, and why the apologies are so rarely to the tower and so often to a named pair of hands that was waiting, at the right height, at the right instant, with the right thing, for someone who was not there.

## Neighbours

**The swifts** are thousands and they have forgotten how to call. In a place where a shout is nothing, voice went the way voice goes; they navigate the hollow by the nine plates of standing dust and by air on the feathers, in long slow arcs, and they sleep on the wing all night inside the sound, turning in the dark of a drum four hundred metres tall. The tenders read them the way sailors read birds. A stack in the wrong band means the pattern has slipped before the scars will show it. Nobody has taught anybody this; it is looked at, and known.

**The frogs** are the household's music. Translucent, in the drip channels, courting by drumming a foot against metal and reading the metal's answer, which means each frog is proposing to the entire building at once and getting a hundred replies from a hundred metres off. At night the tenders come to the rails and lay their forearms flat and hold on. That is what they have instead of song, and they have quite a lot of it, and it is not a poor substitute.

**The moths** are hand-wide, earless, and signal by pressing abdomens together in patterns; they land on people and are read as weather. One of them — small, brown, unremarkable — hatches only in the valve minute, keyed to the pressure and not to the tone, since it cannot hear the tone and never could. Its entire existence is a response to sixty seconds a year. The household considers it a colleague.

**The rats** are deaf, fat, and civil. They come to the hand because nothing has hunted them badly for three hundred years and they are killed with one squeeze at the base of the skull, out of sight of the others, and eaten with respect and without ceremony, and this is one of the harder facts of the place and nobody pretends it isn't.

## The One in the Second Band

In the fermenting pulp, knee-deep and warm, something has arranged the seed husks into a low spiral wall and repairs it.

It adds a course every few years. The spiral opens away from the drip. Nobody has seen it. Nobody has moved a husk. The household's position — never written down, since it isn't the sort of thing that goes on lead — is that the tower has a tenant of longer standing than themselves, and that the correct behaviour toward such a tenant is the behaviour you would want shown to you.

Once a year, on the day of the Ration, a fruit is left at the mouth of the spiral. It is always gone. Nothing has ever been left in return, except once, ninety years ago, when a husk was found on the fourth gallery rail, forty-five metres from any wall, placed.

That is in the locker. In lead. One line.

## What Actually Lives Here

Ask any of the nineteen and they will not answer, because it is not the kind of question their grammar is built to receive; but if you put it in chalk on the rail, and wait, you will get back something like this:

That the Ox is not alive and is nevertheless the only thing here doing anything.

That the pattern is what lives in the Stanchion. The nine jungles are its body, the swifts its quick nerve, the dust its posture, the frogs its idle noise, the vine the length of its reach; and the nineteen are its slowest organ, the part of it that worries, that keeps lead, that leaves fruit for a stranger, that trims its own light with a template older than any living hand.

That the bearing is its illness and its clock, and has been telling the same story for three hundred years, and will one day tell another.

That there is, chalked in the cage at the centre of the emptiness where a person can hear their own voice and generally regrets it, a plan for the green afternoon when the pattern finally goes and nine forests come down through the middle in one long silent fall. It has been read by everyone. It is one sign.

It means *sit down*.
