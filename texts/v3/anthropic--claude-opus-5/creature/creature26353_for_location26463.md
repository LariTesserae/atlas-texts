## The Four

The number is not a rule of the guild. It is an arithmetic of air.

The throat delivers, between rains, enough to keep four working bodies and one idle one alive and thinking clearly, and the idle one is timed. There is a sandglass bolted to the newel post at the foot of the stair, and it runs an hour, and when it runs out a visitor goes up whether or not the visit is finished. Five wardens would not suffocate. Five wardens would simply be slightly stupid, all the time, forever, and stupidity in a chamber of scalding iron and edged rock is a slow way of dying. So: four. There have been four for thirty-nine years. When one leaves, the town sends a child, and the child mostly does not stay.

---

You would not pick them out of a market crowd as miners, because they are not miners. Miners are thick. Wardens are thin the way a rope is thin — nothing spare, and the whole of it in tension.

The heat has taken forty years to make them and it has done thorough work. Their skin is pale and permanently softened, the way your fingertips go in a long bath, except everywhere and always; the wardens call it the pleat, and it is how a doctor above can identify a body from the Cut without asking. They do not tan and they do not burn. They sweat, but sweating here is not a cooling, only a joining — within an hour of coming down, you and the walls wear the same film, and the wardens have worn it since they were nineteen. They salt everything. There is a salt-box on every terrace, wooden, lidded, and a warden dips into it the way another man checks his pocket for a key.

Their hearts are large. This was measured once, above, by a physician curious about a warden's pulse, and reported to the guild in a letter, and the letter is still nailed inside the door of the market office because the town enjoys being told that its lamplight is made by people with big slow hearts.

Their hands are ruined in a particular way. Not calloused — calluses want dryness. A warden's hand is soft, and nicked, and re-nicked, every fracture face in the Cut having taken a little skin off it, and the nicks close slowly and imperfectly in the wet. In this the wardens match their chamber exactly: nothing here heals smooth. But those hands can lay a fingertip on the pile of gauge-moss and say *ninety-two, and falling since the second bell*, and be right. They are useless above. They cannot judge cloth. They cannot feel the difference between a penny and a button. They can tell you the temperature of a door handle across a room by the smell of the moss that isn't growing on it.

Their eyes are wide open all the time. Market day, for a warden, is a white agony conducted under a hat brim and smoked glass, and most of them go up at dawn or after dusk, and some of them stop going up.

---

**Halm** keeps the beam. She is sixty-three and has been down thirty-nine years, which she reckons in turns of the flywheel and not in years — four to the hour, ninety-six to the day, something over one million three hundred thousand of them since she first came down the spiral and put her hand flat on the crosshead and felt the world's most patient pressure move under it. She oils. She listens for the moment before a bearing begins to complain, which comes about a week before the complaint. She has replaced, piece by piece over four decades, every component of the Slow, so that nothing in it is original except the design and the pace, and she can give you the birth year of any bolt.

Her husband died above, in the winter of the long frost. She was at the vent-gate when the cart went over the square, and she knew it by its wheels — she knows all the carts by their wheels, weather that they are — and she stood with her hand on the gate-lever and let it pass overhead, and then opened the gate at the second bell because it was the second bell. She went up for the burial and came back down in the same day. She says the cold of that day is still in her somewhere and hasn't been driven out yet.

**Sennet** keeps the gate, which means he keeps the time, the bells, the rains, and the numbers. Paper does not survive down here; a page put in a pocket at the stair-head is pulp by the sump. So the ledger lives above, in the market office, and once a week Sennet climbs nine meters with the entire week inside his head — moss depths, cistern level, shrimp count, harvest weight, seam temperature to the tenth, the eleven-year fig calendar, the creep of the boilerfern line toward the throat — and recites it to a clerk who writes it down. He rehearses on the way up. If you are on the stair when he passes you will hear a man muttering figures in a flat register, and it will not sound like a man talking to himself; it will sound like a book being carried carefully.

He has a good voice and does not use it. Nobody down here uses a voice. Shouting costs air.

**Ovey** keeps the green. Grafts, prunes, moves the trellises a handspan every season to follow the heat, wedges pots into gaps that are not really gaps, whispers to cuttings — not out of sentiment, he says, but because whispering is what the air allows and he has to say something. He has not climbed the stair in nine years. It is not fear. It is that the surface hurts him: the light, the dry wind that split his lip open for a month, the way cold air feels going in, which he describes as swallowing a spoon. His wife and daughter come down to the stair-foot on the first of the month and the sandglass is turned and they have an hour. On the newel post beside the glass there is a ladder of small scratches where the girl's height has been marked, and they have climbed steadily past the level of her father's shoulder and are now above his eyes, and he has never once seen her outdoors.

**Pell** keeps the sump, and the sump end is cold. She works the flooded apse, the cistern, the channels, the empty glazed terraces where the grey moss grows. She is thirty-one and the only one of the four who can still go up in winter without suffering, and everybody understands what that means and nobody says it. When the last hot pipe drops below the boilerfern's line — the ledger puts it somewhere in the year eighty — it will be Pell, or whoever is Pell by then, who takes the wick-vine cuttings up the stair in a wet sack and goes to find wherever the ground has opened next. She is, without anyone having planned it, in training for the end of the place, by virtue of standing every day in the part of it that has already ended.

There is a fifth bunk, let into the terrace brick with the others. It is used perhaps four months in ten years. In the glaze above it, scratched with a nail, is a list of first names — thirty-one of them, going back to the beginning — each one an apprentice who came down and did not last a season. Nobody added the reason. The reasons are all the same reason.

---

They speak beneath the drip, which is a register more than a volume: a flat, breathy delivery, pitched under the irregular tick of condensation on plate steel, and it carries the length of the hall in the thick air the way a voice carries over water. Newcomers cannot hear it at all for the first day and then suddenly can, and then cannot un-hear it, and find the rest of their lives too loud.

For distance they use the pipes. The pipe-word has perhaps thirty terms — gate, sump, screw, cistern, come, stop, help, water, scorch, fig, hand me, how long — and it has no tense. You cannot say in the pipe-word that something happened. You can only say what is, and what to do. This suits them. The past is kept above, in the office, in the clerk's handwriting.

They eat what comes down the throat in a basket at the second bell and the fourth: hard biscuit, salt fish, pickles, onions. Bread molds in an hour here and is not sent. Everything is eaten immediately and standing up. Twice or three times in a career, a sulfur-fig comes ripe — eleven years from cutting to fruit, dated in the ledger, counted down — and the four of them carry it to the cold empty terraces at the sump end and eat it there, because you need cool to taste anything, and because a thing that has taken eleven years deserves to be eaten somewhere quiet. It tastes of iron and ash. They eat it slowly and say very little, and Sennet carries the date up on Sunday.

They sleep in two watches of four hours. Sleeping in the heat is work. Every one of them dreams of cold. Every one of them also dreams of the market square, which most of them have not walked across in years and which they know entirely by sound: they have each built, privately, out of cartwheels and boot-scuffs and the particular rumble of the fish stall's shutters, a square of their own, and no two are the same shape.

They are paid partly in light. Each warden's household above gets a lamp allowance, and it is generous, and so on any street in the town you can find the warden's house by looking for the brightest windows — burning the wick-vine that the warden pulped and dried and drew and belted, blazing away in a parlour where the warden is not.

---

There was a man called Corse, four wardens ago, who hurried. What he was hurrying for is not written down. He got nine steps, fainted, and came down open-handed on a fracture face, and the cut on his palm did not close for a year and one month in that wet. They took him up in the end. The story survives because it is the only lesson the Cut teaches that cannot be taught by touch, and it is told to every apprentice on the first day, at the sandglass, before they go down: *things here that hurry break.* Forced fruit rots on the vine. A man who runs faints in nine steps. Ungear the Slow and it would shake its own bolts out of the young rock inside a week.

---

The rest of the population.

The **pipe-moth** is palm-sized, dust-colored, and utterly uninterested in the lamps. It navigates by warmth. It goes to the hot iron the way its cousins above go to a candle, and clings there, wings flat, the exact shade of gauge-moss at eighty degrees, and this is not accident — it lays in moss of one particular pile, no thicker and no thinner. So the wardens read the moths as a second opinion. A moth laying where the moss says cool means the moss is lying, means a pipe is going hollow somewhere, means Halm walks that run with her palm out. Two moths a week, perhaps. They are counted.

The **shrimp** in the cistern are blind and colorless and were put there deliberately, in a barrel, in the second year, by the engineer who built the plumbing, on the principle that they die before men do. They have never yet had to. They keep the water sweet. They are counted with a scoop on Fridays and the count goes up the stair in Sennet's head.
