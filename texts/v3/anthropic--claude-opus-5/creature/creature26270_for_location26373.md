## What Lives There

Start with the figs.

The census is honest as far as it goes — forty crickets, the spiders, eleven eels, the wren that comes and gives up — but it leaves one line unclosed, and the line is this: *it fruits, small hard figs, and drops them, and they are gone by the next morning.* Crickets do not carry figs away. Eels do not climb out of the sump for fruit. The wren is an occasional and never stays. And nothing accumulates here, true, but the water's housekeeping is a slope: everything loose goes downhill to the sump. A fig dropped from the eastern shaft lands in thirty centimetres of saturated moss, in a basin, in the one part of the floor that does not drain quickly. It should sit there for a week, softening. It never sits there at all.

So: something eats about eleven figs a year, at night, and leaves no rind, no stalk, no seed-mess, nothing.

Now look for wear.

This is difficult, because wear is the whole aesthetic of the room; every surface is worn, and worn to completion. But there is one place where the wear is the wrong shape. On the west side, where the screw's iron rises through its housing at thirty degrees, there is a stretch of the housing collar — a palm's width, maybe twenty centimetres long, a metre and a half above the floor — that is polished brighter than water polishes. Water polishes evenly and leaves a slight tooth. This is smoother than that, and it is warm even by the standards of a warm room, and it is exactly the shape of something being leaned on.

That is the only mark it has made in seven hundred years, and it was not trying to make it.

---

You have already looked at it. Probably twice.

Everything in this room is a low dome. The bricks are low domes, the basins are low domes inverted, the moss stands in low domes where it has grown over something and the fig's fallen wood beneath has long gone. It sits among all that as a slightly larger low dome, forty centimetres across when folded, the colour of wet lime mortar with something of the fig leaf under it, and the surface of it does not gleam differently from the brick, because the same film of condensation stands on both. It has arrived, like the rust and the brickwork and the iron, at the shape the room permits, and the shape the room permits is *rounded, damp, and not interesting.*

Unfolded — and it unfolds rarely, and slowly, and mostly in the dark — it is about the length of a big dog and nothing like the weight. Four limbs, short, set wide, with long soft digits ending in pads. No claws; claws would be an edge. The pads are the only kind of grip that works on stone this polished, and they work superbly: it goes up the fluted walls the way water goes down them, in the channels, unhurried, with a slight sucking release at each step that you would never hear.

The head is barely a head. It is the front of the body, tapered, with a low mouth, two small pits above it, and eyes that have not been abandoned but have been reduced to the one job worth doing: knowing where the three pillars are. It can tell light from not-light and warm from warmer. That is the entire visual world and it has never needed another, because the three pillars land in the same three places every day, forever, and a creature that knows those three places knows the room.

It has no ears you could point to. It hears the way you heard at the doorway before you understood what you were hearing — in the sternum, in the jaw, in the backs of the knees. That is not a deficiency here. That is the correct organ for this room.

---

Its economy is almost comic in its smallness.

Twenty-four degrees, January and August. Total humidity. No predators, no rivals, no season, no need to move fast, no need to grow, no need to store. Strip a body of every one of those pressures and what remains is an animal that can live on eleven figs, a few dozen crickets, and the standing beadwork of the spiders' webs, which it takes by pressing its face flat into the sheet and drawing back — a drink and a meal at once, spider included, no malice in it.

It breathes about once every two minutes. Its throat flutters and stops. It is the exact temperature of the air, so if it touched you, you would not register touch; you would register pressure, the same pressure you felt in the doorway, and you would look for the sound that caused it.

It does not hunt so much as arrive. A cricket spaced three metres from the next cricket has no strategy against something that takes four hours to cross those three metres and does not care.

---

Here is the thing about the note.

You were told the sound never changes — not by a decibel, not by a semitone, not in seven hundred years — and over the ten minutes you can stand it, that is true. It is true over ten years. It is not true over seven centuries. Iron thins. The screw is fractionally lighter than it was, the culvert mouth fractionally wider, the flutes on the wall fractionally deeper, and the note the room makes has come down, in all that time, by something less than a tone.

It can hear that.

Not as change — nothing here experiences change — but as position. The note is where it is. The note was, at some point that is not a memory but is nonetheless somewhere in it, slightly elsewhere. This is the only clock it has, the only history it possesses, and it consists of one long, imperceptibly falling sound. Everything you would call an event in its life has happened inside that one note, which is why nothing in its life is an event.

This, incidentally, is why it leans on the screw housing. Not affection. Not curiosity. There, and only there, the note comes up through the iron into the body with a clarity it has nowhere else in the room, and a thing that lives inside a single sustained sound will find, and return to, the place where that sound is most itself. It goes there most nights. It has been going there since before the parish had a church.

---

It is missing the second and third digits of the left forefoot, and it has been missing them for a very long time.

The sump is twelve metres by five and black and it does not go in it now. Once it did — small, new, exploratory, in some decade nobody has a number for. The eels down there are thick as forearms and ancient and they go round and round in the churn with nothing at all to think about. One of them took two fingers and did not get the rest.

The stumps are healed to smooth domes. Of course they are. Everything here finishes.

---

Whether it is one animal or a sequence of them is a question the room refuses to answer, and refuses in a way that feels deliberate.

There has never been more than one. There has, on the available evidence, never been fewer. Nothing accumulates in this room: no bones, no shed skin, no cast-off, no grave. When one is done — and *done* is the right word, not *dying*; things here do not decay further, they simply reach the end of being improvable — it goes down the slope of the floor toward the sump, which is where every loose thing in this room has gone for seven hundred years, and the eels are there, and by morning the census is unchanged in every particular.

There would have to be a handover. There is no way to describe it that does not sound tender, so: they lie against each other in the flute nearest the eastern pillar, at the temperature of the air, for something between a week and a year, and then there is one again. No sound is involved. Sound here carries no information; the room deletes it. Everything they have ever had to say to one another has been pressure, weight, and the slow adjustment of position, which turns out to be sufficient for the extremely small number of things that need saying.

You would find this easy to understand. Two of you came down and were using your hands inside a minute.

---

When you are in the room, it is closer than you would like.

Not stalking. It has no framework for stalking; there is nothing in its world that runs. But you are the largest warm thing that has entered in perhaps a decade, you are a moving column of blood-heat in an environment where heat is architecture, and it will come and be near you the way it goes to the screw housing — because that is where the room is most itself just now.

It will stop about a metre and a half away, on the wall, in a flute, folded, at the same temperature as everything else, in a room with no edges and no shadows sharp enough to betray a curve. You will look at it. You will not resolve it. You will feel, instead, the thing that everyone who has sat here reports: that it became peaceful, and that you lost track of how long you stayed.

That is not the noise. The noise is only the reason you stopped listening. The peace is the standard, unmysterious animal peace of not being alone in a warm place, arriving in you by a route you have no name for, at a frequency you have no organ for, from something that has been perfectly still for six hours and could keep it up for six more.

Then you go up the fourteen dished steps, and out into the alders, and you are hoarse for no reason, and you tell whoever asks that there's nothing down there.

You are right. There is nothing down there. It has spent seven hundred years becoming exactly that, and it has succeeded, and it will be exactly this tomorrow.
