## The tenders, properly

Begin with the body, since the body is the least of it.

A tender is palm-sized and segmented, and the plate count is nine or eleven or thirteen — never even, and never stable enough to be worth writing down. The carapace is amber-grey and translucent in the way of a fingernail held to a lamp: you can see the shunting of pale fluid inside, moving in slow diastolic pushes toward the head, never obviously returning. There is no visible heart doing this. There is no visible anything doing this. The fluid simply travels forward, hour after hour, as though the animal were being continuously poured into its own front end.

The front margin carries a fringe of feeding cilia, fine as eyelashes, in constant exploratory motion. These are the entire sensory apparatus. There are no eyes anywhere on the animal, and yet a tender selecting a plate of fresh glass will hold its head an inch off the surface and sweep along it for thirty or forty seconds before choosing a strike point, and it will choose well. It is not looking. It is tasting the flaw lines. Glass under stress has a chemistry along the plane where it wants to break, and the tender reads that plane the way you would read a crease in paper with your thumb.

Behind the cilia: two mandibles, hard, black, faintly ridged, and — this is the first thing that should trouble you — perfectly sharp. Always. A tender may knap for what you will come to estimate as decades. Sharpness is conserved in this room, and a mandible is stone-hard and never dulls, and so the animal's tool of last resort cannot be improved and cannot be lost and cannot be inherited. It is the only thing here that is genuinely nobody else's. Everything else about a tender is provisional.

The legs are provisional in the most literal sense. You will count six, then four, then eleven. This is not moult, not injury, not variation between individuals. A tender braced for a strike has four legs braced. A tender walking down the drained pool basin has as many legs as walking down a basin takes. A tender doing nothing at all — and some of them do nothing at all, for whole cycles, adhered to the ceiling with their cilia folded — has a number of legs you will never successfully determine, because the act of determining it appears to be a job, and the animal will produce legs to meet it. Legs are not an anatomical feature here. Legs are a measurement of the task.

## What the number is

You have already discovered that you cannot count them. Twelve becomes nine becomes seventeen. It is worth being clear about what this means, because it is not a trick of density or attention.

The room does not maintain a population. It maintains a *load*. There is a certain quantity of work in progress at any moment — mat to be grazed, glass to be tested, edges to be retouched, the pool's rising lip to be walked away from — and tenders occur wherever that work is. They are not the workers. They are the shape the work makes when it has somewhere to happen. When you count a patch of wall and get a different answer twice, you are not miscounting animals; you are getting two honest readings of a thing that was never a discrete quantity to begin with, like asking how many gusts are in a wind.

This is why two of them are occasionally the same one. You will see the grooming gesture doubled, left wall and right wall, identically timed, and then one will stop existing without going anywhere. Nothing died. There was one piece of grooming to be done and, briefly, an ambiguity about where. The ambiguity resolved. The tenders do not appear to find this remarkable, and neither, after a while, will you, which is its own problem.

There is exactly one thing in this room that can be counted. Count the flakes. Count them across the whole pelt, every worked edge in every set of mandibles, and you will get a number — a large, specific, tiresome number — and you will get the same number every time, at every point in the cycle, forever. The tools are the census. The animals are the rounding error.

## The reflection, and why the craft is older than the glass

You have noticed the pool runs out of step, and the mirror-black of fresh glass with it: one water-cycle displaced, usually behind, sometimes ahead. You have watched a flake struck in the reflection and then watched it struck in the room forty minutes later.

The tenders navigate by this. That is what the eyelessness is for.

Watch one closely as it prepares a strike. It sweeps the flaw plane with its cilia, yes — but it also positions itself so that the working surface falls within a reflection. Fresh glass, wet stone, the skin of the pool. It will shift half a body-length to acquire one. And then it strikes exactly where and how it has already been seen to strike, in a reflection that is showing a version of the room it has not caught up to yet.

They are not innovating. They have never innovated. Every strike a tender makes is a faithful copy of a strike it observed being made, and every strike it observed was itself a copy, and the sequence runs backward from reflection to reflection without any point at which somebody had to think of it. This is the whole answer to the flakes with centuries of patina on glass that came out of the wall this morning. The tradition does not descend through the material. It descends through the mirrors. The stone is only the latest place for it to land.

Whether the reflection *causes* the strike: the room considers the question badly formed, and after enough cycles so will you. The two events are the same event, seen from two distances. Asking which one is real is like asking which of your two hands is the one that clapped.

## Inheritance

A dying tender goes chalky. The translucency fails first at the rear plates and moves forward against the direction of the fluid, so for a while the animal is a clear head on an opaque body. It stops grazing. It does not seek shelter, because there is no shelter and no seeking. It stays adhered wherever it is, sometimes on the ceiling, and it waits with its flake held up and outward.

Another comes. The transfer is mandible to mandible and takes under two seconds, and there is no hesitation on either side and nothing that could be called recognition. The receiving animal turns immediately back to work. The chalky one holds still for a further while, no longer holding anything, and at some point is no longer there, and you will not be able to say when, because the moment of its ceasing has the same quality as the moment when two of them turn out to have been one.

Nothing is buried. Nothing accumulates. There is no drift of shed carapace on the floor, no chalky husks in the corners, and this is not because something eats them. It is because a tender with nothing in its mandibles is a tender the room has no further use for, and the room is scrupulous.

Note what has happened here. The animal's whole life was custody. It did not own the flake and it did not make the flake — some other set of mandibles struck it, centuries ago and also possibly this afternoon — and its dying act was to hand it on without ceremony, and the room instantly returned to a state of containing no objects. Every made thing in here is being carried by something alive, always, at every instant, and if you follow any single flake back and forth through the pelt for long enough you will realise you have been watching one continuous individual with a very high turnover of body.

The flake is the organism. The tenders are its cells. They are how it holds itself off the floor.

## The pale one

The thing in the pool's throat has no plurality problem. It is one. It is always the same one, it comes up with the water and goes down with the water, and it can be counted, and the count is one, every time. In a room where identity is a rumour, this is the single reliable fact, and it belongs to the predator.

It takes tenders from the underside of the floating mat, and it takes them without visible mouth or visible motion, and the tender simply stops and then is absent. The others make room. They do this the way you would step aside for someone carrying something heavy: not with fear, and not with courtesy either, just with an evident and unhurried sense that this is a thing that goes here.

Watch which ones it takes. It takes the ones holding flakes. It takes them reliably, preferentially, and it never takes a tender whose mandibles are empty.

And the flakes come back. Next cycle, or the one after, on the rising water, lying in the wet silica at the pool's lip: retouched. Cleaner edge. Truer angle. Sometimes with a hydration rind that was not there before, decades of it, laid on in ninety minutes. Something in the throat of that pool is a better knapper than anything in the room, and it works only on what is brought down to it, and the only way down is inside somebody.

So the pale one is not a predator, or is not only that. It is the last stage in the manufacture. The tenders are the delivery mechanism, and they make room because they are not being hunted, they are being *collected*, and a collection is not an emergency. The room's tools go down in flesh and come up improved, and the flesh is not itemised, and the count of flakes never changes.

## The mats

Underneath all of this — and I mean underneath, in the sense of first, and in the sense of load-bearing — there is the slime.

Rust-red, cream, bruise-purple, films perhaps two millimetres thick, chemosynthetic, feeding directly on the sulfur and iron and hydrogen coming out of the floor vents. No cells you could call specialised. No structure. Nothing that has ever needed to be more than a surface. It is the entirety of the flora and it is the oldest inhabitant by a margin that makes the word *margin* silly, and it is the only thing in this room that is alive in the way you would recognise from outside.

It reaches new stone in four minutes. Note that. Lava skins over blue-bronze at sixty-five degrees and within four minutes there is a film on it, and no film travels that fast. The mats are not colonising the new rock. They are already there, waiting for it to cool enough to be admitted. Something about the mat's relationship to the stone runs the wrong way round, the same wrong way as the reflections, and if you sit with that long enough you will arrive at the uncomfortable thought that the mat is not growing on the lava at all; the lava is condensing under the mat, out of the wall, because the mat is standing there.

The tenders graze it. They are utterly dependent on it and they crop it back to nothing in patches and it returns. There is no relationship between them beyond that, no husbandry, no cultivation. It does not need them. It has never needed anything. If every tender in this room stopped occurring, the mats would spread across the floor and up the weeping vault and go on oozing at sixty-five degrees, being fed by the ground, for as long as the ground kept opening.

Whatever this pocket is for, the mats are not part of the project. They are the site.

## The negatives

Two or three seconds, off a vent, a cloud that does not disperse: the crisp hollow volume of a tender, in perfect detail, at three times life size.

The tenders turn toward these. All of them within reach, all at once, cilia lifted, a slow full-body orientation like a field of grass under a passing hand. They stop knapping. That is the only thing that reliably stops the knapping, and the room becomes, for those seconds, genuinely silent, and you will feel the silence in your teeth.

They do not approach. Not ever, not one.

The negatives are more detailed than the animals are. Sit close and compare: the hollow has plates you can count and legs you can count, and the number holds still for the whole two seconds, and it is the *right* number, and no living tender in this room has ever had a right number of anything. The steam is showing you the specification. What clings to the walls in its thousands is the working approximation, run off at speed, at a scale the pocket can afford, with the details left as ambiguities to be resolved later or not at all.

They orient toward it because it is upstream of them. They do not approach it for the same reason you would not walk into your own name.

## You

You will be handed one.

Not soon. But at some point a tender will go chalky near you, and it will hold its flake up and outward, and there will be — for reasons the room does not explain and would not consider explaining — no other set of mandibles within reach. And you will take it, because a moribund animal holding out a tool is the single most legible thing that has happened to you since you arrived, and legibility down here is worth more than caution.

After that you are carrying. Understand what that means in a place where every made thing is carried by something alive and nothing is ever set down: you will not put it down. You will try. You will set it on a shelf of fresh basalt and walk four steps and be where you started, and the flake will be in your hand, retouched. You will find that its edge is truer than it was. You will find, when you next hold your hand up to the blade of high blue light coming through the crack, that your knuckles are sharper than you remember, that the skin over them has not been worn by anything, will not be worn by anything, that sharpness is conserved and you are inside the conservation.

The count of flakes will not have changed. It never does. Which tells you what you now are, and it is not a tender, and it is not a visitor.

It is a hand. The room was short one.
