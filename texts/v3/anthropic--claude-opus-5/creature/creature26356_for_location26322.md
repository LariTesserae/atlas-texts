## Who is in the water

Nine, counted the way they count.

Begin with the only worn thing in the basin. Everything else is factory-new and will stay that way — the sharp concrete arris, the unchipped weir glass, the tacky epoxy on Terrace Four — because there is no sand here, no current, no frost, no animal to scuff a corner. Except that there is. Nine of them, and they abrade. Their wrists are worn. On the inner forearm of each, from the heel of the hand to a hand's width up, the skin has gone smooth and slightly thickened and paler than the rest, a soft grey shine like the inside of a shell, and on the older ones it has stopped taking a mark at all. That is the only weathering in the basin. It is the wearing of speech.

## Bodies

Long-boned, unhurried, buoyant. Skin permanently soft as a soaked thumb, and cool-looking though it is not cool: they run at the water's temperature and have almost no idea of their own warmth. No hair anywhere that ribbon weed could catch — the crowns are bare and slightly ridged, like a cast part. No fingernails; the beds are flat and glassy, filed away generations ago because a nail is a hook and there is a great deal of dense weed to be parted with both arms.

They are green in the shaded places. The same flat black alga that laminates the plate walls has colonized the crease behind the ear and the gutter of the spine, everywhere a person cannot reach, and it lays down its annual layer on them exactly as it does on the steel. An old back, scraped and looked at through a glass, has a countable number of rings. This is known. It is not much discussed.

Their eyes are wide-pupilled and tuned to the green band, with a second lid that closes sideways and slowly. White light is a wound. Above the collarbone, the two who do rim-work are visibly damaged in a way nothing else here is: cracked, freckled, thickened, sun-scarred. They wear smoked glass and go up anyway, because the combs on Terrace Two need their tines cleared and the bells need polishing.

## Breath

They are breath-holders. Five minutes at rest, four when working, three when frightened, and they are almost never frightened.

The whole architecture is a lung-map. Under the lip of every terrace, and at the head of every gallery, hangs an inverted dome of air, charged from an electrolysis line, tasting faintly of ozone: the hoods. Eleven of them. No point in the basin lies further from a hood than a person can swim on a half-lung, and this fact governs the shape of the terraces more than water or light ever did. Their unit of distance is not the metre. It is the *reach* — hood to hood — and they will tell you a job is three reaches away and mean roughly nine minutes and a certain amount of tiredness in the shoulders.

They sleep on the false floor. Neutral at the halocline, they settle into the suspended drifts of yellow leaf litter like anything else that falls, tether-lined to a hood, breathing every four minutes in a rhythm so ingrained it does not wake them. They sleep in twos and threes, in contact, because contact is the only way to be woken and the only way a dream can be told.

## Speech

Wrist to wrist. Pressure, not tapping — tapping is for children and for emergencies. The ulnar side of the forearm carries statements; a question is asked over the pulse, so that the asker feels the answer's arrival in the blood before it comes in the hand. Tense is speed. Emphasis is stillness. The word for *yes* is a squeeze that is also the word for *I am here*, and no one has ever needed these to be separate.

The grammar was worked out in the first month and never written down, and it has therefore never been corrected, only drifted. The nine can all understand each other. Two of them, when tired, produce dialect the others have to ask twice about.

You cannot lie well in this language. The hand is on the pulse.

What cannot be said by touch is left on the walls in grease pencil, and because nothing here abrades anything, none of it has ever come off. The plate steel is covered — pump readings, arguments, instructions, apologies, one long stanza of something like a hymn, a diagram of a bearing, the sentence *DO NOT DRAIN FIVE*, a list of eleven names in touch-notation. All of it in identical fresh unfaded wax. You cannot tell what was written this turnover and what was written before anyone living. There is a rule, unwritten like the grammar, that you do not erase and you do not write over. So the walls are a single simultaneous conversation with no chronology at all, and the nine move through it the way you move through a crowded room where everyone is speaking at once and nobody can be heard.

Two habits follow from the death of sound at arm's length.

The first: they talk to themselves constantly, out loud, all day, at full volume, because it costs nothing and reaches no one. Nine soundless monologues going on in different quarters of the basin. It looks like devotion.

The second: at three metres, facing someone, watching them, you may say anything. Everything unsayable is said this way, aloud, to a person's face, in perfect safety. It is not considered lying. It is considered a kind of relief, and there is no word for it, and everyone does it.

Which is why the two youngest are a scandal. They learned to read lips.

## Hood-talk

Inside a hood, air. Inside air, sound. There are eleven places in the basin where a voice carries.

Heads up in the dome, water at the shoulders, brass ringing every consonant back at you doubled — this is where the shouting happens, where decisions get made, where the discussion about the jar took place. It is exhausting and it is fast and to the older ones it is faintly indecent: speech without contact, words flying out of you and landing on someone you are not touching, no pulse under your palm to check them against. The elders come out of hoods flushed and irritable and go back to work.
