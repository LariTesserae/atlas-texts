## Who Lives Under the Lid

### You will not see them first

You will hear a tap.

They dress in cane — in the silvered cuticle peeled from mature stems in long sheets and sewn with cane needle and root-thread, worn over three or four layers of felted mat that has been beaten soft and stinks pleasantly of the floor. The result is a person-shaped mirror. Against the glare of the plain a Shade-Keeper is not camouflaged so much as *cancelled*: one more bright flat nothing in a world of bright flat nothings. Two of them can pass within forty metres and never know.

So they do not look for each other. They listen, and they smell, and they tap. The long probing staff serves three purposes: it tests whether the white ahead is floor or water; it takes the weight of a stooped body; and its butt struck twice on the mat makes a soft dead *thud* that travels through the fibre for a surprising distance and is answered, if anyone is out there, by three.

Two is *I am here*. Three is *so am I, come.* One, repeated slowly and without stopping, is *I have found a rift, and I am telling the floor about it so that the floor will remember.*

### The body

Begin with the eye, because everything about them is a consequence of the eye.

The pupil is a pinhole and stays a pinhole. The brow is heavy, the upper lid thickened and slightly hooded, the lashes long and coarse and grown down rather than out. The muscles that pull the face into a squint are the strongest muscles they have; the squint is not an expression but a resting state, and a child of four already has the fine white creases at the temple that we would call the marks of a long life. Under the slit-mask the skin around the eyes is paler than the rest, a mask beneath the mask, and it is considered indecent to be seen with that pallor uncovered by anyone who has not been in the dark with you.

They burn in strange places. The light comes from everywhere and the mirror-canes throw it up as generously as the Lid sends it down, so they scorch not on the crown and shoulders but underneath — beneath the chin, behind the knee, the inside of the wrist, the soft skin under the upper arm. They keep their chins tucked. They do not raise their arms above the head if they can avoid it. And they laugh with the mouth closed, a low hum through the nose with the shoulders going, because the roof of an open mouth blisters in an afternoon. Visitors from anywhere else find their humour unnervingly silent and are usually convinced they are being mocked.

The neck bends forward and stays bent. By fifty a Shade-Keeper walks looking at their own feet, which is where the useful world is anyway; by seventy the spine has made the posture permanent, and they say of such a person that *the floor has finished asking.*

Most of them go blind. Not all, and not always fully, but the glare works steadily and the whitening usually arrives somewhere in the fifth or sixth decade. It is not a catastrophe. Out on the plain, sight was never much use — no shadow means no form, no form means no distance, and the eye was only ever a device for detecting whether you were about to walk into something bright. What matters outside is the staff, the grain of the mat under your soles, the drip-song of cane-masses, the pressure of a thermal column against the cheek. The blind have simply stopped being distracted. The best guides in the underlight are all blind, and a family that can afford to be led by a blind elder considers itself fortunate. It is the young, with their intact and useless eyes, who get roped in the middle.

### The smell of a person

There is no fire. Nothing here is dry enough to burn, and nobody living has seen a flame; the word survives only in a few old phrases, the way we keep *sunwise* without thinking.

Warmth comes from the floor. They cut down into the hot fermenting mat and line the cut with matting and lie in it, sometimes four or five together, and the deep rot works around them at the temperature of a slow fever. Every Shade-Keeper therefore carries the smell of the pit they sleep in — sour, sweet, yeasty, faintly meaty, particular. Households smell alike. Kinship is not primarily a matter of blood but of pit: *we have lain in the same heat* is the phrase, and it covers husbands, foster-children, and the stranger who wintered with you and left. To marry is to change your smell. Girls leaving home are warned that they will lose it and mourn it, and they do.

They can identify each other at fifteen paces in fog with their eyes shut, and consider our reliance on faces both crude and slightly obscene.

### Heat, and the dead

The pit ferments because things rot in it. This is not a metaphor.

They bury their dead in the sleeping floor. A body goes down under the matting at the foot of the pit, wrapped in cane-sheet, and for two or three years it gives out heat, and the household sleeps on it and is warmed by it. This is the whole of their funerary practice and the whole of their idea of an afterlife: *you keep the family warm until you are finished.* An old woman dying in a good season will say she is glad, because the cold has been bad and she is about to be useful.

A pit whose dead are exhausted goes cool, and a cool pit is abandoned. This, more than any other thing, is what moves them across the plain — not hunger, not the collapses, but the gradual failure of the warmth beneath the bed. They travel to bury someone new.

This is also why the walkers come.

A walker is blind, two metres at the shoulder, silver-furred, and navigates by fermentation heat, which means that on the rare occasions one crosses a camp it is not curious about the people at all. It wants the pit. It will dig at a grave with the calm persistence of weather, and it cannot be frightened, because nothing in its lineage has ever needed to be frightened, and it will step through a standing human being as through a stand of reeds. There is no defending the dead from it. So they stand aside, and they let it, and afterward they gather what is left and re-bury it deeper, and the proverb is: *the walker takes the heat, not the person.* It is the closest thing they have to a doctrine of consolation.

### What they eat

Everything, and not much.

The green seams under the mirror-cuticle, scraped out in ribbons — sweetest in a stem four days old, which is a stem thirty metres tall, so the cane-cutting is done at height on cane ladders lashed to a cane frame. The flat pale grazers of the root-mat, which are slow and stupid and taste of the floor. Mirror-gnat brood, dug from the colony galleries by the double handful and eaten as a warm paste; the adults are inedible and get into the mask-slits and are the single most complained-about thing in the underlight. And, best of all, the ribbon-things from the black water beneath the rifts, hooked on cane barbs on long lines, hauled up under a canopy of matting because a photon kills them instantly and spoils the meat — brought in blind, killed in the dark, eaten in the dark, so that the finest food they have is a thing nobody has ever seen.

There is no cooking, only rotting. Meat and cane-pith are buried in the hot mat at measured depths for measured times: three days at a hand's depth, twenty at a forearm's, and there are pits kept going for a generation whose contents are traded across a hundred kilometres by people who have never met the family that maintains them. Water comes off the mirror-leaves under a dripping ceiling, collected in cane troughs, and tastes of nothing at all, which they consider a great luxury.

### The dark room

The house is not a shelter. Cold is a mild problem here; the mat handles it. The house is a machine for making a shadow.

Seven and eight layers of woven cane matting, sealed with root-pulp, raised into a squat drum with a crooked entry passage that turns twice so that no glare can follow. It is not black inside. That is the thing outsiders get wrong. It is *dim* — and dimness is the rarest substance in this world, because dimness has a *direction*. High on one wall, at the end of a short bored duct, there is a hole the width of a thumb. It is called the finger. It admits one narrow beam of the general dazzle, and that beam is the only directed light any of them will ever encounter, and it does what light has never done anywhere else in their lives: it comes from *somewhere*, and it makes edges, and it makes shadows.

So the dark room is where a face exists. A dark-adapted eye, wide open for the first time in a working day, resolves a cheekbone, a scar, the shape of a mouth. It follows that visual knowledge of another person is intimate by definition — you have seen the faces of your pit-kin, your lovers, your children, the two or three travellers who came in out of the light and stayed the night, and no one else's, ever. Everyone has two names. The outside-name is loud, blunt, made of open vowels for calling across glare; the inside-name is spoken only in a dark room by someone who has seen your features, and to use it wrongly is a serious offence. *Wide-eyed speech* means candour. *Masked speech* means the courteous lying done outdoors, where nobody can see what anybody means.

Their only visual art is shadow. Hands in the beam of the finger, and the animals of the black water swimming across the matting. Carved cane figures judged not by their form but by the profile they throw, so that a masterwork is often an ugly lopsided thing in the hand and a face of terrible sweetness on the wall. They have no portraits, no pictures, no idols. They have silhouettes, and they take them very seriously.
