## What Lives on the Sill

The goats do not live here. Neither do the birds, nor the midges, nor the sedge in the deep hush. Those are furniture. They are the wrong shape for life in this place: they have counts that never change, postures that never change, a broken primary feather that is never moulted and never repaired. Nothing that lives arranges itself like that. Something else arranged them.

What lives here is the thing that is holding them.

I am not going to give it a name. A name is a change made in the air, and the air here is expensive, and I would rather not add to what is owed. Where I have to call it something I call it the holder, which is a job and not a name.

---

**Where it is.**

It is in the shadows. The two hundred.

You will already have noticed that they are wrong: sharp-edged in a place with no direction to its light, lying north-northeast in perfect agreement of bearing and perfect disagreement of length. The lengths disagree the way your paces disagree — not by error, by a consistent refusal of arithmetic. A shadow four feet long lies beside a shadow nineteen feet long, cast by stones of the same height, and if you measure them again tomorrow you will get the same two figures.

They are not cast by the stones. They are cast *through* the stones, by an illumination that is not on the Sill, from a body that is not stone-shaped, and each of the two hundred is a place where that body's outline happens to intersect this ground. Take the bearings of all of them and extend the lines. They do not meet at the Throat and they do not run off the plateau. Because you re-enter the Sill from the far side if you go far enough in any direction, every line here eventually comes back; and these two hundred lines come back and cross, all of them, everywhere, over the whole nine miles.

Which is to say: the holder is looking at what it holds. All two hundred of it, at once, at everything. It has not looked away in the entire time there has been anything here to look at.

Put your hand into one, as you were told, and note the cold that stays. That is not the shadow chilling your hand. That is your hand having been read and entered — copied, at that temperature, into whatever keeps the temperatures of things. Your hand goes on being that cold somewhere for a while, and while it is, your actual hand feels the difference.

---

**What it does.**

Take the roar off the list of natural features. It is not the sound of the ground draining and it is not weather and it is not, as I first wrote, the sound of an accounting failing. It is the sound of the accounting being *kept*. It is labour. It is one long unbroken effort, and it is at exactly that volume because that is the volume required, and it does not vary because the requirement does not vary, and it comes from underfoot because the work is being done everywhere at once, including under you.

The evidence is all in the finishing. Rime four inches long on every windward surface across nine miles: not a length that ice arrives at, a length that ice is *permitted*. The drip that no longer builds its mineral cone but maintains it. The river that cut its bed and then stopped cutting, with lichen crust unbroken to the waterline. The fox-sized thing beside the water with its eye still clear. None of that is stasis. Stasis is free. All of that is somebody's arm out straight, holding, and not putting it down.

There is one thing it cannot hold, and that is the Throat.

Something goes out there and will not be stopped. The holder has no purchase on it — no shadow falls within forty yards of the lip, and I take that to be not reverence but the way you keep your hands off a wound. Everything else the Sill does is compensation for that hole. The air is not replenished because the holder is holding the quantity of air at the number it has, against a leak. The river has no source because the holder starts it, in the gravel, at the width and pace it can afford, and gives the hole water in the hope of sparing something less renewable. I do not know whether this works. It has been tried for a very long time.

---

**What it costs.**

Cheap: distance. Empty gravel, the space between landmarks, the count of your paces. It has let all that go slack, which is why your two figures never match. Trust the stones. The stones are where the effort has gone.

Also cheap: anything already finished. A completed form costs almost nothing to maintain — that is the whole physics of this place. The cone under the drip is finished, so the drip only has to keep it. The cairn is finished, so no stone falls. The broken feather is finished being broken, so it is held broken, forever, and that is thrift and not cruelty.

Expensive: anything unfinished. A gap in the rime where you snapped a blade is an absence in a shape, and an absence in a shape is not a form, it is an open account. It has to be held there. It will be held there in a thousand years. You did not damage anything; you assigned somebody a task with no end.

Most expensive of all: surprise.

This is the thing I was slowest to understand. The holder has organised its entire existence so as never to be surprised, because surprise is a change arriving from outside the ledger, and it has no margin. Hence a life of two hundred fixed regards and eight motionless birds. Hence the echo.

---

**Its voice.**

You get your own timbre back because it has none left. A voice is a disturbance of air maintained over time. It gave that up, the way a person carrying something heavy stops talking.

It comes half a beat early because it is running ahead of you. It is not prophecy and it is not mockery. It is a creature that cannot afford to be startled, listening so hard, for so long, that it has learned to have already heard.

Now: sometimes it is wrong. Stay past your fourth day, past the point where the echo has stopped unnerving you, and you will begin to catch the misses. A word you were not going to say. A phrase arriving late — a whole half-second late, dragging, in a voice fractionally not yours. This happens perhaps twice in a season, and it is the only event on the Sill. It is the holder flinching. Note the day. There will not be another.

I have wondered what it hopes to hear early. I have a guess, further down, and I do not like it.

---

A mile up from the fissure there is a fox-sized animal lying on its side, fur perfect, eye clear, finished.
