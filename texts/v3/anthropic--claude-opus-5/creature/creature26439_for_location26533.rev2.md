## The ones with the string

You will see the first one before you are sure you are seeing a person. A dark mark on grey-blue rock, kneeling, not moving. You will watch for a while, decide it is a boulder, look away, look back, and the mark will be twenty paces further along and still not moving.

They are small people, most of them, and the ones who were not small when they came have gone that way. Thick through the chest and narrow everywhere else. Their hands are the largest thing about them: broad palms, splayed short fingers, the pads polished smooth and pale from a lifetime of laying flat against wet stone to feel for fall, the thumbnails ridged in bands from decades of being struck a little too often. They read rock with their palms the way you read a page with your eyes, and they do it constantly, distractedly, the way other people scratch. A gutterwright standing and talking to you will keep one hand on a wall, and will be receiving information from it, and will not know they are doing it.

Their knees are ruined by forty. They kneel on a folded pad of moss, in water, eight hours a day, on rock that is glass and eggshell by turns. They walk with a short careful tread, toes down first, and they walk *the same route* — not from superstition but from the identical logic that governs everything here: a footfall on unbroken bubble-crust is a decision, and a path once broken cannot be unbroken, so the town is threaded with worn human first-lines exactly as the stone is threaded with water's. You can tell how long someone has lived here by whether they can cross a bulge in the dark. The old ones can cross the whole dome in the dark. They mostly prefer it.

The ears matter most. A gutterwright hears the town the way a shepherd sees a flock. There are eleven hundred voices of falling water up here and a Welter knows them separately: knows that the fourth comb on the west shoulder is hissing a quarter-tone flat, which means moss, which means slowing, which means spreading, which means go now. They hear through the roar. They can hold a conversation across two hundred paces of chattering weir by pitching the voice into the one frequency the water has left empty, which gives their speech a strange flat carrying edge, like a bird's, and which they cannot drop when they go below. In the lowland villages they are thought rude, or half-deaf, or holy, depending.

They are damp. Always. Their clothing is felted fibre soaked in rendered fat, layered, with waxed cloth over the shoulders, and it smells of fat and moss and cold smoke, and so do they. Their hair is cropped because hair holds water and water on the skull at four thousand paces is how people die. Their feet go in moss-stuffed hide sheaths which are remade every eleven days and are the single most laborious object in their economy.

They cough. By fifty most of them cough.

## Twenty-two

They do not consider twenty-two a small number. They consider it the number that fits.

The limit is not the rock and not the work and not the two thousand empty beds. The limit is the stair. Everything eaten up here is carried up here, three days of it, on a back, eleven times a year, and the arithmetic of grain and fat against the arithmetic of legs comes out at twenty-two, twenty-four in a good decade, nineteen in a bad one. The frontier of the Welt is not the raw lobe on the east flank. It is the carry. They know this perfectly well and it is the only thing that genuinely embarrasses them: that a work measured in millennia is throttled by a sack.

Of the twenty-two, at any time: five or six are *drawers*, who hold the plan; nine or ten are cutters, comb-scrapers, wall-layers, greasers of gates, the hands of the thing; two are carriers, who spend half their lives on the stair and are the only people here with lowland faces; one is the Bowl, who is the oldest surveyor and whose sole remaining work is to walk slowly with a vessel of water and a thread and tell the others the truth about level; one is the Contrary.

The Contrary's job is to be unconvinced. Any plan must survive them. They are chosen young, for stubbornness and for a particular cast of imagination — the ability to look at a fair channel and see, vividly, the flood of the year 900 slopping over its lip at the one place where the lip is a hair low. They are not liked, exactly. They are fed first. When a Contrary finally withdraws an objection it is a formal act, performed in the bathhouse, and the withdrawal is cut into the Table along with the plan, because the record must show not only what was decided but who stopped fighting it and when.

Some you would remember. Ada of the Sixth Comb, sixty-one, mostly blind now, who works entirely by hand and ear and is more accurate than the sighted. Kel, who came up at nineteen from a river town and has been here thirty years and is still spoken of as the new one. The child Miru, four, whose first word was *seven*.

They all have a given name and a line-name. The line-name is the channel you cut and proved; it is what you actually are; it is what survives you by a very long way. A person without a line-name is not a lesser person, but they are, in the grammar of the place, an incomplete sentence.

## Nine years to draw a thread

The problem of the Welt is that you cannot test anything with water.

So they test with everything else. Charcoal dust poured along a proposed run and watched as it drifts. Feathers on strings to read the wind that will push a thin sheet sideways in November. Fine dry sand, which lies where water would lie and tells you nothing about speed and everything about hesitation. The bowl and thread, over and over, nine paces at a time, a hair at a time.

And then, at the end, the skins: a whole proposed channel — eleven hundred paces of it, cut, walled, weired, combed — is lined throughout with waxed cloth and greased hide, every seam lapped downstream, and *then* water is run through it. For a season. For three seasons. Water running the exact line, at the exact fall, through a sleeve, never once touching the stone beneath. They walk it in rain and in thaw. They open the gate wide and let the sleeve carry a flood and watch where the flood wants to go, and adjust, and re-skin, and do it again.

This is the single most characteristic thing about them: they have built a culture whose central technology is *rehearsal*.

The current plan lives in two forms. The past is cut into the Table — a floor of slabs in a roofed gallery, incised, every channel proved since the beginning, a map you walk on. The future is charcoal on waxed cloth, hung, revised, argued over, wiped with a wet thumb. Stone for what cannot be changed, cloth for what can. Visitors always get this the wrong way round and assume the cloth is the draft of the stone. It is the other way: the stone is the wreckage of the cloth, the part that got away from them and became permanent.

## Wrong

There is a word here, *bite*, for an incision water makes where no one meant it to. A bite is forever. Everyone alive on the Welt can take you to seven or eight of them.

The great one is the Wander, opened forty-three years ago on the north shoulder, a fair channel that at its eleventh pace found a hollow no one's bowl had caught and turned three degrees east and has been turning east ever since and will be a gorge in nine hundred years, in the wrong place, across the ground where the east forest was to stand. It cannot be undone. Everything drawn since has been drawn around it. The plan for the year 900 is now the plan-around-the-Wander, and every drawer learns the reroute before they learn anything else, and it is not a scar on the work; it is the work; the Wander has shaped more of the future of this mountain than any decision made correctly.

The woman who opened it was called Sethe. She was not exiled, or shamed, or relieved of duty. She was required — this is the rule, and it is old — to be the one who redrew. She spent her remaining twenty-nine years drawing around her own mistake and she is the most honoured name they have, and her line-name is the Wander, and children are told about her admiringly, and if you suggest to a Welter that this is strange they will look at you with real bafflement and say: *a hidden bite is the only kind that kills us.*

So there is a formal thing. Whoever spoils a line strikes the hanging slab outside the bathhouse, two blows, and everyone comes, from wherever they are, and they go and look, all twenty-two, and they say nothing useful, and then they eat together — the mistake meal, better food than they normally have, grain porridge with fat in it and dried fruit from the carry. Then the cloths come down and the redrawing begins. They have found, over generations, that the meal is the load-bearing part.

## The scribble-ground

On the south-west flank there is a lobe of about ninety paces, walled off, which is a horror to look at. It is gouged everywhere. Water goes in and does thirty stupid things: doubles, ponds, splits, braids, dives into a blind sump and dies there. Nothing on it is fair. Nothing on it was ever proved. It is the ugliest ground on the mountain and it will be ugly for ten thousand years.

It is for the children. From about four they are allowed, there and nowhere else, to pour.

They are given a jug. They are given no instruction. They pour where they like and they watch it take, and then the next day they come back and pour again and it goes exactly where it went before and not where they now want it, and they cry, and this is the entire curriculum. There is no lesson afterwards. Nobody explains.

Grown Welters go to the scribble-ground sometimes, alone, and stand and look at the grooves they made when they were five, which are a finger deeper every century, which are the handwriting of a child who no longer exists, and which are the only truly private thing anyone here owns.

## Warm water, once a week

The bathhouse is the whole civic life of the place. One generous echoing room, corbelled, fed off a cistern on warm rock, steam, midges, the white spider in the wall-joints waiting on the midges. Everyone goes. Rank dissolves in it because the Bowl at eighty and the carrier at twenty are both just bodies in water, and all the great decisions of the Welt have been made there over years, naked, half-asleep, arguing about a hair.

It is also where they sing, which they do badly and only in the bath, in a droning stacked-fifths way that fits under the roar. The songs are mostly numbers. Sequences of levels, fall-rates, the litany of the proved channels in order of opening. A song is a way of carrying a survey four generations without a slab. They sing the whole Table twice a year and it takes most of a night and by the end only the old ones are still in the water.

They sleep afterwards in one long gallery, all of them, in a town with two thousand beds. They could each have a palace. They lie down in a row like bricks, because it is cold, and because they are twenty-two.

## What they put in the rock

The dead are wrapped in moss, laid in a scoop on the crest, walled about with dry courses, and marked with a cut of thumbwood — which throws one branch every eleven or twelve years, so that the mark is a clock, and you can read how long a person has been dead by counting elbows on a stick.

Then the mountain comes up and takes them. New lobes break over the crest, the walled scoop is engulfed, and in a century the grave is a seam.

Every generation or so a fresh lobe breaks out with something in it: a course of laid stone standing in raw rock, a hand's worth of dry-walling, a black stain of moss and a bone. When this happens the work stops. They go and sit with it, all twenty-two, for a day. They do not dig it out. They wall it round again and leave it and write down the date and the position, and the drawers add a small note to the cloth: *ancestor at this bearing, survey accordingly*, because the dead are now a feature of the terrain and water must be routed with respect to them like any other fact.

## The others who live here

The bird is the only creature they treat as a colleague. It ticks like the cooling stone, it nests only in bubbles in rock under fifty years old, and so it lives exactly where they work: on the frontier, in the new, moving outward at the same two hand-widths a year. A found nest delays a plan by one year. This is not sentiment and it is not negotiable and it costs them, sometimes, badly. A gutterwright will tell you the reason, if pressed, in a flat carrying voice: *the bird is also writing the first line.* Then they will not elaborate.

The spider is tolerated. The shrimp in the blind ponds are counted, pond by pond, once a year, in a tally that no one can explain the purpose of and no one has ever proposed stopping.

And the green. The green is the thing they do not discuss.

Moss arrives on the Welt faster than moss should. Faster than spores in this air can manage. It plates the shadows, furs the channels, upholsters every north curve six inches deep, and it is half the labour of every life here — scraping, scraping, because a hair of moss is a hair of fall and a hair of fall is a hundred generations of wrongness.

What the older drawers have noticed, and recorded, and not theorised about, is that the moss sometimes comes *early*. That it thickens along a bearing that has been agreed on cloth but not yet cut. That the green, now and then, is already lying in the line before anyone has put a chisel to it.

They write it down. They do not say it in the bathhouse. If you ask directly, a Welter will look at you for a moment with an expression that is not fear and not faith and not quite anything you have a name for, and will say that the record is in the gallery and you are welcome to read it, and will go back to work.

## What they are

They will tell you, if the conversation has gone well and the water is loud enough for privacy, that they are not inhabitants. A town implies people who live somewhere. Nobody lives on the Welt; the Welt is not a place yet, it is an event still occurring, and they are part of its occurring — a small warm organ the mountain has grown on its own back for the purpose of deciding where its water goes.

Twenty-two of them. Coughing, damp, half-deaf, superb. Holding a bowl of water and a thread on new stone, cutting a hair at a time, two hundred generations short of finishing, entirely certain of the thing they are for.

A gutterwright, dying, is asked one question, and it is not about the soul. Someone kneels by the pallet and says: *is there anything you got wrong that we don't know about?*

And they answer. Always. That is the whole of their religion, and it has never once failed them.
