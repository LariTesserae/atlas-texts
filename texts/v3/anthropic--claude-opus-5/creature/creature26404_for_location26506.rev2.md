# The Inhabitants

Begin under the mud, because that is the only thing here with tenure.

Two centimetres into the base of a fresh cut bank, in the dark where the pore water is the colour of strong tea and smells of iron and old eggs, they sit in their thousands: the chalk-coloured nodules, forty thousand to the square metre of active face, warm to a fingertip that has already gone stupid with cold. They are the only residents of the Increase who cannot leave. Everything else here — every goose, every willow, every person, every wolf — arrived, and arrived recently, and can go. The nodules were put here and they stay here and they die here and their bodies become the beach.

They are all cousins. A hundred billion of them, and every one descended from five tonnes of pale powder released off the stern of a landing craft over a single August, thirty-one years ago, into water that was then forty kilometres of open fetch. They recruit rather than reproduce, if you want the manual's word for it. At about three years a nodule begins laying down a daughter in its own lee, using the last of its capacity to gather, and having finished it goes chalky, then friable, then it is sand — a specific grade, 0.15 millimetres, angular, pale. It shows in every core as a thin light band, and the loggers, who are not poets, call it ash.

A quarter of them are dying at any given moment. Twenty-five billion deaths a year and no funerals, and the delta is built out of the corpses at a rate you can measure from orbit.

Their thinking, such as it is, travels at the speed of water through silt, which is a few centimetres a day. A decision to abandon a bank and let a channel swing east takes eleven weeks to propagate and cannot be recalled. They have no model of the river. They have never experienced the river; they experience the pressure of it the way you experience weather in an old joint. Local shear, local temperature, local gradient, and a rule about how much of the neighbour's opinion to weight. That's the whole mind. A thermostat's worth, spread over four hundred square kilometres, and it is right more often than the modellers are.

Nobody planned for them to diverge, but they copy themselves imperfectly and the copies are selected on, and the eastern stock is now measurably not the western stock. The manual has no word for this. The crew says *the east stuff is hungrier.*

---

If you want to know what lives here by weight, the answer is worms.

Under the sedge, in the anoxic warm silt where the nodules are working, there are eighty thousand chironomid larvae to the square metre, blood-red with haemoglobin because there is no oxygen down there worth the name. Oligochaetes threading the peat. Amphipods in the channel margins in numbers that turn a headlamp beam grainy. Ninespine stickleback in every flooded thumbprint of ground, and when the ice-jam water drains in ninety minutes at three in the morning it leaves them stranded in the moss in their tens of thousands, and by noon the gulls have eaten them, and this is not a disaster, it is the system working.

Below all of that, in the drowned peat, the methanogens, which are the oldest kind of thing here and are quietly running the argument in the other direction. Every band of buried willow is carbon locked away. Every band of buried willow is also a slow bubble factory. There is a person whose entire professional life is that ledger. In October, when the new ice is black and clear, the overwintering hand punches holes along the old sloughs and puts a match to the seeps, and the flames stand up half a metre, blue, hissing, and this is the only fire anyone in the Increase enjoys.

And on every nodule, unplanned, a biofilm. The bacteria arrived on their own and their slimes turn out to be flocculants too. Roughly eight per cent of the mud-catching in the Increase is being done by organisms that were not invited and are not on anyone's asset register.

---

The geese do not live here. They stage here, which is a different tenancy and a stronger one.

Two hundred thousand at the peak, most years: lesser snow, greater white-fronted, cackling, a wedge of brant that keeps to itself down near the outer bars. A white-front comes in off the last leg at two and a half kilos with seven hundred grams of fat on it, and that fat was assembled in stubble and rice four thousand kilometres south, and what the bird does not spend on the flight it spends here, and a good part of what it spends stays in the willows as nitrogen. Another continent's agriculture, imported by air, unloaded onto a mud flat. The willow mat grows the way it does — that obscene, heaving, half-metre-deep springiness — because it is being fertilised by Texas.

They pay for it. Snow geese grub; they pull sedge rhizomes out of new marsh and can strip a shoreline to grey pan in three springs. Here the willow outruns them, mostly, and the design assumes it will, and there is a modeller who does not sleep well about that assumption and says so annually.

The important thing about the flock is that it remembers. A staging site is not a place, it is a habit, carried in juveniles who learned it from adults who learned it from adults. The Increase took eleven years to get into the culture of that population. It is in there now, and it will outlast the contract, the funding, the country that signed the funding. The geese do not know they are employed.

They are also, individually, idiots. Nothing panics forty thousand birds off shallow water; the decision is made in two hundred milliseconds by no one, propagates like a crack in glass, and every bird in the air would tell you, if it could, that it went because the others went. So: two minds working this delta at the same time. One that thinks in eleven-week increments through pore water and never makes a sudden move, and one that thinks in a fifth of a second and does nothing else.

The cranes come in under them, lower and rustier, in pairs, and stand about in the drowned meadow with the manner of landowners. Later — not tonight, not for weeks — swans, dunlin, godwits, phalaropes spinning in the ditch water, and by June the willow passerines: redpolls, yellow warblers, tree sparrows, a sound like a jeweller's shop compared to this. Tonight there are geese, gulls screaming about nothing, three resident ravens who overwinter at the bunkhouse and once stole a glove off a drying line and would do it again, and one peregrine on the count mast, which is the tallest object for twenty kilometres and is therefore hers, not ours.

---

Everything with fur here is either swimming or walking the drift line.

Tundra voles, which swim well and drown anyway, in numbers that boom and crash on a two-year rhythm laid over a flood cycle nobody can predict, so that the owls and the jaegers come in some years and not others and no one has ever managed to say why in advance. Muskrat in the older lake basins. Mink, which are vicious and everywhere and rarely seen. Otters working the ice edge, the only mammal in the Increase that appears to be enjoying breakup.

One wolverine. Possibly two. Known entirely by track and by what it does to a cache, ranging over the whole of this and a great deal of country beyond it, and the crew have given it a name that is not written down anywhere official.

A bear, thin from the den, working the drift line for whatever the flood killed. Deaf this week, like everything else.

Moose arrived in year nineteen, when the willow finally got tall enough to be worth a winter. The first one was a yearling bull and somebody photographed him from the catwalk and the print is still on the bunkhouse wall, curling, water-stained, the date in biro. There are perhaps sixty now. Where there are sixty moose there are eventually wolves, and there were wolves in year twenty-six, and now there is a pack that is hard on the moose and hard on the fish camps' dogs and is, by any honest reading of the project documents, an unbudgeted success.

---

Six bunks. Rarely six people. In breakup, five.

Two hydrologists, a bird tech, a systems hand who services the housings that do not blink, and the mechanic, who is the most important person in the Increase and knows it and is pleasant about it. They sleep in four-hour pieces. They eat standing up. During the three weeks of the count they cannot speak to one another outdoors and so they don't; they use the signs.

There are about sixty of the signs, taped inside the door on a chart drawn in year eleven and amended since in four different hands. Three of them are obscene. Most came out of fish-camp hand talk, a few out of old radio protocol, one or two out of nowhere. *Ice moving. Ice stopped. Water coming. Go back. Bring the boat. I'm fine. Don't.* There is a sign for the specific event of a bank calving out from under you — one flat hand dropping away from the other — and it does not mean *look*, it means *run*.

Everyone who has done more than five seasons here has the same audiogram: a clean notch at four kilohertz, both ears, from the geese and not from the ice. The company issues plugs. Nobody wears them on the bank, because you need to hear the ice, and the ones who have been here longest say they don't hear it anyway, they read it through the soles of their boots, and this is probably true and is certainly what they tell the new ones.

---

The families were here first, in the sense that matters.

Their grandparents' village stood on a spit that the sea took at three metres a year, and in the second bad decade they moved upriver, and some of them came back down for fish and never stopped coming. Three camps now, drying racks and nets and a boat lashed down under the silt. Whitefish, broad and humpback; least cisco; burbot in the deep bends; sheefish, which are the ones worth talking about; and every few years a salmon that has no business being this far north and is photographed like a UFO.

They have names for the islands. The audit numbers them by year and channel — N-19-3, W-24-1 — and the families call them after events and people: the island where the outboard was lost, the island named for a woman who is still alive and finds the whole thing funny. Where the two systems disagree, the crew uses the families' names on the radio and the audit's names in writing, and everybody understands exactly what each document is for.

On one of the older islands, under four metres of laminated silt and a stand of alder, is a house. The family knows which island. They do not dig.

---

The chapel was put up by a man who had the posts in the boat for something else. His name is known and he did not want it known and that request has been honoured for nineteen years. Everybody uses it, including the ones who would tell you they don't, because it is the only structure in the Increase that is not part of a machine — you can sit in it and be counted by nothing.

It is also where the drowned are held in mind, because they cannot be held in ground. You cannot dig a grave in a place that lays down a metre of silt a decade and swings a channel through a meadow in two days. Two of the lost were never recovered; the river has them and has moved them and has very likely made land out of them by now, and the families have said this out loud, and found that they could live with it.

Everyone else goes sixty kilometres upriver to the first gravel bluff with rock under it. That graveyard is the oldest human thing connected to this place, and it is not in this place, and that fact tells you more about the Increase than any figure in the audit.

---

Nineteen names on the winter list, in thirty-one years. This year it is a woman in her sixties who runs the ice-thickness transects alone and does not explain herself and is currently rebuilding the stove for the third time. She is the one who lights the methane in October.

The auditors come in August, two days, clean boots, helicopter, questions about the carbon ledger. Somebody is always polite to them. The ledger is why any of this continues, and everyone knows that too.

And out past the last seeded bank, beyond the line you can see from the air, the coast is still going at three metres a year, because nothing was put in the water there. There is a village on that coast. Its people come here to fish and are welcome and the conversations do not get finished.

---

Nothing here has finished arriving. There are no specialists in the Increase — no lichens that need a century, no cavity nesters, no fungi that want undisturbed ground, no human being who has lived their whole life in one spot of it. The place selects hard, and what it selects for is things that move fast, breed faster than they can be buried, or know when to leave.

Ask any of them what lives here and each will name a different animal. The crew says geese. The families say whitefish. The audit says carbon. The nodules, if they could be asked and if you were prepared to wait eleven weeks, would say that the gradient on the east face is too steep and something ought to be done about it.

They are all describing the same creature from different ends of it.

At three in the morning the jam lets go somewhere upstream and the whole bunkhouse comes awake sitting up, five people in the pitch black with a noise in the walls like the end of the world, and nobody says anything, because nobody would be heard. Each of them makes the sign for *I'm fine* — flat hand, sternum, twice — in a room where it cannot possibly be seen.

Then they lie back down, and the geese go on, and outside the land gets bigger.
